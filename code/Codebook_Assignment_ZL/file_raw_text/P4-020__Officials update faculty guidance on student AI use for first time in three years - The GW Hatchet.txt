Officials update faculty guidance on student AI use for first time in three years
By Arunmoy Das, Assistant News Editor
August 27, 2026
Arwen Clemans | Staff Photographer A student uses Chat GPT.
Officials updated their guidance for students' use of artificial intelligence in classes for the first time since 2023 as GW works to establish a University-wide strategy. 
Provost Ed Balleisen announced Wednesday in an email to students new guidelines for using AI tools and platforms in academic work after consulting the Faculty Senate's Educational Policy & Technology Committee, school deans and deans' leadership teams. The Provost Office's updated guidelines build on former Provost Chris Bracey's 2023 guidance, encouraging professors to discuss AI usage with students early in the semester and expanding the scope of the guidelines to include not just AI chatbots but also AI-embedded technologies.
Bracey's original guidelines - which his office published in April 2023 - highlighted the promise of generative AI tools, encouraging students and faculty to thoughtfully use it in their teaching and research. The guidance provided model language for three tiers of AI policies that professors may choose from. Professors could allow students general permission to use AI in all assignments, only allow them to use AI on specific designated assignments or completely prohibit its usage on all assignments.
Bracey's guidance states if professors do not list a specific AI policy in their syllabus or discuss one in class, the default rules in the Code of Academic Integrity apply, which state students may not submit content generated, in whole or in part, by AI, and doing so without an instructor's explicit permission constitutes cheating. 
Bracey also announced additional guidance in August 2023, encouraging professors to reinforce what plagiarism is, include a declaration of academic honesty on assignments for students to fill out and have students submit draft submissions so professors better understand what a student's voice sounds like. The statement also affirmed that the guidelines are suggestions for professors to implement in their classes and are not binding policies.
Balleisen's updated language largely maintains Bracey's guidance, but adds an explanation that growing research shows excessive reliance in AI can prevent students from developing crucial skills, like close reading, developing a distinct, effective writing style and applying logic. Balleisen's model language for professors seeking to provide general permission for AI usage in the classroom also warns students not to simply "cut and paste" large chunks of GAI outputs into their work. 
"Given these cross-currents, it is incumbent on all instructors to engage with the fundamental implications that AI poses for their teaching," the guidance states.
While Bracey's guidance called for professors to specify their AI policies clearly in their syllabus, Balleisen's update also encourages professors to discuss their policy with students within the first weeks of classes. Balleisen's guidance states this in-class discussion can provide "pedagogical context" for students to understand why a professor has a certain policy. The guidelines also advise instructors to consult the Center for Teaching Excellence in the Division of Libraries & Academic Innovation on more resources for incorporating AI tools into their teaching.
The guidance calls on students to ensure their claims are still accurate and that outside ideas, text and images are given proper citations when using AI. The guidance largely maintains Bracey's model language for professors allowing general permission, selective permission and general prohibitions of AI. 
Balleisen states officials updated the guidelines in response to the "quickly" evolving AI landscape, including greater availability of GAI tools - like ChatGPT and other large language models - increasing GAI integration into basic software and web browsers and emerging "powerful" AI agents, along with its potential negative impact on students and their academic growth. The guidance specifically mentions "AI-embedded technologies" like Google Glasses as prohibited under the Code of Academic Integrity, an update from the 2023 guidance. 
The update comes as officials announced earlier this month that professor of engineering management and systems engineering Zoe Szajnfarber will serve as a special advisor on artificial intelligence to University President Ellen Granberg and Balleisen as GW enters its next phase for developing an AI strategy. The announcement followed the completion of a University-wide AI mapping exercise that launched last fall to assess how GW currently uses AI and identify opportunities and risks associated with the technology.
Faculty members last fall said since the 2023 guidance was released, they've crafted their own policies to adapt to how the technology has advanced in the past few years and its application to different academic fields has evolved.
The Student Government Association and Faculty Senate in the spring passed contrasting resolutions for how they envision the University's AI policy. The SGA's resolution urged officials to reframe the University's AI policy, emphasizing AI's potential as an educational resource and accessibility tool, and encouraging faculty to view students' AI usage from a less critical lens and reduce rising academic integrity cases by clarifying guidelines. While the Faculty Senate resolution recommended the University consult EPT before implementing technological decisions, and members said the SGA's resolution ignored decreased student engagement with course material stemming from increased AI usage.
