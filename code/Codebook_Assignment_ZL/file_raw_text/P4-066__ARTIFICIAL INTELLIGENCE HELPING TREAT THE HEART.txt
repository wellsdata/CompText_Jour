Artificial intelligence is helping clinicians interpret the human heart.
Carnegie Mellon University researchers and Cleveland Clinic's Cardiovascular Innovation Research Center in Ohio developed an AI system to read cardiac Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI) - the "gold standard for evaluating heart structure, function and tissue health," according to a joint statement on Tuesday.
"Cardiac MRI interpretation is highly specialized and time intensive," said David Chen of Cleveland Clinic, a co-principal investigator on the project.
Using 13,000 patient studies from the clinic and more than a million images collected over a decade, the team trained a system called CMR-CLIP.
A specialized clinician can spend at least 40 minutes reviewing hundreds of images and manually annotating exams, and that expertise is in low supply as demand grows, the release said. The CMR-CLIP system interprets scans (which can include hundreds of thousands of individual frames) using existing resources - a trove of past scans and the radiology reports that clinicians already fill out for every exam.
"Instead of relying on manual labels, the team trained CMR-CLIP to align MRI image sequences with these natural language clinical summaries, enabling the model to learn directly from how physicians describe and interpret scans in practice," the release said.
Data is the foundation of any AI model, but "in cardiac imaging, expert annotations are scarce, time-consuming to produce and costly to scale." Using radiology reports "already embedded in routine clinical workflows" was the team's way around their limited data problem.
"Systems like CMR-CLIP have the potential to support clinicians through automated screening, and interpretation support, particularly in settings where expert readers are limited," Chen said. "Such reader assistant tools are critical to improving patient access to this powerful diagnostic technology."
This kind of technology not only saves time, the researchers said, but can interpret complex heart scans up to 35% better than existing general AI models. CMR-CLIP was able to identify conditions it had never been trained on "simply by matching images to descriptive prompts like 'enlarged left ventricle.' "
And rather than representing the exam as static images, the system represents the images as a video, which allows it to monitor not only structure, but movement across time - just as a clinician would interpret the images from multiple angles and time stamps by hand.
"This work demonstrates that domain-specific foundation models can significantly outperform general-purpose AI systems in specialized clinical applications," said Ding Zhao, associate professor in CMU's Department of Mechanical Engineering and co-principal investigator on the study. "By designing models that reflect the structure and complexity of cardiac MRI data, rather than adapting generic image models, we can unlock new levels of performance and clinical utility."
Researchers said that CMR-CLIP proved it was "truly learning meaningful representations" when it successfully performed with separate datasets it never "saw" before - one set collected in France, and the other from Cleveland Clinic Florida - meaning the technology can potentially be generalized across hospital systems.
