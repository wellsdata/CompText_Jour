Q: I have two yellow butterfly vines (Mascagnia macroptera). I've tried nurseries here for the past two years and they no longer stock them. I tried planting seeds early this spring into small containers, and luckily one out of 24 did germinate. It's now 6-8 inches tall. A friend had no success after planting about the same number. Do you have suggestions for us for this year? See photo of the seeds forming from this year's crop. 
A: I'll begin by saying that I have not grown this plant from seed. However, since it is a heat-loving plant from South Texas and Mexico, I would consider planting the seeds a little later -- perhaps late March into April when it's warmer. 
Soak them overnight and then sow them into 4-inch pots, maybe three seeds per pot. If more than one germinates in a pot, so much the better. Use a loose, well-draining potting soil for the planting, but keep it moist at all times. Place the pots in a sunny, very warm spot. The seedlings need to grow much faster than your one plant has grown. 
Most resources recommend taking softwood cuttings for your propagation method. That would be tip growth from this upcoming season's new shoots, and the time would be late spring into early summer. 
Cuttings should be 4-6 inches long. Pinch off the end 1-2 inches of each cutting. Dip the basal ends into rooting hormone powder. Tap off the excess and carefully insert the cuttings into pots filled with the same planting mix described above. 
Cover them loosely with dry cleaner's plastic to hold the humidity at a high level, then place them in a bright spot but out of direct sunlight until they form roots. Pot them into 1-gallon containers, several cuttings per pot, and they should be ready for planting into the garden later this season. 
Q: I bought an orange tree from a local nursery in early 2022. It was supposed to be really sweet and hardier to cold than normal. I do not remember the variety. We had a hard freeze in December that year and the top froze back to the ground. In spring 2025, I was ready to cut down the tree when I noticed it had regrown from below the graft. To my surprise, that growth produced seven oranges. I harvested some of those oranges recently, but they had hardly any juice, and were white and stringy on the inside. Should I remove the tree, or is there something else I should be doing to get quality oranges? 
A: You have the rootstock orange, not the high-quality sweet orange you originally bought. If you still want to try an orange, you'll need to replant. Texas A&M has information on the best varieties that offer somewhat more cold tolerance, but even they were hurt by the cold of February 2021. Do your homework before making your purchase. 
Q: How can I prevent new growth from sprouting from the ends of branches I trim off my fig tree? 
A: Make your cuts flush with remaining branches. In other words, don't leave stubs. 
That advice goes for other types of plants you may be pruning, crape myrtles for example. You never want to leave stubs. You'll notice the stub isn't healing properly. Decay is setting in. That's a quick way to lose an entire branch, sometimes even an entire plant. 
Q: I planted ryegrass so my lawn would be green this winter, but it isn't very dark green like some of the other lawns I see in our city. Should I have been fertilizing it? 
A: Most lawn maintenance people do feed their ryegrass plantings in late fall, commonly December before the cold weather begins. 
You could still apply a high-nitrogen lawn food at half the rate recommended for normal lawn feeding. Follow with a thorough watering. Your warm-season turfgrass won't be functioning vigorously enough to try to grow now, but the rye should benefit from it. 
Repeat that application in a warm spell in late February, and the rye should be off to the races. 
Q: I have a short row of five glossy abelias that have gotten very leggy over the 20 years we have been in our house. They are 6-7 feet tall. Can I trim them back to 3-4 feet so they will fill in? If so, when should I do it, and how? 
A: Yes. Use lopping shears to remove all the stubbly dead or unneeded branches one at a time. You may end up with only half as many main stems when you're finished, but that's OK. 
Do that pruning in the next three to four weeks, and follow it up in late February with a feeding of a high-nitrogen lawn food to promote vigorous new growth when spring's warm weather begins. 
If the plants send out long, straight shoots beyond where you want them, keep them headed back to encourage thickened growth. 
Q: Why won't my weedkiller spray control clover in my lawn? 
A: There are several weeds that get lumped together as "clovers." A couple of them are difficult to eliminate. White clover is a perennial weed that is a tough survivor. Sheep sorrel is oxalis, a plant with compound leaves that resemble clover. However, it produces distinctly upright stems, and its small yellow flowers are bell-shaped, unlike those of the other clovers. 
Control of both of those cool-season weeds can be improved by adding one drop of liquid dishwashing detergent to each gallon of broad-leaf weedkiller containing 2,4-D. That will help the spray remain on the leaves without beading up and running off their waxy surfaces. 
I also recommend using a tank sprayer rather than a hose-end sprayer. You'll use far less of the active ingredient because you'll be better able to adjust the spray to a fine droplet size. The herbicide will coat the leaf surfaces without running off. 
Finally, don't mow for two to three days before or after you spray, and don't irrigate for a couple of days after as well.
