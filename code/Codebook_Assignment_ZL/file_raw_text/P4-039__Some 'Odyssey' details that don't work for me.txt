Body


Can you stand a few more words about "The Odyssey?" 
Actually, that would have been a great opening line for the Bard (played by hip-hop artist Travis Scott), sort of a Homer stand-in who kicks off Christopher Nolan's much-debated and well-attended movie adaptation. 
"The Odyssey" has already grossed more than $300 million worldwide. It has become, in several senses, a destination movie. At a time when it's tough to get people to go to movie theaters, "Odyssey" tickets are being scalped, especially if they're for 70mm IMAX screens, which Nolan called the "best possible experience" for movie-watching. (There are only 25 proper 70mm IMAX screens in the entire United States.) 
I saw it on a standard screen, which is considered only slightly better than hand puppets, apparently. 
I'm hesitant about sharing my misgivings, because so much of the pre-hatred of this movie came from the Moron Tabernacle Choir of Right-Wing Culture Warriors, for whom I do not wish to provide succor, possibly in contravention of Zeus's law. 
Let me begin by listing persons and things that are not problems: Travis Scott (see above). Anne Hathaway as Penelope, wife of Odysseus. John Bernthal as Menelaus (king of Sparta and occasional husband of Helen of Troy). Samantha Morton as Circe. Robert Pattinson as the story's most hate-worthy villain. John Leguizamo as the blind servant Eumaeus. Lupita Nyong'o, who has taken enormous amount of crypto-racist grief for being cast as Helen of Troy and her ornithologically twin sister Clytemnestra, wife of Agamemnon. 
Also not a problem: the American accents, complained about by some Moron Choir members because, apparently, the ancient Greeks spoke with British accents. The multi-national, multi-ethnic cast, complained about by Moron altos and tenors who abruptly decided this was not a timeless, universal story about the human condition but "a Greek thing," basically an ethnic pageant to be staged only by the locals in town squares. 
Adapting Homer is cool. It's so widespread that last spring Yale classics professor Benedek Kruchió taught an entire course on adaptations and reimaginings of "The Odyssey." Presumably this includes the novel set in Dublin where the characters pick their noses and touch themselves. Kruchió has pointed out that Homer's poem itself is almost certainly an adaptation of preexisting material. 
Another strength of the movie: all of the scenes set in Ithaca, where 100 or so "suitors" have parked themselves for years, eating, drinking and carrying on as they wait for official confirmation that Odysseus is dead, so they can seek Penelope's hand in marriage. 
Nolan artfully conveys the menace, the instability, the arrogance and the necessity of the eventual slaughter of all suitors. 
Nolan gives us a kinder, gentler Odysseus. In the poem he slaughters the suitors and then instructs his son Telemachus to make the slave women clean up all the blood and flesh, after which the slave women themselves are to be cut to pieces for making sexy time with the suitors. Telemachus tweaks the order and hangs the women, presumably because he doesn't feel like paying SERVPRO to come in and clean up another mess. This second mass killing is omitted from the film. 
Now we come to what does not work for me. I adore Matt Damon. I am happy that my stay on Earth has coincided with the Matt Damon era in film. And I can't really tell whether Damon is a bad choice because he simply does not contain (Tom Ripley notwithstanding) the dark depths of Odysseus or whether Nolan chose Damon because he was planning all along to make Odysseus kind of a Gen. James Mattis figure -- a guy who got caught up in something before he realized how ugly it could get. 
But Matt is not right in this role, and the problem is exacerbated by some of his lines. A caveat: I'm a fan of Emily Wilson, the superstar translator who believes Homeric characters should talk the way people talk. 
But Odysseus cannot be what Bill Parcells used to call Just a Guy. He can't talk like the guy Damon played in "We Bought a Zoo." ("We Built a Horse?") Asked by his men if the boats are fully provisioned, Odysseus says, "A couple of stops on the way home won't kill us," and it sounds like they're going to be on the Mass Pike. 
When Odysseus finds Sinon (Elliot Page), a young soldier from Ithaca, dying on the beach in Troy, Sinon begs him "If I never make it home, take this (keepsake) back to my father. Tell him everything I did." And then he adds, "Odysseus! Feel free to add some stuff." 
I laughed. Should I be laughing when poor Sinon is croaking? 
Nolan's challenge is not what stuff to add but what stuff to subtract. Homer's poem turns the Greater Peloponnesian Transit District into a vast amusement park full of one-eyed giants, whirlpools, multi-mouthed monsters, hypnotic singers and sundry sorceresses. Audiences kind of expect these rides to be open. It's a lot to cover while doing justice to the Ithaca narrative and Telemachus' gap year in Sparta. 
The result feels a bit like speed-dating, where you barely get to know one person ("Have you ever turned one of your dates into a pig?") before you're on to the next. When Calypso (Charlize Theron) pops up, she and Odysseus already need couples counseling. Why? How? 
Last thing: Who among us does not love Zendaya? Nolan cast her as Athena, the only god in the movie, apparently with strict instructions not to upstage Damon. She is oddly bland. I wouldn't have guessed anyone could turn Zendaya into a wallflower. 
Despite these misgivings, I tip my hat to Nolan for trying to make an important movie that questions some of our linkages between valor and brutality. 
Whether he succeeded or not may take another 3,000 years to decide. Or at least until October.
