Tenants start lining up at the Eviction Defense Collaborative offices on San Francisco's Mission Street hours before its drop-in clinic opens at 10 a.m. 
Some are facing eviction due to nuisance or nonpayment of rent, while others are worried about a big rent hike or an owner moving into their unit. 
"Today we had a line waiting when we opened this morning and a line at lunch of people coming in," said EDC Executive Director Gail Gilman on a recent Friday. "We were busy until the moment we closed our doors." 
The eviction notice statistics are way below the levels of the last two tech surges, but tenant advocates say the real estate pressures that have historically come along with San Francisco booms are back again. 
Despite San Francisco having some of the strongest tenant protections in the country, advocates are worried that struggling residents will be pushed out of their homes. The concerns come amid an intensifying housing crisis accelerated by decades of underbuilding and the high-flying artificial intelligence industry. 
For property owners, the incentives to turn over units are clear. After the pandemic-fueled downturn, there is money to be made again in San Francisco real estate, with median rents up 26% in a 12-month period. A one-bedroom unit fetches a median of about $3,900, and the median single-family home is now valued at almost $1.6 million. 
Malea Chavez, managing attorney for the eviction prevention program at the Mission Economic Development Agency, said the number of tenants seeking help more than tripled in the past year to more than 300 a month. 
In the first seven months of the year, the Chinatown Community Development Center saw a more than 70% increase in eviction cases compared with the same period last year, according to senior counsel Gen Fujioka. 
"The central pattern is the same -- the market picks up and so does the efforts to evict existing tenants," he said. 
The Chronicle looked at eviction notices to gauge whether tenants are feeling more pressure. Most notices don't wind up translating into actual evictions. 
While eviction warnings are well below the heights of the last two booms, they jumped 44% in the most recent 12-month period tracked, hitting nearly 1,500. 
The San Francisco Rent Board tracks notices of eviction, a first step in the process that often leads to a buyout agreement between the landlord and tenant. By far the leading cause of eviction notices is nonpayment of rent, although the rent board doesn't require that those cases be registered with the city. The second most common is "committing a nuisance," followed by "breach of rental agreement." The rent board tracks statistics from March to February, rather than by calendar or fiscal year. 
During San Francisco's last two tech booms, eviction notices also skyrocketed. Eviction notices more than doubled to a high of over 2,800 in a year spanning 1998-99, up from about 1,000 during various 12-month periods in the early and mid-1990s. They dropped in the years after the dot-com crash in 2001, but spiked again to almost 2,400 during a 12-month period spanning 2015-16, which coincided with the growth of Twitter, Salesforce and Airbnb. 
There are other ways to measure the market beyond evictions. Negotiated buyouts, where landlords often pay tens of thousands to get rid of rent-controlled tenants, jumped from 250 in 2024 to about 400 last year. 
The majority of rental units in San Francisco are covered by rent control, which limits how much a landlord can increase the rent. But most single-family homes and condominiums are exempt from rent control rules, as are buildings built after 1979. 
Attorney Joe Tobener, who represents tenants, said nearly a quarter of the inquiries his firm gets daily are from tenants in condominiums and single-family homes who have received rent increases that are "off the charts." 
"People are trying to find out if they have rights," he said. "The way the cycle works is, first you get the single-family homes and condos because that is the low-hanging fruit. The next thing will be a rise in evictions and buyouts for the larger buildings. Then the real estate speculators start coming in." 
For tenants, the current surge is both better and worse than previous booms, according to attorneys and nonprofit housing leaders. 
There are more protections in place. A 2018 voter-approved proposition guaranteed free legal counsel to tenants facing eviction. The city budget includes $17 million for the "tenant right to counsel" programs, which supports about 2,500 cases a year. The demand, though, is closer to 3,500 cases, according to Ora Prochovnick, director of litigation and policy at the Eviction Defense Collaborative. 
The city has also boosted its investment in affordable housing, although the demand for it has only grown. 
Organizations like Mission Economic Development Agency have developed thousands of affordable units. MEDA has built about 500 affordable housing units and has another 700 in its pipeline. It has also acquired another 400 apartments through the city's small sites program. The new affordable units complement the organization's investment in eviction defense and relocation assistance. 
"The difference between now and before is now we have the infrastructure to help families," said MEDA Executive Director Luis Granados. 
One tenant who avoided eviction thanks to MEDA was Monica Briggs, a senior caregiver who lives in a studio in the Inner Mission. Briggs received a three-day notice to "quit or pay" last spring because her property manager had misplaced a rent payment and started assessing penalties on the uncashed check. 
"I was afraid I would have to live in the street," she said. "I cried a lot and was just wandering around asking for help. Someone told me to come to MEDA." 
At MEDA, counselors helped her track down and cancel the missing money order and cut a new check with the help from the city Emergency Rental Assistance Program funds to stop the eviction process. 
The city has been taking other steps to get ahead of what could be a wave of evictions, according to Sam Cobb, the CEO of the anti-poverty nonprofit Tipping Point Community, which Mayor Daniel Lurie founded in 2005. 
Tipping Point invested $11 million in Lurie's family homelessness prevention plan, an 18-month effort offering financial assistance, employment support and legal services. 
For Civic Center resident Esmerelda Celis, the program was a godsend. 
A mother of four children, Celis was on the brink of losing her $2,700-a month McAllister Street apartment last spring after her husband, a server at a Fisherman's Wharf restaurant, was deported to Mexico and got stuck there for four months while awaiting approval to reenter the United States. 
"By February we were a month behind," said Celis. "I was starting to stress out and my health was starting to decline. My biggest fear was getting an eviction notice. I didn't want my children to have to live out of a vehicle." 
Through a grant from the homelessness prevention pilot, Celis was able to cover three months of rent and stay housed. 
Cobb said the current boom could be "harsher" than previous cycles in terms of keeping families in housing. 
"We are going from a place where the costs are already unreasonable and unsustainable, and they are just going to go higher," he said. 
"More people who are at higher income levels are coming into the city, which is a good thing," Cobb said, "but it also squeezes out other people." 
There are indications that the next wave of evictions will hit a broader cross section of households, both in terms of geography and income, Prochovnick said. While previously the Mission District was the focus of much of the displacement, cases are increasingly spread across the city, including in the Sunset, Richmond and Marina, she said. In addition, the number of Eviction Defense Collaborative clients who earn at least $72,000 a year increased 53% year over year to 98. 
The stress of eviction can be intense. Meagan Cunningham is among a group of North Beach tenants facing an Ellis Act eviction at 2055-2059 Powell St. The Ellis Act, which became popular during the dot-com boom of 1999, allows property owners to clear out rent-controlled units if they don't plan to rent them out again. 
A group of buyers who all live in North Beach, including one of the current renters, bought the seven-unit rent controlled building recently for $2.4 million with the intention of moving in. 
Cunningham, a voice-over actor who moved into the building as a teen in 1987, said the situation goes against the spirit of inclusivity that has long defined North Beach. 
"I don't want to attribute it to one kind of particular kind of worker or industry, but there is a real sense of arrogance and entitlement. It's like we don't matter," said Cunningham, who lives in the unit with her 31-year-old son. "There has always been wealth in this city. But it used to be more mixed economically. That was the beauty of North Beach." 
Situations like the one on Powell Street cause "compression," Gilman said, with evicted households ending up in one-bedrooms or studios, increasing competition for those units and potentially forcing lower-income tenants out. 
While the current boom won't last forever, the question is how many families will be forced out of the city in the meantime, Prochovnick said. 
"The AI bubble will eventually burst, as the previous two did, but the damage won't get undone," she said. "Each time we have had these cycles, it has caused great pain to the rental market and to the diversity of our city. I fear we are seeing that again."
