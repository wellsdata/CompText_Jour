


UCSF, Stanford launch $200 million cancer hub to drive breakthroughs in care

Body


UCSF and Stanford are launching a $200 million cancer hub that will be the largest collaborative project of its kind on the West Coast, with the goal to dramatically improve cancer care, especially for the most stubborn diseases, over the next decade.
The Weill Family Foundation is funding the center with a $100 million matching donation, for which Stanford and UCSF together have raised about $25 million in institutional funds; each university will raise $50 million over the next decade.
The first four grants from the hub, announced on Wednesday, will support “moonshot” projects focused on harnessing immune therapies, artificial intelligence and breakthroughs in metabolic research to fight the most stubborn cancers.
Scientists at both institutions said the financial support is remarkable, and comes at a time when other funding sources — most notably federal money — are becoming less reliable. The National Institutes of Health, the country’s largest scientific funding mechanism, has ended or delayed more than 2,000 grants since President Donald Trump took office in January, ostensibly because the research was too political. Many scientists fear their work will be impacted, if it hasn’t already.
The Weill donation demonstrates strong support and respect for scientists who need that message right now, administrators from both campuses said.
“It’s a huge boost to the community to see private individuals believing in them and the mission of science,” said UCSF Chancellor Sam Hawgood. “It feels really good for people who are already in their career to see that their work is being respected. But it’s also sending an incredibly strong signal to young people: This shows there’s a path, and there are people out there who are excited about what they’re doing.”
Joan and Sandy Weill are longtime philanthropists who have contributed more than $400 million to scientific research in the Bay Area, and more than $1 billion globally. They launched the Weill Neurohub at Mission Bay in 2019, drawing scientists from UCSF, UC Berkeley, the University of Washington and the Allen Institute to focus on neurological and psychiatric diseases.
The Weill Cancer Hub West is the second collaborative cancer center of its kind — the first, Weill Cancer Hub East announced in March, brought together Weill Cornell Medicine, Princeton University, the Rockefeller University and the Ludwig Institute for Cancer Research.
“A lot of cancers have been cured, yet there’s still plenty of things that have been very complicated,” said Sandy Weill. He said it made sense to bring together scientists from the two “best cancer facilities” in the Bay Area for a second hub. “A good percentage of people at Stanford came from UCSF and vice versa — there’s great respect there,” Weill said.
Collaboration in cancer research is becoming increasingly vital as scientists work to translate recent breakthroughs to actual patient care, researchers from both Stanford and UCSF said. Since the 1970s, when President Richard Nixon declared a “war on cancer,” the United States has made enormous investments in research and made astounding progress toward preventing and treating disease, largely by refining tools like chemotherapy and radiation.
Now scientists are working on the next generation of tools, many of which are still in their infancy in terms of how well they’re understood and how they’ll be applied. 
“We’ve seen enormous advances in the cancer field, but we’re just now at the beginning of a revolution to diagnose, treat and prevent cancers,” said Lloyd Minor, dean of the Stanford University School of Medicine.
Minor noted that breast cancer survival rates have improved dramatically over the past 40 years in large part because of the personalized approach to treating patients — a strategy that should be applied more broadly across cancer treatments. “No patient with breast cancer gets a standard treatment — it’s individualized to their tumor in their body,” Minor said.
The Bay Area cancer hub will focus on four projects for the first funding phase, each with a 10-year goal to develop meaningful findings: creating the next generation of cellular therapies; leveraging AI to more precisely target cellular therapies to treatment-resistant tumors; using diet and nutrition to boost drug interventions for deadly cancers; and building AI systems that will use biological and clinical data to develop personalized treatment plans for patients.
Of particular interest is CAR T-cell therapy, which involves using a patient’s own immune cells to fight their cancer. Doctors are already successfully treating blood cancers with the treatment, but it involves engineering a patient’s own cells outside the body then reinfusing them, in a procedure that is incredibly expensive and time-consuming. The UCSF and Stanford team is looking at ways to engineer patients’ cells inside their body, which would be much less logistically complicated. 
“The goal would be to have an (investigational new drug approval) from the FDA to start a clinical trial within three years,” said Justin Eyquem, a UCSF associate professor who is co-leading the cellular therapy team. “This is an ambitious goal. But these grants are aimed at funding ambitious projects.”
His colleague at Stanford described the funding as “manna from heaven,” especially at this point in cancer research.
“Cellular therapy has not been fully realized,” said Dr. Crystal Mackall, director of the Stanford Center for Cancer Cell Therapy. “We have this in our sights, we know what can be done, this is not the time to take your foot off the gas. We think this is exactly the time to invest, to keep the acceleration going, and good things will happen.”
The other projects also are capitalizing on recent advances in technology and medicine. AI, though problematic in many creative fields, could be transformative in medicine, in terms of how scientists interact with large amounts of data and apply findings that are too nuanced for a human to identify.
Meanwhile, scientists have made strides in understanding the human metabolic system and what role that may play in disease. Of special interest is the recent development of drugs to treat obesity, which early studies are showing may help prevent certain cancers too. One team of scientists funded by the hub will be looking at those drugs and how diet in general may interact with certain hard-to-treat cancers, including pancreatic cancer.
“There was an unmet need to think about how nutrition can actually prevent cancer from arising, slow it down if it does arise, or even better, be able to augment therapies,” said Rushika Perera, a UCSF anatomy professor and co-lead of the nutrition team who studies pancreatic cancer. “We should be thinking about the whole body — how do we augment our whole body metabolism in order to combat cancer?”
Her partner at Stanford, pathology associate professor Jonathan Long, said he’s looking for a “salt for blood pressure” finding — some dietary tweak, like reducing salt intake, that could lead to dramatic improvements in care.
“Right now, if you’re diagnosed with pancreatic cancer, there’s very little we can do in the way of treatment,” Long said. “We think there might be a toggle, something you could change like salt for blood pressure.”
