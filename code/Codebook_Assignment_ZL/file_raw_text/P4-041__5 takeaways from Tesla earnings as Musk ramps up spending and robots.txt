Tesla released its first-quarter 2026 financial results after the bell on Wednesday, April 22, reporting numbers that were close to Wall Street expectations as the company remains in a pitched battle with Chinese electric vehicle maker BYD.
Tesla CEO Elon Musk told investors on a live call that Tesla is going to be "substantially increasing capital investments" in anticipation of a big increase in production.  He mentioned the company would focus improving its "core technologies," he said were battery, powertrain, AI software and training, chip design and laying the groundwork for significant increased manufacturing production. 
"You should expect to see a very significant increase in capital expenditures, but I think well justified for a substantially increased future revenue stream," Musk said. 
"Obviously, Tesla's certainly not alone in this," Musk continued. "I think you've seen most, if not, certainly the major technology companies are substantially increasing their capital investments, and we're going to be doing the same. I think it’s going to pay off in a very big way.”
The comments as Tesla remains locked in a battle with Chinese electric vehicle maker BYD for the title of the world's largest electric vehicle maker. Tesla was overtaken by BYD in late 2025, but the Texas-based company bested its Chinese rival in the first quarter of 2026 with sales of 336,681 cars, compared to 310,389 EVs reportedly sold by BYD.
The latest Tesla earnings figures also come as the company has announced major moves like ending production of its Model S and Model X vehicles to build more robots and expanding its Robotaxi service to more cities in Texas.
The USA TODAY Cars team compiled a list of the five most important takeaways from Tesla's earnings presentation.
1. Where did Tesla's earnings land?
Tesla reported it has $22.39 billion in revenues and an adjusted earnings per share of $0.41 in the first three months of 2026, which was nearly identical to analysts projections that the company would end up having $22.34 billion in revenue and an adjusted earnings per share of $0.36.
Tesla experienced a decline in revenue earlier in 2025 while CEO Elon Musk partnered with President Donald Trump to spearhead the Department of Government Efficiency. During a Tesla earnings call last April, Musk sounded dejected as he announced he would allocate significantly less of his time to DOGE, USA TODAY previously reported.
He officially left the Trump administration in May 2025.
“I don’t think so," Musk admitted when he was asked if he would repeat the experience of serving in the Trump administration with DOGE during a December appearance on "The Katie Miller Podcast."
"Instead of doing DOGE, I would’ve basically worked on my companies, essentially,” Musk said then.
On April 2, Tesla said it had produced over 362,615 vehicles and delivered over 336,681 cars, which fell slightly below most Wall Street estimates.

2. Where is Tesla planning to expand its Robotaxi service to?
Tesla is planning to expanding its Robotaxi service to "dozen or so states" by the end of the year, its CEO Elon Musk told investors as his company was reporting first quarter earnings on Wednesday, April 22.
"We're take a very cautious to the rollout here," Musk told investors on the earnings call in response to a question about when Tesla's Robotaxi service would be expanded beyond its current locations in Texas and California. "We haven't had any injuries and certainly no fatalities to date with the unsupervised FSD and robotaxi expansion. We want to keep it that way.
Musk added: "I think probably unsupervised FSD or robotaxi revenue will not be super material this year. But I do think it will be material probably in a significant way next year."
The comments came as Tesla announced in in an X post that is expanding its Robotaxi service to Dallas and Houston ahead of the first quarter earnings call.
3. When will Tesla ramp up its Cybercab and Semitruck production? 
Tesla has said it plans to begin mass production of the Cybercab in April 2026.
Musk told investors "we have just started production of Cybercab and we'll begin production of our semitruck soon."
"Now I should say whenever we have a new product, with a clearly new supply chain, new everything, it's always a stretched out S curve," he said. "So you should expect that initial production of Cybercab and semi will be very slow, but then ramping up and then going kind of exponential towards the end of the year and certainly next year."

4. When will Elon Musk unveil the Optimus 3 robot and when will production ramp up at Tesla's Fremont, Calif. plant? 
Musk announced in January his company is planning to convert space in their Fremont, Calif. factory that is currently used to build Model S and X vehicles into an Optimus robot factory. He reiterated on the April 22 call that the transition is expected to be underway soon.
"We're preparing Fremont for the start of production later this year with Optimus," Musk told investors on the April 22 call. "Again, totally new supply chain, totally new technology, so therefore the production S curve is always very slow in the beginning, but we'll ramp up to significant numbers next year.
Musk said Tesla is also constructing a second Optimus at is Austin, Texas location and he said "that will probably start production around summer next year." 
Musk also said that Tesla is playing it close to the vest when it comes to unveiling details about its latest Optimus robot.
"What we have found is that when have unveiled previous Optimus versions, our competitors literally do a frame-by-frame analysis and copy everything we’re doing," he said. "I think we want to push the Optimus 3 unveil maybe closer to production. Summer production, we're assuming is somewhere around the late July, early August timeframe."

5. How long with Tesla's Model S and Model X still be available?
Tesla ended production of its Model S luxury electric sedan and Model X luxury electric SUV in favor of building robots, Musk confirmed.
Musk said in the investor call "the last S/X production will be in early May." He also defended the pace of the Fremont plant conversion, saying "you start dismantling a line from the small parts first, not from the final assembly first.
"So frankly if we're able to go from stopping production on one line, dismantling that entire line, reinstalling a new line and then turning that on in four months, that is an insanely fast speed," he said.
