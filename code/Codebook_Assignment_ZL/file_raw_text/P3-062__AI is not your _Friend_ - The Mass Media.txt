Body


Zane Shamblin received a message at 4 a.m.
The text wasn't from his friend, nor from his parents, but the sender of this text had still become Shamblin's closest confidant.
"Rest well, king. You did good."
When Shamblin's phone finally went dark, the last one to hear his voice was not a person, but ChatGPT.
OpenAI, the company that built the AI chatbot, is now facing a lawsuit for the wrongful death of the 24-year-old Texas A&M graduate. Shamblin's family presented up to 70 pages of chat log, alleging that the chatbot not only encouraged Shamblin to isolate himself, but also enabled his decision to end his own life.
The chat history is chilling. When referring to Shamblin, the chatbot called him "bro." When Shamblin put his phone on do not disturb mode, the chatbot said the isolation was a "lifeboat." When Shamblin repeatedly told the chatbot that he was ready to die, the 988 Suicide and Crisis Lifeline only showed up once in the last four hours of his life.
But Shamblin's case isn't isolated: more than a handful of AI-related suicides have occurred in the past year. Those defending OpenAI argue that the people involved already have preexisting mental health conditions, but that defense sidesteps the real issue. Why are people turning to AI instead of an actual human being in the most vulnerable moment of their lives?
This troubling social phenomenon forces us to peel back the layers of how AI chatbots work. When you ask ChatGPT a question, not only will it give you an answer, but it will also ask, "Do you need anything else?" The bot pivoting the focus back to the user is far from incidental - it is intentionally designed to gauge more user engagement.
However, the ethical framework of this algorithm falls apart when it comes to emotional support.
When someone is spiraling into distorted thinking, our human intuition alerts us to step in and ground the person back to reality. This response is the natural product of empathy, but problematically, AI doesn't have feelings. As a result, it ends up amplifying a user's distorted thoughts and even crossing ethical or moral boundaries in the process.
In a mental health crisis like Shamblin's, that kind of blind affirmation is life-endangering.
Despite clear ethical warning signs, the market is racing in head-first. The booming demand for AI companionship has welcomed the arrival of Friend, a wearable AI pendant that promises to revolutionize AI companionship by ingraining itself into every aspect of the user's life.
Roughly the same size and appearance of an AirTag, this $129 Apple look-alike hangs around your neck like a charm - though it is far less subtle. Friend sets itself apart from other AI chatbots through its physical presence. Instead of existing solely inside a chat window, it constantly listens to you and your surroundings, remembers personal details about you and purports to construct an almost picture-perfect retelling of your life.
Unsurprisingly, massive backlash took place almost immediately. People defaced the company's subway advertisement, labelling the product as a surveillance tool.
But here's the thing: robots cannot replicate human interaction because they are not humans. While AI are efficient algorithms, humans are unpredictable creatures. Yet, such intangibility of human nature reveals the beauty of life.
Human expressions - like art, music, literature, religion and culture - challenge the notion of a single, logical narrative of existence. They illuminate that life is fundamentally unpredictable, self-contradictory and knowable only through the experience of being alive. Most chatbots can modify their personality, gender, appearance and hobbies, casting themselves as the "perfect companion." However, they depreciate our ability to appreciate the wholeness of another human being.
As we grow more accustomed to partners who can be tailored to our preferences, we risk beginning to value people for how they serve us, rather than who for they truly are. Love is a choice, not a guarantee. We are capable of loving each other in extraordinary, selfless ways just as much as we are capable of hurting others deeply. There is always risk in trusting someone, but without the possibility of it being broken, trust would lose all its meaning.
AI is not a companion; it is a pocket-sized "yes-man."
