Body


Consider the source. 
It is a principle journalists know well, and it helps them distinguish whether a tip or piece of information is reliable. Unfortunately, sharing on social media is often done in seconds, without considering the source. 
When Gov. Greg Abbott and Attorney General Ken Paxton --two of the highest political officials in Texas-- posted an AI-generated image on X that purported to be a photo of the airman who was rescued in Iran last weekend, they fell victim to a common trap. It was an image we may all have wanted to believe, but it wasn't real. 
This is a cautionary tale for how easy it is to be fooled by fake photographs, and we should all take heed when deciding what to share. 
Checking the source is a tenet of journalistic credibility that everyone should follow. While it is extremely easy to click "share," we now live in a world permeated by altered photographs and AI-generated images and video, making it necessary to slow down and check the source. This is especially true for leaders and influencers with millions of followers. 
What is the problem with sharing a meme or funny fake photo you created just for friends on social media to get a laugh? Those images or videos can take on lives of their own. They can get shared without context and without labeling as fake or AI. 
In the wrong hands, those images and videos can quickly be shared around the world as real, regardless of the creator's intention. We now see it happen with disturbing regularity. 
Whether shared by politicians, influencers or a well-meaning family member, the proliferation of fake images has left much of the public asking: How can we believe what we see? As photojournalists, when we see news photographs shared by legitimate news organizations, we typically believe what we see. Nevertheless, we know skepticism and source checking is a must with unknown sources. 
If it appears to be too good to be true or you ask yourself, 'Is that real?' check the source. If you can't find the source, contemplate the possibility of harm and misinformation. 
Many social media platforms include fact-checking of photos and video, which makes verification easier, although often that verification and labeling as fake or AI occurs after the content has spread 
As artificial intelligence and AI-generated images and video become ever more present in our digital world, they compete with reality for our attention and our interpretation of facts. The average person can no longer look at an image and tell for certain where it came from. 
As leaders in the photojournalism field, we would love nothing more than to see breathtaking news photos of one of the bravest and most complicated rescue operations of this decade. But we don't assume that a photo from an unknown source, no matter how exciting it seems, is real. 
The lightning-fast spread of misinformation and fake news permeates nearly all reaches of society, accelerated by the pace of social media. The problem is exacerbated by the spread of unchecked material, whether in the form of words, photos or videos. 
One of the greatest harms is not just misinformation, but the impact it has on our trust in real news and visuals, which is another reason why double-checking the source before sharing is so important. The spread of disinformation, misinformation, and fake photos and video is a crisis needing attention regardless of who spreads them. 
The only way to see through the chaos of this onslaught is to get information from reliable sources. 
For readers and viewers of the news media, this means finding trustworthy news sources. For politicians, that means being a reliable source of the truth. Politicians who curate their sources to include reliable media instead of anonymous accounts are much more likely to have accuracy as a baseline. 
Alicia Wagner Calzada is an attorney in San Antonio and the deputy general counsel of the National Press Photographers Association. Lisa Krantz is an assistant professor at the University of Montana's School of Journalism, a member of the National Press Photographers Association Board of Directors, a former San Antonio Express-News photojournalist and a part-time San Antonio resident.
