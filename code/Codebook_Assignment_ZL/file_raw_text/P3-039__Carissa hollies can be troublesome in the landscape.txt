Q: I have eight different types of hollies and generally do very well with them except for the Carissas. I have them in three different areas where they are planted with other hollies. All receive at least four hours of sunlight. The Carissas' leaves start to cup like a spoon, then get yellow and dry along each edge or develop yellow spots within the leaves. After the leaves die, the twigs die. Any suggestions? 
A: I've grown several dozen types of hollies in my own landscape, and of the more common ones, Carissas seem to develop problems more often than most. Usually, it's associated either with too much sunlight or drought, although the winters in recent years have been severe enough to hurt them badly. Your samples look like they might have gotten too dry one or more times, but I also wonder about a fungal leaf spot. The Texas A&M Plant Disease Diagnostic Laboratory could culture them for you (fee involved) and identify any pathogen present, or you could put those dollars toward replacement plants. I've gone back to dwarf Chinese holly in many of the same applications and have been very happy with the results. 
Q: This oak was planted a few years ago. Initially it was diagnosed with sun scald. Despite measures to try to help it, it just seems to be going downhill. When it budded out this spring there was new growth, but now much of that has died back. At this point I think the best thing I can do is to put it out of its misery and replace it, but if there is anything you think might revitalize it, please do tell. 
A: First things first, it's obvious that you enjoy landscaping and that you have a good eye for design. Nice work on what you are accomplishing. That said, everything you have written is correct. This is, indeed, sun scald -- the worst case I've seen for a while. Oaks are especially vulnerable since they have such thin bark. When they are moved from a nursery environment into a landscape where the west or southwest sun hits their trunks, after two or three years enough damage is done that the bark cracks and peels off. In the process, it takes the phloem tissues with it. That's the cylinder of conducting cells just inside the bark. They carry manufactured sugars from the leaves down to the roots. Your photo taken near the soil line shows how serious this damage is. There are no conducting tissues left around most of the trunk. It's time for a replacement. If you decide to plant another oak, a maple, or a Chinese pistachio, all notorious for their thin barks, protect the new tree's trunk with paper tree wrap for the first couple of years. 
Q: I have been in my home for 46 years. In spring 1980, we planted 60 feet of waxleaf ligustrums across the front of the house. The past two winters have been unkind to them. Two years ago, I covered them and they still were damaged by the cold. This past winter, I did not cover them and they are spotty. They face west and the sun is unbearable. What can I do? I'd rather not have to go to the expense of planting so many replacements, but if I do, what would you suggest? 
A: I'm going to read between some lines. If you've had these across the front of your house for 45 years, that's a long time for waxleaf ligustrums. Normally you would expect them to grow to 8 to 12 feet tall in that time. I'll bet you have pruned them many times over the years. If so, that is a weakening process, and that, added to the cold, may have been the reason they have started failing. I would not replant now going into the hottest part of the summer. Wait until late September. That will give you time to decide on whether you need to replant. If you do, I'd suggest dwarf Burford or Willowleaf hollies. However, you'll need to pledge to water any new plants by hand every couple of days, spring through fall, for their first two years in your landscape. Normal lawn and landscape irrigation won't be enough. Stiff-leafed evergreens like hollies, ligustrums and magnolias can get too dry without our knowing it. You must force yourself into watering deeply and frequently. 
Q: The leaves on my Diamond Spire gardenias are turning bright yellow before they turn brown and drop off. Another one 6 feet away is doing just fine. Both are in full sun in clay pots. Thanks for any help. 
A: Everyone worries about iron deficiency when the subject is gardenias, but this is definitely not that. Iron deficiency shows up on the newest leaves first -- those at the tip ends of the branches. And the veins remain dark green longer that the rest of the leaf surfaces. Plus, the deficient leaves don't fall off. 
This is moisture stress. The potting soil looks like it has a great deal of gravel or some other very porous material in it. Gardenias need exactly the opposite. I'd also suggest moving both plants to spots where they will receive sun only until 10 or 10:30 in the mornings. Afternoon Texas sun is too much for gardenias, especially when they're growing in pots. Come late fall, consider repotting into a highly organic potting soil that doesn't have so much gravel. 
Q: What is going on with my oak tree? The bark is coming off and there is new bark beneath it. Is this because of the drought of recent months and years? 
A: I'm going to answer this note even though it seems redundant with the earlier one. This is not sun scald. This could be mechanical injury that has healed. That could be from being gouged by a mower wheel or a line trimmer. However, the thing we've seen so commonly since February 2021 has been the aftermath of "radial shake" brought on by that year's awful cold spell. The canopy of the tree will tell you a lot. If it is thinning significantly on this side, the tree is in jeopardy. If not, the tree is on its way to healing. Let the canopy be your guide to the plant's future. It may be time to call in the help of a certified arborist.
