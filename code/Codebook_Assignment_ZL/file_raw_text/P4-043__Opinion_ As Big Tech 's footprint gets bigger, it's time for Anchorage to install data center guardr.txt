


Opinion: As Big Tech's footprint gets bigger, it's time for Anchorage to install data center guardrails

I've opined before that Anchorage should lean into its role as an innovation hub, and I still believe that's true. We can and should. Opportunity abounds if we are willing to embrace change and modernity. However, wisdom dictates that the impacts of quickly evolving technological advances be evaluated for their tradeoffs.
Artificial intelligence is increasingly integrated in our daily lives, or at least it appears that way on our social media feeds. I'll leave it to others to make predictions about when we might be hooked up to the matrix by our robot overlords or to speculate as to whether future humanity will more closely resemble Wells' Eloi creatures or the denizens of the Axiom starliner from "Wall-E." In any event, AI is here to stay and, as its usage becomes ever more pervasive, it also presents tangible challenges to communities. Among them are the large data centers that store the servers needed to power this emerging technology. We should think proactively about what that might mean for our community, should they come to Anchorage.
[Related opinion:The future of AI should plug in to Alaska]
First, the economic tradeoffs. The building of data centers means construction jobs. It can also mean substantial increases in property tax revenue, which could help fund city services. For example, Loudoun County, Virginia, which has the highest concentration of data centers in the country, gets 38% of its revenue from the centers every year. However, these warehouse-sized buildings   some the size of multiple football fields   take up valuable land that could be used for other purposes, such as much-needed housing or commercial development that provides permanent jobs.
Then there are the environmental tradeoffs. It has been reported that one data center can use as much electricity annually as 200,000 homes. Three facilities could consume double that of the city of Seattle. This is hard to square as Anchorage and state policymakers contemplate an impending natural gas shortage and other utility issues. Data centers also require a large amount of water for cooling the heat-emitting electronics they house. Public records from Memphis have shown a data center there using 11 million gallons in one month, nearly equivalent to what 150 homes use in an entire year.
Additionally, large data centers may be associated with health concerns such as noise and air pollution. Loudoun County residents have complained of loud humming noises, and researchers have demonstrated elevated levels of nitrogen dioxide, a cause of smog and asthma. Residents of Southaven, Mississippi, have similarly complained about the noxious noise created by the gas turbines, which can be used to power data centers and bypass grid capacity limitations.
Taking these factors into consideration, Assembly Vice Chair Anna Brawley and I sponsored an ordinance, which was recently passed by a majority of our Assembly colleagues, to ensure that Anchorage can be smart about where and when we allow data centers.
What does this new code update do? Importantly, it does not outright ban or prohibit data centers. First, it adds a definition to our code of what data centers are. Second, it clarifies which zoning will allow data centers and makes data centers adhere to conditional use permitting. Simply put, this means data centers won't be built in neighborhoods and will involve more municipal oversight and public process if a project is proposed. Additionally, our ordinance requires 200-foot setbacks, visual screening and landscaping, fire suppression, noise mitigation studies, and documentation of power generation and storage.
In Anchorage, we should envision the future thoughtfully because innovation can bring benefits, but it can also mean unwanted disruption. At the end of the day, no one wants a large, humming data center right next to their house.
Daniel Vollandis a health care provider and small-business owner and represents District 1, North Anchorage, on the Anchorage Assembly.
