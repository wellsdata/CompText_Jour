At the 2026 State of the University Address on April 22, Chancellor James T. Minor covered updates regarding the budget and enrollment. 
Throughout the address, Minor often brought the discussion back to the student perspective, showing videos of students receiving the Meridian Scholarship or using the new Health Sciences Complex. He also gave special thanks to Enrollment Management and Marketing and Communications for their work in moving SIUE closer to the university's goals. 
Staying ahead of the budget
"For [Fiscal Year] 2026, we were able to balance the budget, which is a big accomplishment for the university. Thank you to those of you who had to make tough choices at the university level [and] at the department level to get us to this point," Minor said. "We can move beyond the structural deficit conversation unless we slip backwards, but it gives us an opportunity to go forward."
Minor said that ensuring the university stays on a strong financial footing is crucial to SIUE's success, both short-term and long-term. Staying ahead of the budget, he said, means being "fair and realistic" about what the university can do. 
"There's no question that at every level across the university, we have some gaps to close in terms of being competitive with respect to salary. There's no question about that," Minor said. "The question is, 'Can you do it responsibly without busting the budget, and can you do it in a way that gives you requisite effectiveness?'"
Minor said that part of this process, however, involves a rethinking of job responsibilities and organizational structure. 
"There's not a unit I go to across campus [that isn't saying], 'We need more people.' Do we need more people to do the same thing the same way?" Minor said. "If 'more people' is walking a piece of paper from one office to another office, and that's our process, is that the right investment for the university?"
"We will go as far as we responsibly can to close some of these gaps, but it will not magically happen in one cycle," Minor said. "This is a 20-year problem to adjust and to resolve. It won't happen in one contract. I want to be very clear about that."
Minor also shared information on federal-level legislation that could impact higher education, particularly regarding "relevant earnings threshold" and "shared risk." Relevant earning threshold, which Minor said is expected to come into effect in July, mandates that graduates from bachelor's programs earn more money on average than high school graduates, and master's graduates must earn more than bachelor's graduates. 
"If you have a program that doesn't meet the relevant earned income threshold two out of three years, that program no longer qualifies for direct federal student loans," Minor said. 
Shared risk, Minor said, will hold universities partly responsible for any students who default on their loans, if implemented. There are now also upper limits on federal loans for students. 
"We now have new caps on federal loan programs, both at the graduate and professional level," Minor said. "$100,000 for graduate school, $200,000 for professional school, which will send some students out into the private market for loans to finance dental school education, pharmacy, law school, medical school if you're in Springfield."
Minor also touched on program prioritization and the need for adaptability in universities. 
"Cybersecurity didn't exist as a major when I was in college. It does now. Our ability to keep track with industry is going to be important," Minor said. "If we continue to add programs and invest in these programs, it does raise the question of the composition of our overall set of degree programs. It is necessary to do addition - and occasionally subtraction - and that is a reality."
"We constantly have to ask the question [of] whether or not the degree is as relevant today as it may have been a decade ago," Minor said. 
Another project in the university's future is various solar panel arrays. One idea Minor shared was placing solar panels above parking lots as a canopy. While these panels would not be "a one-time solution" to offsetting the university's electricity budget, Minor said it would help and is an opportunity the university should explore. 
Enrolling the future
One area Minor has emphasized over the past few years is first-to-second-year retention, and that focus has not shifted. Though the 283 campaign in the 2023-2024 academic year did not meet its goal, Minor said SIUE is moving closer to his 90 percent retention target. 
"We're now, with domestic students, just north of 80 percent [retention], which is remarkable," Minor said. "To close that 10 percent gap ... for some departments, we're talking retaining 19 additional students. It is an admirable goal, and we're close."
Minor said he asked the university community to do "three simple things" to help with retention: "affirm the student" to make them feel welcome on campus, "disrupt the runaround" to connect students with resources and "intervene early" instead of waiting until the end of the first semester to address struggles students may be facing. 
"You would be surprised the number of students who arrive at college who are unsure, even high-achieving students ... whether or not they're gonna be successful here. Imagine for a moment these students in their first week of college, that 20 people have stopped them and said, 'You belong here. We're so glad you're here,'" Minor said. 
However, graduation rates are still relatively low, a fact that Minor called "sobering." Just 37 percent of students admitted to SIUE end up graduating in four years, and roughly half graduate in six years. 
"These numbers have to improve," Minor said. "The idea that roughly half of students are graduating after six years is probably not where we wanna be as an institution. Is it that we admitted 50 percent of the students in error, or is it that they met the admission criteria, we enrolled them and there's something happening in our environment that doesn't allow students to graduate or to persist at a higher level?"
"Maybe 20 years ago, if a student didn't graduate ... we assigned the problem to the student. 'You didn't try hard enough, you weren't serious about college, you didn't study hard enough.' In the last five years, institutions have begun to look inward. It's a question for us that we have to tackle as a university."
Another issue universities face today, according to Minor, is messaging about the value of a college education turning potential students away from universities. 
"A college graduate will earn $1.2 million more than a high school graduate over their lifetime," Minor said. "These are economic statistics, and I don't wanna boil a college degree down to economics, but I think it's important to have a counternarrative about the value proposition of earning a college degree today. The idea that you don't need to go to college, I think, is a conversation that families are having around the kitchen table, even among some of our most accomplished students."
Despite these roadblocks, Minor said he is hopeful about SIUE's contributions - both current and potential - to the economy and the region as a whole. 
"Even with the challenging statistics around degree completion, SIUE is still the number one producer of bachelor's degrees in the St. Louis metro and Metro East," Minor said. "That is a very important message for the business community ... and the future of the workforce here, and I think we have an opportunity to be even better in terms of degree productivity."
AI at SIUE
"I don't think we could have a university address without a conversation about the role artificial intelligence is expected to play across all university communities," Minor said. "The reality is this technology has the fastest uptake compared to any other technology in our lifetime."
Minor said he wants SIUE to be "a part of the experimentation" with AI as it grows and becomes more widespread. 
"It is clear to me that the position for this university can't be on the sidelines, waiting to see how this is going to play out," Minor said. "We don't even have a policy framework to talk about how we litigate it. We don't have a responsible use framework for artificial intelligence."
Minor said SIUE will be piloting AI use in four areas in the near future: enrollment management, campus experience, course scheduling and human resources. 
In enrollment management and course scheduling, Minor said AI would be used to remind students to register and automatically create a sample schedule with recommended courses to progress in their degree. It would also be used to prevent students from inadvertently dropping a class required for their degree. 
"We're not turning the university over to an AI model or some chatbot - that is not going to happen. This is not a two-feet-in-the-water moment in time for the university," Minor said. "This is about responsible experimentation and testing of whether or not these technologies are appropriate to deploy in the university."
Minor gave similar addresses on the Alton and East St. Louis campuses in an effort to improve accessibility for members of the SIUE community.
