Body


For Tom Young of West Lampeter Township, simple tasks like reading the thermostat in the house or checking the price of a grocery store item aren’t so simple anymore. Young has macular degeneration, a progressive eye disease that causes vision loss over time. A widower, Young lives alone, but technology is helping him regain the independence he thought was behind him.
Young now has his own pair of Ray-Ban Meta smart glasses, which can do things like read the newspaper in your ear, take a picture of the scoreboard and read it aloud to you, even capture videos and answer calls — all by the command of your voice.
“Using a magnifying glass gets very, very tiring,” Young says. “Being able to capture this with the Meta lenses and have it read back to you is just amazing.”
Taylor Newswanger, occupational therapist at vision loss nonprofit VisionCorps’ Lancaster center, says the glasses fall under the category of optical character recognition, which is what enables them to read your prescription bottle or oven temperature. The glasses also use generative artificial intelligence to answer any question you throw their way, acting as a mobile encyclopedia of sorts.

The glasses come in three styles — Wayfarer, Skyler and Headliner — and can be ordered in a range of colors and fitted with a variety of lens types, like transition, prescription and blue-light blocking lenses. There are other optical character recognition glasses on the market, including from Envision AI, but these are the first to put real downward pricing pressure on the market. Whereas Envision glasses run around $2,500, and other magnification-based headwear can run as high as $5,000, Ray-Ban Meta glasses cost as little as $299 for a new pair or $239 for a certified refurbished pair. Educators and students with a .edu email address can get an additional 15% off of their purchase.
Young says he’s receiving shots to slow the progression of his macular degeneration, and he’s getting ready to move into a retirement condo where he’ll have all the support he needs. But the glasses, he says, will help him read the Eagles and Ravens football game scores, read the newspaper and complete any number of tasks in his home or around town without having to call in for help. And while there is a brief learning curve, he has support with that, too.
“It’s initially like most things electronic — the older you get, the less understanding you have of how things are working,” Young says. “But I must say, VisionCorps has been super helpful in that respect.”

A new era for vision loss tools
People who have vision loss but still retain some level of vision are likely to use a combination of tools, including light, magnification and now auditory glasses. But unlike earlier technology, auditory support places no strain on the eyes.
“Not only is (magnification) physically tiring for your eyes, but mentally having to read through and remember and process everything that you’re seeing, it just makes reading not fun,” Newswanger says.
She adds that the higher the magnification, the smaller area you can see, which means you might be reading letter by letter. Additionally, because our retinas take in visuals upside down, super high magnification causes our brains to be unable to reverse the image, forcing us to look at things upside down.
Vision-loss technology also includes digital magnification. Instead of using a handheld or headwear-based physical magnification lens, digital magnification operates like a camera zoom, where you put your reading material in front of a screen, and then you can make it larger or smaller and even change the color contrast for easier reading. All of these tools, including the Meta glasses, are available for demo on site at VisionCorps’ Queen Street location in Lancaster.
Newswanger says she’s shown the glasses to around 30 people so far, and almost all of them have gone on to purchase the technology. Young got his glasses fitted with prescription lenses at the local LensCrafters. The smart frames themselves are available for purchase on both the Meta and Ray-Ban sites, but Newswanger says more customization options are available on the Ray-Ban site.
Those who do want the glasses will need a smartphone and the no-cost Meta View app. The glasses wirelessly connect via Bluetooth. They also work with the Be My Eyes app, where you can use your voice to call up live volunteers who see your point of view through the glasses and help you navigate your world. (The Be My Eyes app also works without the glasses, but users must navigate through their phone to get to it without voice command technology.) Young hasn’t used Be My Eyes yet, but he’s open to it as he anticipates needing more support over time.
Life has changed a lot for Young — from losing his wife to being unable to enjoy old hobbies like reading. But things are looking up.
“I gave up books and stuff,” he says. “Now, I think this is gonna open that back up.”
Newswanger has been with VisionCorps for eight years, and she’s seen her own fair share of technological advancements in the field. With auditory assistance, some patients feel like they’re not getting the chance to actually read, but Newswanger wants to remind folks they can use different technology together, creating a larger toolbox of tools to help you live your life.
“There are going to be times that you need to rely on your eyes and other times that you can rely on technology,” Newswanger says.
For example, someone with vision loss could use auditory technology to read a bill to find out how much they owe and then use magnification to write a check.
“You don’t have to wait until your daughter comes next week when your bill ends up being late,” she says. “It’s a different kind of independence — relying on your other senses and technology to help you be independent, not independently using your eyes.”
