Body


In a digital world, even rural employees' well-being is impacted by internet accessibility and quality.
According to official U.S. Census data, about one in five Americans lives in a rural area, and the Census report itself says the word rural"evokes images of farmlands and pastoral landscapes." But a new white paper suggests that rural employers need to think about one extra detail: how their location negatively impacts workers' well-being. And before you go blaming those fields of green and clouds of white, here's what you should think to yourself-we live in a digital world.
In an era of  rapidly evolving AI tools, it may be time your company took steps to boost the rural data issue.
The study, from University of Phoenix organizational wellness researcher Stella Smith, leaped off the University of Phoenix's recent report "Generating Rural Opportunities in the Workforce." This study showed that rural workers are "significantly less likely to feel that they have access to the right tools and resources to achieve their career goals than the nonrural workforce (56 percent vs. 73 percent)," while another "67 percent of rural workers say they need support learning new skills." Smith's study highlighted a link between rural workplaces and reduced scope for modern methods of working, directly tied to common internet limitations in more rural parts of the country.
It's more than a mere business slowdown caused by unreliable internet service. Smith wrote that this is actually a snowballing issue. When workers lack reliable, fast connections, and thus access to modern workplace tools, it can cause "cascading effects for organizational health, from employee engagement and retention to access to training and long-term career resilience," she explained. None of this is limited to remote workers living in rural locations, since companies located in rural areas may be impacted in a systemic way.
The 21st-century workplace relies on digital tech, and in many cases these systems are best served by (or are completely dependent on) fast broadband-speed internet connections.
But rural America has a host of internet service problems. Smith's paper noted that these internet restrictions can actually have a negative effect on employee well-being, by driving workers' stress levels up. Slow or unreliable internet can also impact the ability to learn new skills online, harming upskilling efforts and, as Phys.org noted, it can also reduce organizational agility-the ability to pivot and react to swift changes.
Rural internet woes are so common they spawn numerous, recurrent discussions and support commentary and expertise on social sites like Reddit. Users often seek technical advice from other members, including on setting up internet for new, small rural businesses. Discussions on the issues center on limited fiber connections, excessively high connection costs from established providers, and slow data speeds. The recent rise of space-based internet Starlink as a viable alternative to traditional cable connections has led to multiple users saying "just go for Starlink," a trend that could show traditional ISPs are falling behind.
Smith's guidance for employers in rural areas essentially requires companies to improve internet access for employees. Her paper framed this as a move that's good for business longevity, which makes sense in a world where digital tools seem to be evolving faster than ever. There are significant opportunities "presented by AI integration and digital skill-building," Smith noted, and this requires better connectivity. Essentially, Smith argued rural companies can no longer tolerate slow or unreliable internet.
Smith's paper went one step further, highlighting that AI may be able to play a role in solving the rural workplace digital divide once a company has boosted its net connectivity. The report noted that the recent "lapsing of the Affordable Connectivity Program" has worsened things, because the federal program was designed to help boost rural internet connectivity with discounts for low-income households. Funding for the Federal Communications Commission-administered program lapsed in June 2024.
By building bridges to cross the digital access divide in rural areas, employers (and also local policymakers) can actually help boost healthier, happier, and more productive workplace environments. Ultimately, this can boost productivity and economic participation by rural companies and their employees, Smith said. 
Moves to support worker upskilling by improved connectivity can also lead to a more stable workforce, and boost workers' "career mobility." As a bonus, efforts like this may boost your company's image and reputation-useful at a time when this may be more important to future hires than ever before.
