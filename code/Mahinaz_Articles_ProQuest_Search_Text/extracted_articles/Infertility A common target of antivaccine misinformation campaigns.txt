1 Introduction Misinformation and disinformation about vaccines are a key drivers of vaccine hesitancy and refusal [1] . Compared to the past, the spread of incorrect and misleading information about pharmaceutical products, both organic and intentional, has been selectively and massively amplified via social media because of algorithms keyed to engagement, allowing the rapid dissemination of false claims to huge audiences. Refuting, correcting, and/or updating these misleading claims is a difficult task, as individuals are prone to recall dramatic misinformation more readily than science-based factual information that counters it [2] . Before the pandemic, misinformation about childhood vaccines encompassed a wide variety of false claims, the most well-known of which was the claim that vaccines, particularly measles-mumps-rubella (MMR) vaccines, cause “autistic enterocolitis” [3,4] and autism. Other misinformation ranged from claims that the vaccines cause neurological injury, to claims of vaccine-induced metabolic disorders, infertility, and even sudden death [5,6] . Unsurprisingly, many of these claims have been resurrected to be applied to COVID-19 vaccines, in particular the claim that these vaccines cause infertility. Here, we will discuss specifically the longstanding antivaccine claim that vaccines cause infertility, particularly female infertility, and how this tradition of misinformation laid the groundwork for the misinformation about COVID-19 vaccines impairing female fertility, which arose almost immediately after the vaccines were released.
Before we discuss how the antivaccine movement has demonized these vaccines as causing infertility, we must note that misinformation about COVID-19 vaccines has been rampant on both social media and “traditional” media, for the latter in particular conservative media such as Fox News and the Wall Street Journal op-ed page. There are many reasons why concern about these new vaccines has surpassed concern about vaccines approved prior to the pandemic for other diseases. First, COVID-19 vaccines are the first-ever human coronavirus vaccines to be granted emergency use authorization (EUA) and later full approval by the FDA, after a history of failure of pharmaceutical companies to develop effective vaccines against, for example, SARS. More importantly perhaps, the first available COVID-19 vaccines were not “traditional” vaccines, but rather a novel technology (mRNA vaccines) that had as of 2020 never produced a safe and effective vaccine before, much less been administered at the population level. Although mRNA vaccines had been under development for over a decade prior to the pandemic [7] , the seeming newness of the technology has understandably led to increased skepticism about the safety and efficacy of these vaccines, providing more fertile ground for misinformation and conspiracy theories to take hold than for older, more “traditional” vaccines (although the arrival of the Novavax COVID-19 vaccine has made it clear that “traditional” recombinant protein antigen-based vaccines are far from immune against becoming targets of misinformation [8] ). Finally, because there was no pre-existing immunity in the population to the SARS-CoV-2 virus, the vaccination campaign targeted all populations and age groups, initially prioritizing because of initially limited supply those most at risk (frontline healthcare workers, then the elderly and those with chronic diseases like diabetes that made them susceptible to severe disease and death from COVID-19), then younger adults, and then children. This differed from most vaccine roll-outs, which focused on children. Issues of safety and misinformation therefore were mainly of concern to parents, while with the COVID-19 vaccine campaign, the entire population was potentially affected.
One of the most often-repeated claims that antivaccine activists have made regarding mRNA-based liposomal COVID-19 vaccines is that their use may interfere with reproductive health in women, resulting in problems ranging from menstrual irregularities to more frequent miscarriages to outright infertility. This is a particularly insidious claim promoted by antivaccine activists on social media [9,10] . Though multiple observational studies have found no deleterious effects on infertility attributable to the vaccine in men or women [11–20] (but a significant negative effect of COVID-19 infection [13,14] ), antivaccine advocates argue that some effects of the vaccines may not show up for years or decades, suggesting that the vaccines put young persons and children at risk of fertility loss at some unspecified future time that we cannot know.
Unfortunately, the claim that vaccines cause infertility is not new. It has been commonly made for many years about other vaccines, particularly HPV vaccines and, in the developing world, vaccines against tetanus and polio, as we discuss below. However, with COVID-19 vaccines, this misinformation has taken on new forms and spread far beyond where it had been common before. In order to illustrate how COVID-19 vaccines have been blamed for infertility and how that has stoked vaccine hesitancy, we examine three case studies, two historical (one of which inspired a pandemic-era film by antivaccine activist turned independent Presidential candidate Robert F. Kennedy, Jr.) and one involving recent claims that COVID-19 vaccines cause infertility. We conclude by discussing strategies to address this narrative.
2 Case study #1: HPV vaccine “on trial” in the US Human papilloma virus (HPV) is the most common sexually-transmitted infection in the United States [21] . There are more than 200 known genotypes of the virus [22] , a small fraction of which are responsible for most cervical cancers [22] as well as a significant fraction of head and neck cancers [23] . To prevent HPV infections, two different vaccines have been introduced since 2006 [24] . Cervarix is a bivalent vaccine, protecting against HPV types 16 and 18, which are the most common oncogenic types. The original Gardasil vaccine also provided protection against HPV 16 and 18, as well as HPV 6 and 11, which are types most commonly associated with genital warts [25] . In 2014, the US Food and Drug Administration approved Gardasil9, which additionally provides protection against oncogenic HPV types 31, 33, 45, 52, and 58 [24,26] . The effect of the vaccine has been dramatic. In Australia, where it was developed and first licensed, it has been suggested that use of the vaccine in combination with screening programs could eliminate cervical cancer as a public health problem in that country [27] . While vaccination rates are lower in the U.S., a substantial reduction in prevalence of the vaccine strains has already been observed [28] .
Because this is a vaccine that prevents an infection and condition that is primarily transmitted via sexual activity, there has been considerable push-back against its use in children and adolescents, primarily from conservative religious groups. Based on the argument that vaccination against a sexually transmitted disease will encourage promiscuity and risky sexual behavior among teens, many dissuaded parents from vaccinating their children for HPV. This message was prominent as early as the mid-2000s, an example being California Sen. George Runner (R-Lancaster), who told the Los Angeles Times in 2007 that American money would be much better spent on other types of vaccines since cervical cancer is a result of lifestyle choices, asking “Is there a more productive way for us to spend the money that may help someone who’s in a health situation that has nothing to do with their personal choices?” [29] . Since then, numerous studies have found no evidence of a correlation between HPV vaccination and risky or increased sexual behavior [30–32] . It is an implausible link, as was noted in 2007 by journalist Meghan O’Rourke, who observed that “HPV is hardly a major deterrent to kids who might be squeamish about STDs” and that adolescents “have a hard enough time thinking about next week, let alone a decade from now.” [33] .
This initial anti-vaccine campaign may have provided fertile ground for later spread of vaccine misinformation focused on the effect of HPV vaccination on female fertility. A number of publications have addressed the potential for the HPV vaccination to have an effect on fertility or fecundity in vaccinated individuals. Though one case report and a case series suggested that the HPV vaccine could lead to primary ovarian insufficiency (POI, sometimes called premature ovarian failure, a condition in which a woman’s ovaries cease functioning before the age of 40) [34,35] , larger studies found no effect of HPV vaccination on fecundability [36] and another no effect on POI [37] . Studies in rodent models likewise found no issues regarding reproductive health of male or female animals after HPV vaccination [38–40] .
A 2018 study by Gayle DeLong using data from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Study (NHANES) suggested that females who received the HPV vaccine had a lower probability of becoming pregnant than those who were unvaccinated [41] , and further suggested this purported effect of HPV vaccination had resulted in falling birth rates in the US from 2007 to 2015. Though this analysis did not include adjustments for use of contraception, a key variable in pregnancy risk, the study was widely shared on antivaccine websites, such as Natural News [42] , Children’s Health Defense [43] , and Age of Autism [44] , and, even now, five years after its initial publication, retains an Altmetrics score of 2,157 [45] , which would put it in the top 75 Altmetric scores from 2018 [46] . It was retracted in 2022 after the editors consulted additional experts in the field and found “serious flaws in the statistical analysis and interpretation of data in this paper” which led to concerns about the validity of the study [47] .
Other studies have found no risk regarding HPV vaccination and fecundability. A preconception cohort study of North American pregnancy planners and their partners found no difference overall in fertility by vaccination status, but HPV vaccination was associated with higher fecundability in those who had a history of sexually-transmitted infections or pelvic inflammatory disease (that is, a group at high risk of exposure to HPV infection), suggesting HPV vaccination was protective of fertility [36] . Another large study including almost 200,000 individuals in a Kaiser Permanente database showed no increased risk of POI following several different vaccines, including HPV [37] , and Schmul et al used the same NHANES database as the retracted DeLong study but found no evidence of impaired fertility using their methodology [48] .
Unfortunately, the myth that HPV vaccines can impair the fertility of young women remains active today, with antivaccine social media activists making analogies between the two. Indeed, there is now crosspollination of misinformation between the two vaccines, such as when Ana Maria Milhalcea, a physician and scientist, recently claimed to have found “nanobots” in HPV vaccines [49] . Another example is a pseudonymous blogger on Substack, A Midwestern Doctor (AMD), who has made direct comparisons between HPV and COVID-19 vaccines with respect to infertility, in particular charging that the clinical trials for the two vaccines covered up serious adverse events and that the Vaccine Adverse Events Reporting System (VAERS) has been inadequate to detect these instances of infertility [50,51] . In doing so, AMD references more recent, pandemic-era claims about COVID-19 vaccines and infertility, as well as a film by Robert F. Kennedy (RFK) Jr, which features in our second case study.
3 Case study #2: Tetanus vaccination of young women in Africa In 2022, RFK Jr.’s antivaccine group Children’s Health Defense released a documentary entitled “ Infertility: A Diabolical Agenda” [52] , with Kennedy serving as executive producer. Co-producers included Mary Holland, a lawyer who has long promoted the false claims that vaccines cause autism and that HPV vaccines cause infertility, and Andrew Wakefield, the UK physician who has been one of the most famous antivaccine activists in the world since he published his now-retracted case series in The Lancet in 1998 that claimed to find an association between the MMR vaccine and autistic enterocolitis, arguably the main spark behind the late 20th century resurgence of antivaccine sentiment. Infertility has been characterized as a “docuganda”; i.e. , a documentary propaganda [53] , a genre which includes prior anti-vaccine films “ The Greater Good” and Andrew Wakefield’s previous directorial effort, the 2016 film “ VAXXED: From Cover-Up to Catastrophe ”.
Infertility: A Diabolical Agenda is is largely an updated rehash of a 1990s-era disinformation campaign that spread fear about a push to prevent neonatal tetanus in developing countries by vaccinating young women, and one of the earliest antivaccine campaigns to use the Internet as a powerful tool to spread such fear. As part of this fear-based campaign, antivaccine advocates conflated ongoing clinical trials of an experimental antifertility vaccine, which conjugated a subunit of human chorionic gonadotrophin (hCG) hormone with diphtheria or tetanus toxoid proteins, to ongoing tetanus immunization programs led by the World Health Organization [54] . Though there was no connection between the immunization program and the clinical trial, the confusion between the two led to distrust of the tetanus vaccines. Once such distrust spread, tetanus vaccines were tested for the presence of hCG. However, ordinary pregnancy tests were used, which were not appropriate to test for this hormone within vaccine vials, leading to false positive results and further misrepresentation of the outcome by groups opposed to the tetanus vaccine campaign. Vaccines tested by reputable laboratories did not find presence of the hormone [54] .
To accentuate the sense of dread, Infertility employs clips from several films. These include Children of Men , a dystopian movie in which humanity faces extinction due to total infertility of the entire population until the first pregnant woman in nearly two decades emerges. Also referenced is The Constant Gardener , whose plot involves the discovery that a pharmaceutical company had tested a dangerous drug in Kenya with disastrous results that it would go to any lengths to cover up. Prominent is a scene in which a woman discusses her repeated miscarriages. When this tetanus vaccine misinformation campaign was active in the 1990s, Milstien et al noted that it led to “a strong negative impact on immunization programs” in four targeted countries: the Philippines, Mexico, Nicaragua, and Tanzania. Worse, in 1995 The Philippines experienced a major decline in the number of women receiving tetanus vaccines, and immunization against infections such as measles also declined [54] .
Fearmongering about “depopulation” by vaccines remains a longstanding antivaccine conspiracy theory, the base of which (that chemicals have been added to vaccines to deliberately cause infertility) has been twisted and modified over time. For example, depopulation conspiracy theories involving vaccines routinely appear on one of the most trafficked alternative medicine, antivaccine, and general conspiracist websites, Mike Adams’ NaturalNews.com, where in 2018 there appeared an article entitled “ U.S. government approves STERILIZATION VACCINES to reduce deer populations, proving yet again that depopulation vaccines exist” (emphasis in original) [55] . The post described anti-fertility vaccines used to sterilize deer, a most cost-effective and humane way to reduce deer overpopulation than hunting. However, the article twisted this into a nefarious message about human population control:“Whether or not this newfangled vaccine actually works as claimed is perhaps less of a concern than the fact that authorities are now openly admitting what Natural News has been claiming for quite some time now: that vaccines are, in fact, being used by authorities as a depopulation tool to sterilize mammals and prevent them from reproducing”,
suggesting Microsoft founder Bill Gates was a leader in this movement.
Adams is far from alone in this niche, which has morphed from the tetanus vaccines to include COVID-19 vaccines, as discussed below. Indeed, following naturally from the successes of anti-HPV vaccine misinformation, rumors suggesting COVID-19 vaccines would affect fertility started circulating well before the first vaccines were released. Misinformation about COVID-19 vaccines and fertility has taken several different narratives, some mutually conflicting.
4 Case study: COVID-19 vaccination and infertility While “Infertility: A Diabolical Agenda” [52] is basically a retelling of a nearly 30 year old conspiracy theory about vaccines causing infertility in Kenya and other developing nations, it has a wider purpose, which is to use this old piece of misinformation as the starting point to render more plausible a conspiracy theory that vaccines in general—and COVID-19 vaccines in particular—are responsible for an alarming decrease in birthrates, suggesting an ongoing depopulation agenda. Indeed, a one-hour question-and-answer session following the movie featured other antivaccination advocates discussing primarily COVID-19 and HPV vaccines rather than the key claims in the documentary involving tetanus vaccines. More recently, Alex Berenson, once referred to as the pandemic’s “wrongest man” [56] due to his prolific spread of misinformation about COVID, has claimed that births are “plunging exactly on schedule” in Singapore, “nine months after mass Covid vaccinations” [57] .
Like many anti-vaccine tropes, some suggestions may have a core of truth. Christiane Northrup, a trained OB/GYN who is active in the anti-vaccine movement, in a Q&A follow-up to “ Infertility ,” suggested that COVID-19 vaccination affected menstrual cycles. “90 year olds beginning bleeding again, and we’re not talking mild bleeding, we’re talking clotting,” suggesting some women had passed clots for “weeks and weeks” [52] . Feminist author Naomi Wolf made a similar suggestion, tweeting a link to a Facebook page with the claim:“Hundreds of women on this page say that they are having bleeding/clotting after vaccination, or that they bleed oddly AROUND vaccinated women. Unconfirmed, needs more investigation, but lots of reports.”
Research has shown that vaccination may change the menstrual cycle briefly, but alterations were found to be less than a day in length [58] .
Wolf and those who operate in areas of women’s “health and wellness” have been particularly influential at branching out into social media parenting groups. A recent analysis has demonstrated that anti-vaccine wellness groups acted as conduits of misinformation into mainstream parenting groups on Facebook [59] , exposing members to anti-vaccine misinformation, including claims of COVID vaccine-induced infertility. Returning to Twitter following reinstatement after a years-long ban, Wolf suggested she had been correct about “menstrual injuries” that affect fertility [60] . It was also not long after the vaccines received their Emergency Use Authorization (EUA) that another old antivaccine narrative returned, specifically the claim that vaccinated individuals “shed” spike protein, making others sick. This narrative is based on older live attenuated virus vaccines, such as for the oral polio vaccine, which can lead the recently vaccinated to shed vaccine strain virus. In a Q&A follow-up to “ Infertility ,” Northrup suggested that the deaths of two 18-month-old girls were due to hemorrhages following exposure to their vaccinated grandparents, and that unvaccinated women who spent time around vaccinated individuals experienced serious menstrual clotting [52] .
While some previous vaccine misinformation has been politically targeted (such as campaigns aimed at evangelical Christians suggesting that vaccination against HPV encourages promiscuity [61] ), polarization regarding COVID-19 vaccines appears to be even more extreme. Politicians including Florida Governor Ron DeSantis contributed to false information regarding the vaccine and infertility [62] , suggesting that nurses may not want to take the vaccine if they are trying to get pregnant—while former Fox News host Tucker Carlson suggested that the vaccine could harm pregnant women [63] .
Collectively, misinformation about COVID-19 vaccines led to increased concern regarding infertility even prior to their release [10] . Diaz et al. found that Google searches for terms related to COVID vaccines and infertility spiked in the month before and after initial FDA emergency use authorization in December 2020 [10] . After the rollout, a number of orthodox Jewish communities in the US were targeted for misinformation regarding COVID-19 vaccines and infertility, resulting in vaccine hesitancy given how much motherhood and large families are valued in these communities. In Black communities, such targeting played on fears of medical experimentation, like the Tuskegee syphilis experiment [64] . By October 2021, a KFF poll reported that nearly one-third of American adults either believed that or were unsure whether COVID-19 vaccines could cause infertility [65] . The reality is that COVID-19 vaccines do not have a deleterious effect on fertility, as shown in a number of studies [11–19] . Meanwhile, like HPV infection, SARS-CoV-2 infection appears to have deleterious effects on fertility in men [66] . Because infection can also increase the chance of miscarriage or harm to the pregnant person, vaccination is also recommended during pregnancy [67] and also may provide the newborn with protection after birth [68] .
5 Countering misinformation Despite the success of vaccines, a cottage industry has developed focused on misinforming the public about vaccine benefits and risks, discouraging vaccine uptake. This may involve exaggerating immediate risks of vaccines, such as pointing to sudden deaths of individuals who had been previously vaccinated, suggesting that vaccines were the causal agent. Use of infertility as a vaccine fear allows for an additional metric of safety: “What about long-term effects?” It can be used to suggest vaccines cause immediate outcomes (such as miscarriages), but also employed as a tactic to drive fear of vaccination outcomes that might not be recognized for years or decades in the future, such as POI or infertility due to other causes, even though it is known that the vast majority of adverse events due to vaccines tend occur within days to months of vaccination, not years. Even so, any poor health outcome in a vaccinated person is employed to manufacture fear and drive the narrative that vaccines are inherently unsafe, despite decades of positive safety data. Fear mongering about “long term effects” of vaccines has been a staple of antivaccine messaging since the last century, when American immunologist and longstanding antivaccine activist J. Bart Classen, claimed that vaccines could cause type 1 diabetes [69] ; more recently he has claimed that vaccines cause prion diseases.
By its nature, collecting this type of long-term data takes several years after testing and authorization, allowing anti-vaccine activists to exploit infertility as a gap in safety data for a decade or longer. Even when long-term studies show no differences in fertility between vaccinated and unvaccinated populations or demonstrate a risk to fertility due to infection with the wild-type pathogen, as we see with both HPV and SARS-CoV-2—especially for male infertility [70,71] —safety results are rarely accepted by anti-vaccine advocates, and calls for “more research” inevitably continue. As such, these tactics should be anticipated for any new vaccines released in the future. Messaging regarding the COVID-19 vaccines currently should emphasize the risks of COVID-19 infection to pregnant persons, the potential for actual fertility risk in men, and the evidence to date of vaccine safety during pregnancy and breastfeeding.
Countering misinformation of this sort is incredibly difficult because misinformation spreads very quickly. It is also purposely amplified via social media by anti-vaccine influencers [72] and inadvertently through social media algorithms that prize engagement because of the financial model of their businesses in which more engagement results in more time spent on the social media platform and therefore more opportunities to encounter targeted advertising. Though several social media companies have established guidelines to reduce such spread, Twitter in particular has recently rescinded its policy on COVID-19 misinformation, and reinstated the accounts of many individuals banned for the intentional spreading of antivaccine misinformation [73] . A number of debunking resources have been developed, such as the Kaiser Family Foundation’s series of videos on COVID-19 and fertility [74] . Recorded in English and Spanish, this series takes on major anti-vaccine talking points and shows how they are false. However, debunking alone often has limited reach once an idea has gone viral, and ongoing relationships are important between the audience and the debunker for such efforts to have a significant effect [75] .
The concept of “prebunking,” or teaching individuals how to spot potential misinformation as a way to inoculate against its spread, has gained ground in recent years as a way to prevent ideas from spreading in the first place, and to teach audiences to skeptically evaluate claims [76] . The rationale used to justify prebunking as a strategy to counter misinformation is that it easier to teach laypeople the general forms that antivaccine misinformation and conspiracy theories take because, although misinformation is protean in its specifics, its claims do generally fall into a much smaller, easier-to-digest, number of specific categories of claims that recur again and again for new vaccines; e.g., the claim that COVID vaccines cause infertility, like prior claims that HPV and tetanus vaccines cause infertility. Teaching general principles of skepticism and critical thinking is likely to be useful in “inoculating” the population against the specific forms of misinformation that can be anticipated, particularly when combined with education about more general rhetorical forms of misinformation, such as straw men arguments, appeals to false authority, and conspiracy theories. Unfortunately, rigorous studies on the application of these techniques to countering misinformation and disinformation about COVID-19 vaccines are still scant. They are desperately needed because, by the time the next pandemic arrives, the misinformation problem is almost certainly going to be much worse than it has been for COVID-19.
