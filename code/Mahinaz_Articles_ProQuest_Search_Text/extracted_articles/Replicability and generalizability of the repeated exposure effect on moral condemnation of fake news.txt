Introduction
In recent years, there has been growing concern about the spread of misinformation online 2,3 , including false or misleading claims about the 2016 and 2020 U.S. Presidential Elections 4,5 , the COVID-19 pandemic 6 , and climate change 7 . As a result, academics and policy-makers alike have devoted a great deal of time and resources to understanding what drives the sharing of misinformation on social media.
In a recent influential paper, Effron & Raj 1 demonstrated a particularly pernicious way in which social media may facilitate the spread and impact of falsehoods. Effron & Raj (2020) showed that repeated exposure—a common characteristic of the user experience on social media platforms—reduces the extent to which people see it as immoral to publish or share falsehoods, a specific instance of the moral repetition effect in the context of fake news 8 . This finding is of substantial importance, given that moral leniency has been linked to the sharing of misinformation consistent with one’s beliefs 9 , and moral condemnation is integrally related to why people punish 10, 11–12 , acting as the glue that holds social norms together. Without the threat of moral condemnation, bad actors can spread falsehoods in an unconstrained fashion. Furthermore, to the extent that users see the sharing of misinformation as less unethical, this may induce even greater inattention to accuracy when deciding what to share 6,13 . Thus, by undermining condemnation and making it seem less unethical to share falsehoods, Effron & Raj (2020)’s results suggest that repeated exposure to falsehoods on social media facilitates the publication and sharing of misinformation and helps to explain why fake news spreads online.
Given the scientific and societal importance of these findings, it is essential to assess their replicability and generalizability, and to investigate the robustness of the results to experimental design choices. To that end, we conceptually replicated Study 4 in Effron & Raj (2020), with an expanded and updated set of headlines, and with an additional set of analyses exploring potential moderating effects of a more ecologically valid environment.
In their Study 4, Effron & Raj (2020) presented participants with a randomly chosen set of fake news headlines and subsequently elicited judgments about moral condemnation, accuracy, and sharing intentions for a second set of false headlines, which included both the first set of headlines and a set of novel headlines. Effron & Raj (2020) found that relative to the novel headlines, the headlines that were previously presented elicited less moral condemnation, were perceived as more accurate, and had higher sharing intentions. Out of the set of studies conducted in Effron & Raj (2020), we chose Study 4 to replicate because it is the most ecologically valid study (in Studies 1-3, individuals were explicitly told that all headlines were fake, which they would not be told in most social media contexts).
An important aspect of news-related studies is that headlines may become out of date quickly. To address this issue, we complement the original set of headlines, updating the wording of six that are outdated (shown in Edited Headlines)—with the approval of one of the authors of Effron & Raj (2020)—with a larger updated set of false headlines that are current (for a total of 100 false headlines). While Effron & Raj (2020) showed all participants the same set of 12 false headlines, we showed each participant 12 false headlines (presented in a random order) that are sampled from this much larger set of 100 false headlines. Following the method used to select the original headlines, which themselves came from Pennycook et al. 14 , the additional headlines were selected using pre-tests to be balanced on partisan lean. This will allow us to assess the extent to which the repetition effect on condemnation generalizes, rather than being idiosyncratic to the particular 12 headlines used by Effron & Raj 15 . We expect to see successful generalization based on recent follow-up work showing that the moral repetition effect occurs with (i) a different set of fake news headlines in two independent samples (studies 1a and 1b), and ii) a range of headlines and stimuli beyond fake news 8 .
In the original study design, the authors asked participants to complete multiple outcome measures for each headline: moral condemnation, sharing intentions, and sharing behavior. Additionally, Effron & Raj (2020) measured four further control variables to isolate the pathways through which the effect occurs: perceived accuracy of the headline, how much a participant likes the headline, perceived popularity of the headline, and the headline’s writing quality. An extensive literature has shown that asking people to judge headline accuracy—and therefore delivering an accuracy prompt 16 by forcing people to consider a headline’s veracity—can have dramatic effects on sharing decisions, for example, reducing the sharing of false claims 13,17 . It is possible that drawing attention to accuracy also plays an important role in Effron & Raj (2020)’s experiments because drawing attention to accuracy may result in greater recognition of false headlines, in turn increasing the outrage experienced by the participants and their subsequent condemnation 8 . Furthermore, having participants decide whether to share headlines has also been found to change perceptions of accuracy—and thus all outcome measures may potentially be affected in unpredictable ways by being jointly elicited 17 . While accuracy may not be an important or relevant component of the moral repetition effect in other domains, it could play a central role in its relationship with fake news.
One reason that moral condemnation of fake news may occur, in addition to reasons such as unethical framing and real-world harm, is the fact that it is false—and one must first realize that the news itself is false before falsity can cause outrage. Drawing attention to accuracy may make it more likely that participants realize the news is false, implicitly heightening moral condemnation. Additionally, the illusory truth effect suggests that seeing content multiple times can make it appear more true 18 which, when combined with a prompt for accuracy, can cause differential perceptions of accuracy, leading to a reduction in moral condemnation of previously seen content. A recent paper 19 shows a relationship between perceptions of veracity and ethicality outside the context of news. Repeated descriptions of unethical behavior increased belief in them while decreasing ratings of their immorality. These ties may be even deeper in the context of fake news, where the ethicality of publishing and sharing fake news is contingent on whether the news is true or not.
Given that information encountered online is rarely combined with explicit appeals to consider accuracy, we explored how the effect of repeated exposure on moral condemnation varies depending on whether attention is drawn to accuracy. If moral condemnation is unaffected by repetition without explicitly drawing attention to accuracy, the effect of repeated exposure on moral condemnation may not be of first-order importance on social media where individuals are often inattentive to accuracy 13 . Additionally, examining the effect of asking about accuracy on condemnation can shed light on the mechanism underlying accuracy prompts. If asking about accuracy increases moral condemnation of false headlines, it may be that part of how accuracy prompts reduce misinformation sharing is by making participants more concerned about the negative social consequences of sharing such content.
Thus, we implemented two treatment arms to directly test the role of accuracy prompting: 1) the Full Condition which closely replicates the original study by asking for all outcome and control measures from the original study (except for writing quality, which the authors exclude from analyses and is not something that users naturally consider when on social media), and 2) the Ecological Condition which asks all of the measures in the Full condition except for accuracy. The second condition provides a more ecologically valid environment, as on social media, attention is naturally drawn to sharing (in the form of a button), liking (in the form of a button), and popularity (in the form of share and like statistics), but not accuracy.
To mirror the design of the original study, we used an identical outcome measure for moral condemnation as Effron & Raj (2020). We presented a slider from 0 to 100, with a label at 0 of “Not at All” and a label at 100 of “Extremely”, and a default value of 50, and ask “How unethical would it be to share the above headline on social media?” In the first arm, we asked for a measure of perceived accuracy (measured with a slider from 0-100) and all other outcome and control measures as in the original study, in addition to moral condemnation. In the second arm, we removed the question asking about accuracy. All analyses used the untransformed 0–100 scales, and all other variables were measured exactly as in Effron & Raj (2020).

Results
We recruited 1884 participants on Prolific to be evenly split between Democrats and Republicans. 150 participants failed the first attention check, while an additional 49 participants failed the captcha. A further 65 participants were excluded for reporting their party identification as true Independent. Finally, an additional 20 participants were excluded for failing to complete the outcome measures. This leaves a final sample of 1600 participants ( M a g e  = 42.8, female = 61.8%) with 813 identifying as Democrats and 787 identifying as Republicans. We note that we oversampled participants relative to the pre-registered sample size, given the uncertainty surrounding how many participants would be excluded for failing attention checks, failing to complete the survey, or stating that they are a true Independent. We ended up with a final sample that is 100 participants larger than the pre-registered sample size.

We begin by looking at the effect of repetition on ratings of how unethical it would be to share a piece of content in the Full condition. This provides a direct test of the replicability of the original findings with an expanded headline set. Exploratory analyses
In a pre-registered analysis to test the main effect of the accuracy evaluation on moral condemnation rather than the interaction of the accuracy evaluation with repetition, we estimate an identical model to Equation (2) but remove the fixed effects. We find that the moral condemnation of sharing novel headlines is 3.71pp (CI: [−6.45, −0.97], t (99) = −2.69, p  = 0.008) lower in the Ecological condition than in the Full condition, suggesting that drawing attention to accuracy may be important in evaluating the morality of sharing behavior online.
Finally, we estimate whether there is moderation in the effect of repetition by the political concordance of the headline with the participant. In our first pre-registered specification, we exclude headlines from the original set as they were not included in the same pre-test as the larger set of headlines, making direct comparisons difficult. We find that there is no significant difference in the effect of repetition between headlines that are concordant relative to discordant ( β old×concordant  = 0.64, CI: [−1.10, 2.38], t (87) = 0.73, p  = 0.47). We also find no significant interaction with a second specification that was not pre-registered that includes the pre-tested partisan ratings of the original headlines in the model ( β o l d × c o n c o r d a n t  = 1.04, CI: [−0.56, 2.63], t (99) = 1.29, p  = 0.20). We emphasize that this analysis is exploratory and underpowered.
In non-pre-registered analyses, we explore the effect of repetition on the auxillary outcome measures. We repeat model (1) in the Full condition with each measured outcome separately—how much a participant likes the headline, their estimate of the percent of Americans who have seen it, their likelihood of sharing, and their perceptions of its accuracy. First, we confirm the presence of the standard illusory truth effect 18 —the finding that repetition increases belief in the truthfulness of a statement. Repeated items are believed 3.40pp (CI: [2.54, 4.28], t (99) = 7.87, p  < 0.001) more than novel items. It is notable that this effect is approximately 1pp smaller in magnitude than the effect on moral condemnation. Second, repeated items are liked 2.90pp (CI: [1.76, 4.03], t (99) = 5.07, p  < 0.001) more than novel items. Third, they are estimated to have been seen by more people (β old = 3.14pp, CI: [2.39, 3.89], t (99) = 8.30, p  < 0.001). Finally, there is no significant effect on sharing intentions (β old = 0.02, CI: [−0.02, 0.07], t (99) = 0.94, p  = 0.35).
Discussion
The finding that more frequently seen headlines receive lower moral condemnation for sharing is concerning, given that moral condemnation is a motivation for enforcing norms and punishing transgressions. Without this condemnation, the publication and spreading of online misinformation may be more common. Given this important result, we replicated the study conducted by Effron & Raj (2020) using an expanded headline set and a larger sample. We successfully replicated the finding that repeated exposure decreases moral condemnation, and found evidence that the effect generalizes across items and samples. In fact, the point estimate in the replication is nearly identical to that in the original study.
While repetition caused a clear reduction in moral condemnation in the experimental condition where accuracy is primed, there are theoretical reasons to expect drawing attention to accuracy to moderate the effect. In particular, drawing attention to accuracy could make the moral transgression of sharing misinformation clearer, as identifying the content as misinformation is a precondition for morally condemning its sharing. We test this proposition through the use of a condition that does not ask about accuracy, and find that not only does the effect of repetition on moral condemnation still appear, but there is no significant difference in the effects between the two conditions. This important generalization of the moral repetition effect to a setting that does not prime accuracy, as is generally the case on social media, suggests that the effect is likely to appear online. In non-pre-registered exploratory analyses, we find evidence that baseline moral condemnation (of novel headlines) is lower in the Ecological condition, suggesting that drawing attention to accuracy does increase moral condemnation. This exploratory finding provides preliminary evidence that one pathway through which accuracy prompt interventions 13 can reduce the sharing of misinformation is by making potential sharing more morally dubious.
Theoretically, repetition may be related to perceptions of normativity, potentially through an association of processing fluency with what is common 20 . Beliefs such as moral condemnation, accuracy, and affective liking may be downstream of such normative considerations (i.e., what is common is likely morally right, correct, and likable). The repetition of misinformation may increase the fluency with which it is processed, changing perceptions of how common it is, as seen here via increases in belief about the percentage of Americans who have seen the headline. If the headline is more standard and normative, it may be viewed as less condemnable and more likable. Existing research has reached mixed conclusions about the role of fluency and normativity in the moral repetition effect with some work 8 finding that response times did not mediate the moral repetition effect (and standard measures of processing fluency suffering from ceiling effects) and other work 19 finding that perceptions of normativity partially mediates the moral repetition effect with anger serving as a stronger mediator. Beyond the moral repetition effect, the illusory truth effect may change perceptions of what is true through perceptions of what others, particularly credible sources, express to be true 21 . Future research can further explore the importance of beliefs about what is normative in the effect of repetition across domains.
Methods
The research complies with all relevant ethical regulations and has been determined to be exempt by the Massachusetts Institute of Technology Committee on the Use of Humans as Experimental Subjects (COUHES) under protocol E-6031. Participants provided informed consent and were paid $1.25 for completing the task (which we estimated to take about 8 minutes). Data collection and analysis was not performed blind to the conditions of the experiment.
The original study design, which we followed closely, is comprised of three stages. In the first stage, called the familiarization stage, participants are presented with 6 false headlines randomly selected from the set of possible headlines. Participants are asked to evaluate each headline on a scale evaluating how 1) interesting, 2) funny, 3) engaging, or 4) well-written it is. In the next stage, the participant is asked to complete a 9 question demographic and political preference survey which serves as a distractor test. We used the exact survey from Effron & Raj (2020) to collect demographics and political preferences, updating the questions about vote choice to reflect the 2020 election rather than 2016. Lastly, in the judgment stage, participants are shown 12 headlines—the 6 from the familiarization stage and 6 additional randomly selected headlines—and asked for their evaluation on each of the outcome measures of interest. Given this design, the primary effect—that of the difference in moral repetition between repeated and non-repeated headlines—was within-subject. However, the moderation between the two conditions leveraged a mixed-design.
We directly replicate the above experimental procedure with the following changes: 1) the 12 headlines participants are shown were sampled from a larger set of headlines (including all original headlines) and 2) we use the familiarization questions from a more recent paper by an author of the original paper 8 which mixes the framing of the questions to be balanced between positive and negative wording. We are concerned that the use of four positively worded familiarization questions may paint the repeated headlines in a positive light while introducing them to the participant. As such, the familiarization questions we use are: “How [engaging/boring/well-written/poorly-worded] do you find this headline?” While Effron (2022) 8 only uses two familiarization questions (engaging/boring), we have added (well-written/poorly-worded) to have four familiarization questions as in the original study to guarantee the same level of exposure as the original study. We have maintained the original study design that randomizes the order of headlines presented for each question sequentially. This means that each headline is shown 4 times in the familiarization stage. We also kept the original familiarization stage scales which are 7 point Likert scales ranging from “Not at all” to “Very.”
In classifying the characteristics of headlines, we used data collected from pre-tests (which are also used to ensure political balance in the final headline set). We collected eight measures, which are standard in pre-tests of headlines which can be used as continuous scores to find generalizable effects of headline characteristics. The measures are 1) the favorability to Republicans versus Democrats, 2) the plausibility, 3) the sensationalism, 4) the informativeness, 5) the impact, 6) familiarity, 7) willingness to share, 8) positivity. Each of these characteristics were measured on a 5 point Likert scale.
We determine the partisanship of a headline by splitting on the midpoint of favorability to party. Those headlines that are rated by pre-testers to be more favorable to Democrats are considered to be Democrat-leaning headlines and vice-versa for Republican. Political concordance of a headline is be defined as whether or not this measure is equal to the participant’s self-reported political party preference. In the distractor task, participants are asked “In politics today, do you consider yourself [A Democrat/A Republican/An Independent/None of the above].”
Participant sampling procedure and power calculations
We planned to recruit 1500 participants on Prolific evenly split between Democrats and Republicans, matching the recruitment platform and sample composition of the original study. We employed identical participant screening checks as the original study (preventing use of mobile devices, restricting to only US IP addresses), and both attention checks were from the original study. While few participants should identify as Independents given our sampling, we also exclude all participants who identify their political party as true Independents while including those who lean towards one party or the other. Any survey participant who failed any of these checks was removed from the sample and not counted towards the quota-matching (meaning the final sample is quota-matched). All participants who passed these screeners are included in the final analysis. This sample selection process ensures a sample as close as possible in demographic characteristics, including party identification, to the original while being substantially larger. Participants were randomized with equal probability to the two conditions.
To asses the power of our experimental design to detect a replication of the original effect, we use bootstrapping. In each iteration, we take data from the original study and randomly draw 750 times at the individual-level (including all 12 of their observations) with replacement. Given that our proposed design includes 100 headlines, rather than the original 12, we evenly split observations for each of the original headlines into multiple synthetic headlines, providing a total of 100 synthetic headlines while preserving the clustered nature of the data. We then estimate the replication model presented in Equation (1). We record whether the p-value on β 1 is less than 0.05, providing an α  = 0.05 criterion for the power calculations. We implement this procedure 500 times and calculate the power as the percent of iterations where β 1 is significant. Our study design is powered at over 99% to detect an effect size equivalent to that in the original paper. We also implement an identical bootstrapping procedure, but estimating the original multi-level model rather than the fixed-effects model, and find we are still powered at greater than 99%.
To find the minimum replication effect that we can detect, we implement an identical procedure to that above, but we scale the individual-level difference between novel and repeat headlines. To do this, we compute an individual’s average difference in unethical ratings between repeat and novel headlines. We then add this difference, scaled by a constant with minor individual-level mean-zero noise, to the outcome ratings for novel headlines. We test three constants—0.75, 0.8, and 0.85—and find we can detect β  = −1.27, corresponding to a roughly 1pp drop in moral condemnation or 75% reduction in the original effect, with α  = 0.05 and 95% power using a two-sided hypothesis test. We can detect a 80% reduction in the effect, with β  = −1.04, with 95% power using a one-sided hypothesis test. For context, this equates to detecting a standardized effect size of D  = 0.04 with 95% power using a two-sided hypothesis test and over 99% using a one-sided hypothesis test or D  = 0.03 with 95% power on a one-sided test. These are effect sizes below which there is likely little relevance in real-world settings, and are substantially smaller than the effect size for moral licensing [ D  = 0.31] 22 and the moral repetition effect size reported in the original paper ( D  = 0.28). As specified in the design table, we use the one-sided hypothesis test to determine a successful replication. These power calculations additionally apply to the Ecological condition, suggesting that we can detect an effect in that condition equivalent in size to the original effect size with 99% power and a 75% reduction of the original effect with 95% power, with the pre-registered two-sided hypothesis test.
To determine the number of participants necessary in the study, we conduct similar power calculations for Model (2). We test the power of five potential sample sizes—1000, 1250, 1500, 1750, 2000—to determine the minimum sample that will produce 95% power for a half-moderation—corresponding to a reduction in the effect of 2.21pp and a resulting effect of −2.40 in the Ecological Condition—between the two conditions. We selected a half-moderation as a smaller moderation of the effect is unlikely to be practically meaningful, corresponding to a standardized difference of approximately D  = 0.06. As a benchmark, the smallest significant moderator of the illusory truth effect in a comprehensive meta-analysis of the effect corresponds to a standardized difference of D  = 0.10 23 . In each iteration of the bootstrapping procedure, we select N (the proposed sample size) synthetic participants with replacement from the original data. Half of the synthetic participants are assigned to the Ecological condition and half to the Replication condition, as will occur in the actual study. We implement the same procedure as above for creating the synthetic headlines. For participants in the Ecological condition, we compute the individual-level difference between novel and repeated headlines. To induce moderation, we add half (with minor individual-level noise) of that difference to the unethical rating of novel headlines. We, then, estimate Equation (2) and compute the proportion of draws that detect a significant effect with α  = 0.05. We find that 1500 participants is the minimum tested sample size that detects the moderation with 95% power and α  = 0.05.
Analysis plan
The analysis plan closely follows the original study for the replication of the main effect with the addition of a linear regression model that presents another method of dealing with clustered data. The analysis exploring the potential interaction of drawing attention to accuracy and repetition uses linear regression (ordinary least squares estimation) rather than multi-level models given that it is sufficiently different from the original study.
Main effect replication
In the replication portion of the study, we are primarily interested in testing the effect of repeated exposure on moral condemnation, where moral condemnation can be interpreted as the participant’s view on the ethicality of sharing that particular headline. We subset to the full condition for the analysis. As mentioned in Methods, we are powered at over 99% to detect an effect size equivalent to that in the original study with α  = 0.05 and an effect size 75% smaller than the original effect with 95% power and α  = 0.05 using a two-tailed hypothesis test. With a one-tailed hypothesis test, our preferred specification for evaluating replication, we are powered at 95% to detect an effect size 80% smaller than the original effect.
To keep the replication as close to the original as possible, we estimate the exact model in the original paper. However, the multi-level model used in the original paper includes random intercepts but not random slopes, which has been shown to inflate the chance of false positives 24 . Given that random intercepts only models are problematic, we estimate an additional model that deals with clustered data. We fit a linear regression that accounts for repeated observations across headlines and participants by including vectors of headline- and participant-dummies (termed headline and participant fixed effects) and two-way clustered standard errors.
Each model is implemented at the level of the participant-judgment stage rating (12 observations per participant). Specifically, in each model, we predict moral condemnation rating in the judgment stage using a dummy indicating whether the headline was shown in the familiarization stage (an exposure dummy). In the linear regression model, we also include dummies for each participant and headline (which are analogous, without making distributional assumptions, to the random intercepts in the multi-level models, accounting for variation in average condemnation across participants and headlines), and use two-way clustered standard errors clustered on participant and headline 25 , to account for the non-independence of repeated observations from the same participant, and the same headline. condemnation i j = α i + γ j + β 1 old i j + ϵ i j where α i and γ j are a vector of dummies for individual and headline, respectively. β 1 gives the effect of an individual having seen that headline before, represented by the dummy variable o l d i j . Removing α i and γ j gives the level-0 equation for Effron & Raj (2020)’s original multi-level model.
We provide estimates for both of these models to allow readers to evaluate the strength of the replication. We provide a plot that demonstrates where each model’s coefficient estimate and confidence intervals, using two-tailed hypothesis tests, fall in relation to the original study’s estimate and confidence intervals. We report p-values and t-statistics from both a one-tailed and two-tailed hypothesis test for each model. We have confirmed that this linear regression approach yields similar results to the multi-level model approach used by Effron & Raj (2020), producing an almost identical estimate of b  = −4.6 ( t two−tailed (11) = −3.72, p two−tailed  = 0.003). We do, however, use the multi-level model in the original paper to determine whether the experiment replicates. We consider a lack of a negative and significant coefficient on β 1 in the original multi-level model, with a one-tailed hypothesis test, to be an unsuccessful replication.
Accuracy
After examining whether the original results replicate in the Full Condition, we explore the potential moderation of the moral repetition effect for fake news by excluding the accuracy prompt using a similar linear regression model as above. The fixed effects structure remains the same for the linear regression model, yielding equation: 2
condemnation i j = α i + γ j + β 1 old i j + β 2 old i j × ecological i + ϵ i j where all variables are the same as above, with the additional moderating effect estimated by β 2 , which gives the difference between the Full Condition and the Ecological Condition. This coefficient can be interpreted as the moderation of the moral repetition effect for fake news by removing the accuracy nudge. A positive and significant β 2 suggests that drawing attention to the accuracy explained at least part of the original paper’s results.
Additionally, we estimate Equation (1) with only the Ecological Condition to verify whether the moral repetition effect for fake news is robust to removing accuracy as an outcome. The analysis is identical to that for the Full Condition, and an insignificant effect of β 1 means we would fail to reject that null hypothesis that there is a difference in moral condemnation between repeated and novel headlines. As outlined above, with this sample size, we could detect a minimum main effect that has been moderated from the original effect by 75% corresponding to a β 1 of ~−1.27 or D  = 0.04 with α  = 0.05 and 95% power.
We also include a version of Model (2), removing the participant fixed effects and including the ecological dummy. This allows us to estimate whether there are baseline differences in moral condemnation between the two conditions. This coefficient estimate isolates the effect that accuracy nudges have on baseline moral condemnation.
Pre-registered exploratory analysis
Given the possibility of a non-replication of the main findings, there were multiple possible exploratory analyses to address potential heterogeneity between our study and Effron and Raj’s study. If either the multi-level or fixed effects model in the main analyses did not produce a negative and significant coefficient on β 1 , we would use item-level random-effects estimates from a multi-level model with participant and headline random slopes and intercepts to locate the original headlines within the distribution of item-level effects. We would have plotted a histogram of the distribution of random-effect estimates and highlighted the location of each original headline. If the random effects of the original items had been highly affected by repetition relative to the rest of the distribution, this would have provided suggestive evidence that the original effects were driven by idiosyncrasies in the headlines used.
In the case that the coefficient estimate for β 1 had not been negative and significant for both of the main models, we would have formally tested whether the original items have different effects by using the same linear regression presented in Model (1) but removing the headline-level fixed effects and including a dummy variable and its interaction with o l d for whether a headline was from the original set. Furthermore, we would have compared the perceived accuracy of the original headlines in our experiment to those in Effron and Raj’s experiment and to our broader set of headlines using a two-sample t test. This would have tested whether the headlines differ systematically in terms of their perceived accuracy.
Next, we are interested in exploring potential interactions of political concordance and the main effect. However, as this analysis is exploratory and requires a large sample size, we only perform this analysis since we found no evidence of moderation by condition. As such, we pool data from both conditions. To examine the potential strengthening effect of alignment of headline with one’s political beliefs, we use the model described in Equation (1), adding the interaction of repeated exposure with a dummy indicating whether the headline is politically concordant versus discordant with the participant’s partisanship (as in, e.g., Pennycook et al. 13 ). The political lean of the headline was determined using a binary classification of a pre-test question asking whether a headline is more favorable to Democrats or Republicans. To deal with participants who report their party identification as Independent but leaning towards one of the parties, we include those leaners with the party they most identify with.
The regression we use for the political concordance analysis is presented in Equation (3): 3
condemnation i j = α i + γ j + β 1 old i j + β 2 old i j × concordant i j + ϵ i j where all variables are as above, with the addition of concordant i j , which is a dummy indicating if the headline is politically discordant or concordant with the participant’s party.
To estimate the strength of a headline-level moderator that our design would be powered to detect, we conduct bootstrap power calculations using a similar process to that above. However, for each of the 1500 synthetic participants, we randomly select half of the headlines to be moderated. We draw the mean of that headline towards the participant’s grand mean by a scalar value. With 1500 participants, we are powered to detect a full moderation with α  = 0.05 and power of 0.99, an 80% moderation with 93% power and a half moderation with 52% power for Equation (3). Given that a half-moderation may be practically meaningful, but we are underpowered to detect the effect, any analyses of headlines should be considered purely exploratory. Using the moderation by political concordance observed in the original data, we are powered at 55% to detect the effect.
