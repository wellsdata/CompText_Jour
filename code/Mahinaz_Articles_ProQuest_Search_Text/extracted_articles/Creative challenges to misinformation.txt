Two anatomy books, open at the sections describing female genital anatomy, are currently on display at the State Library of Victoria, Melbourne, VIC, Australia. Both have been defaced. In Quain and Wilson's 1842 Series of Anatomical Plates , a direct source for the first edition of Gray's Anatomy , the page devoted to “Female Organs of Generation” is patched up with tape after repeat vandalism by generations of readers who must have felt there was something inherently obscene about documenting female biology. Next to it is a copy of Last's Anatomy that belonged to Professor Helen O’Connell when she was a young medical trainee. The text is etched with underlinings and exclamations—early signs of O’Connell's indignation at the sheer paucity of information about the female genitalia available when she was studying for her final examinations. Such knowledge gaps would later spur this extraordinary surgeon to become not only a professor and Australia's first female urologist, but also the person whose comprehensive 1998 study of the clitoris finally put this long forgotten organ on the anatomical map. Of course, standing in an exhibition, with everything clearly signposted, it is easy to tell the difference between censorship and healthy scepticism. But in the real world, when we find ourselves bombarded by information, how can we distinguish between facts designed to obscure the truth, and those which elucidate?
Make Believe: Encounters with Misinformation , the exhibition of which these anatomy texts form just one part, engages with exactly this question. Its very design seems to encourage curiosity and questioning. Walled by mirrors, with a soundscape of rain, and lighting that alternates through the colours of the rainbow, visitors are invited to sit comfortably in one of eight cloud-upholstered armchairs, and put on a headset. During a short video, kaleidoscopic images of the natural and urban world are interwoven with key prompts, asking why we are all susceptible to misinformation as well as scrutinising its causes, consequences, and possible remedies. A posse of librarians, academics, and artists offer their thoughts in a way that feels exploratory rather than dogmatic, perfectly priming the visitor for what the rest of the exhibition has in store, four satellite rooms surrounding the central hub, in which different case studies examine misinformation in a variety of ways.
In the first, Wiradjuri and Ngiyampaa artist Charlotte Allingham employs a clever artistic juxtaposition to tackle the enduringly difficult national issue of terra nullius , a legal concept meaning land belonging to no-one, which was used by the invading British to whitewash their colonisation of a country that had been inhabited for millennia by Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander Peoples. During the late 1950s, Shell Petroleum launched a successful campaign called Discover Australia. Departing from previous advertisements that had focused on the benefits of oil and petrol, this one encouraged Australians to celebrate the natural beauty of their country by embarking on road trips powered by Shell. Beautiful maps, posters, and collectable swap cards, featuring drawings of Australian flowers, sea life, and shells, were offered by the company to inspire brand loyalty in their customers. Allingham's own dark reinterpretations interrogate this history, contrasting the harmful environmental impacts of colonial and industrial extraction with Indigenous relationships to the land. Across one wall, a huge monochromatic dystopia painted in grey and black depicts fuel containers looming over the remains of dead fish and oil spills, illustrating how commercial misinformation played a part in destroying the fauna and flora it purported to prize.

A more personal focus emerges in the work of Hong Kong-born artist and drag queen Scotty So. When So started sifting through the State Library's archive for photographs of ancient Chinese clothing as part of a new creative project, he was disturbed to discover that the only images they had were pictures of white people from Victorian times, often using “yellowface” and wearing Chinese costume to appear, for example, as magicians. He also noticed that many items of clothing were being worn incorrectly. Outraged and fascinated, So resolved to explore this example of graphic misinformation, and the way in which historical artefacts become credited as authentic simply by dint of being in a museum archive. The result is an intriguing room in which images of So himself dressed in Chinese costume are inserted alongside pictures taken from the library's archive or Wikipedia. Sartorial pieces on display provide a kind of concrete reference point, within which So's work poses deep questions of what makes things believable and how we can ever really know what is true.
In the third space, artist and academic Sofi Basseghi shows how censorship can be a form of misinformation that erases perspectives from history. Growing up in Tehran, Iran, Basseghi has first-hand experience of the devastating ways in which knowledge can be restricted. On one side of the room, ancient and modern texts are displayed under glass, like talismans of truth. Opposite, a fluorescent green neon sign immediately foxes the viewer because it is written in Farsi. By the time I read the translation—“Freedom is not Free”—on the small plaque beside it, I had already imbued the message that text can be used to obscure as well as inform. As if in response, a triptych of video installations, entitled Seven Portraits Unbound , explores the concept of freedom and women's resistance, as seen by female poets over the past millennium, with actress Salme Geransar, amplifying these long forgotten voices.
Of course, I was saving my favourite until last, and not only because a room entitled “Anatomy, biases and understanding the clitoris” would exert a special pull on any female surgeon. A couple of days before seeing the exhibition I was lucky enough to spend time with O’Connell in person, so entering the space devoted to her work felt like a sort of pleasurable reacquaintance. Here were some of the many ancient anatomy texts O’Connell pored over for her doctoral thesis, and in which she observed an alternation between information and erasure in the history of clitoris anatomy depending on the contemporary political mood.
There, under glass, were copies of the clitoris models O’Connell had allowed me to handle in her office, as we looked out over the Melbourne skyline and shared notes about training as women in surgery. Watching the exhibit video, in which O’Connell explains how she came to excavate and rewrite the anatomy of the female genitalia, supplementing the available data with her own meticulous cadaveric and MRI studies, I was charmed again by the combination of irony and penetrating intelligence that I had been so struck by when I met her. In our conversation O’Connell described the defiance she developed as a result of her convent education; the paradoxical feminism she learned from a mother who, despite parroting the traditional view that a woman's place was in the home, was always clearly happiest when she was working. But, as I stood in front of the last display case and looked down on the least flashy exhibit in the clitoris room, O’Connell's doctoral thesis elegantly bound in red leather, the importance of this wonderful exhibition landed. In a world rife with misinformation and disinformation about vaccines and other anti-science views, it is clear that correcting misinformation is not just an academic pursuit. Each of the sections of Make Believe shows how challenging false facts can be the first step towards a kind of human justice. O’Connell has done more than provide urologists with the information they need to minimise surgical morbidity; she has granted women the route by which to claim their right to pleasure. At a time when health professionals and the public are increasingly discovering new examples of how anatomy is skewed towards white, male patients, we should follow in O’Connell's footsteps. There is still a great deal of work to do.
