IN A 1988 ESSAY ENTITLED "Signal-to-Noise Ratio," German technology and media theorist Friedrich Kittler discusses the postwar development of information theory and cybernetics with an eye toward the task of hermeneutics. Kittler constructs an analogy between receptive technical devices like radio, radar, and telephones and the textual receptivity of readers. In this analogy, like our devices, we are situated within noisy environments; to make sense of our world entails discerning signals in the presence of noise. 1 Challenged by myriad technologies of signal-processing, Kittler believes that "an unoccupied space has emerged" within our theories of reading, a space where "one might substitute the practice of interception for the theory of reception," a space where machine reading is not rigorously distinguished from that of the human. 2
In suggesting this substitutability between "interception" (technical) and "reception" (textual), Kittler proposes a posthuman semiotics as one corollary of the cybernetic revolution. Indeed, the manner in which signal-processing in itself can give rise to sense-making, agentive subjects has been a question at the heart of cybernetic research from its inception to the present. Cybernetics' foundational paper "Behavior, Purpose and Teleology" demonstrated that signal-processing feedback loops in simple organisms and simple automata alike were sufficient to give rise to purposive faculties. 3 Cybernetics treated agents who modified their behavior based on signals processed from their environment as equally purposive, whether organic or machinic. Austrian-American physicist Heinz von Foerster's work in the "cybernetics of cybernetics" inaugurated a second era for this interdisciplinary science, replacing the objectivity of "observed systems" with the reflexive "cybernetics of observing systems," a "social cybernetics" that views the subjective agent as participating rather than merely acting. 4 Von Foerster's subject-focused, second-order cybernetics prompted speculations as to what third- and fourth-order cybernetics might entail. 5 These contemporary permutations on cybernetic frameworks grapple with interactions between human and nonhuman agents, intelligences operating within systemically complex environments of distributed agency and networked control—of governance and emergence.
Kittler coins a term for the interpretive practice of navigating this space opening between human and machinic intelligence: "hermenautics." Combining interpretation (hermeneutics) with the art of navigation (ναύτης, Gk. sailor), Kittler's term hermenautics also evokes the "steersman" or "pilot" that gives cybernetics its name (κῠβερνήτης, Gk. steersman): " hermenautics —a pilot's understanding of signals, whether they stem from gods, machines, or sources of noise." 6 In proposing this interpretive practice of the pilot and the sailor, Kittler outlines third-order cybernetics avant la lettre , where participants in any environment are judging while unmoored, precariously afloat on a medium of messages resounding with gods, machines, and noise-sources. If the traditional notion of textual hermeneutics is practiced by humans, for humans, Kittler's notion of hermenautics, read through the paradigm of third-order cybernetics, captures the reading practices of what philosopher Luciano Floridi calls "inforgs": "informationally embodied organisms" operating within an infosphere. 7 If hermeneutics, classically conceived, extracts nonobvious meanings from texts (ἑρμηνεύς / hermēneús , Gk. "one who translates"), then hermenautics navigates an immersive environment of signification. The act of steering through this hybrid environment of machinic and human signals is a nontrivial task.
At every turn, the economy of attention encounters vectors of persuasion originating from not-always-transparent actors. The motivations—let alone the meanings—behind the signals that litter our digital environment are nonobvious. To "interpret" while embedded in the "smart" landscape of the fourth Industrial Revolution is to do so in the presence of a deluge of signals. How one might best respond is often an uncharted task. The infosphere is increasingly rife with risks for the navigator/interpreter (risk > risicum , Lat. rock, crag, reef). 8 These risks for agents making sense of their collective world are in no way captured by the tidy binary of signal versus noise. The signal-scape (to coin a term for the merger of messages and environments) is increasingly saturated with that special type of sense-making we call "disinformation"—a species of sense-making that includes all manner of traps for political agency, pitfalls for public health awareness, a many-pistoned engine churning with hatreds and suspicion leading from conspiracy clickbait to capitol riots.
"Disinformation" first appears in English in 1955, when the Times of London speaks hopefully of "the elimination of every form of propaganda and disinformation, as well as of other forms of conduct which create distrust or in any other way impede the establishment of an atmosphere conducive to constructive international cooperation and to the peaceful coexistence of nations." 9 While "misinformation" is a word first attested near the dawn of print, "disinformation" is first attested during the Cold War's aspirational gambling on "the peaceful coexistence of nations." Just as Maxwell's demon haunts the telecommunications engineering project of information theory, wresting signals from noise, so too the demon of disinformation haunts the project of "peaceful coexisting nations" from earliest moments to the present day. 10

In closing his essay on cybernetics and a future hermeneutics, Kittler speaks not only of supplanting textual theories of reception with "the practice of interception"; he also observes this moment as one of substituting "polemics for hermeneutics." 11 We find ourselves today in Kittler's prophesied age of interception and polemics, where disinformation and "alternative facts" discourage thoughtful receptivity and interpretive subtlety. What responsibilities devolve onto literary hermeneutics as a practice within the so-called "post-truth" environment?
Von Foerster explicitly connects the "cybernetics of cybernetics" to social responsibility, one in which agents practice self-determination: "If we fail to do so somebody else will determine a purpose for us. Moreover, if we fail to do so, we shall provide the excuses for those who want to transfer the responsibility for their own actions to somebody else: 'I am not responsible for my actions; I just obey orders.' Finally, if we fail to recognize autonomy of each, we may turn into a society that attempts to honor commitments and forgets about its responsibilities." 12 In this statement about how cybernetic theories of subjectivity support a more socially responsible world, we hear von Foerster alluding to infamous statements by made Nazi functionary Adolf Eichmann at his trial in Jerusalem in 1961, famously conveyed to US audiences by Hannah Arendt in 1963 in her articles in The New Yorker . 13 Von Foerster, a scientist from a Jewish family in Vienna, draws on these expiatory attempts by Eichmann to illustrate the dangerous rhetoric through which human subjects would pass their own moral culpability off onto the external will of systems.
Yet Arendt's "report on the banality of evil" traces a bureaucratic process that had its origin in the sensationalism of virulent hatred. Thus, banality was preceded by a sensationalism of evil , a culture industry of deep-seated antisemitic conspiracy theories. This sensationalism of evil produced "fake news" about imagined Jewish treachery in Europe (e.g., the Dreyfus Affair, 1894-1906) and culminated in such disinformation campaigns as the Dolchstoßlegende , designed to transmute Germany's military defeat in World War I into a story of the nation's victimization.
Within the networked yet trenchantly polarized digital sphere, nations like the US are seeing two distinct public spheres emerge, driven by the polarization of content. While a liberal understanding of social hermeneutics might argue that being exposed to diverse viewpoints ameliorates this situation, studies of digital platform users suggest opposite effects. 14 If hermenautics has anything to offer to this situation today, it will not supplement literary hermeneutics in its task of deconstructing the ethos of narratives; it will develop a set of tools to combat disinformation through demystification of its sensationalist scenarios. Literary hermenautics must take up the third-order cybernetic task to assess how codeterminations of selves and others arise amid environments of signals, whether authored by humans or botnets, whether by "machines, or sources of noise" at times untraceable. The risks to navigation are high, but the alternatives are worse. Sense-making is a massively distributed ethical responsibility. Unfollowing the future is not an option.
