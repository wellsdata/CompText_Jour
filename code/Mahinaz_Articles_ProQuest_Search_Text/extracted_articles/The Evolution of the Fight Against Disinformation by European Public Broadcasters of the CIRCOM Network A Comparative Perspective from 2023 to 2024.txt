1. Introduction Disinformation, coupled with the rise of populism, has become a serious problem in Europe. Furthermore, the European Parliament elections of June 2024 saw increased support for far-right and Eurosceptic parties in countries such as France, Italy, Germany, Austria, and Belgium.
In response, French President Emmanuel Macron dissolved the National Assembly and called snap legislative elections for late June and early July 2024. This came after noting the growing rise of Marine Le Pen’s Rassemblement National (National Rally) Party, one that had already implemented a remodeled strategy for its online communication in order to gain the support of a broader spectrum of voters (Carral and Tuñón-Navarro 2020).
A similar phenomenon can be seen in Spain with the emergence of far-right influencer and Member of the European Parliament Alvise Pérez, founder of the anti-establishment group Se Acabó la Fiesta (The Party’s Over), which won three seats in the 2024 European Parliament elections. Alvise Pérez utilizes a social media strategy (essentially, the Telegram app) full of hoaxes and conspiracy theories to forge what has been labeled “mainstream fascism” (Senso Vila 2020) and the new discursive order of the Spanish far right (Camargo Fernández 2021). This is done in a way that utilizes audiovisual narrative strategies of disinformation that are based on hate speech, Islamophobia and anti-Europeanism (Rodríguez-Serrano et al. 2019).
Populism, social networks and the extreme right form an “elective affinity” (Gerbaudo 2023) of complementarity and mutual reinforcement, which has led some to question whether the future of democracy is at stake (Guerrero-Solé et al. 2023).
Similarly, disinformation, understood as “verifiably false or misleading information that is created, presented and disseminated for profit or to deliberately deceive the public and may cause public harm” (European Commission 2018), has become a global problem that also undermines the foundations of democracy and contaminates public debate, eroding citizens’ trust in their representatives and public institutions. This underscores the importance of quality, verified journalism that society can hold accountable (Rodríguez-Pérez 2020).
As such, in recent years, the leading European press has viewed that information disorder is caused by specific international actors who contribute to the gradual increase in political polarization and disinformation, posing a major threat to the stability of democratic institutions (Teruel-Rodríguez 2023).
Furthermore, the ease of producing false content online, coupled with its rapid dissemination and reach, means that the internet has become a breeding ground for ultra-right groups that attack media outlets and journalists who do not share their views (Pérez-Altable 2023). Such manipulated content promotes the creation of digital pseudo-media that habitually spread disinformation (Palau-Sampaio and Carratalá 2022). These actors ignore the most basic professional standards of journalism (Garde-Eransus and Salaverría 2024), instead opting for a discourse that incites hatred.
What is noted is a hatred that can be used for propaganda purposes and is often disguised as humor in all its forms: irony, satire, ridicule and insults (Domenach 1963). These messages are widely disseminated through social networks in the form of memes, which act like viruses and contagious ideas that take advantage of our social genetics and need for communication (Dawkins 1990; Rúas-Araújo and García-Sanz 2018).
Satirical content going viral has led to the coining of expressions such as “memecracy” (Rodríguez-Marín 2014). However, this use of satire is just a pretext to hide misinformation, as seemingly innocent jokes are disseminated with the aim of mocking politicians or social groups.
All of this has prompted reflection on the role that social media and truth play in society and whether social media has contributed to improving our relationship with that which is verifiable and demonstrable or whether truth has ceased to matter in society, having been replaced by a new social truth (a ‘trending truth’) (López-Borrull 2023) based on trending topics.
At the same time, artificial conflicts or ‘non-problems’ are generated to foster division and polarization. These issues are also created to erode public debate, weakening it as a rational vehicle for decision-making and an essential tool for democracy. Furthermore, reactionary harangues work to fracture the basic consensus that holds societies together, as well as to reduce the credibility of traditional institutions and elites that disseminate culture and knowledge, whether political, journalistic or academic (Lozano 2020). This is not to suggest that dissent is inherently prohibited.
1.1. EU Measure to Combat Disinformation By 2019, then-European Commissioner for the Security Union, Julian King, was already warning of the need to control the continued and orchestrated disinformation campaigns that were taking place in the run-up to that year’s European elections. The EU called on the three major online platforms (Google, Facebook, and Twitter) to counter interference in electoral processes intended to destabilize and cause chaos and to control political advertising that utilized hoaxes and deception.
In the 2024 elections, the European Parliament again warned of foreign interference, mentioning Russia and China in particular. It also mentioned the danger of so-called commissioned disinformation, advising the European Commission of the need to ensure control over the traceability of donations from third countries to national political parties, adopting a resolution specifically aimed at controlling disinformation (EuDisinfo 2024).
All of this prompted the recent approval of Regulation (EU) 24/900 of the European Parliament and of the Council of 3 March 2024 on the transparency and segmentation of political advertising. The aim is to promote a transparent information ecosystem that requires the identification of those behind all political advertising and online advertisements to preserve ethical political debate, campaigns and free elections.
In the Spanish context, this was also the motivation behind the creation of the Coordination Network for Security in Electoral Processes by the Ministry of the Interior, following the 2023 Annual Report on National Security prepared by the Department of Homeland Security (DHS).
In addition to this is Regulation EU 2024/1083 of 11 April 2024 on the freedom of the media (EU 2024), which all Member States must implement from 2025. In reality, most Member States display a selective approach, intervening only where clear gaps have been identified. As a result, the transposition of the law is slow or is being resisted (Centre for Media Pluralism and Media Freedom 2025). This is intended to address growing concerns about the politicization of the media and the lack of transparency regarding their ownership and allocation of public funds and subsidies. Furthermore, it aims to prevent political interference in editorial decisions and to monitor initiatives that protect users from harmful content and disinformation (Article 19). This law has enabled the creation of an independent European Media Services Committee, which will replace the group of regulatory bodies (ERGA) previously established in the Audiovisual Media Services Directive.
The law will require media companies to publish information about their owners and, consequently, address the impunity enjoyed by some pseudo-media outlets financed by extremist parties that spread disinformation.
At the legislative level, EU Regulation 2022/2065 on digital services (known as the Digital Services Act), applicable as of 17 February 2024, should also be noted. This requires digital services companies throughout the EU to be accountable for the content published on their platforms and will require large technology companies to remove information when it is illegal. In addition, it establishes more effective guarantees of transparency and protection of users’ fundamental rights.
1.2. Reports and State of the Question Following the European elections, the European Digital Media Observatory (EDMO n.d.) noted that no significant last-minute incidents related to disinformation were detected in this election. However, it was observed that disinformation about the EU increased significantly in the months leading up to the vote, with huge amounts of fake news. This led the group to declare that “the war of attrition is far from over” (EDMO n.d). As such, constant vigilance in the fight against disinformation remains necessary.
The European Fact-Checking Standards Network (EFCSN) is an entity that includes different European fact-checking platforms. Just before the recent European elections, they collected a total of 1321 pieces of verified false information in 26 European countries and found that almost half (46%) of the disinformation content spread through social networks did not receive any visible response or was not countered. As a result, some of the false publications and content achieved viral status, gaining more than 1.5 million visits on X (Twitter) and 500 thousand videos viewed on YouTube (EFCSN n.d.; Maldita.es 2024).
Similarly, within the disinformation detected, a series of false narratives were also found. There were those that related to immigration (57% of cases), those that questioned the integrity of the elections (56%) and those that promoted polarization, mistrust and discontent encouraged by pro-Kremlin agents, as well as attempts to defame the main European leaders (EuDisinfo 2024). Of particular relevance was also the abuse of gender-based disinformation in line with misogynistic narratives, as noted by The Global Disinformation Index (GDI 2024). In fact, one of the victims of these attacks was the President of the European Commission, Ursula von der Leyen, who, in her speech to the European Parliament on the occasion of her re-election, referred to the need to establish a barrier in Europe against disinformation. Significantly, the European Media Freedom Act (EMFA) 2024/1083 calls for the establishment of safeguards to ensure the independent functioning of public service media, as well as enhanced transparency, through the promotion of a healthy media ecosystem. Additionally, various resolutions of the Council of Europe have underscored the role of media in countering disinformation, which is regarded as a threat to democracy and fundamental rights.
Added to all of this is a media environment and context characterized by a loss of trust and credibility in the media, as well as information overload and fatigue among audiences (Fitzpatrick 2022). Indeed, it is well recognized that there are people who systematically avoid the news (Toff et al. 2023). News avoidance is more common among disadvantaged groups who, in general, receive less news coverage. This creates information deserts and threatens to accentuate existing inequalities as a result of social, political and technological boundaries.
The 2024 Digital News Report itself warns, among its conclusions, about the “communication and product problem” that journalism currently faces, arguing that when trust in the news is low, it is not usually because people do not know what they are looking for but rather because “many feel they are not getting it”. This report points out the importance of factors such as the divide in political orientation as well as interest and trust in the news, highlighting the influence of polarized environments. Furthermore, this report noted above reinforces the notion that there is a fundamental divide between those who make politics a central part of their lives and those who do not (Krupnikov and Ryan 2022).
1.3. Verification on CIRCOM Network Television All of the above illustrates that the problem is twofold. There is a lack of both credibility and trust in the media, partly due to the impact of misinformation, as well as issues and differences between the supply of and demand for news. Public TV in particular has a duty to face this challenge and to respond to the need for quality information that is accessible, in terms of real and symbolic capital, to the most disadvantaged classes or to those who cannot or do not want to access it (Díaz-Noci and Pérez-Altable 2023).
According to the European Broadcasting Union (EBU 2023), the majority of European public media outlets remain leaders in terms of trust in news services. Nonetheless, the truth is that the main weapon with which public media can combat disinformation is by strengthening their information mission by providing quality information, because it is this practice that underpins citizens’ trust (Rodríguez-Castro and Pérez-Seijo 2024).
In the current audiovisual landscape, viewers are influenced by the emergence of new international VOD platforms and expensive promotions. In this context, the defense of the public value of public service media is a differential factor when compared to commercial interests and is, above all, a guarantee for their future (Túñez López et al. 2021). It must be noted that truthfulness is a fundamental element of the public value that conditions the merit of public media.
The CIRCOM network is an entity that currently represents a total of 44 broadcasters from 29 European countries. The fight against disinformation constitutes one of the strategic plans included in its statutes and codes of good practice. Given CIRCOM’s status as public local media, the issue of misinformation acquires even greater relevance considering the relationship and connection it maintains with its community (Rúas-Araújo et al. 2023). As such, disinformation is an issue that CIRCOM members consider a priority, and they understand that it threatens the democratic and cultural integrity and identity of the territories they represent (Rúas-Araújo et al. 2023).
Previous studies, carried out via a series of interviews with professionals from these networks (Seijas-Vidal and Rúas-Araújo 2024), indicate the following data: the majority show great concern about disinformation (60% see it as very important and 20% as quite important) and recognize the need to incorporate new tools and resources in their newsrooms to combat it (55% view this as very necessary and 25% as quite necessary). At the same time, 75% of those interviewed saw the need to incorporate more human resources in the fight against disinformation, with personnel specialized in preventing, identifying and responding to false information. Social concern about disinformation in Europe is closely linked to the negative view of media practices, as other recent studies point out (Rodríguez-Pérez et al. 2024).
Most public television stations in the CIRCOM network admit that they do not have sufficient resources to manage disinformation at the regional level and state their concern about the capacity of false information to overpower the political and media agenda. For this reason, they consider it essential to strengthen the connection between these media and their audiences (Rodríguez-Martelo et al. 2023).
Network-dependent broadcasters already have numerous examples of television programs and training videos aimed at combating fake news, which cover different audience segments. There are those aimed at the youngest, such as “Escape News” (France Télévisions) and “RTP Ensina” (RTP), and there is content for teenagers, such as “WTFake” (France Télévisions), “Fake or Real” (RAI), or “Vrai ou Faux” (French-speaking Belgian RTBF). Of course, there are also programs for adults, like “La fabrique du mensonge”, also from France Télévisions. There are also examples of broadcasters that have their own fake news sections on their websites, such as the Norwegian NRK with “Faktasjekk”, the Belgian RTBF with “Faky”, and the BBC with “BBC Verify”. In Spain, apart from the state-owned RTVE with “Verifica”, the regional EITB developed “inFORMAZIOA” (Seijas-Vidal and Rúas-Araújo 2024; Rúas-Araújo et al. 2023).
2. Materials and Methods This study seeks to examine the perspective from which regional television channels address disinformation, focusing specifically on how it has evolved over the years 2023 and 2024. According to the European Audiovisual Observatory (2024), local and regional channels provide 42% of television services in Europe. Their role in combating fake news is vitally important within the realm of their respective territories (Rodríguez-Martelo et al. 2023), influencing multiple aspects of society such as culture, politics, linguistic identity and the economy (Garitaonandia 1997). In short, they are local media outlets closely linked to their audiences (Túñez López et al. 2021). For this reason, the scope of this research is based on the corporations associated with the CIRCOM network, the largest association of regional public television stations in Europe. The objectives established for this research are as follows.
The general objective (GO) is to understand the evolution of the position adopted by CIRCOM’s regional television networks regarding disinformation between 2023 and 2024. Based on this objective, the following specific objectives have been established: SO1: Analyze the perceptions of European regional public television managers regarding the importance of fighting disinformation and identify the main resources used in this fight on their respective channels; compare the results obtained for the years 2023 and 2024 to identify similarities and differences between regions.
SO2: Compare the approach of Spanish regional television stations with that of other European regional television stations.
SO3: Determine the main needs and challenges of channels in the fight against disinformation.

With these objectives in mind, an exploratory study with a qualitative approach has been carried out in which face-to-face semi-structured interviews were conducted within the framework of two consecutive editions of the CIRCOM Annual Conference: the 39th edition held in San Sebastián (Spain) on 1 and 2 June 2023, and the 40th edition held in Poznań (Poland) on 23 and 24 May 2024. This event is held annually in different European cities depending on the organizing television corporation and is the most important public meeting point for the association’s professionals, bringing together executives, managers, content creators and producers from the different broadcasters (CIRCOM Regional n.d.).
As previously mentioned, the CIRCOM network is made up of 44 television stations from 29 European countries, of which 29 representatives of 21 television corporations belonging to 16 different countries were interviewed for this research. The analysis pays particular attention to Spain, as it has 12 regional broadcasters affiliated with CIRCOM, that being the highest number of any country. The territorial division model proposed by Reuters Institute for the Study of Journalism (2023) of northern, southern, eastern and western Europe was adopted. Though academic sources like the Hallin and Mancini model (2008) classify media systems according to their relationship with political systems, while others opt for the State Media Monitor platform ( https://statemediamonitor.com/ ), where they are grouped into three zones (north–west, south and east), the classification used in the different annual reports of the Reuters Digital News Report was considered most appropriate for this study. It establishes four zonal areas (north, south, east and west) and therefore can more accurately reflect the differences observed in the context of this study. While this approach carries inherent limitations, such as selection bias, uneven regional representation and reliance on a single informant in some broadcasters, the resulting sample covers almost two-thirds of the regional broadcasters and more than half of the countries that make up the CIRCOM network.

Semi-structured interviews allowed for greater flexibility in adapting to each professional while maintaining sufficient homogeneity to achieve interpretations consistent with the research objectives.
Regarding the questionnaire, a mixture of open and closed questions was used, the latter following a Likert scale of 1 to 10. The combination of different types of questions made it possible to situate the context of each radio and television station while also obtaining numerical data.
For the open-ended questions, recurring patterns were identified across responses, and the most representative answers are reported.
The closed-ended questions were recoded into five categories in order to facilitate interpretation (Table 3): Unimportant (1–2), Of low importance (3–4), Of moderate importance (5–6), Quite important (7–8), and Very important (9–10). In the figures, the results are presented as percentages of responses within each category. However, in some cases, the data were also analyzed at the broadcaster level using the original Likert scale to better identify changes between 2023 and 2024.
For this particular study, ethical approval was not required, as it complies with European and national guidelines (e.g., EU GDPR and Spanish national research protocols), following established standards in social science research.
3. Results The first question in the questionnaire asks each of the channels about the importance of combating disinformation. The survey results indicate that in three out of the four regions, participants maintained the same level of importance for the issue in 2024 as they did in 2023. However, a slight drop-off from one year to the next can be seen in northern European countries (Figure 1). The most notable case is that of Irish free-to-air television channel TG4, with a three-point drop, followed by Finnish public broadcaster Yle, with a 10% drop.
Yle’s apparent reduced concern from one year to the next is not cause for alarm because, according to the latest European Media Literacy Index (2023) by the Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD), the European Commission and Open Society Institute Sofia (2024), Finland leads the global ranking in terms of resilience to misinformation in the media. This is thanks to the country’s national education policies, which are applied not just to schoolchildren but also to society as a whole. This creates a context in which viewers are more resistant to the negative impact of fake news and misinformation. This means that it is less necessary for the channel to address this issue, and it leads to greater media freedom and public trust in media outlets.
Despite this slight decline, it is clear that there is a continuing interest in recognizing and eradicating disinformation and that it remains a high priority for all the European regional media outlet representatives surveyed. This is also evidenced by the data obtained from the regional media interviewed in Spain (Figure 2), who uphold the fight against disinformation as one of their flagships. Furthermore, answers given by the Basque television station EITB show them to be more invested in the fight against disinformation than before, scoring three points more in 2024 than in 2023. This growth equals that of the Galician regional television station (CRTVG), as well as that of La Mancha (CMM) and the Canary Islands (RTVC).
Regarding the need to incorporate new tools to combat disinformation (Figure 3) and the need to incorporate new workers and profiles in these actions (Figure 4), the channels interviewed show disparate understandings regarding both requirements among different countries. Firstly, regarding the introduction of new mechanisms and techniques against disinformation, a very significant decrease is recorded in 2024 by regional channels RTP (dropping four points on the Likert scale compared to the previous year), followed by Yle (three points), and RTBF and RTVSLO (two points). Only RAI and RTVSLO express significant interest in incorporating these resources year on year, with increases of four and three points, respectively, on the scale.
Secondly, the data obtained in regard to the incorporation of new employees and profiles shows another downward trend, led by NRK and RTP (with a drop of five points each), followed by YLE (down three points), RTBF (down two points), and RAI and RTVSLO (down one point). On the other hand, channels HR and TVP increased by four and one point, respectively.
Significantly, the Spanish regional channels belonging to CIRCOM showed particular interest and concern in both the addition of tools (Figure 5) and in the integration of new professionals with specific knowledge on disinformation (Figure 6). Regarding the former, EITB and CMM seem to be particularly more aware of this need (with five more points each), as is CRTVG (four more points). In terms of the latter question, there is a notable increase in interest from EITB, who scored three points more in 2024 than in 2023.
Furthermore, over the last year, 19% of respondents stated that their outlet had a department to combat disinformation. On average, this team would typically consist of 5–6 people. The remaining 81% of respondents do not work at media outlets that have a department for this purpose.
As was the case in previous studies conducted by Rodríguez-Martelo et al. (2023), the responses of the representatives of the channels interviewed in this study do not differ from one year to another. This seems to confirm that there is a lack of economic resources available to dedicate to innovation and digitalization and that there is still a strong link to an older target audience. As such, TV channels opt for various resources to address the impositions that SVoD platforms have had on traditional television programming.
It also appears that patterns identified in previous research (Rúas-Araújo et al. 2023) remain in place, specifically, those in relation to the presence/absence of specific departments as well as to the need for human and technological resources to combat disinformation. Only four of the 21 networks interviewed have a department dedicated to fact-checking (BBC, France 3, NRK and RTBF). For the most part, these departments consist of groups of fewer than 10 people. The BBC is the exception, which has a department of 30 people. Again, this fact is not related to the importance given to neutralizing this phenomenon but rather to the economic capacity to establish human and material resources for this purpose on a continuous basis. To this, it is necessary to add one of the statements present in all the interviews: the ultimate goal of the journalist is to verify all the facts presented to them.
Regarding the work process carried out to combat disinformation, respondents were asked whether they rely on human resources, digital resources or a combination of both (Table 4). The results show that no channel uses digital resources exclusively without oversight. Indeed, 77% rely on a combination of human resources and digital techniques, and only 23% use solely human resources. This data demonstrates the need to continue to invest in both journalistic professionals dedicated to verifying and cross-checking news and in automated resources and tools, such as artificial intelligence, for fact-checking.

4. Discussion and Conclusions Over the last two years (2023/2024), the European regional public television stations that make up the CIRCOM network have actively sought to combat disinformation. It continues to be, in general terms, an issue of growing importance and interest. As it specifically relates to the general purpose and objective (OG) of this research, 80% of the outlets have not only demonstrated sustained interest but have also evolved their approach over time.
However, there is also evidence of the early stages of unevenness and even some decline in commitment, especially among the broadcasters corresponding to the countries of northern Europe when compared to the other regions (SO1). In some cases, it even seems that an inverse relationship is developing: as more measures are implemented from the social base, and more educational and media literacy resources are provided, the need for and pressure on regional public television stations decreases. This demonstrates their capacity for resilience and adaptation to a changing environment, something probably conditioned by a favorable political context. However, this has also influenced other regions and countries in a negative sense, in terms of the availability of public policies, means, resources and tools. Therefore, the close relationship between the realms of media and politics (Hallin and Mancini 2008) cannot be ignored when dealing with the problem of disinformation among different countries and areas of Europe. This context and relationship between political systems and the media seem to reinforce the fact that northern European countries offer greater resistance and resilience to disinformation than southern European countries, something that has already been indicated in some previous studies (Humprecht et al. 2020).
Regional channels have undoubtedly been the focus of more attention from viewers, given the recent regional, national and European elections. In Spain, the importance of combating disinformation has increased from 60% in 2023 to 80% in 2024 (OE2), though, in this case, there is a need to incorporate new tools rather than workers and professionals; that is, to focus on training and specialization while continuing to make use of existing human resources.
Lastly, in response to the open question and the objective of identifying the main needs and challenges (OE3), most of the interviewees agree on the need for media literacy training as a requirement that would facilitate the work of information professionals. There is also broad consensus on the need to be vigilant of the technological factors that shape disinformation, such as artificial intelligence (AI) tools, and that this must be done without forgetting the importance of human and professional intervention. Most are also in agreement that it is essential to maintain contact with citizens and audiences and to focus on the origins, intentionality and processes of disinformation, as opposed to just the consequences.
In this regard, it is necessary to reaffirm the role and public service vocation of the television stations analyzed here. The need to guarantee truthful and quality information, in line with the different European and national provisions, also cannot be overlooked. This includes mediating, moderating and, ultimately, attending (as much as is possible) to the debates on their social networks to guarantee the fundamental rights of users. This is especially vital when there is a call for electoral processes. The use of derogatory language and expressions on the networks they manage must also be avoided, as well as the introduction of AI technologies, bots and algorithms with a marked ideological bias, as these attack the groups and audiences most vulnerable to disinformation.
Considering the findings, it is advisable for CIRCOM Regional to position itself as a benchmark for reliable and accurate information and assume a leadership role in advancing initiatives that strengthen broadcasters’ fact-checking capacities and foster the development of dedicated spaces for verifying and debunking disinformation. Future research could explore the tools employed in content verification (such as artificial intelligence) and address how these tools address potential biases or limitations. Moreover, it would be worthwhile to evaluate the real impact of media literacy strategies in order to determine their effectiveness.

Radio/Television Outlet Actions to Be Developed
