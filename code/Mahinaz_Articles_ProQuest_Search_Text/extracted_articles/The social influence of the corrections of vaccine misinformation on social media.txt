1 Introduction
The COVID-19 pandemic has significantly highlighted the critical challenge of vaccine hesitancy, a phenomenon that poses a significant threat to global public health. Even before the current crisis, the World Health Organization (WHO) had identified vaccine hesitancy as one of the ten major threats to global health in 2019 [ 43 ]. The challenge of vaccine hesitancy has been significantly exacerbated by the concurrent proliferation of misinformation, particularly through social media platforms. Recognizing the severity of this issue, the WHO has coined the term “infodemic” to describe the overwhelming flood of information, both accurate and false, that characterizes the current information ecosystem[ 44 ].
While information overload in an infodemic can be attributed to both true an false news, research has demonstrated that false news spreads more rapidly and reaches more people than truth on social media platforms [ 37 ]. Recent research has also shown that the links to negatively toned external news articles are 1.91 more likely to be shared on social media [ 41 ]. During the early stages of the COVID-19 pandemic in the UK, a substantial portion of the population consumed pandemic-related news from social media. According to Ofcom [ 28 ], in the first week of March 2020, 49 % of respondents in a news consumption survey reported using social media as their main source of news related to the coronavirus outbreak. Additionally, nearly half of the online population (46 %) was reported to be exposed to false information about COVID-19 during this period in Ofcom survey.
Recent empirical evidence has begun to quantify the detrimental effects of misinformation on vaccine acceptance. A randomised controlled trial by Loomba et al. (2021) has demonstrated, using a sample of 8000 participants from UK and US, a clear causal link between exposure to vaccine-related misinformation and a significant decline in vaccine intentions. Past research has also demonstrated the effectiveness of various correction strategies in reducing health-related misperceptions. A comprehensive meta-analysis by Walter et al. [ 40 ] discuss various correction interventions for health misinformation on social media i.e., interventions which first participants to misinformation and later correct it. Their meta-analysis revealed that interventions to correct health misinformation on social media are generally effective in reducing health-related misperceptions. Additionally, debunking efforts conducted by experts proved more influential than those carried out by non-experts, highlighting the importance of perceived authority in information correction. Furthermore, It has been also noted that while debunking is effective in addressing misinformation among individuals with moderate beliefs [ 18 ], the backfire effect—where corrections reinforce existing beliefs—can occur [ 27 ].
Given the potential for backfire effects when debunking misinformation, researchers have increasingly explored alternative approaches. One promising strategy to foster resistance to false information is “pre-bunking” or inoculation, which draws on the metaphor of medical vaccines. This approach borrows the principle of cognitive immunization, where weakened doses of persuasive arguments are presented to individuals along with refutations, strengthening their resistance against stronger persuasive attempts they might encounter later [ 25 ]. As Roozenbeek and van der Linden [ 30 ] further explains, this process works by pre-emptively exposing individuals to small amounts of misinformation and simultaneously providing them with specific explanations about the techniques being used to manipulate them. By exposing people to milder forms of misinformation and providing them with the cognitive tools to recognize and counter such techniques, prebunking helps develop mental antibodies that protect against more potent misinformation challenges. This psychological inoculation creates cognitive resistance that is not specific to a single example of misinformation but generalizes to similar persuasive attempts using the same underlying deceptive techniques, thus providing broad protection against a range of misinformation strategies. Specifically, in context of Covid-19, studies have demonstrated the efficacy of a pre-emptive approach in improving individuals' ability to detect Covid-19 misinformation and reducing susceptibility to subsequent misleading content [ 2 ]. Using a pretest-posttest experiment design, Bode and Vraga [ 8 ] explored both pre-emptive and responsive strategies for addressing COVID-19 misinformation on social media using a WHO graphic and found somewhat similar effect of both approaches. They conducted an experiment testing the effectiveness of World Health Organization (WHO) infographics in combating COVID-19 misinformation on social media. They recruited 1596, randomly assigning them to six conditions: a control, a misinformation-only condition, and four correction conditions varying the source (WHO vs. user) and placement (pre-emptive vs. responsive) of a WHO graphic debunking myths about hot baths preventing COVID-19. Participants viewed a simulated Facebook feed with the experimental stimuli and answered questions about their beliefs. A follow-up survey was conducted one week later with 1122 participants. Results showed that exposure to the WHO graphic in any form reduced immediate misperceptions about the science behind why hot baths do not prevent COVID-19, with this reduction persisting for at least one week in three of the four correction conditions. Effects were consistent whether shared by WHO or another user, with limited differences between pre-emptive and responsive corrections. There was some evidence that a responsive correction from WHO may be more effective in reducing prevention efficacy misperceptions after one week.
Vivion et al. [ 36 ] showed that prebunking messages based on inoculation theory can effectively protect individuals against COVID-19 vaccine misinformation. They evaluated responses to two types of disinformation: one claiming mRNA vaccines alter DNA, and another suggesting COVID-19 vaccine were approved too quickly without proper safety checks. Participants were randomly assigned to three groups: a disinformation-only group, an inoculation group (who received a prebunking message before seeing disinformation), and a control group. Key findings showed that prebunking messages had a protective effect on vaccination intentions. While exposure to disinformation alone significantly decreased vaccination intention (by 3.9 %), those who received prebunking messages first showed only a marginal decrease (1.23 %). The inoculation message addressing mRNA misconceptions proved more effective than the one addressing quick approval concerns. Wong [ 42 ] conducted an experiment with 120 college students to test the effectiveness of inoculation messages in protecting positive attitudes towards HPV vaccination from anti-vaccination messages. Participants were randomly assigned to either an inoculation condition or a control condition before being exposed to anti-vaccination messages. The results showed that participants who received inoculation messages before encountering anti-vaccine content maintained more positive attitudes towards HPV vaccination compared to the control group. The study found that inoculation messages effectively created resistance to persuasion from anti-vaccination messages, particularly when the messages addressed safety concerns.
Some interventions have combined multiple strategies. In the Netherlands, a randomised controlled trial conducted by Yousuf et al. [ 45 ] during a COVID-19 vaccination campaign used videos containing information about the vaccine, influential figures from the community encouraging its uptake, and corrections of misleading information. This multi-faceted approach contributed to promoting the rejection of misconceptions about the vaccine and enhancing trust in the government.
Social media-based counselling has also shown promise. In Nigeria, Talabi et al. [ 33 ] conducted an experiment where participants were first exposed to misinformation about vaccines through WhatsApp conversations and then guided through social media platforms to correct this misinformation. The participants who received counselling showed significantly positive perception of Covid-19 misinformation than who were not exposed to counselling.
While wider misinformation research has extensively explored prebunking and debunking strategies to correct misinformation [ 13 , 31 ], correction of health misinformation on social media has developed a unique focus on “social corrections” [ 7 ] over the last decade. The research on social corrections, where users on social media actively correct health misinformation, essentially capture the natural interactive nature of social media platforms where corrections are performed by social media users compared to algorithm, factcheckers and health agencies. Bode and Vraga [ 9 ] surveyed 1043 U.S. social media users during the early stages of the COVID-19 pandemic to assess attitudes towards peer correction of misinformation. The majority valued corrections, with 56.9 % liking when people correct others and 67.5 % believing it is everyone's responsibility to address misinformation. Exposure to corrections was linked to reduced concerns about negative consequences, suggesting an emerging norm supporting correction despite awareness of risks. More recent evidence suggest that social media users both actively engage in and frequently encounter corrections of misinformation on these platforms. Tang et al. [ 34 ] conducted a cross-national survey examining self-reported experiences with observing and performing corrections of misinformation on social media in the United States, United Kingdom, Canada, and France. They surveyed over 1500 participants in each country in early 2021, asking about their experiences seeing misinformation and corrections on social media in the past month. The study compared the frequency of these experiences across countries and examined demographic and contextual factors associated with reporting observed and performed corrections. Key findings showed that self-reported observed corrections were relatively common, with 41.5–56.3 % of those who saw misinformation also reporting seeing a correction. Performed corrections were less common, with about a quarter of participants reporting correcting misinformation. There were some differences by country, with higher rates of both observed and performed corrections reported in the United States compared to the other countries. Across countries, younger adults, those with higher education, and those who reported seeing more misinformation were more likely to report both observing and performing corrections.
Past research has supported the effectiveness of social corrections in reducing health misperceptions. Bode and Vraga [ 7 ] explored the effectiveness of both user-generated and algorithmic corrections in the context of global health misinformation. Their study, involving 1872 participants in a 2 × 3 between-subjects experiment, examined the impact of user corrections and algorithmic fact-checking on misperceptions about the Zika virus. They found that both user-generated corrections and algorithmic fact-checking significantly reduced misperceptions, and this effect was consistent across different social media platforms (Facebook and Twitter). However, while this study demonstrated the potential of both social and algorithmic corrections in addressing health misinformation, it did not explicitly investigate the social influence aspects of these corrections. Both types of corrections contain implicit social cues - user-generated corrections come directly from other users, while algorithmic corrections reference posts shared by other users that debunk misinformation. The study didn't explore how these social cues might affect perceptions of social norms or consensus, nor did it explore how such perceptions might influence the effectiveness of the corrections. The role of source credibility in corrections has also been a focus of investigation. Vraga and Bode [ 38 ] specifically examined the effectiveness of expert sources in correcting health misinformation on social media. Their study used a 2 × 2 between-subjects experiment with 767 participants, comparing the effectiveness of expert (CDC) and non-expert (another user) corrections of Zika virus misinformation on Twitter. They found that expert corrections from the CDC were more effective in reducing misperceptions compared to corrections from non-expert users. This effect was especially pronounced for those with low existing attitudes towards the CDC. Importantly, both expert and non-expert corrections were more effective than no correction. These findings highlight the importance of leveraging authoritative voices in correction efforts.
Moreover, recent research has extensively explored the efficacy of social correction in mitigating health related misperceptions. A recent study by Bautista et al. [ 3 ] has provided important insights into the effectiveness of different social correction methods on vaccine sceptics' intentions to take the COVID-19 vaccine. Examining six social correction methods that varied in terms of the corrector's expertise and the correction strategy, they found that exposure to priming-based corrections, regardless of the corrector's expertise, was positively associated with intention to take the COVID-19 vaccine if the information was perceived as trustworthy. The study also found that perceived trustworthiness of the information was more relevant than the explicit expertise of the corrector, indicating that the content and perceived reliability of the correction may be more important than the credentials of the person providing it.
Despite the effectiveness of such corrections, Ruggeri et al. (2024) has shown that vaccine-related misinformation on social media propagates more swiftly than efforts to counteract it. Therefore, there is a need for improved understanding of how corrections to vaccine misinformation influences behaviour in online environments. This calls for further reflection on misinformation corrections in online social environments, particularly on corrections which occur naturally like social corrections, to develop more effective public health interventions.
While social corrections effectively reduce belief in misinformation, their broader social influence remains underexplored. Evidence suggests that social endorsement or rejection of content extends beyond factual accuracy to shape perceived social consensus. In the context of social media, perceived consensus refers to individuals' estimates of what proportion of a population holds a particular belief or attitude—formally defined as “the proportion of a population who support a particular attitudinal position” [ 17 ]. For climate change specifically, Lewandowsky et al. [ 23 ] demonstrated that comments endorsing a blog significantly influenced perceived consensus around climate change, with this perception directly mediating attitude change. More recently, Traberg et al. [ 35 ] found that social cues accompanying misinformation substantially impact belief formation, with this effect mediated by perceived social consensus.
These findings align with established theoretical frameworks. The Theory of Normative Social Influence[ 12 ] explains that individuals conform to perceived group norms primarily to maintain social approval and belonging. This mechanism becomes particularly powerful on social media platforms, where users are continuously exposed to contextual elements such as likes, comments, and sharing metrics that signal widespread agreement or interest in particular information [ 24 ]. Complementarily, Social Impact Theory [ 21 ] support that witnessing others correct misinformation may serve a dual function: it signals both informational validity and establishes social expectations regarding acceptable beliefs within a community. This aligns with research showing that explicit endorsement cues (comments suggesting belief) significantly increased misinformation susceptibility by altering perceived consensus, while mere engagement metrics had less consistent effects [ 35 ]. Therefore, its is important to explore how witnessing user-generated correction, can influence social perceptions regarding vaccination as a behaviour during a pandemic. This exploration is important for two reasons, first social perceptions such as perceived social norms have long been discussed as major determinants of behaviour [ 1 , 4 , 11 , 14–16 ]. So, this exploration can hugely enhance our understanding of the social influence of misinformation corrections online beyond the informational influence of providing accurate information to counter misinformation. Secondly, there has been a huge focus on positively influencing the perceived social norms by providing normative information in public health communication during the Covid-19 pandemic to increase vaccine uptake [ 26 ].
The present research expands on previous investigations into social corrections, i.e. witnessing corrections performed by other social media users and assess its impact on the perception of vaccination social norms and vaccine intentions in a pandemic scenario about a hypothetical virus ‘Covid-66’. Social norms have long been categorized in social psychology as injunctive norms (beliefs about what behaviours important others approve of) and descriptive norms (beliefs about what important others do) [ 11 , 16 ]. In experimental economics, these are referred to as empirical expectations (beliefs about others' actions) and normative expectations (beliefs about what others approve of) [ 4 ]. Recent research shows that second-order normative beliefs—perceptions of what most people believe—can significantly influence behaviour, such as energy-saving actions [ 19 ]. The assessment of social norms in the present study included both empirical expectations (beliefs of vaccination intentions of others) and normative expectations (regarding vaccination as the socially appropriate behaviour) [ 4 , 20 ]. Moreover, it included a second-order normative belief, which is the belief about others' safety beliefs. The personal vaccination intentions were elicited sequentially after assessing social norms.
We include algorithmically generated corrections used by Bode and Vraga [ 7 ] in the present study to obtain a more comprehensive comparison with social correction treatments. Additionally, we investigate the role of perceived social norms as a mediator in the relationship between vaccine misinformation correction and vaccine intentions, aiming to disentangle the direct and indirect effects of social corrections to vaccine misinformation on vaccine behaviour.
2 Research Questions & Hypothesis
2.1 Do user-generated corrections and algorithmic corrections positively affect perceptions of social norms regarding vaccination?
Hypothesis 1 (H1): We hypothesize that participants exposed to user-generated corrections will experience significant positive changes in their perceptions of social norms compared to those exposed to no corrections. The perceived social norms is formed by three variables i.e. empirical expectations, normative expectations and second order normative beliefs about vaccine safety. So, the present study hypothesized a positive shift in all three variables after exposure to social corrections.

H1a User corrections will lead to a positive shift in perception of vaccination as a socially appropriate behaviour in the given pandemic scenario i.e. normative expectations, but algorithmic corrections will not show this change.
H1b User corrections will lead to a positive shift in perception of other's beliefs about safety of vaccines i.e. second order normative belief but algorithmic corrections will not show this change.
H1c User corrections will lead to a positive shift in empirical expectations of other's vaccine intentions, but not algorithmic corrections will not show this change
2.2 Do user-generated and algorithmic corrections influence personal vaccine intentions?
Hypothesis 2 (H2): We predict that both user-generated and algorithmic corrections will lead to positive changes in personal vaccine intentions compared to no corrections. Based on previous research suggesting that algorithmic corrections can be more effective in maintaining high levels of vaccine intentions especially when it contains credible sources, we expect that algorithmic corrections will show a significant increase in vaccine intentions from before to after the correction exposure compared to the user-generated corrections ([ 6 ]; [ 7 ]; [ 38 ])

2.3 Does the perceived social norms mediate the effect of corrections on personal vaccine intentions?
Hypothesis 3 (H3): We propose that perceived social norms will mediate the effect of social corrections on vaccine intentions.

3 Methodology
The Experiment was programmed using oTree—an open-source platform designed for behavioural research [ 10 ]. Data were collected using the online experiment platform Prolific ( www.prolific.com ). Participants were compensated for participation at a rate of £12/per hour. On average, it took participants approximately 7–8 min to complete the study, based on the median time taken reported by the prolific. The ethical approval (Ref: BSREC 11/23–24 AM01) for the present study is granted by the Biomedical Research Ethics Committee (BSREC) at the University of Warwick, UK.
3.1 Sample size
To determine the appropriate sample size for our study, we conducted a power analysis using G*Power 3.1. The analysis was based on a repeated measures ANOVA with between-subjects' factors, targeting a small effect size (f = 0.15), an alpha error probability of 0.05, and a power of 0.95. The analysis indicated that a sample size of 621 participants was required. To account for an estimated 15 %–20 % attrition rate, we adjusted this number to 720 participants.
3.2 Experimental Procedure
A total of 720 participants, who were residents of the UK, were recruited for an online experiment. Eligibility criteria required participants to be UK residents and at least 18 years of age. Informed consent was obtained from all participants before the commencement of the experiment, ensuring voluntary participation and confidentiality of responses. The study uses a mixed online survey experiment design, incorporating both between-group and within-group comparisons of normative beliefs and vaccine intentions in a given pandemic scenario of a hypothetical virus’COVID-66′. This design allowed us to capture the evolving nature of beliefs and intentions during a pandemic in the presence of vaccine-related misinformation and its correction. See Fig. 1 for a full overview of experimental flow and tasks.
Before enrolment, the Prolific participant pool was presented with a study entitled “Vaccination in a Pandemic,” accompanied by the description: “In this study, we will ask participants to respond to survey questions related to hypothetical pandemic scenarios and social media posts related to vaccines.” The participants were randomly divided into three groups ‘social correction’, ‘algorithmic correction’ and the ‘no correction’ (control) group. Each participant was then shown an information sheet detailing the experiment's objectives, the specific tasks involved, ethical considerations, the overseeing research ethics committee, and protocols for data storage. The information sheet was tailored to each condition and following this briefing, participants provided informed consent to proceed.
Those who consented were directed to attention checks designed to ensure careful participation, while those who did not consent were prompted to exit the study. Participants who passed at least one of the two attention checks were then guided through the remaining experimental procedure. Our procedure follows Prolific's official Attention Check Policy [ 29 ]. According to this policy, “if your study is 5 minutes or longer, then participants must fail at least two checks to be rejected, any shorter studies can use a single failed check to reject” [ 29 ]. Since our study duration exceeded 5 min, we implemented this two-failure threshold before exclusion. Moreover, the participants in both groups were informed that they would receive task-specific instructions before each task.
Each experimental group contained the same social media posts, presented as shared by anonymous user on Facebook and twitter, containing false claims related to Covid-66 vaccines but differed in terms of how the misinformation was corrected. The use of a hypothetical virus scenario allowed to avoid any potential post-experiment disinformation to the participants given the nature of false claims presented during the experiment and the ongoing COVID-19 pandemic. In the social correction condition, the false news about vaccine was corrected by a social media user providing a detailed rebuttal to the false claim in the comments underneath the post (see Fig. 2 ). In the algorithmic correction condition, the post containing false information was tagged with two other posts from a hypothetical factchecker and a hypothetical UK health agency (see Fig. 3 ). The UK health agency represents a national institutional authority with domain expertise in health matters, while the independent fact-checker represents journalistic authority focused on accuracy verification. This combination mirrors real-world algorithmic corrections on platforms like Facebook and Twitter, which typically tag posts with information from both types of sources [ 7 ].The tagged posts contained headlines which debunk the claims in original social media post. In the no correction condition, participants only saw misinformation containing posts identical to other two conditions, but the false claim was not corrected.
Within each experimental condition, in the first task, participants were given a pandemic scenario related to the outbreak of the hypothetical virus COVID-66. The participants were asked to read the scenario carefully and were asked questions based on the given scenarios. Firstly, their empirical expectations about the intent of other participants in the experiment to get vaccinated in the given scenario. This was followed by elicitation of the participant's perception of about other's vaccine safety beliefs. Moreover, participant's normative expectations were elicited followed by their personal vaccine intentions in the given pandemic scenario.
3.3 Experimental stimuli
The core experimental material consisted of five social media posts, each containing misinformation about a hypothetical illness named “Covid-66.” These posts were inspired by social media posts about COVID-19 which were fact-checked by a UK-based fact checker Fullfact www.fullfact.org , a registered charity in the UK. Some of the posts used in the present study were not already in the form of social media posts but rather existed as specific claims fact-checked on fullfact.org .
To present these claims in social media posts, the link of the original false claim was backtracked from the fact-checked version and a procedure of temporarily posting the link and capturing the link preview on social media platforms like Facebook was followed. This procedure has been used and recommended by Pennycook et al. (2021). This approach of developing fake news stimuli balances ecological validity with ethical considerations surrounding ongoing public health emergencies. Specifically, we utilized real headlines related to COVID-19 that had been fact-checked and widely circulated on social media during the pandemic, but recontextualized them to refer to a hypothetical virus called “COVID-66”. For example, as shown in Figs. 2 and 3 , we captured an authentic Facebook post claiming that “COVID-19 VACCINES ARE UNDER A CRIMINAL INVESTIGATION.” This post contains typical features of vaccine misinformation: urgent language (“Please share this EVERYWHERE”), claims of authority (“RECORDED CALL WITH CITY POLICE UK”), and visual formatting that mimics official announcements. By capturing this post as it naturally appeared—including the link preview, engagement options—we maintained high ecological validity while avoiding further amplification of false claims. Pennycook et al. (2021) emphasize the importance of using actual content that has spread on social media, rather than researcher-generated fabrications, to ensure stimuli reflect the features that facilitate real-world dissemination. They note that “using stimuli in a study (for the purpose of generalizing beyond the study) has to be informed by what is happening in the world” (p. 2). Our approach adheres to this principle by basing our stimuli on verified examples of COVID-19 misinformation. However, we also recognized the ethical implications of using active misinformation about an ongoing crisis. As Pennycook and Rand (2021) discuss, exposure to misinformation in studies may have unintended consequences, potentially increasing belief in false claims. To mitigate this risk while maintaining the essential characteristics of real misinformation, we adapted the content to reference a hypothetical “COVID-66” virus.
3.4 Variables
Vaccine intentions were assessed using an ordinal 0–100 % scale by posing the question, “On a scale from 0 to 100 percent, what are the chances that you will decide to get vaccinated in the given scenario?” The scale was divided into ten levels as intervals ranging from 0 to 10 % to 90–100 %. This method, also employed in a recent study Vriens et al. [ 39 ], helps to circumvent focal responses such as 0, 50, or 100 %, which respondents often default to. The current study focuses solely on the relative differences in pre-post changes between the treatment and control groups and changes in mediators; therefore, this scaling technique effectively prevents focal responses while maintaining the aim of the study.
The respondents' empirical expectations regarding others' intentions to get vaccinated in the given pandemic scenario, were assessed using similar questions from Bicchieri et al. [ 5 ]; Cialdini et al. [ 11 ]. Participants were asked the question, “On a scale from 0 to 100 percent, how many other people in today's experiment will choose to get vaccinated in this scenario?”
The normative expectations regarding vaccination were assessed using a five-point scale ranging from “very inappropriate” to “very appropriate” similar to Krupka and Weber [ 20 ]. The question asked was: “In this scenario, how would most people judge the appropriateness or inappropriateness of someone's decision of not to get vaccinated?” The reported appropriateness refers to the average perceived social appropriateness of not getting vaccinated, measured on a scale from −1 (very inappropriate) to 1 (very appropriate).
The respondents' perceptions about others' beliefs regarding vaccine safety in the given pandemic scenario, were assessed using question adapted from Jachimowicz et al. [ 19 ] and Vriens et al. [ 39 ]. The question asked was, “On a scale from 0 to 100 percent, what are the chances that most people in today's experiment believe vaccines are safe in this scenario?”
4 Results
4.1 Sample characteristics
The present study assessed the demographic and selected characteristics of 720 participants equally divided into groups exposed to three experimental conditions. The median age of the participants was 38 years, distributed across three age groups (18–30 years: 18 %, 30–49 years: 54 %, 50+ years: 28 %), with no significant differences in each age group distribution across the experimental groups ( p  = 0.5). The gender distribution was balanced (50 % female, 50 % male), and the participants represented diverse ethnic backgrounds with the majority being White (84 %), and other groups including Black (6.3 %), Asian (5.7 %), Mixed (2.7 %), and Other (1.2 %). Employment status included full-time (53 %), part-time (23 %), and non-paid work (14 %). The literacy levels ranged from no formal education to doctorate degrees. Prolific also offered pre-screening and balancing sample for ‘Covid-19 Vaccine opinions’ with approximately one-third of participants each holding views against, for, and neutral towards COVID-19 vaccines. Likewise, self-reported vaccine status by participants in the end of experiment survey was categorized into four levels of vaccination i.e. unvaccinated (10 %), first dose only (2.1 %), first and second doses (30 %), and booster shot (58 %). 4.2 Social corrections lead to a positive change in normative expectations about vaccination as a behaviour
To test the H1a that there will be larger positive changes in normative expectation in social correction condition, we conducted a two-way mixed Anova with ‘social appropriateness ratings’ of not getting vaccinated as the response variable and Time and Correction type as the independent variable. The interaction between ‘Correction’ type and ‘Time’ was statistically significant (F(2,717) = 4.64, ηp2  = 0.013), suggesting that the average pre-post changes in appropriateness rating over time differs significantly across correction condition.
First, we explored the specific within-group contrasts by performing pairwise comparisons of pre and post mean appropriateness ratings within each correction condition using paired t -tests, with p -values adjusted using the Bonferroni-Holm method for multiple comparisons. This allowed us to determine how much the perception of the social appropriateness of vaccine refusal shifted more or less towards inappropriateness/appropriateness ends at post intervention compared to pre- intervention levels after seeing the correction of different types.
In the social correction condition, the mean appropriateness of not getting vaccinated significantly shifted more towards very inappropriate from −0.24 (SE = 0.03) at pre-intervention level to −0.30 (SE = 0.03) at post-intervention (M diff  = 0.06,
SE = 0.02, t(717) = 2.22, p  = 0.02). For the Algorithmic correction condition, the mean appropriateness had a small but statistically insignificant shift towards very inappropriate from −0.20 (SE = 0.03) at pre-intervention to −0.24 (SE = 0.03) at the post intervention levels (M diff  = 0.03 SE = 0.02, p  = 0.18). In the control condition where no correction was present, mean appropriateness of not getting vaccinated showed a negative but non-significant change from −0.30 (SE = 0.03) at pre-intervention to −0.25 (SE = 0.03) at post-intervention (M diff  = −0.05, SE = 0.03, p  = 0.06.). So, when there was no correction to misinformation, the average appropriateness ratings shifted towards perceiving not vaccinating as relatively socially appropriate behaviour. These pre-post changes have been graphically represented in Fig. 4 .
Given the significant interaction between ‘time’ and ‘correction’ type, we conducted contrast analyses that compared the magnitude of within-subject changes (pre-post differences) between the experimental and control conditions. This approach allowed us to directly test whether the pre-post change in perceived social appropriateness in the social correction condition differed significantly from the corresponding change in the control condition, while applying appropriate corrections for multiple comparisons. In other words, this approach allowed us to assess how the different correction methods varied in their effectiveness over time in shifting perceptions towards viewing non-vaccination as socially inappropriate. The contrast showed that the average change in perceived social appropriateness i.e. average pre-post change significantly differed in social corrections compared to the ‘No Correction’ condition (b = −0.12, SE = 0.04, t(717) = 2.90, p  = 0.01). The Algorithmic correction showed a similar trend but did not reach statistical significance (b = −0.09, SE = 0.04, t(717) = 2.26, p  = 0.13). See Table 1 for within-group differences across all conditions.
4.3 Social corrections lead to a positive change in the perception of other's beliefs about safety of vaccines
To test the H1b, we conducted a two-way mixed Anova with perception of other's beliefs about vaccine safety as the response variable. The main effect of ‘Time’ variable was significant (F(1,717) = 7.46, p  = 0.006, ηp2 = 0.010). The interaction between ‘Correction’ type and ‘Time’ was not significant (F(2,717) = 0.03, p  = 0.967, ηp2 < 0.001).
Since the main effect of ‘Time’ was significant, we further explored within-group differences. We conducted post-hoc pairwise comparisons and tested for significant differences in means using t -test and adjusting p -values for multiple comparison using Bonferroni-holm method. These contrasts reveal that for the social correction method, the mean perception significantly increased from 64 % (SE = 1.16) at pre-intervention to 66.4 % (SE = 1.17) at post-intervention (m diff  = 2.35, t(717) = −2.92, p = 0.01). For the Algorithmic correction method, there was a positive but statistically insignificant change in means from 65.6 % (SE = 1.17) at pre-intervention to 67.1 % (SE = 1.18) at post-intervention (m diff  = 1.45, t(717) = −1.81, p = 0.13). For the control condition where there was no correction, there was also small but statistically insignificant change from 64.5 % (SE = 1.16) at pre-intervention to 65.9 % (SE = 1.18) at post-intervention (m diff  = 1.38, t(717) = −1.71, p  > 0.13). Hence, the H1b is supported i.e. larger positive differences in perception of other people's safety beliefs when misinformation is corrected by a social media user.
4.4 Social corrections did not lead to any changes in empirical expectations about other's vaccine Intentions
To test H1c, we ran a two-way mixed Anova with empirical expectations about other's intentions as the response variable, we found that the main effect of Time was significant F(1,717) = 7.46, ηp2 = 0.010. The interaction between ‘Correction Type’ and ‘Time’ was not significant, F(2,717) = 0.03, p = 0.96, ηp2 < 0.001, suggesting that the average pre to post changes in empirical expectations may not significantly vary across correction types.
Since the main effect of ‘Time’ variable was significant, we further explored within group mean differences. We ran post hoc contrasts within each correction type. For the social corrections, mean empirical expectations at pre-intervention level was 64.8 % (SE.
= 1.17) at pre-intervention and 66.0 % (SE = 1.20) at post-intervention (m diff  = 1.18, t(717) = −1.386, p = 0.23). For the Algorithmic correction method, the means were 64.9 % (SE = 1.16) at pre-intervention and 66.4 % (SE = 1.19) at post-intervention (m diff  = 1.49, t(717) = −1.768, p  = 0.23). For the ‘No Correction’ method, the means were 64.8 % (SE = 1.16) at pre-intervention and 66.2 % (SE = 1.20) at post-intervention (m diff  = 1.33, t(717) = −1.578, p = 0.23). H1c was not supported as we did not find larger positive changes in empirical expectation when participants saw false information corrected by another social media user compared to algorithmically generated correction and no correction.
4.5 Corrections mitigate the negative effect of misinformation on vaccine intentions
To test the H2, we conducted a two-way mixed ANOVA to examine the differences in changes in personal vaccine intentions over time and across the correction conditions. With an initial focus on whether different types of misinformation corrections could significantly impact vaccine intentions over time. The results revealed a significant main effect of time (F(1, 717) = 12.86, p  < 0.00, ηp2 = 0.018), indicating that overall vaccine intentions changed significantly over the observed time points. However, the interaction between correction and time was not significant (F(2, 717) = 0.71, p  = 0.48, ηp2 = 0.002), indicating that the pre-post changes in vaccine intentions did not vary significantly across the type of correction conditions.
We further explored the specific within-group contrasts by conducting post-hoc pairwise comparisons of pre and post mean vaccine intentions in each correction condition while adjusting p -value using bonferroni method. in the no correction condition, mean vaccine intentions significantly decreased from 75.6 % (SE = 1.98) at pre-intervention to 73.9 % (SE = 1.98) post-intervention (m diff  = 1.7, p  = 0.02). However, for social correction, the mean vaccine intentions showed small but statistically insignificant decrease from 77 % (SE = 1.99) at pre-intervention to 75.5 % (SE = 1.99) post-intervention (m diff  = 1.5, p  = 0.05). similarly, algorithmic corrections also showed a statistically insignificant decline, with a pre-intervention mean of 73.6 % (SE = 1.97) to 72.9 % (SE = 1.97) post intervention (m diff  = 0.7, p  = 0.26). These findings indicate that algorithmic correction and social corrections were effective in preventing a significant decline in vaccine intentions, unlike no correction conditions where vaccine intentions significantly decreased over time. These pre-post changes have been graphically represented in Fig. 6 .
4.6 Perceived social norms do not mediate the effect of misinformation corrections on personal vaccine intentions
To test H3, we conducted a mediation analysis which allows for the examination of whether a third variable, known as a mediator, explains the relationship between an independent variable and a dependent variable. Similar analysis is also performed by recent research to gain a comprehensive understanding of social context of misinformation [ 35 ].
A mediation analysis conducted with R's mediation package (using 10,000 bootstrap samples) examined whether social corrections' influence on vaccine intentions was mediated by empirical expectations about vaccination or changes in perception of others' safety beliefs. Results showed that neither mediator significantly impacted the relationship between social corrections and vaccine intentions. For empirical expectations, neither the indirect effect (b = −0.002, 95 % CI [−0.037, 0.030], p  = 0.90), direct effect (b = 0.02, 95 % CI [−0.168, 0.210], p  = 0.82), nor total effect (b = 0.02, 95 % CI [−0.172, 0.210], p  = 0.84) reached statistical significance. Similarly, for perception of others' safety beliefs, the indirect effect (b = 0.014, 95 % CI [−0.019, 0.050], p  = 0.40), direct effect (b = 0.007, 95 % CI [−0.188, 0.200], p  = 0.93), and total effect (b = 0.020, 95 % CI [−0.175, 0.210], p = 0.82) were all non-significant. Overall, neither empirical expectations about others' intentions nor changes in perception of others' safety beliefs significantly mediated the effect of social corrections on vaccine intentions.
5 Discussion
This study examined the effects of social corrections to vaccine misinformation compared to algorithmic corrections on social media on the perceptions of social norms regarding vaccination. Additionally, the effect of both types of corrections on personal vaccine intentions was assessed in the context of a hypothetical pandemic scenario. The findings of present study showed a significant effect of social corrections of vaccine misinformation on social media. The participants who saw user-generated corrections showed a significant positive shift in their perception of the social appropriateness of not vaccinating in the given pandemic scenario i.e. normative expectations of vaccination. Furthermore, the participants in the social correction condition also showed a significant positive revision in their perceptions of what most people believe about vaccine safety. Additionally, a significant decline in vaccine intentions was observed in the control group when only misinformation was presented without any correction. However, both algorithmic and social corrections showed effectiveness in preventing a decline in vaccine intentions. These results need to be interpreted under the condition that both algorithmic and social correction groups initial had a high mean vaccine intent at the pre-intervention level. Therefore, the effectiveness of correction should be interpreted as whether these corrections maintained the high intentions over time rather than increasing them.
5.1 Impact on perceptions of social norms
Our findings indicate that social corrections to vaccine misinformation can positively influence certain aspects of perceived social norms surrounding vaccination. Specifically, social corrections led to a significant positive shift in perceptions of others' beliefs about vaccine safety and in normative expectations about vaccination as a socially appropriate behaviour. This aligns with previous research highlighting the importance of social cues in shaping perceptions of public consensus [ 23 , 35 ]. The effectiveness of social corrections in shifting these norm perceptions may be attributed to their inherent social nature, providing direct signals about community standards regarding vaccination (Cialdini & Trost, 1998).
Interestingly, neither social nor algorithmic corrections significantly impacted empirical expectations about others' intentions to vaccinate. This suggests that while social corrections can influence perceptions of others' beliefs and normative expectations, they may have limited impact on expectations of actual vaccination behaviour in the community. This distinction is important, as it highlights the multifaceted nature of social norms and the need for targeted interventions to address different aspects of norm perceptions.
5.2 Effects on personal vaccine intentions
A critical finding of this study is the high baseline level of vaccine intentions across all conditions. For instance, in the social correction condition, the mean vaccine intention was 77 % at pre-interventions. This high initial level of vaccine acceptance creates a ceiling effect, limiting the potential for significant increases in intentions following corrections.
In this context, we observed that both social and algorithmic corrections appeared to mitigate the negative effect of misinformation exposure on vaccine intentions observed in the control condition. Rather than increasing intentions above the already high baseline, corrections served to maintain these high levels in the face of misinformation. This finding is particularly important given the significant decline in intentions observed in the no-correction condition (from 75.6 % to 73.9 %), highlighting the protective effect of corrections against erosion of pro-vaccine attitudes.
Algorithmic corrections from expert sources, such as health agencies, were relatively effective in maintaining vaccine intentions, consistent with prior research on the importance of expert-driven misinformation correction [ 6 , 7 , 38 ]. Interestingly, social corrections, while somewhat less effective, still prevented a decline in intentions. The credibility and perceived authority of the correction source likely played a role in this variation in effectiveness. Notably, the intensity of social endorsement (i.e., multiple users correcting misinformation) was not tested, as the study focused on a single-user correction. Despite earlier findings suggesting the limited effectiveness of single-user corrections [ 38 ], this study found that even a single correction can have a positive, though modest, impact on mitigating the impact of vaccine misinformation.
5.3 Mediation effects
Contrary to expectations, our mediation analysis did not support the hypothesis that perceived social norms mediate the effect of corrections on personal vaccine intentions. This indicated that the total effect of vaccine misinformation correction on maintaining vaccine intentions is not primarily driven by changes in social norm perceptions.
5.4 Limitations and future directions
Several limitations of this study should be noted, pointing to important avenues for future research. First, the use of a hypothetical pandemic scenario, while allowing for controlled experimental conditions, may limit the generalizability of findings to real-world pandemic situations. Future research could examine these effects in the context of ongoing or recent real-world health crises to enhance ecological validity.
Second, the study focused on immediate effects of corrections on norm perceptions and intentions. Longitudinal research is needed to understand the long-term impacts of different correction types on social norms and vaccine behaviours.
Third, and importantly, while our study examined the effects of corrections on perceived norms and intentions, it did not directly measure actual vaccination behaviour. This limitation highlights the well-known intention-behaviour gap in social psychology [ 32 ]. While intentions are often used as a proxy for behaviour in research, they don't always translate directly into actions.
However, there is evidence supporting the predictive validity of intention measures in vaccination contexts. For instance, Lehmann et al. [ 22 ] conducted a study investigating the relationship between vaccination intentions and actual behaviour among Dutch healthcare personnel during the H1N1 pandemic. They found a significant correlation between expressed intentions and subsequent real-world vaccination behaviour, with healthcare workers who indicated higher intention to get vaccinated being more likely to actually receive the vaccine when it became available.
Despite this supporting evidence, future studies could strengthen findings by incorporating behavioural measures or leveraging real-world vaccination data to directly assess the impact of corrections on actual vaccine uptake. This could involve follow-up studies tracking participants' vaccination behaviour over time or linking intention data with official vaccination records where ethically and practically feasible.
Fourth, the study primarily focused on individual-level perceptions and intentions. Further research could explore how these effects manifest at a community or population level, potentially incorporating network analysis to understand the spread of norm perceptions through social networks.
Lastly, given the high baseline levels of vaccine acceptance in our sample, future research should examine these effects in populations with more diverse initial attitudes towards vaccination. This could provide insights into how corrections might function differently in contexts where there's more room for positive change in intentions.
6 Conclusion
In conclusion, this study provides valuable insights into the complex relationship between misinformation corrections, social norms, and vaccine intentions in a pandemic context, particularly in populations with high baseline vaccine acceptance. While social and algorithmic corrections may not directly increase vaccine intentions in such populations, they play a crucial role in shaping perceptions of social norms, a key determinant of vaccine behaviour and preventing the negative impact of misinformation exposure.
The differential effects of social and algorithmic corrections on various components of social norms and their interaction with personal intentions underscore the need for sophisticated, multi-faceted approaches to public health communication. While correction of misinformation has been hugely focussing on provision of correct information during pandemics, this study shows that public health policy should also consider the social context and influence of the social media platforms. The present study shows that the social information such as social cues accompanying the factual information in the corrections can also significantly influence key social perceptions such as perceived social norms of vaccination in the community. These insights can effectively be leveraged by policy makers to not just correct misinformation but also persuading people to correct others on social media to reshape positive social norms of vaccination.
The study also underscores the value of algorithmically generated corrections on social media citing credible sources in maintaining high vaccine intentions. Although corrections may not increase vaccine intentions in populations already supportive of vaccination, they serve an important role in preventing the erosion of positive attitudes in the face of misinformation. This shifts the focus from increasing vaccine acceptance to maintaining it, emphasizing the protective value of effective corrections.
As global communities continue to grapple with challenges related to vaccine hesitancy and misinformation, understanding these intricate social and cognitive processes becomes increasingly crucial. Future research building on these findings can further refine our approaches to combating misinformation and promoting public health in an increasingly complex information landscape, ultimately contributing to more effective pandemic preparedness and response strategies.
The limitations of this study, particularly the focus on intentions rather than behaviours and the use of a hypothetical scenario, point to important directions for future research. By addressing these aspects in future studies especially intention-action gap, researchers can continue to refine our understanding of how to effectively combat misinformation and promote public health in increasingly complex information environments.
