Headnote ABSTRACT
Objective: The objective of this article is to critically analyze the regulatory strategies adopted nationally and internationally to deal with online disinformation, seeking to understand their implications in mitigating the effects of fake news in the digital sphere, such as the Brazilian Internet Civil Rights Framework, investigation of the impacts of Bill 2630/2020 (Fake News Law) on freedom of expression online
Theoretical Framework: The approach to dealing with the spread of disinformation is multifaceted, involving citizen participation, the actions of digital platforms, and guidelines from international organizations. There is a growing emphasis on regulatory strategies that seek to establish norms, whether through state means or other forms of regulation, with the aim of influencing the agents involved and mitigating the effects of disinformation. It is important to consider that the concept of regulation has roots associated mainly with the role of the State, but authors such as Poulantzas (1981) and Jessop (2015) offer a more complex view of the State, highlighting its polymorphic nature and its adaptation to the competition of ideas and projects in society. In addition, there are perspectives that expand the notion of regulation beyond the State, encompassing forms such as self-regulation (carried out by private agents) and co-regulation (a combination of public and private initiatives), as discussed by Baldwin et al. (2012).
Method: The research adopts a qualitative, exploratory and descriptive approach, anchored in the hypotheticaldeductive method. Primary and secondary sources were used for the analysis, through a detailed literature review, analysis of relevant case studies, and field survey to provide an overview of the regulation of online disinformation in both the national and global contexts.
Results and Discussion: The research results highlighted the importance of regulatory policies that balance the containment of the dissemination of fake news with the preservation of fundamental rights to expression. The comparison between national legislation and international approaches highlighted gaps in Brazilian regulation, evidencing the need for continuous and adaptive analysis in view of the evolution of the fake news scenario.
Research Implications: This study can play an important role in the dialogue on the regulation of online disinformation, highlighting the relevance of assertive policies that balance the containment of the dissemination of fake news with the guarantee of individual rights to expression. Future work recommends a more in-depth investigation of the ethical, legal, and social implications of regulatory strategies, as well as a continuous assessment of the impact of policies implemented in different cultural and social contexts.
Originality/Value: This study contributes to the debate on regulating online disinformation, highlighting the importance of policies that balance the containment of the spread of fake news with the guarantee of fundamental rights to expression. In addition, it points to the need for continuous and adaptive analysis in light of the evolution of the fake news scenario.
Keywords: Disinformation, Fake News, Internet, Regulation.

1 INTRODUCTION
The main focus of this study is to analyze the regulatory challenges associated with online disinformation, especially in relation to national legislation and international approaches to the phenomenon of Fake News. Given the increasing proliferation of disinformation in the digital space, the objective is to understand the regulatory approaches adopted globally to deal with this challenge.
In order to contextualize this topic, it 1s essential to explore the different legislation and regulatory approaches implemented globally to combat online disinformation. Thus, we seek to understand the different mechanisms employed to mitigate the impacts of disinformation in the digital sphere and analyze how these strategies manifest themselves in different social, political and cultural contexts.
Aiming to address the challenges faced by national and international laws in the fight against online disinformation, this study is justified by the need to understand how regulations targeting Fake News, such as the Fake News Bill (PL 2630/2020) in Brazil, affect freedom of expression and demonstrate effectiveness in the face of the complexity of false information disseminated on the internet. Considering global regulatory experiences as a backdrop for the analysis, we seek to investigate this delicate intersection between the need to contain the spread of disinformation and the guarantee of fundamental rights to freedom of expression.
The overall objective of this research is to critically analyze the regulatory strategies adopted nationally and internationally to combat online disinformation. More specifically, we sought to analyze how the Brazilian Internet Civil Rights Framework influences freedom of expression online and investigate the aspects and impacts of Bill 2630/2020 in the context of freedom of expression on the internet.
The methodology used comprised basic research, with a qualitative approach and exploratory nature, through a detailed bibliographic review, analysis of relevant case studies and field survey to offer an overview of the regulation of online disinformation in both the national and global context.
1.1 NOTES ON FREEDOM OF EXPRESSION ON THE INTERNET
Freedom of expression is a fundamental principle of human rights, essential for democratic societies, in the Brazilian context, expressly and prominently provided for in the text of the Federal Constitution of the Federative Republic of Brazil of 1988 (CRFB/88), through its articles 5, item IX, and 220, guaranteeing the right of individuals to express their opinions and ideas without censorship or coercion.
In this regard, Lopes (1997, p. 67) states that:
Freedom of expression is a fundamental right that has been built throughout history and, through it, various peoples have established thoughts and ideals, breaking with paradigms of power inherent to their time. The Declaration of the Rights of Man and of the Citizen, of revolutionary France in 1789, stated in its article XI that the expression of thought and opinion is free, ensuring that every man has the right to speak, write and print freely, except for the abuse of this freedom, for which he will be held accountable in cases determined by law.
In a democratic society, freedom of expression plays a crucial role in enabling public debate, promoting diversity of opinion and allowing citizens to actively participate in the political process. It is an essential pillar of government transparency and social control, allowing people to voice criticism, expose abuses of power and contribute to the formation of informed public opinion.
Freedom of expression is not limited to speeches that are in accordance with the prevailing ethical and moral standards in a specific society and historical period. It also includes speeches that are considered controversial, deviant, critical of political and economic systems, as well as minority positions that may displease certain segments of society. This right ensures the free expression of ideas, even if they contradict or upset certain groups, and is essential to promote diversity of opinions and ensure an open and democratic debate.
Supreme Court Justice Gilmar Mendes Ferreira and Paulo Gonet Branco argue that freedom of expression, as established in the Constitution, protects all opinions, convictions, comments, assessments or judgments about any subject or person. They argue that it is not appropriate to make an assessment of the importance of the content, since this freedom applies to any type of speech, from the most trivial to those of extreme importance to society, verbatim:
The guarantee of freedom of expression protects, at least as long as there is no conflict with other fundamental rights and other constitutionally established values, every opinion, conviction, comment, evaluation or judgment on any subject or on any person, involving a topic of public interest, or not, of importance and value, or not - even because "differentiating between valuable or valueless opinions is a contradiction in a State based on the conception of a free and pluralist democracy (2014, p. 403).
However, with the increasing democratization of internet access via mobile technologies and the emergence of social networks, the spread of misinformation and fake news news presents significant challenges to ensuring this freedom. Disinformation, often disseminated deliberately and with the aim of deceiving, distorts reality and compromises the formation of fact-based opinions.
A more recent example would be the impact of misinformation related to the COVID19 pandemic on freedom of expression. During the health crisis, false information and conspiracy theories about the origin of the virus, fake treatments, vaccines and public health measures were disseminated on a large scale.
This not only created confusion and distrust towards health authorities and vaccines, but also led to an environment where health professionals, scientists and experts were targets of verbal and even physical attacks by individuals who believed in conspiracy theories.
The spread of misinformation about the pandemic has not only impacted the freedom of expression of experts and authorities seeking to communicate accurate, evidence-based information, but has also created a climate of polarization and distrust that has made it difficult to communicate effectively on public health issues.
Disinformation not only undermines the quality of public discourse, but can also lead to undue restrictions on freedom of expression. Governments and online platforms may be pressured to impose control measures that, while intended to combat disinformation, may also unduly restrict legitimate freedom of expression.
2 FAKE NEWS AND MISINFORMATION
In the past, information was rigorously checked and disseminated by newspapers, radio and television. Today, anyone with internet access and a social media account can share information with millions of people. This is the remarkable contribution of the information society: the ease of exchanging data. The speed and accessibility of news dissemination allow for anonymity or the disguise of the sender's identity. This attracts more people to the networks and makes them a significant basis for forming opinions and critical thinking in globalized societies.
Despite the democratization of access, the problems of excessive use of networks to disseminate news are worrying. The Internet stands out as a highly effective means of disseminating facts, but the speed of the networks prevents verification of veracity before sharing.
The outlook is even more alarming when you consider that, according to Adelaide Carvalho Franca and Carlos Augusto Alcantara Machado, citing a survey by the Quartz agency , around 70% of Brazilians use social media as a source of information and to shape their opinions. Compared to countries like England (22%), Germany (26%) and the United States (37%), this rate in Brazil is very high and worrying (2019, p. 55-74).
With a significant number of people using social media as a source of information, excessive and irresponsible use, combined with the dissemination of false information, contributes to the spread of fake news. news and disinformation. This not only violates the right to information and freedom of expression, but also poses a threat to democratic balance.
The ability to distort news and influence points of view makes misinformation spread by fake news influences society's ability to develop a critical sense, leading to decisions based on inauthentic facts. Although it is not a recent phenomenon, fake news news gained greater reach with the internet.
Before the internet, the dissemination of news was restricted to official media outlets, committed to disseminating verified and true information, as highlighted by René Morais da Costa Braga:
Before the advent of the internet, spreading fake news could be disastrous for the reputation of a media outlet, which would have credibility issues and face legal action, even criminal charges. The world wide web, especially through social media, makes the spreading of fake news much more virulent, cheaper and difficult to track. So it doesn't matter if the news turns out to be completely implausible, even after a cursory reading (2018).
It is important to highlight semantically that the term "fake news" originally refers to the dissemination of false information about a particular event. However, in the current context of misinformation, its application has become broader, encompassing distortions, manipulations, satires and untruths disseminated virtually or not.
Disinformation, therefore, is a broader concept than "fake" "news", covering three distinct categories. The first is "disinformation", which involves the propagation of false news with the intention of defaming the honor and dignity of individuals or communities. On the other hand, when false information does not have the potential harming someone's honor is called "misinformation".
Finally, "mal-information" refers to information based on facts, but disseminated in a way that infringes or disrespects society's rights.
Despite this terminological differentiation, due to the popularization of the term, this work will use "fake news" to unify the previously mentioned meanings.
To understand the phenomenon of misinformation, especially in the context of the Covid-19 pandemic, it is crucial to discuss the reasons behind the spread of fake news. news . These motives range from economic gain through mass messaging to promoting social control through ideological manipulation.
René Morais da Costa Braga highlights that, in the United States and Brazil, fakes News is used as a way to dominate and influence the political and social context. In these cases, fake and distorted news is designed to attract the attention of certain population groups and intentionally cause misinformation. This view is shared by Ingo Sarlet and Andressa Siqueira: (...) there is a wide range of reasons that lead to the often unbridled dissemination of misinformation: financial, with the aim of gaining profits; political-electoral, aiming to elect a politician by resorting to falsifying the truth or other similar expedients, in order to prevent the election of opponents and/or promote one's own; social, with the creation of cohesive groups that disseminate this information; psychological, aiming at prestige and strengthening others (...) (2020, p. 558).
According to Hunt АПсой , the financial factor plays a crucial role in the spread of fake news. news , especially considering the profits made through clicks on misleading news. Furthermore, in line with the perspectives mentioned above, these authors from the United States emphasize that the imposition and manipulation of ideologies also play a determining role in the spread of fake news:
There appear to be two main motivations for providing fake news. The first is pecuniary: news articles that go viral on social media can draw significant advertising revenue when users click to the original site. This appears to have been the main motivation for most of the producers. (...) The second motivation is ideological. Some fake news providers seek to advance candidates they favor (2017).
Therefore, it demonstrates that because they are intentionally used to manipulate opinions, fakes News becomes harmful not only to society, but also to the entire democratic and humanistic structure on which it is based. The situation is aggravated when disinformation is used to alienate part of the population regarding subjects of which the average individual does not have a deep understanding.
Topics of extreme importance for the survival of humanity, such as science, the environment, health and the new Covid-19 pandemic, are easily subject to manipulation and distortion, which intensifies the severity of the problem of misinformation.
3 THE CIVIL RIGHTS FRAMEWORK FOR THE INTERNET AND NETWORK NEUTRALITY
3.1 INTERNET CIVIL RIGHTS FRAMEWORK
The Internet Civil Rights Framework Law (Law 12,965), composed of 32 articles, represents a significant advance in the Brazilian legal scenario. This legislation has become fundamental for the resolution of conflicts arising from the use of the internet, directly influencing the field of Digital Law, previously regulated by the Civil Code, Consumer Defense Code and other regulations due to the absence of a specific law on the subject ( Araujo , 2016).
Of the 32 articles in force, the first three stand out. Article 1 establishes the principles, guarantees, rights and duties for the use of the Internet in Brazil, outlining the guidelines for the actions of the Union, the States, the Federal District and the Municipalities in this matter.
Article 2 of Law 12.965/2014 is of utmost importance, basing the use of the internet in Brazil on respect for freedom of expression, recognizing the global scale of the network, human rights, the development of personality and the exercise of citizenship in the digital environment, in addition to promoting plurality, diversity, openness, collaboration, free initiative, free competition, consumer protection and the social purpose of the network.
Pontieri (2019) highlights when analyzing the Internet Civil Framework that its main foundation is respect for freedom of expression (art. 2), with one of its principles being the guarantee of this freedom, communication and expression of thought, as recommended by the Federal Constitution (art. 3).
Article 7 of this law lists rights and guarantees for users, including the inviolability of privacy and private life, as well as protection and compensation for material or moral damages resulting from their violation, among other guarantees. Its central purpose is to ensure the protection of users, covering their freedom of expression and other reserved rights, aiming to eliminate censorship and preserve texts, except in cases where there is legal justification for their removal.
3.2 CRITICAL ANALYSIS OF THE INTERNET CIVIL RIGHTS FRAMEWORK AND ITS PRINCIPLE OF NETWORK NEUTRALITY
According to Mendes (2023), when analyzing the Brazilian Internet Civil Rights Framework (Law 12,965/2014), it is notable that the self-regulation model for digital platforms outlined in Article 19 is outdated, allowing the dissemination of abusive and rights-violating content. To improve moderation on social media, the ideal approach would be to find a middle ground between total freedom for platforms and absolute state control over content. An effective strategy would be to establish guidelines directed not at the content itself, but at the content management processes of internet companies.
At the seminar "Freedom of expression, social networks and democracy", which was promoted by the Center for Innovation, Administration and Research of the Judiciary of FGV Conhecimento, supported by the Brazilian Institute of Education, Development and Research (IDP), where Supreme Court Justice Gilmar Mendes participated, the Minister elucidates the current Brazilian scenario of regulations on digital platforms and the renewal of this debate, in a reaction to the attacks made on institutions on January 8, 2023, which were virtually orchestrated by extremist groups on social networks.
As Mendes explained:
"The brutality of the scenes of attacks on democratic institutions was preceded by the circulation of online content produced by extremist groups in the days leading up to the acts of massacre and terrorism. There is a growing awareness that the cruel episodes experienced at the beginning of the year were orchestrated virtually, without the intermediaries participating in the dissemination of this content having adopted minimum measures to deal with the systemic risks generated by hateful publications," said the dean of the Supreme Court (2023).
In this context, there is a general agreement that the role played by digital platforms in the construction of public discourse demands a democratic regulatory policy, in order to improve their responsibility in moderating harmful content.
The minister explains that the concept of protecting online content neutrality ( content neutrality ), associated with the tradition of negative rights of freedom of expression:
This first paradigm, which is traditionally adopted, is structured based on legal regimes of weak liability of intermediaries for third-party content. In addition to state laws, moderation of online content is mostly carried out by self-regulation mechanisms of the platforms themselves. In general terms, article 19 of the Brazilian Internet Bill of Rights corresponds to this paradigm of content neutrality (2023).
Gilmar argues that the role of providers is not neutral and impacts communication on the internet. Social networks are often tasked with mediating conflicts between fundamental rights, and also play a normative role by establishing rules for publications and defining what is prohibited.
However, these specific rules are considered inadequate to fully ensure users' rights related to freedom of expression, as noted by the minister. Given this scenario, he expresses doubts about the sufficiency of Brazilian legislation, which focuses on the self-regulation model adopted by platforms.
Article 19 of the Internet Civil Rights Framework stipulates that the internet provider "may only be held civilly liable for damages resulting from content generated by third parties if, after a specific court order, it fails to take steps to make the content identified as infringing unavailable."
Gilmar Mendes argues that the device, currently being questioned in the STF, played an important role in building a plural and open internet in the country, but is now outdated.
To corroborate this diagnosis, several foreign initiatives have created more sophisticated liability regimes for the operation of digital platforms. It is important to find a middle ground between a regime of total freedom for platforms and a regime of complete state control over content. This has been the attempt of foreign democratic jurisdictions so far (2023).
As this becomes clear, although the Internet Civil Rights Framework has been crucial to preserving freedom of expression online in Brazil, it is now clear that it needs to undergo revisions to adapt to our current needs of controlling disinformation.
4 ANALYSIS OF BILL NO. 2,630 - " FAKE NEWS LAW "
The proponents of Bill 2630/2020 argue that the urgency of new legislation is based on combating the growing spread of misinformation during the Covid-19 pandemic. After years in progress, the bill reveals ambivalences, such as the attempt to regulate specific uses of the internet, limiting commercial activities of digital platforms, including small and medium-sized companies, and establishing controversial rules to control user behavior, boost political propaganda and even criminalize it (CONJUR, 2021, p.1).
On this topic, Barros and Oliveira emphasize that harmonization between the two parameters is closely related to the legal responsibility attributed to intermediaries, such as social networks. They mention a scale that ranges from the complete absence of responsibility for the content published, in which there is no motivation for any interference, with any exclusion of content being determined by official bodies (model adopted in the United States); the establishment of conditional immunity, in which the moderator must follow pre-defined procedures, notifying the interested party and removing the content (adopted by the European Union, Singapore, Ghana, Uganda and South Africa), or notification of a demand from third parties to remove the content (Canadian model); or, even, full responsibility, in which intermediaries are responsible for all content on their platforms, allowing the monitoring and immediate removal of content with potential liability (Thai model) ( Barros; Oliveira, 2021, p. 1).
No fundamental right in Brazil is absolute, which makes regulation feasible to meet social demand against disinformation. However, Polido emphasizes that, on the other hand, the project has recovered authoritarian normative policies, resembling regulatory schemes of past laws, such as the Press Law (Law No. 5,250/1967) and the Legal Framework for Advertising Activity (Law No. 4,680/1965), which were not completely absorbed by the Constitution, according to a decision by the STF (2021).
Although the project may, in some aspects, affect individual freedoms, such as freedom of expression, its regulation is crucial, given the consequences already experienced throughout the country, which is on constant alert in the face of disseminated information.
Barros and Oliveira emphasize the pressing need to establish regulations that meet the demands of social dignity in the face of the phenomenon of fake news:
It is absolutely essential to develop and apply transparent and objective criteria for algorithmic news removal, in order to guarantee, in addition to equal and impersonal treatment for all individuals/ideologies, prior knowledge and potential control of the reasons and circumstances that give rise to the removal of content. It is also essential that civil society is involved in combating and promoting news capable of refuting falsehoods, a strategy that has proven to be effective, as can be seen from the research carried out by the Getúlio Vargas Foundation regarding the untruths spread online regarding the death of councilwoman Marielle Franco and the reaction aimed at deconstructing it, which reached a much greater scale and scope after a concise and effective popular mobilization (2021).
The proposal for Bill 2630/20 of the Chamber of Deputies aims to establish the Brazilian Law of Freedom, Responsibility and Transparency on the Internet. Its main objective is to combat the dissemination of false content on platforms such as Facebook, Twitter, WhatsApp and Telegram , applying to platforms with more than 2 million users, including foreign platforms serving the Brazilian public.
Bill 2630 seeks to establish measures to contain the spread of fake content, requiring legal representation in Brazil for companies on these platforms and regulating conduct to promote a democratic virtual environment. This has become urgent in light of the proliferation of fake content. news in the 2018 elections and during the pandemic, persisting in the 2022 elections.
In the National Congress, the bill proposes rules for social media providers with more than 2 million users, including foreign companies that offer professional services to the Brazilian public. In addition, it suggests a self-regulation system overseen by the Internet Steering Committee (CGI.br), seen as more effective than the creation of an Internet Transparency and Accountability Council (CONJUR, 2021).
Bill 2630 addresses three fundamental aspects: content moderation on social media, transparency rules for platforms, and the creation of mechanisms to investigate encrypted messages. It prioritizes the prompt removal of false information, demands greater accountability from platforms, and proposes investigations without violating the mass privacy of users.

Application providers must provide an accessible and prominent mechanism, available for at least three months after the decision, so that the user who created or shared the content, as well as the user who made any complaint, can appeal the decision. §1° The user must be given the option of submitting additional information to be considered at the time of the review. §2° If the review is considered admissible by the application provider, the latter must act to reverse the effects of the original decision.
In addition, there is a section intended to establish penalties for those who deliberately disseminate or propagate information in bad faith, in accordance with Article 28. These penalties, without prejudice to other civil, criminal or administrative sanctions, subject application providers to punishment by the Judiciary, guaranteeing due process, full defense and adversarial proceedings.
Possible penalties include fines, suspensions and even a ban on carrying out activities in the country. Of course, all punishments must be proportionate and reasonable, taking into account the severity of the offense, motives, individual and collective consequences, recidivism and the economic capacity of the offender:
Art. 28. Without prejudice to other civil, criminal or administrative sanctions, application providers are subject to the following penalties to be applied by the Judiciary, ensuring due legal process, full defense and adversarial proceedings:
I - warning , indicating a deadline for adopting corrective measures;
II - fine ;
IIT - temporary suspension of activities;
IV - prohibition of carrying out activities in the country.
§1° To establish and grade the sanction, the following must be observed:
I - the seriousness of the fact, based on consideration of the reasons for the infraction and the consequences at the individual and collective levels;
II - repeat offences under this Law;
Ш - the economic capacity of the offender, in the case of application of the sanction provided for in item II of the caput.
§2° For the purposes of §1°, the imposition of sanctions contained in items Ш and IV of the caput is subject to the prior application of those set out in items 1 and II in the twelve months prior to the commission of the infraction."
It is clear that these penalties are applied in a balanced manner, and may be subject to adjustments over time, since the inclusion of this law in the National Legal System represents a significant advance in the socio-political sphere. This is because it puts into practice the idea that freedom of expression cannot be used uncontrollably as a result of freedom of information.
In summary, this project aims to regulate the right to information and freedom of expression, in line with the democratic principles in force in the country. There is no violation of rights, just necessary regulation, since a Democratic State of Law without laws to guide the exercise of freedoms can result in a fragmented environment, with arbitrary and discrepant political provisions.
5 INTERNATIONAL REGULATORY APPROACHES TO DISINFORMATION AND FAKE NEWS
The approach to dealing with the spread of disinformation is multifaceted, involving citizen participation, the actions of digital platforms and guidance from international organizations. A growing emphasis is placed on regulatory strategies that seek to establish norms, whether through state means or other forms of regulation, with the aim of influencing the agents involved and mitigating the effects of disinformation.
It is important to consider that the concept of regulation has roots associated mainly with the role of the State, but authors such as Poulantzas (1981) and Jessop (2015) offer a more complex view of the State, highlighting its polymorphic nature and its adaptation to the competition of ideas and projects in society.
Furthermore, there are perspectives that expand the notion of regulation beyond the State, encompassing forms such as self-regulation (carried out by private agents) and coregulation (a combination of public and private initiatives), as discussed by Baldwin et al. (2012)
Authors such as Black (2001) propose a decentralized theory of regulation, which not only highlights different ways of coordinating social processes, but also reduces the importance of the State and power relations in this regulatory context.
In examining regulatory strategies to combat disinformation, the focus will be on the responses of public administrations, especially state strategies, given the influence of digital platforms. These have required responses from governments and public institutions, such as parliaments and electoral courts, due to the significant impact they have on the contemporary information landscape.
5.1 SELF-REGULATION APPROACH
The self-regulation approach to controlling the spread of misinformation has been used by several countries to address this growing challenge. In the United States, for example, initiatives such as the "Honest Ads Bill" in the Senate aim to regulate the transparency of ads on digital platforms, as a way to mitigate the spread of misleading information.
Canada has also taken a similar approach by investing in media literacy programs, seeking to empower citizens to identify and deal with false or misleading content ( Aielo , 2019). In addition, Nigeria launched an educational campaign to raise awareness about the dangers of misinformation, partnering with platforms where this practice was common, such as Facebook ( Oyeyemi , 2018).
In Denmark, concerned about outside influences, plans have been established to deal with disinformation, including the formation of intergovernmental groups to monitor and respond to misleading content ( Danish Ministry of Foreign Affairs, 2018).
In Brazil, although there is no specific legislation aimed at self-regulation in this context, initiatives have emerged from civil society, non-governmental organizations and even some digital platforms. For example, some social networks have implemented internal policies to identify and remove false or misleading content. In addition, there are bills under discussion in the National Congress that seek to regulate the transparency of online political ads, such as the aforementioned PL 2630/20.
Although Brazil does not have a formal self-regulation strategy in place, discussions on the topic have been growing and actions in this regard are being debated in various areas, reflecting a global trend in an attempt to deal with disinformation through self-regulation on digital networks and platforms.
5.2 CO-REGULATION APPROACH
Co -regulation is a collaborative model that stands out in addressing the spread of disinformation. A notable example is the European Commission, which convened experts to create a report with key recommendations. These proposals seek to increase transparency in the production and dissemination of information, strengthen quality journalism and promote media diversity in the European Union, in addition to emphasizing the importance of media literacy ( Cock Buning et al ., 2018).
The core of this model is the proposal for a code of practice for digital platforms, based on a co-regulatory approach . This code would involve the implementation of specific guidelines by companies, including adjustments to advertising policies, clear differentiation between sponsored and non-sponsored content, transparency in the management of user content and access to data for fact-checking.
The recommendations include measures such as enabling access to fact-checking data, making verification tools available to users, promoting accurate and reliable news, and providing relevant information to independent researchers. These guidelines aim to address the spread of misinformation by establishing clear standards for digital platforms to act responsibly and transparently when handling information.
5.3 STATE REGULATION APPROACH
Several countries have adopted direct command and control strategies through legislative measures to address the spread of disinformation. In Malaysia, a law passed in 2018 criminalized the dissemination of wholly or partially false information, imposing penalties of up to six years in prison, sparking considerable controversy and debate over its repeal ( Hafidzah , 2019).
Singapore followed a similar path by enacting a law in 2019 that criminalizes the dissemination of false statements of fact, with fines and up to five years in prison. The legislation allows for corrections to messages deemed false, granting ministers the authority to intervene, and in cases where posts are posted by inauthentic accounts, penalties can be increased (Reuters, 2018).
In Europe, France and Germany have also adopted legislative initiatives. In France, a specific regulation was established to combat the spread of fake news during election periods, granting judges the power to order the removal of false content. Meanwhile, in Germany, the NetzDG law requires the removal of illegal content, although it does not directly classify disinformation, addressing issues such as defamation and data falsification.
Other countries, such as Saudi Arabia and Brazil, have implemented generic laws to address the spread of disinformation. In Saudi Arabia, the autocratic regime has threatened severe punishments, including up to five years in prison, under cybercrime legislation for the dissemination of false material (Novak, 2018). In Brazil, the Electoral Code already provided for punishments for the dissemination of knowingly false information during elections. The country recently updated its electoral legislation, creating the crime of false reporting for electoral purposes, punishable by up to eight years in prison, a measure that has generated intense debate following presidential vetoes and the subsequent reinstatement of the law by the National Congress. These state actions reflect the diversity of legislative approaches adopted globally to address disinformation.
6 CONCLUSION
The research explored the regulatory challenges associated with online disinformation, focusing on the analysis of Brazilian national legislation in contrast to international approaches to tackling Fake News. Throughout the work, the complex relationship between freedom of expression, disinformation and internet regulations was examined, especially in the context of the Fake News Law (PL 2630/2020) in Brazil, considering the impacts of this legislation in relation to international strategies.
The study aims to identify gaps in national legislation and compare these policies with international examples, aiming to understand the balance between mitigating disinformation and preserving individual rights of expression. The research explored regulatory approaches and their implications for freedom of expression as practiced in the international arena.
In this context, it was found that online disinformation represents a complex and multifaceted global challenge. The results revealed the need for assertive regulatory measures, considering not only national legislation, but also lessons learned from strategies adopted in other countries. It was observed that there is a need to update legislation with the civil framework for the internet.
Thus, it is concluded that this study contributes to the debate on the regulation of online disinformation, highlighting the importance of policies that balance the containment of the dissemination of fake news with the guarantee of fundamental rights to expression. In addition, it points to the need for continuous and adaptive analysis in view of the evolution of the fake news scenario. news .
In view of these considerations, it is recommended that future work deepen the analysis of the ethical, legal and social implications of regulatory strategies, as well as a continuous assessment of the impact of policies implemented in different cultural and social contexts.
