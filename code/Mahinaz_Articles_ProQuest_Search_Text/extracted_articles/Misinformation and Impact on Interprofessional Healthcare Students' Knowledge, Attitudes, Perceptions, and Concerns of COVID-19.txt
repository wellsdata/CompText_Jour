Headnote BACKGROUND: The COVID-19 pandemic has highlighted the importance of information sources (e.g., Internet, social media) and their role in spreading misinformation. PURPOSE: To describe the information sources and frequency of use by health professional students and to compare users of dependable and nontrustworthy news sources on stressors, stress relievers, safety, and preventative activities, worries, and attitudes toward COVID-19. METHODS: 123 students from nursing (38%), medicine (33%), and health professions (28%) completed online surveys on disaster preparedness training, knowledge of the COVID-19 virus, and safety and prevention practices. Students were mostly female (81%), white (59%), and aged 21-30 yrs (72%). RESULTS: Students who relied on credible news sources scored higher on knowledge of the COVID-19 condition and reported less stress than their counterparts. CONCLUSION: The findings emphasize the importance of students avoiding untrustworthy news sources. Informed students are less stressed and can help initiate necessary safety measures in the areas they serve. J Allied Health 2023; 52(2):97-103.
OVER TWO BILLION people use the Internet for health information. However, the quality of the information varies. Content on the Internet is unregulated, and the burden is left to the user to assess the quality of health information. During the original coronavirus 2019 (COVID-19) pandemic, government authorities and health organizations were concerned about an "infodemic," which characterizes the transmission of potentially doubtful to low-quality information.(1) Misinformation, and thus panic and anxiety, may spread due to an infodemic.(2) One of the most prominent search engines, Google, prioritizes search results based on link popularity rather than the quality of material on a page.(3) As a result, most users do not go past the first two pages of search engine results, which guide them to popular but potentially low-quality websites.(4) Dr. Vivek Murthy, the Surgeon General of the United States, published a recommendation titled "Confronting Health Misinformation" in July 2021, outlining the potential danger misinformation poses to public health and how preventing its spread is a moral and civic responsibility shared by all members of society. The report includes actionable recommendations for individuals, families, communities, educators, health professionals, journalists, academics, and even funders, recognizing that combating the effects of misinformation will require a collaborative effort from everyone.(5)
Scoring systems have been developed as a set of indicators for evaluating the quality of information on a website. The Health on the Net Foundation's Code of Conduct (HONcode), the Journal of the American Medical Association (JAMA) standards, and the DISCERN instrument® are the most often used scoring systems.(7-10) Cuan-Baltazar et al.(10) discovered that only 1.8% of the first 110 websites surveyed carried the HONcode seal. According to the JAMA benchmark, 39.1% of websites lacked any of the tool's essential categories, and only 10% of websites met the tool's four quality requirements. The DISCERN score indicated that 70% of websites received a low score, with none receiving a high rating.
Throughout the pandemic, the dissemination rate of low-quality information swiftly outpaced the availability of strong, evidence-based practice resources. In 2020, for example, Raharusun released a preprint article, which was subsequently removed from the website, reporting research on COVID-19 and vitamin D that had far-reaching implications.(10a) The story was widely shared on the Internet, was quoted in numerous other publications, and received many downloads/11 The findings were covered by major news sites worldwide, and users took to social media platforms to discuss them. However, while studying the Henrina et al.(11) group's pre-print publication, none of the authors were searchable. Additionally, there were significant quality control issues with the article itself, including a lack of scientific rigor. The author ultimately removed the paper from publication. Throughout the pandemic, information was rapidly disseminated with dubious vetting methods, making it difficult to determine if it was accurate or misleading.
Poor information quality can spread over social media platforms as a rumor, a hoax, or misinformation/12) or even be seen in scientific publications/13,14) As a result, a greater number of individuals are exposed to a high volume of information, resulting in media fatigue and the cessation, omission, or nonrecognition of healthy behaviors that would aid in preventing the spread of the disease,(15) managing disease symptoms, or appropriately educating others about the disease.(16) Gupta et al.(17) found that social media was an essential source of information for respondents to access during the pandemic (63%) and the most likely source of misinformation (67%). Nsoesie et al.(18) discovered trends in online searches across multiple countries. For instance, the investigators discovered that "coronavirus and 5G" had a rapid start, peak, and doubling time in several nations, owing to the rate of information transmission. The issue of information overload or misinformation overload looked multifaceted, stemming from the publisher or sharer of the material and reader misinterpretation, which was then shared and disseminated within a circle of influence. This literature review supports the importance of understanding how misinformation is spread to identify remedies and interventions at the local, state, and national level to limit the overall societal and health impacts in the current and future pandemics.
University students in the health field are at increased levels of stress and anxiety during this pandemic, with documented fear and worry about their health and of their loved ones, difficulty in concentrating, disruption in sleep patterns, decreased social interactions and increased concerns on academic performance and career disruption.(19) However, it is unknown whether the infodemic adds to increased stress and anxiety in this population. The purpose of this study was to: 1) describe the information sources and frequency of use by nursing, medicine, and health profession students; and 2) compare users of reliable and less credible news sources and effect on stressors, stress relievers, safety, and preventative actions, concerns, and attitudes toward the COVID-19 pandemic.
Methods
Research Design
A subset of data from a larger descriptive cross-sectional survey was examined in this study. All students from the Schools of Nursing, Medicine, and Health Professions in a Texas academic health science center (University of Texas Medical Branch, Galveston) were invited to participate in the study by email, which solicited voluntary participation in an anonymous online survey. A link to the online survey was included in the email. It could be completed at any time between October 1, 2020, and December 31, 2020, before the COVID-19 immunization became widely available. This sub-study enrolled only students who interacted with patients. The Institutional Review Board at University of Texas Medical Branch (# 20-0127) examined the study and protocol and provided a letter of exempt determination which granted the study an exemption from review status.
Survey Instruments
The survey was based on the epidemic/pandemic literature, which looked at similar scenarios in the early phases of COVID-19 before and after it was declared a pandemic.(20-22) The survey included demographic questions, disaster preparedness training, knowledge of the COVID-19 virus and pandemic, and several Likert-type scale instruments to assess a student's perception of factors that induce stress, factors that reduce stress, safety, and prevention practices like effective use of personal protective equipment, social distancing, hand hygiene, and surface cleaning.
Use of reliable information source. Students were asked to rate their sources for accurate information about COVID-19 on a 4-point scale of least used, sometimes, more often, and most utilized. Government websites (e.g., Centers for Disease Control and Prevention [CDC], National Institutes of Health [NIH]), the World Health Organization (WHO), medically based websites such as WebMD, and university websites were regarded as more reputable news sources. Mainstream news media, social media, family, and friends were less reliable. Finally, Wikipedia and search engines like Google may be less trustworthy.
Disaster preparedness training. Students were asked if they had ever participated in any formal or informal disaster preparedness training. This may include talks with faculty, educational sessions or courses within an academic program, or state-sponsored public health education. A binary variable indicates if a student attended a disaster preparedness training session.
Knowledge related to COVID-19. The following questions assess students' knowledge of the coronavirus: source of infection, incubation period, mode of transmission, prevention methods to prevent the spread, signs of infection, illness progression, and therapy. Each accurate response was worth 1 point, and the points were added together to determine the final score. The total score was scaled to a range from zero to 100.
Scaled instruments. The study utilized five scaled instruments with Likert-type responses to COVID-19related issues. The first scale covered 25 items/factors that may cause stress, such as a lack of appropriate COVID-19 treatment, fear of infection, and overcrowding in hospitals or public settings. The second scale included 15 items/factors that may help lessen stress. The third instrument had 6 items and addressed the frequency with which safety and preventative practices were used. Finally, the remaining 2 questionnaires contained 29 and 26 items, respectively, to elicit students' concerns and attitudes toward the COVID-19 pandemic.
Data Analyses
Descriptive statistics were generated to describe the study sample and responses to items on the scaled instruments. Each scaled instrument was scored by taking the mean of all items with valid responses. The score was set to missing if more than half of the items contained a missing response. K-mean cluster analysis separated students into two groups based on the information source and frequency used. The cluster centroids were obtained using the least-squares estimation in the k-means clustering method for Euclidean distance for up to a maximum of 20 iterations or until convergence was achieved. The association between disaster preparedness training and the two groups of news users based on the clustering was assessed using the chi-square test of independence. Statistical significance was set at p<0.05. Differences between users of reliable news sources and users of less reliable news on factors that induce stress, factors that reduce stress, safety, and prevention practices, and concerns and attitudes toward the COVID-19 pandemic source were tested using the nonparametric Wilcoxon two-sample test. The test is appropriate for skewed distribution, which was found for many of the measures.
Results
Study Participants
The survey was completed by 123 students from nursing (38%), medicine (33%), and health professions (28%). Twenty-eight percent of students were in the first year of their degree program, 33% in their second year, 23% in their third year, and 15% in their fourth year. The majority (56%) expected to graduate in 2021. Table 1 shows demographic characteristics of the sample. The responders were representative of the student body within the schools.
Survey Responses
Use of reliable news sources. The amount of time spent on each news source by the two categories of users described by the cluster analysis is shown in Table 2. Students in Cluster 1 (C1) indicated a greater reliance on credible sources of information, such as government websites, university websites, the WHO, NIH, and medically oriented websites such as WebMD. Cluster 2 (C2) students tended to rely on fewer reputable sources of information-these included news organizations, social media platforms, as well as family and friends. Only a small percentage of participants used Wikipedia. In both cluster groups, approximately a third of students frequently used search engines to obtain news.
Disaster preparedness training. An important part of preparing for events like the pandemic is disaster preparedness training. However, 40% of students reported no formal or informal education. Moreover, 21% of all students had informal disaster preparedness education through discussions with faculty members, while 39% had formal education through an academic program. There was no association between reliable news sources and disaster preparedness training (c2(1, n = 113) = 0.11, p = 0.74).
Knowledge related to COVID-19. Most students (70%) receive a score of 90 or higher. Only 4% scored less than 80, and 26% scored between 80 and 90. The lowest score was 32.5, and the highest score was 100. Students who used more reliable news sources scored higher on knowledge related to the COVID-19 disease than students who used less reliable news sources.
Safety and prevention practices. No significant difference existed between users of more reliable news sources and users of less reliable news sources for safety and prevention practices.
Factors that increased and reduced stress. No significant difference between users of more reliable news sources and users of less reliable news sources were found for factors that increase stress. Less reputable news sources users had no significant difference in factors that cause stress. Table 3 lists the students' stressors and their responses. There was a significant difference in parameters that reduced stress between users of more dependable and less reliable news sources. Students who used credible news sources reported less stress than those who did not.
Concerns and attitudes toward COVID-19. No significant difference in concerns toward COVID-19 between students who used more reliable versus students who used less reliable news sources was found. Likewise, the difference in attitudes toward COVID-19 between students who used reliable and students who used less reliable news sources was not statistically significant. Discussion
According to the WHO, the world is undergoing a large "infodemic," which leads people to navigate an overload of information, including incorrect information about COVID-19.(23) Students frequently access information via the Internet, social media, and social networks in addition to material distributed by university sources and statements made by government health authorities.(23,24) As a result, students were frequently exposed to incorrect information due to these information-seeking behaviors, contributing to psychological distress. The evidence reveals that medical and nursing students already have lower mental health than the general population,(25'26) with academic stress as a primary predictor of ill health.(25·27) According to the studies, students studying health professions exhibited increased levels of anxiety during the COVID-19 pandemic.
Increased stress levels were assumed to be related to the exceptional circumstances created by the COVID19 pandemic, including social isolation, economic instability, future uncertainty, difficulties connected with remote learning, and fear of infection.(26) Surveys of health professions students revealed that anxiety for one's health and fear of infecting relatives were major sources of concern during the COVID-19 pandemic.(23·28) Additionally, Savitsky et al.(26) discovered that students who had greater resilience and self-esteem reported lower levels of worry.
Our findings indicate an increase in stress levels among healthcare students across the three schools, with no significant difference in reported stress levels between students who accessed more credible information sources and those who accessed primary information via social media and social networks. While the use of less credible information sources was not associated with a rise in factors that increase stress, use of more credible information sources was associated with factors that reduce stress. Use of more credible information sources resulted in a difference in information accuracy between the two groups. Students who relied on more reliable sources of information provided more accurate data on COVID-19.
Along with information accuracy, the student group that indicated primary use of more credible information sources scored higher on items identifying stress-relieving factors. While both groups expressed concern about pathogenicity and transmissibility, as well as fear of exposure, the findings of improved practices and behaviors associated with stress reduction in the reliably informed group support the need for health education programs to incorporate methods for reducing anxiety and stress among interprofessional healthcare students during times of crisis. According to the study's findings, early curricular training on how to evaluate the information for quality and the ability to recognize real and accurate information sources will benefit students in minimizing anxiety in unpredictable scenarios.
Recent research from health professional students during the COVID-19 pandemic demonstrates the critical nature of monitoring student knowledge, perspectives, and attitudes during times of crisis to give targeted student assistance and protection against disinformation.(23) Along with training in data collection and evidence review, resilience training for health professions students may benefit students by teaching them how to use coping skills to overcome the problems associated with ambiguity and unpleasant situations. Resilience training entails developing confidence in the workplace's support, honing adaptive coping methods, and increasing self-efficacy.(25,29) Addressing knowledge acquisition and the ability to successfully navigate situations involving an abundance of information, in conjunction with training that teaches students to recognize fear and emotional responses during crises and develop skills that facilitate coping strategies in the face of adversity or surprise, would benefit student resilience and wellbeing.
Pandemic preparedness (material unique to pandemic/crisis) has been theorized to help avoid the negative mental health consequences linked with distress.(25) Evidence suggests that preparation should be provided before a pandemic to build resilience.(25,30) However, few students report feeling equipped to help in emergencies based on their current skills and experienced25'31 We found similar findings; 40% of the students reported get- ting no official or informal education, which would have prepared them for a pandemic catastrophe. Of the 60% of students who reported receiving readiness training, 21% reported receiving informal preparedness training through discussions with faculty members, while 39% reported receiving formal preparedness training through an academic program. This notion of insufficient preparedness results in intense feelings of pain and emphasizes the critical nature of psychological support for students and healthcare staff during global pandemic events.(28) Effective training programs affect clinical confidence and readiness.(32,33)
The COVID-19 pandemic demonstrates the importance of preclinical and clinical disaster planning to provide proper preparation for future public health calamities.(25,33,34) Courses on crisis preparedness should include navigational information resources, safety, preventative techniques, emergency decision-making, interdisciplinary readiness, and team roles in interprofessional practice. Additionally, these preparatory courses could benefit from covering essential information and skills in global health emergency preparedness, such as safety and preventative techniques, basic life support, vaccination, and triage(33) and use of credible information sources.(9)
Numerous limitations apply to this investigation. First, the response rate was low. While the survey was confidential and anonymous, students were not required to participate. The poor response rate could be attributed to the numerous demands placed on students during the pandemic. Second, the sample was limited to students who had direct patient care. Around 27% (n=46) of respondents had no interaction with patients and were not included in the analysis. As a result, certain items in the survey were slightly different depending on whether respondents encountered patient care or not. Limiting the sample to students who had contact with patients means no harmonization of the survey items was required, and consistency was maintained. This, however, presents a knowledge vacuum for students who have never encountered a patient. Due to the small sample size, it was impossible to do a subset analysis of students who did not encounter patients. Finally, due to the study's small sample size, the conclusions cannot be generalized to the entire student population.
Nonetheless, the study offers several significant strengths. This was the first study investigating the relationship between trustworthy news sources and students' responses to the COVID-19 pandemic. The study examines numerous adjustment areas, including awareness about COVID-19, safety and prevention activities, factors that contribute to and alleviate stress, and attitudes and concerns about the COVID-19 pandemic. Additionally, students were recruited from all three schools that comprise a patient care team: nursing, medicine, and allied health.
Conclusions
Our findings highlight the importance of the use of credible news sources by interprofessional healthcare students. Students who used more credible news sources scored higher on knowledge about COVID-19 and reported fewer factors resulting in stress than students who used less credible news sources. The findings underscore the critical need for students to exercise caution regarding untrustworthy news sources. Not only were informed students less stressed, but they can also provide more support for initiating proper safety measures in the communities they serve. Future larger studies are needed to examine further the influence of news sources on students beyond COVID-19.
