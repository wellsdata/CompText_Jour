Introduction
With the advent of the internet, the paradigm of news distribution has been modified ( Becker et al. , 2010 ). Especially with social media growing exponentially, more and more people are deeply engaged and rely on online content for news. As Kim et al. (2020) outlined, in the traditional media system, few opinion leaders are responsible for communicating information from the media to others. However, professionals and amateurs alike can participate in the news production, consumption and distribution process on social media. By January 2021, Sri Lanka had 7.93 m social media users, 36.8% of the total population ( Digital, 2020: Sri Lanka, 2021 ) and between 2020 and 2021, there has been a 1.5 m growth (+23%) in the number of social media users in Sri Lanka. Accordingly, the role of social media platforms as agents of spreading news and exchanging views regarding political, societal and other matters ( Kim et al. , 2020 ) is growing both in global and local contexts.
Furthermore, Dhir et al. (2018) stressed that information sharing and integration have changed dramatically due to online social media platforms like Facebook, Twitter and LinkedIn. Interactive media technology (for example, Facebook’s “share” function) allows people to share content quickly and extensively ( Nisbet and Kotcher, 2009 ). Social media’s sharing behavior is its lifeblood and the method by which it creates value ( Mangold and Faulds, 2009 ) for its users, and motivation plays a crucial role in this sharing behavior ( Ham et al. , 2019 ).
While existing research offers essential insights into sharing decisions, thorough studies examining social media sharing motives remain relatively absent in the mainstream literature ( Talwar et al. , 2019 ; Hanna et al. , 2011 ). It is essential to identify why consumers make voluntarily and consciously informed decisions about what they consume and how they distribute the information. This becomes even more important since, for a social media user, sharing represents the primary method for interacting and communicating with other social media users, thus becoming one of the primary purposes of social media use in the first place ( Ham et al. , 2019 ). Accordingly, consumer sharing behavior is likely to be influenced by social connections, adding another dynamic to already complex interactions. Furthermore, this highlights the importance of investigating the interaction between internal motivation and social motivation to understand better the behavior of sharing on social media platforms.
Interestingly, as shown by the existing research, online social media also has a limitation regarding its use as a news medium. Among those limitations, the absence of a credible source that can spread false/fake news has been prominent. Spreading fake news on the internet goes beyond the mere organizational level. It can have adverse impacts on members of society at all levels, including the government and policymakers as well as non-governmental organizations, companies and individuals due to its nature ( Talwar et al. , 2020 ). Depending on the nature, some fake news intends to spread distrust and exaggerate social and cultural dynamics by manipulating political, regional and religious information ( Wardle and Derakhshan, 2017 ). Business according to Shu et al. (2017) , in the business context, fake news can negatively affect people and society because it intentionally persuades consumers to embrace false ideas that are disseminated to advance particular agendas.
The spread of fake news presents significant challenges for organizations and brands. Indeed, false information that promotes a particular viewpoint or opinion on a specific product, brand or organization but is not genuine may be deliberately created to mislead customers ( Potthast et al. , 2017 ). For instance, customers threatened to boycott McDonald’s after false information about the company’s use of ground worm filler in its burgers spread virally on the internet ( Taylor, 2016 ). There have also been some recent studies that have examined the effect of fake news on brands and organizations (e.g. Cheng and Chen, 2020 ; Visentin et al. , 2019 ). Fake information such as this can harm retailers by impacting consumer purchase decisions. Jayawardena et al. (2023) further confirmed the negative effect of fake news dissemination across social media on organizations and individuals’ purchase decisions. Conversely, consumers might be tricked into buying products due to reviews, known as online counterfeits ( Martens and Maalej, 2019 ). News spread and the speed with which it is shared are thus a major concern given the new age of social media, where users are anonymous, content is created by users themselves and the geographical distance between users does not play any role in spreading false information ( Talwar et al. , 2020 ).
Academic research on using media, such as feeling overloaded by information, getting exhausted from social media, fear of missing out and the corresponding coping mechanisms, has been examined over the past years. Nonetheless, the knowledge concerning the motivations and behaviors that lead to the distribution of news is still lacking ( Dhir et al. , 2018 , 2019 ; Malik et al. , 2020 ). Jang and Kim (2018) and Talwar et al. (2020) suggest that there is little knowledge about why people post about brands on social media platforms. It may be speculated that discovering the motives and actions underlying people who tell others about the brand on the internet might be the means to address the problem that grows every day. Furthermore, marketers, merchants, brand managers and academics may all benefit from a more profound knowledge of the subject matter. As a result, the lack of research studies on the variables that intensify the spread of fake news constitutes a gap in the existing literature that has to be filled. The present research tries to minimize this gap by analyzing the behavior of social media users regarding authentication and the dissemination of fake news.
Literature review and hypotheses development Social media and fake news According to the definition, social media is “a collection of internet-based apps that are built on the conceptual and technological underpinnings of Web 2.0, and that enable the creation and sharing of user-generated content ( Ismail, 2017 )”. Those applications come in several forms, including weblogs, social blogs, microblogging, wikis, podcasts, images, videos, rating systems and social bookmarking systems. Moreover, as Appel et al. (2020) outlined, it is possible to describe social media as a digital-based software package that is usually displayed as apps or websites and that users may use to transmit or receive digital material or information via an online social network. Social media is the most popular platform because of the services it offers, such as Facebook, Instagram, Twitter and so on ( Appel et al. , 2020 ).
Molina et al. (2019) argue that fake news and false information are two different concepts. According to Mustafaraj and Metaxas (2017) , fake news is described as internet falsehoods that are designed and distributed in such a manner that they seem genuine and legitimate to the readers. The dictionary definition for fake news is “false stories that appear to be news, spread on the internet or using other media, usually created to influence political views or as a joke” (Cambridge Online Dictionary, 2018). On the other hand, in order to identify information as false, it is essential to establish a universal truth, which is a challenging attempt due to the requirement of a collective agreement among a group of people ( Southwell et al. , 2017 ). However, some research studies have identified fake news as an umbrella term that comprises seven categories, including false information ( Molina et al. , 2019 ; Talwar et al. , 2019 ). Consequently, this research study focused particularly on fake news, of which false information is also a part.
From the academic point of view, the number of academics who are interested in researching fake news has grown in recent years. A significant majority of the fake news studies conducted are conceptual and qualitative (e.g. Mustafaraj and Metaxas, 2017 ; Shu et al. , 2017 ). Many empirical studies on fake news have been conducted. However, they are mostly focused on the study of newsfeeds, tweets and Facebook postings to understand the spread of fake news on the internet (e.g. Jang and Kim, 2018 ; Shin et al. , 2018 ). Other empirical studies have relied on text analysis using time series, examination of root content, writing style analysis of hyper-partisanity, an investigation into the sharing history of the identified fake story, evaluation of the information presented through tweets, comments and articles and a randomized controlled trial of political mobilization messages (e.g. Allcott and Gentzkow, 2017 ). A research gap exists in the area of studies on the behavioral elements of users spreading false information on social media.
Motivations for sharing behavior of social media users – uses and gratification approach Understanding the motivations underlying customer behavior is critical for recognizing consumer behavior. According to Kankanhalli et al. (2005) , this is especially true in terms of sharing behavior. This study’s theoretical foundation was inspired by the uses and gratification approach which was introduced by Katz et al. (1974) . Ruggiero (2000) pointed out that studies have confirmed the viability of assumption according to which the theory of uses and gratification is a fundamental theoretical framework that reveals the motives and ways of human activity, in the course of active dissemination by various means of the media, to satisfy the needs that arise in different live situations. However, whereas alternative theories establish how the media affects individuals, and uses and gratification theories recognize how individuals are using the media.
For the very first time, the uses and gratifications theory became popular, mainly in the study of the usage of mass media ( Kang and Atkin, 1999 ; Towers, 1987 ), and later in studying and explaining the usage of the internet ( Ruggiero, 2000 ; Stafford et al. , 2004), and today, this theory is particularly successful in the study of the usage of social media ( Chen and Chan, 2017 ; Ku et al. , 2013 ; Li et al. , 2016 ; Luo et al. , 2011 ). Moreover, this theory has also been extended to studies of online behaviors, such as chat room activities ( Bagozzi and Dholakia, 2002 ), virtual communities ( Ardichvili, 2008 ), virtual games ( Dholakia et al. , 2004 ) and social networking sites ( Cho et al. , 2015 ). In the same vein, the topic of motives for sharing has also been studied in various digital environments, including general Internet content sharing ( Ho and Dempsey, 2010 ), email sharing ( Phelps et al. , 2004 ) and participation in online forums ( Hennig-Thurau et al. , 2004 ).
Introne et al. (2018) suggested that the perceived benefits of using social media were entertainment, communication with others, information seeking and information learning. In addition, Dunne et al. (2010) discovered that the usage of social networking sites is associated with several gratifications, including entertainment, information search, acceptance by peers and relationship maintenance. Apart from the scholarly work stated above ( Ham et al. , 2019 ), it has specified social presence, social conversation, easy connection and self-management as motivations for content sharing on social media. The rationale for selecting the sharing behavior motivations specified by Ham et al. was because it is a novel adaptation of the U&G theory, and it has also not been analyzed in most of the previous literature.
Third-person effect (TPE) The spread of the media has necessarily led us to think about the consequences of many media messages on us and our behavior. Davison (1983) introduced the TPE hypothesis, which suggests that “the recipients of these messages are likely to believe that these messages will have a significantly greater impact on other people than on themselves.” TPE is concerned with the psychological processes involved when persons make a distinction between themselves and others in judging the consequences of the media message. This theory can be understood by examining the two corollaries.
The first corollary is based on the perceptual component Third-person perception, which states that “on items of socially unacceptable and negative media content, people tend to perceive others to be more influential than themselves.” Such a point of view is shared by previous research; for example, Rojas et al. (2012) showed that people overestimate the influence of sexually oriented television programs, violent programs and pornographic imitation ads. In addition, Houston et al. (2011) proved that news stories with biased reporting will exhibit greater third-person perception. Thus, considering the results of prior studies, the social desirability of media messages plays an important role in TPE and negatively oriented messages are considered to be harmful to others.
The second corollary of TPE, “Social distance,” is explained by a concept of social distance, which according to Cohen et al. (1988) , is “The finding that persons perceive that a negative media impact is greater for persons with whom they are socially distant than close.” Social distance consists of many aspects, such as geographic distance, psychological characteristic distance or other socialdemographic differentiations. Social distance provides individuals with the motivation to take action toward keeping close persons safe from negative messages that they assume to be affected by media. In common, TPE supposes that individuals contemplate negative media impact on themselves as well as regarding other persons. They also believe that individuals who are more socially distant are more affected. Furthermore, according to the TPE, individuals hold the belief that media messages have a more substantial impact on altering the attitudes of others rather than themselves ( Davison, 1983 ).
Considering the above-mentioned third-person effect, the moderating and dependent variables of the research study were identified.
Social presence (SP) Social presence is one of the reasons that motivate social media users to share their content, and this concept implies that consumers share to give others a better understanding of who they are and what matters to them ( Ham et al. , 2019 ). This is compatible with Goffman’s (1959) concept of self-presentation, which claims that people constantly control others’ perceptions of themselves to eventually influence others’ attitudes and behaviors ( Papacharissi, 2002 ). Indeed, Hayes and King (2014) demonstrated that consumers share and consume branded content (for example, viral advertisements) for both pragmatic and expressive reasons; sharing branded content allows users to express themselves while simultaneously building trust among their network to ensure future sharing. In order to protect an individual’s social presence, some may take actions to authenticate news about brands before sharing it across social media. However, some people may instantaneously share news about brands to create awareness among their network. Accordingly, researchers proposed the following hypotheses.
H1. Social presence has a significant impact on authenticating news before sharing.
H2. Social presence has a significant impact on the instantaneous sharing of news to create awareness. Social conversation (SC) Following John (2012) and Wittel (2011) , consumers communicate their ideas and emotions to others through sharing relevant material on social media. The items in this social conversation variable demonstrate that consumers’ sharing motivation is not directly related to the value and quality of the shared content but to the development of social relationships. In other words, social conversation means “to socialize” and “to participate in social discussions,” in which people share fake news about brands ( Ham et al. , 2019 ). To create social relationships, some people may instantaneously share news for awareness. Some people may take action, others may instantaneously share news for awareness and others may take action to authenticate news before sharing. Consequently, researchers proposed the following hypotheses:
H3. Social conversation has a significant impact on authenticating news before sharing.
H4. Social conversation has a significant impact on the instantaneous sharing of news to create awareness. Easy connection (EC) The easy connection element shows that customers share information since it is convenient to remain in touch with their friends and family members ( Ham et al. , 2019 ). Consumers may easily remain linked via sharing, which is similar to Internet use ( Stafford and Stafford, 2001 ; Youn and Lee, 2005 ). Consumers can easily share because of the rapid development of social media apps and the many share buttons that can be found on websites in general. In order to stay connected with others, some people may authenticate news about brands before sharing and some may instantaneously share news to make other people aware. The researchers proposed the following hypotheses:
H5. Easy connection has a significant impact on authenticating news before sharing.
H6. Easy connection has a significant impact on the instantaneous sharing of news for creating awareness. Self-management (SM) Consumers exchange information to control their knowledge and learning, as shown by the self-management component ( Ham et al. , 2019 ). According to a previous study, people who find fascinating content on the internet would click the “like” or “share” button to store it in their account for future consumption ( Ham et al. , 2019 ). The “self-shared” content is primarily intended for information consumption and self-improvement, for instance, “to learn things to better me,” “to constantly learn” and so on. Consequently, the researchers proposed the following hypotheses.
H7. Self-management has a significant impact on authenticating news before sharing.
H8. Self-management has a significant impact on the instantaneous sharing of news to create awareness. Instantaneous sharing of news for creating awareness (INS) Tajfel and Turner (1986) developed the social identity theory to explain why people instantly share news to raise awareness among their peers and generate awareness within their communities. They observed that being a member of a group increased people’s self-esteem because the groups offered a social identity and a feeling of belonging to the social environment. To be a member of a social network dedicated to sharing information. The danger of instantaneous news sharing, on the other hand, is that it may unintentionally lead to the spread of false information ( Talwar et al. , 2020 ). According to a relatively recent study, individuals are more likely to spread fake news without verifying it because they think it to be true rather than because it is false ( Hunt, 2016 ). Moreover, according to several researchers, fake news is created to be spread through social bots, which allow them to be shared in vast amounts in a very short period and also that does not help the recipient verify the information through fact-checking ( Jun et al. , 2017 ). In simple words, as soon as a social media user receives a message, the person can immediately post it elsewhere ( Talwar et al. , 2020 ).
Authenticating news before sharing (AN) Prior research suggests that there is a self-enhancing bias in third-person perception ( Shen et al. , 2015 ). Referring to this self-enhancement, people are more likely to fact-check information before spreading it to protect others around them ( Sun et al. , 2008 ). Particularly, the authentication behavior is in line with the social exchange theory of Malinowski (1922) , that individuals will consistently choose and act based on good results or long-term benefits rather than immediate rewards. Therefore, it is more likely that people would check the news before sharing it as it may lead to rewarding outcomes such as earning others’ trust in the social network and enhancing one’s reputation. However, according to prior research studies, trust has been identified as the most important measure of interpersonal relationships and group behaviors ( Golembiewski and McConkie, 1975 ). Furthermore, trust has been applied as a necessary quality in various online environments ( Talwar et al. , 2020 ). Consequently, trust may emerge as a determinant that drives social media platform users to disseminate the information upon checking it. Indeed, it would appear that pre-verifying news before sharing is a great opportunity for people to get social approval and raise their social capital as trustworthy sources. Users, motivated by the self-enhancement bias, are inclined to be more careful in spreading information.
Active corrective (AC) and passive corrective (PC) actions on fake news The corollary most frequently touched upon in the studies of the Third-person effect is the social distance ( McLeod et al. , 2001 ). It lies in the concept that a person considering oneself as the referential point believes that all people occupying an inferior position in the social hierarchy are more likely to be influenced by negative messages and need protection. The corollary of social distance lays the basis for acting against the spread of fake information. People may be additionally motivated to apply a corrective to the expansion of false information in order to shield those who are socially distant from them. Correction might be imposed in a wide range of methods, including but not limited to media prohibition ( Xu and Gonzenbach, 2008 ) and online engagement through public comments ( Xu and Gonzenbach, 2008 ; Chung et al. , 2015 ). According to the results of the qualitative studies conducted previously, social media users engage in two corrective actions: active and passive correctives. Active corrective strategy involves the sender of the fake news to stop spreading the information. The sender should also ensure to verify the piece of information before he can broadcast it again. The other measure he should take is to inform people about the fake news. On the other hand, passive corrective actions refer to reporting or blocking the social media user. Active corrective and passive corrective are also consistent with the propositions of third-person perception, which hypothesizes that social media users attempt to protect brands from the unfavorable effects of fake news through their corrective action, which may be positive (e.g. advising others) or negative (e.g. reporting false information and blocking). Accordingly, this study attempts to test whether individuals are taking active corrective or passive corrective actions after authenticating the fake news about brands. Methodology
This study adopted a quantitative research technique and a single cross-sectional study design. Social media users in the Colombo district provided the required data by filling out a self-administered questionnaire. The sample consisted of 211 individuals who used social media in the Colombo district and were chosen using a non-probabilistic purposive sampling method. Respondents were chosen from the Colombo district because the Western province showed the highest computer literacy rate according to the Department of Census and Statistics (2021) , and Colombo district has the largest population and the densest population in the Western Province ( Department of Census and Statistics, 2021 ).
There were three stages of the research. First, a thorough examination of the literature was performed to gather sufficient information on the research’s subject matter. After a comprehensive study of the literature, the constructs were operationalized ( Ham et al. , 2019 ; Talwar et al. , 2020 ), and the findings were computed on a Likert scale from 1 to 5, with 1 indicating strong disagreement and 5 representing strong agreement. Second, three expert evaluations were carried out to verify that the questionnaire was valid and relevant to the given context. This was followed by a 20-person pilot study, where minor modifications to the wording of the final questionnaire were made in light of the pilot study findings.
Filter questions were included in the questionnaire in order to choose the most relevant respondents for the study. The questionnaire was divided into two parts. The first part began with a set of demographic questions to understand the participants, and the second section assessed the independent mediator. The first part began with a set of demographic questions to understand the participants, and the second section assessed the independent, mediate and dependent variables.
Data analysis and presentation
Following the data collection, the collected data was screened to identify missing values and outliers. Because the questionnaire has been updated in response to expert reviews and pilot testing, it is now simpler for respondents to complete the questionnaire. As a result, there were very few missing values in the data set. As suggested by Little and Rubin (2002) , the median was imputed for the detected missing data. In order to detect the existence of outliers, box plots were employed ( Aguinis et al. , 2013 ). The ten cases recognized as outliers were removed from the data set.
The normality of the data was derived from the values of skewness and kurtosis. Normally, from the standards of normal distribution, the values should not go beyond +2 and −2, as asserted by Hair et al. (2013) .
Values of average variance extracted and composite reliability were used to test the convergent validity. According to the standard, AVE values must be higher than 0.5 and the CR value must be higher than 0.7 ( Malhotra, 2010 ). From the convergent validity testing, it was found that the data were acceptable in terms of convergent validity since the findings of the values were observed in the acceptable range. To test the reliability, Cronbach’s alpha values were used. Cronbach’s alpha tests the internal consistency of the data. Generally, when Cronbach’s alpha score is higher than 0.7, the data are found to be internally consistent. The reliability of the data can be confirmed since Cronbach’s alpha of the data was greater than 0.7. Also, the VIF value was used to check the multicollinearity among the variables. According to Hair et al. (2012) , a VIF value of less than 10 will be assumed if multicollinearity was not feasible. In this study, all variables, VIF values were less than 10 and it is concluded that multicollinearity does not exist. Sample profile of the study This study’s sample consists of 126 females and 85 males; it can be inferred that the majority of the respondents are between the ages of 20 years and 30 years, constituting 52.6% of the total sample, while the second largest number of the respondents is between the ages of 30 years and 40 years. Discussion and conclusion

Considering the prior literature, Apuke and Omar (2020) have investigated the predictors of fake news sharing among social media users about COVID-19 and identified that socialism, which means a desire to form relationships with other people and interact with them ( Lee and Ma, 2012 ), has a significant impact on fake news sharing behavior. In this study, we also found that social conversation significantly impacts the instantaneous sharing of news for creating awareness. Considering this, we can conclude that people instantaneously share fake news about brands without authenticating in order to develop social relationships with others. Moreover, Dunne et al. (2010) reported a strong desire to socially interact with predictions about how people will disseminate news. On the other hand, Fu et al. (2017) outlined that people are more inclined to share news on social media that a friend has shared. Considering fake news sharing, Chang et al. (2017) reported that socialization positively affects the dissemination of fake news across social media platforms. However, the social conversation shows a negative, insignificant impact on the authentication of news before sharing.
Considering social presence and easy connection, those variables have positive but insignificant impacts on authenticating news before sharing, and instantaneous sharing news awareness creates awareness. The impacts of social presence and easy connection on attitude toward sharing social media news were identified by Ham et al. (2019) . They reported that social presence and easy connection significantly impact attitudes toward sharing social media news. Prior studies have not tested the impact of social presence and easy connection on the authentication of news before sharing and instantaneous sharing news to create awareness. The insignificant impact might occur in order to protect one’s social image; people are much worried about the content that they share. Even they will not take action to authenticate news about brands because they may feel that it will affect their image. Considering easy connection, the insignificant impact might occur as individuals may share their content on social media rather than brand-related fake news to keep in touch with their family and friends.
Self-management shows a positive significant impact on both authenticating news before sharing and instantaneous sharing news to create awareness. Ham et al. (2019) reported that self-management has an insignificant impact on attitudes toward sharing news across social media platforms. However, considering the fake news sharing about brands, people share fake news instantaneously and authenticate news before sharing to manage their knowledge and learning.
Most importantly, authenticating news before sharing has a significant impact on both active corrective actions and passive corrective actions on fake news. As responsible social media users, taking active or passive corrective actions after authenticating news is a crucial factor. Talwar et al. (2020) identified authenticating news before sharing, active corrective actions on fake news and passive corrective actions on fake news as three different independent variables and tested the impact of sharing fake news due to religiosity and sharing fake news due to lack of time. In that study, they found that sharing news has an insignificant impact on sharing fake news due to religiosity and sharing fake news due to lack of time.
Implications of the study
Considering the theoretical implications, this study provides a significant contribution to the existing knowledge on disseminating fake news on social media platforms by suggesting and evaluating the validity of novel and lesser-known measures. In the past, methods such as instantaneous news sharing on the internet, active and passive corrective action against fake news sharing, authenticating news before sharing, and motives behind sharing behavior such as social pressure, social conversation, easy connection and self-management have not been explored earlier. While the study on fake news sharing behavior is still in its early stages, the measures created may assist the scholarly community in improving their understanding.
As managerial implications, disseminating news without authentication, mainly if it is fake, may have very negative consequences for organizations and brands. A brand’s reputation may be negatively affected by poor reviews on social media platforms, which can serve as a source of advertising. This is especially true in today’s more connected society, characterized by consumer interaction’s growing significance ( Islam et al. , 2019 ; Zhou and Duan, 2016 ). It is possible to create methods to combat the spread of fake news by understanding the behavior of people who disseminate false information without taking necessary corrective action. For example, they may create communication that encourages people to take corrective action against the false news they get on social media and, as a result, promote excellent and useful information about their businesses.
Limitations and future research suggestions
One significant limitation of the study is that it was conducted using a sample of just 211 respondents who live in the Colombo area. Therefore, future research studies can be expanded geographically to cover other districts of Sri Lanka. In addition to that, another limitation of this research study is the use of self-administered questionnaires to collect primary data from the respondents. Google Forms were used to collect data for the study. It is crucial to note that due to the nature of this data collection method, the researcher had little control over the replies received. Therefore, in future research studies, data can be gathered by meeting respondents face-to-face. On the other hand, since this study was restricted to a quantitative approach, the researchers were unable to examine some of the most significant insights from the respondents on the sharing behavior of fake news about brands. Therefore, future studies can be conducted using a qualitative approach. Moreover, sharing behavior can be further understood using demographic variables such as gender, educational background, and so on in future research studies.
