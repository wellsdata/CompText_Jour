1. Introduction In the 21st century, health misinformation has emerged as a critical public health challenge, exacerbated by the unprecedented reach and influence of digital communication platforms. Health misinformation refers to false or misleading health-related information disseminated in various forms, including false claims, manipulated content, and pseudoscientific advice. The phenomenon of the “infodemic”, coined by the World Health Organization (WHO), captures the overwhelming flood of both accurate and inaccurate information that complicates individuals’ ability to make informed decisions about their health [1]. This issue has profound implications, not only for personal health choices but also for public health strategies, requiring urgent, multidimensional interventions to counter its effects.
The consequences of health misinformation are neither abstract nor confined to isolated incidents; they reverberate globally, impacting societies and economies alike. The COVID-19 pandemic starkly illuminated the pervasive and destructive nature of health misinformation [2]. In the early months of the pandemic, inaccurate claims, such as suggestions that drinking bleach could kill the virus or that 5G technology was responsible for the disease’s spread, circulated widely [3]. These claims led to tragic outcomes, including loss of life and widespread public confusion [4]. Similarly, vaccine misinformation has perpetuated hesitancy and refusal, directly undermining global vaccination campaigns against diseases such as measles, polio, and COVID-19 [5].
Beyond the direct health impacts, misinformation contributes to societal polarization and complicates efforts to reach vulnerable populations [6]. For example, during the Ebola outbreaks in West Africa, misinformation regarding the origins and spread of the disease led to resistance against healthcare workers and attacks on treatment facilities [7]. The repercussions of misinformation, thus, extend beyond individual health to the broader structural systems designed to protect and sustain public welfare.
The advent of social media platforms has transformed the way information is disseminated, consumed, and amplified. While these platforms provide unprecedented access to health-related resources, they have also become fertile ground for the proliferation of misinformation. Algorithms designed to optimize engagement inadvertently prioritize sensationalist or polarizing content, often spreading falsehoods more rapidly than verified information [8]. A study conducted during the COVID-19 pandemic revealed that misinformation was 70% more likely to be shared on social media compared to accurate news [9].
Social media’s unique dynamics, virality, anonymity, and echo chambers, compound the challenge of combating misinformation [10]. Echo chambers particularly exacerbate the problem by reinforcing pre-existing beliefs, creating insular communities resistant to correction. Influencers and pseudo-experts, armed with persuasive rhetoric and significant followings, further amplify false claims, lending them an air of legitimacy [11]. Consequently, the public’s ability to discern credible sources is diminished, and misinformation becomes entrenched.
On a broader scale, public health initiatives are undermined when misinformation influences collective behaviors [12]. Vaccine hesitancy is a particularly poignant example, as it jeopardizes herd immunity and allows preventable diseases to resurface. Furthermore, misinformation fosters distrust in health institutions, scientific research, and healthcare providers [13]. This erosion of trust poses long-term challenges for health systems. If left unaddressed, it weakens the social contract between health authorities and the public, making it increasingly difficult to implement evidence-based interventions during crises. Thus, combating health misinformation is not only a matter of improving health literacy but also of safeguarding public trust and resilience.
The complex and dynamic nature of health misinformation demands innovative solutions, and artificial intelligence (AI) has emerged as a pivotal tool in this battle [14]. AI technologies are uniquely equipped to analyze large volumes of data, identify patterns, and detect misinformation with unparalleled speed and accuracy [15].
AI-powered natural language processing (NLP) models can scan digital platforms to identify health-related misinformation by analyzing linguistic patterns, sentiment, and content provenance. For instance, machine learning algorithms have been employed to detect fake news by comparing claims against trusted databases and flagging inconsistencies [16]. During the COVID-19 pandemic, AI systems were used to monitor social media platforms and identify emerging misinformation trends, allowing public health authorities to respond with targeted campaigns [17].
Moreover, AI facilitates the development of real-time fact-checking tools. These tools leverage deep learning techniques to cross-reference claims with verified scientific data, providing users with immediate feedback about the credibility of information [18]. One notable example is the WHO’s partnership with tech companies to develop AI-driven chatbots that debunk myths and provide accurate health advice to millions of users globally [19].
AI also plays a role in audience segmentation, enabling health communicators to tailor messages to specific demographics and counteract the personalized nature of misinformation [20]. By analyzing user behavior and preferences, AI can craft interventions that resonate with diverse populations, increasing the likelihood of engagement and adherence to evidence-based guidance [21].

The reviewed studies highlight a range of advanced technologies for detecting COVID-19 misinformation across diverse datasets and contexts. Transformer models like BERT, RoBERTa, ELECTRA, and DeBERTa, including social media-specific variants such as BERTweet, excel in text embedding and classification tasks. Deep learning architectures, including CNN+Bi-GRU, Bi-LSTM, and MC-CNN, effectively analyze textual structure and temporal sequences, while hybrid models like ACOVMD combine CNN and Bi-LSTM with semi-supervised learning to adapt to both labeled and unlabeled data. Classical machine learning algorithms, such as Logistic Regression, SVM, and Multinomial Naïve Bayes, are utilized for foundational analyses, particularly in underrepresented languages like Swahili. The paper [22] explores an AI-powered system to combat the spread of COVID-19 misinformation on social media platforms. Recognizing the damaging effects of false health information on public trust and health outcomes, the study introduces COS2, a tool combining machine learning algorithms with social network analysis to detect and map misinformation in real-time. The system identifies misinformation hubs, tracks its dissemination pathways, and enables targeted interventions to curb its reach. By leveraging scalable computational methods, COS2 demonstrates significant promise in addressing the infodemic and supporting health authorities in maintaining the integrity of public health messaging during crises.
The study [23] examines the effectiveness of transformer-based neural network classifiers in identifying COVID-19 misinformation on Twitter, focusing on the South African context. The study uses publicly available datasets to evaluate transformer models such as BERT, CT-BERT, ELECTRA, and LAMBERT for text embeddings coupled with classifiers optimized via hyperband algorithms. While the models achieved strong performance on test data (best F1 score of 0.899), the results revealed challenges in accurately generalizing to South African Twitter data due to unique linguistic features, cultural expressions, and limited domain-specific training data. The authors emphasize the need for localized datasets and multilingual transformers to enhance misinformation detection in diverse linguistic environments.
Mulahuwaish et al. [24] introduce the CovidMis20 dataset, comprising over 1.3 million tweets, to detect and analyze misinformation on Twitter. The study employs two deep learning models, an ensemble CNN+Bi-GRU and Bi-LSTM, achieving testing accuracies of 92.23% and 90.56%, respectively. The ensemble CNN+Bi-GRU model demonstrated superior performance, effectively classifying tweets as real or fake. The study emphasizes the scalability of CovidMis20 for future research and highlights the importance of evolving detection techniques to mitigate misinformation’s societal impact.
The paper [25] investigates the use of advanced attention-based deep learning models to identify misinformation about long COVID and reinfections. Utilizing diverse datasets sourced from open databases, fact-checking websites, and government institutions, the study applies models such as BERT, RoBERTa, DeBERTa, XLNet, and HAN to classify content as genuine or fake. With an emphasis on accurate text representation and leveraging the strengths of each model, the researchers achieved an F1 score of 94.96% with DeBERTa and XLNet, demonstrating superior performance in distinguishing false claims. This research highlights the effectiveness of attention mechanisms in NLP tasks and their potential for combating health-related misinformation.
Bozuyla et al. [26] present a robust framework for detecting COVID-19-related fake news in Turkish. The researchers generated a novel dataset from Twitter, fact-checking websites, and machine-translated English datasets. They evaluated conventional machine learning algorithms (e.g., Naïve Bayes, SVM) and advanced deep learning methods (e.g., LSTM, Bi-LSTM) alongside transformer-based models (e.g., BERT, RoBERTa, and BerTURK). Among these, the Turkish-specific transformer model, BerTURK, achieved the highest accuracy of 98.5%, outperforming other methods significantly. The study highlights the effectiveness of transformer architectures in processing complex, morphologically rich languages like Turkish for misinformation detection.
The study [27] addresses the critical issue of misinformation spread on social media during the COVID-19 pandemic, focusing on Swahili content on Twitter. The study developed a novel dataset comprising 1252 labelled tweets collected using common Swahili hashtags related to COVID-19. The dataset was preprocessed and analyzed using five machine learning algorithms: Logistic Regression, Support Vector Machine, Random Forest, Multinomial Naïve Bayes, and Bagging Ensemble. Among these, SVM achieved the highest accuracy of 83.67%, while Multinomial Naïve Bayes excelled in precision with 92.00%. The findings highlight the effectiveness of machine learning in detecting misinformation in underrepresented languages and underscore the potential for extending this work to other platforms and languages.
The paper [28] proposes three advanced machine learning models, LSTM, MC-CNN, and KNN, for detecting COVID-19-related misinformation on Twitter. The study uses the CoAID dataset, a healthcare misinformation corpus, and performs data preprocessing, feature extraction with TF-IDF, and hyperparameter optimization to enhance model performance. Among the models, KNN achieved the best overall accuracy at 99.03%, while MC-CNN delivered the highest precision at 99.57%, demonstrating the efficacy of these models in classifying real and fake tweets.
The research [29] investigates the classification of garlic-related COVID-19 misinformation on Twitter using BERT-based models. The study develops a garlic-specific dataset of 5929 labelled tweets and compares the performance of fine-tuned BERT models against a COVID-19 rumor dataset. BERTweet-large, pre-trained on English tweets, achieved the best results with an accuracy of 91.1% and an F1 score of 0.894, significantly outperforming other models. The findings underscore the effectiveness of fine-tuned BERT models for domain-specific misinformation detection and the challenges posed by ambiguous or sarcastic tweets.
The paper [30] introduces a novel approach to detecting COVID-19 misinformation on Twitter. It presents a self-trained semi-supervised hybrid deep learning model that combines CNN and BiLSTM architectures with a confidence layer to handle labelled and unlabeled tweet datasets. Experimental results demonstrate that the model achieves 98.15% accuracy when trained with 80% labelled data, outperforming conventional methods.
The study [31] investigates the challenges and methodologies in detecting misinformation. It compares machine learning classifiers on three datasets (FakeCOVID, CoAID, and COVIEWED), evaluates word-embedding techniques, and demonstrates that embedding models like Sentence-BERT improve classification performance and reduce bias.

3. Materials and Methods 3.1. Data The COVID19-FNIR dataset [32] is a specialized resource designed to facilitate research on detecting fake news related to the COVID-19 pandemic. It comprises fact-checked news articles from multiple regions, including India, the United States, and Europe, collected between February and June 2020. The dataset includes two primary files: fakeNews.csv and trueNews.csv, which contain news labelled as false or true, respectively. Fake news entries are derived from Poynter’s fact-checking website and are annotated with detailed explanations, origins, and labels. Conversely, accurate news entries are sourced from verified Twitter handles of reputable publishers like The New York Times, The Hindu, and BBC News. This dual-source structure ensures that the dataset reflects diverse linguistic, cultural, and geographical contexts, making it valuable for training robust machine learning models.
From the dataset distribution as presented at Figure 1, 50% of entries are labeled as “False” (3795 samples) and 50% as “True” (3793 samples), ensuring a balanced dataset for machine learning models.
The dataset underwent thorough preprocessing, including removing non-essential characters and information, to enhance its usability. The fakeNews.csv file includes columns such as publication date, article text, explanation of falsehoods, multi-class, and binary classification labels. This granular detail helps in understanding the origins and reasons for misinformation. Meanwhile, the trueNews.csv file organizes verified tweets from trusted news sources by columns like date, text, publisher, and Twitter handle, ensuring a reliable foundation for identifying authentic content. By providing such structured and fact-verified data, the COVID19-FNIR dataset is an essential tool for researchers combating misinformation and improving the reliability of automated news verification systems.
3.2. Methodology This paper proposes a comprehensive methodology integrating data classification and clustering through topic modeling to extract meaningful insights from large-scale textual datasets. The methodology is tailored to analyze data from social media, web pages, and other digital platforms to identify key narratives, themes, and sentiments embedded within the data. The type of information (e.g., true or false) is determined through classification, while topic modeling uncovers thematic clusters and overarching trends. This integrated approach equips policymakers with tools to assess the informational landscape and devise strategic communications informed by evidence-based insights, crucial for addressing crises, combating misinformation, and orchestrating impactful information campaigns.
The pipeline of the methodology, illustrated in Figure 2, commences with a preprocessing phase to ensure the data is clean and prepared for analysis. This includes case normalization, standardizing text by converting it to lowercase and simplifying subsequent processing. HTML tags and URLs are removed to eliminate extraneous metadata, while special characters and punctuation are stripped to focus on meaningful textual content. Hashtags are retained, as they often provide valuable contextual and thematic cues. The data is then tokenized into smaller units, such as words or subwords, essential for machine learning models. The next step in the data preprocessing pipeline involves applying oversampling techniques to ensure equitable label distribution and address class imbalance in the dataset, thereby improving model performance. While this step was not relevant for the COVID-19 dataset, as its two classes are balanced, it remains a pivotal preprocessing step for other datasets that may be analyzed using this methodology.
After preprocessing, the data undergoes classification to ascertain whether a given information is true or false or can fit other classes that were previously determined by researcher. This phase begins with a pre-trained BERT model, which generates rich contextual embeddings of the input text. These embeddings are then passed through a Bidirectional Long Short-Term Memory (Bi-LSTM) layer, enabling the capture of both forward and backward dependencies within the text for deeper contextual understanding. An attention mechanism further refines the output by prioritizing the most relevant segments of the text. To enhance model generalization and mitigate overfitting, dropout layers are incorporated before and after a dense layer, which performs the final classification into predefined categories.
Following classification, samples identified as misinformation (or other classes required for future analysis) are subjected to topic modeling to uncover underlying narratives and themes. This process begins with encoding the text using token and positional encodings. The encoded data is processed through transformer encoding layers to derive high-quality text embeddings. These embeddings are aggregated into fixed-size sentence representations using a mean pooling mechanism, capturing the semantic essence of the text. Clustering techniques are then applied to group similar text samples, enabling the identification of dominant topics or themes present in the dataset.
The pipeline progresses to topic representation, where each cluster is assigned a meaningful label or descriptor to summarize its content. A hierarchical grouping mechanism organizes the topics into broader categories, facilitating the identification of overarching narratives within the misinformation samples. This hierarchical structure allows researchers to map the flow and connections between various themes, providing a structured understanding of misinformation narratives.
The final phase involves human narrative analysis, synthesizing the outputs from classification and topic modeling to generate actionable insights. By integrating these results, this phase offers a holistic understanding of misinformation dynamics, highlighting how it propagates, the dominant themes, and the interrelations among topics. These insights serve to inform public health communication strategies, design targeted interventions, and bolster efforts to counter information manipulation effectively, thereby supporting informed decision-making and policy development.
3.3. Classification Model The proposed Classification Model architecture presented in Figure 3 is designed to effectively handle textual data for classification tasks, such as information manipulation detection. It integrates pretrained embeddings, sequential modeling, attention mechanisms, and dense layers, providing a robust framework for extracting meaningful features and generating accurate predictions.
The model begins with an input layer, which accepts tokenized sequences produced by the BERT tokenizer. The input consists of two components: input_ids, a tensor representing the token IDs of the input sequence with a maximum length of 128 tokens, and attention_mask, a tensor indicating which tokens are relevant (1 for meaningful tokens and 0 for padding). These inputs are essential for distinguishing between actual content and padding tokens, enabling efficient processing by the BERT model.
The core of the architecture is a pretrained BERT-base-uncased model, which serves as a feature extractor. It processes the input sequences and outputs contextual embeddings in the form of last_hidden_state, a tensor of shape (None, 128, 768). These embeddings capture semantic and syntactic relationships between tokens in the context of the input sequence. Leveraging BERT ensures the model benefits from large-scale language pretraining, resulting in rich and comprehensive token representations.
The embeddings generated by BERT are then passed through a Bi-LSTM layer to enhance sequential understanding. The Bi-LSTM layer processes the embeddings with a hidden size of 128 in both forward and backward directions, producing an output tensor of shape (None, 128, 256). This step enables the model to capture dependencies across the entire sequence, incorporating both past and future context, which is critical for understanding the nuanced relationships in text data.
A custom attention mechanism is applied to further refine the Bi-LSTM output. The attention layer takes the Bi-LSTM output of shape (None, 128, 256) and computes attention scores using a weight matrix of shape (256, 1). These scores assign importance to different tokens in the sequence, generating a context vector of shape (None, 256) that focuses on the most critical parts of the text. This mechanism enhances interpretability and ensures the model prioritizes relevant features for classification.
The context vector is then passed through a dense layer for feature condensation and non-linear transformation. Before the dense layer, a dropout layer with a rate of 0.2 is applied to reduce overfitting. The dense layer consists of 64 units with a ReLU activation function, producing an output of shape (None, 64). A second dropout layer with the same rate is added after the dense layer to improve generalization further. These layers ensure the model retains robust features while mitigating the risk of overfitting during training.
Finally, the model outputs predictions through a dense output layer configured for binary classification. This layer has a single unit with a sigmoid activation function, which maps the output to a probability range of [0, 1]. The final output tensor of shape (None, 1) represents the probability score for the input sequence, allowing the model to make an interpretable binary prediction (e.g., true or fake). This architecture combines the power of pre-trained embeddings with sequential modeling and attention to deliver accurate and interpretable predictions for textual data classification tasks.
The model’s hyperparameters were fine-tuned using Optuna 4.1, an efficient optimization framework that leverages a tree-structured Parzen estimator to explore the search space and minimize validation loss. We conducted an extensive search across the following hyperparameters: - Learning Rate: Explored within [5 × 10 −6 , 5 × 10 −4 ] on a logarithmic scale, with 2 × 10 −5 selected based on convergence stability and generalization performance.

- Batch Size: Evaluated within {16, 32, 64, 128}, with 16 providing the best trade-off between stability and computational efficiency.

- Dropout Rate: Searched within [0.1, 0.5] in increments of 0.05, with 0.2 selected to prevent overfitting while maintaining expressiveness.

- Bi-LSTM Hidden Size: Tuned within {64, 128, 256, 512}, with 128 yielding the best balance between model complexity and performance.

- Number of Bi-LSTM Layers: Explored within {1, 2, 3}, with a single layer performing optimally in terms of generalization and computational cost.

- Dense Layer Size: Investigated within {64, 128, 256}, with 64 selected for optimal feature transformation.

- Optimizer Choice: Compared Adam, AdamW, and RMSprop, with Adam demonstrating superior convergence behavior.


The hyperparameter search was conducted over 100 trials with early stopping based on validation loss. Bayesian optimization was employed to refine promising configurations iteratively. The selected hyperparameters consistently outperformed alternative settings across multiple datasets, ensuring robust generalization.
3.4. Topic Modeling The initial stage of the Topic Modeling architecture based on all-MiniLM-L6-v2. Model [33] and presented in Figure 4 focuses on data preparation, ensuring the input text is clean and standardized for subsequent processing. Raw text data is subjected to a series of preprocessing steps for classification, including lowercasing and removing special characters, punctuation, and unnecessary whitespaces if not done during a previous preprocessing stage. This normalization process reduces noise in the data and facilitates effective embedding generation. The cleaned text is then tokenized, breaking sentences into smaller units such as words or subwords, essential for transformer-based models. Proper preprocessing ensures that the model receives well-structured input, enhancing the accuracy and reliability of the subsequent sentence embeddings.
The cleaned text is converted into numerical representations using a transformer-based SentenceTransformer model. First, tokens are embedded into dense vectors (token embeddings) to capture their individual meanings. Positional encodings are then added to these embeddings, enabling the model to preserve the order of tokens within the sequence. The transformer’s core comprises multiple encoding layers, where multi-head attention captures contextual relationships among tokens across the sequence. Each attention sub-layer is followed by residual connections and layer normalization, ensuring model stability and efficient gradient flow during training. A feed-forward network processes each token independently to enhance feature representation, followed by an additional residual connection and normalization. The final sentence embedding is derived by pooling the outputs of the encoding layers, often using mean pooling or extracting the [CLS] token representation. These fixed-dimensional embeddings serve as a rich representation of the semantic content of the input text.
Once the sentence embeddings are generated, BERTopic is applied to extract meaningful topics from the text. BERTopic begins by clustering the SentenceTransformer embeddings, grouping semantically similar documents into clusters. Clustering algorithms like UMAP (for dimensionality reduction) and HDBSCAN (for density-based clustering) are employed to identify these groups. A vectorizer such as CountVectorizer is used for each cluster to extract keywords and phrases that best represent the cluster. A representation model like KeyBERTInspired further refines these keywords, ensuring they are semantically relevant. Optionally, hierarchical relationships between topics can be explored, merging topics into broader categories or splitting them into more granular subtopics. This stage transforms the raw textual data into a structured set of interpretable topics and associated documents.
While BERTopic is the foundation of our topic modeling approach, we introduce refinements that enhance topic coherence, clustering effectiveness, and interpretability, making the method more suitable for misinformation analysis. These modifications involve optimizing the sentence embedding model and refining the clustering approach to improve the quality of extracted topics.
To enhance the representation of text embeddings, we systematically evaluated multiple SentenceTransformer models beyond BERTopic’s default paraphrase-MiniLM. The models considered included all-mpnet-base-v2, LaBSE, multi-qa-mpnet-base-dot-v1, multi-qa-MiniLM-L6-cos-v1, clip-ViT models, all-roberta-large-v1, stsb-xlm-r-multilingual, sentence-t5-base, and sentence-t5-xxl. All-MiniLM-L6-v2 demonstrated the best balance between computational efficiency and contextual representation. This choice resulted in more coherent and distinguishable topics while ensuring scalability for larger datasets.
In addition to improving text embeddings, we refined the clustering methodology by tuning UMAP parameters, particularly n_neighbors and min_dist, to optimize dimensionality reduction. Adjusting n_neighbors allowed better control over topic granularity, ensuring that closely related topics were grouped appropriately while avoiding excessive fragmentation. Based on empirical evaluation, the values n_neighbors = 15 and min_dist = 0.1 provided the most effective trade-off between topic separation and cohesion. Further, we explored alternative clustering strategies beyond BERTopic’s default HDBSCAN, including Agglomerative Clustering and KMeans. These refinements improved the differentiation of overlapping narratives, enhancing the interpretability of extracted topics and ensuring a more robust analysis of misinformation patterns.
By incorporating these optimizations, our approach enhances topic modeling’s accuracy and interpretability, making it more effective for the nuanced challenges associated with misinformation analysis.
The model outputs visualizations that represent the relationships between topics and their structure to enhance interpretability. A topic distance map provides a 2D projection of the distance between topics, allowing users to identify similar or distinct themes at a glance. Hierarchical clustering visualizations show how topics can be combined or split further, offering insights into the granularity of the topic space. Outputs are saved in CSV files, including per-topic data (e.g., top words, topic sizes) and per-document data (e.g., topic assignments for each text sample). These visualizations and outputs ensure that the extracted topics are both comprehensible and actionable for downstream applications.
The described model is beneficial for analyzing large, unstructured datasets to uncover hidden patterns and narratives. By transforming textual data into semantically rich embeddings and clustering them into interpretable topics, this methodology enables users to derive actionable insights from otherwise opaque datasets. Policymakers, researchers, and industry professionals can leverage these outputs to understand prevailing themes, monitor trends, and identify key areas of interest or concern. For instance, in the context of misinformation analysis, the extracted topics and their hierarchical relationships can inform targeted interventions and communication strategies tailored to specific narratives.
The modular design of the pipeline ensures flexibility and adaptability for various use cases. The preprocessing steps can be tailored to specific languages or domains, while the SentenceTransformer model can be replaced with alternative embedding models for specialized tasks. Similarly, BERTopic’s clustering and representation components allow customization to meet unique requirements, such as merging closely related topics or refining keyword extraction.

Each bubble corresponds to a distinct topic, with the size of the bubble reflecting the relative proportion of documents in the dataset assigned to that topic. The distinct clustering observed in the map suggests that the dataset encompasses well-defined and separable topics, which is ideal for understanding the thematic structure of the data. The size and positioning of the bubbles can guide further analysis, with larger and more central topics warranting deeper exploration to identify key narratives. Meanwhile, smaller or peripheral topics may highlight niche discussions or emerging trends. This visualization offers valuable insights into the dataset’s thematic landscape, aiding in interpreting dominant and emerging narratives for strategic communication or intervention planning.
Narrative analysis includes human interpretation of the CSV file with topics generated by the clustering model. The data contains the following columns: ‘Count’, which indicates the number of data units classified under each topic; ‘Name of the topic’; ‘Representation’, which lists the most frequent collocations associated with the topic; and ‘Representative_Docs’, which provides the data units most relevant to represent the topic. The model also generates a −1 class, representing outliers that cannot be classified into any of the identified topics.
For the COVID-19 dataset, the interpretation of the eight topics in the misinformation dataset is as follows:
Topic 0: Public Interaction in India. This topic centers on narratives related to “social distancing” and public behavior in India during the COVID-19 pandemic. Narratives include a mix of compliance and non-compliance stories, such as a “photo of people following social distancing in a market in Manipur” and a “video of an RPF jawan collecting bribes from migrants walking home during the lockdown in Gujarat”. Other examples highlight violations, such as “people in Mumbai ignoring distancing rules while shopping for Eid”.
Topic 1: Conspiracies around Coronavirus Science. This topic shows pseudoscience and misinformation about the virus including claims about faulty PCR tests in Tanzania and unverified remedies, such as high doses of vitamin D. Other narratives assert that the coronavirus is an unnatural mutation or perpetuate misinformation about temperature resistance.
Topic 2: Local Italian Context. This topic centers on pandemic-related developments in “Italy” and the “Italian” response, with representative phrases such as “Italian doctor” and “COVID pandemic”. Documents include stories of exhausted doctors succumbing to the virus, official government communications regarding lockdown measures, and media misrepresentation of events. These narratives often highlight Italy’s early struggles with the pandemic and humanize the crisis through emotional stories, although some accounts appear to be exaggerated or fabricated.
Topic 3: Narratives on Pakistan and Indian Politics. This topic discusses “COVID patients” and “Pakistan”, with a particular focus on political figures and their statements. Representative narratives include claims about Pakistan’s Prime Minister misinterpreting COVID-19 data and statements by Indian leaders about treating millions of patients for free.
Topic 4: Conspiracy Theories Involving Bill Gates. A significant portion of the dataset centers on conspiracy theories about “Bill Gates” and the pandemic being a “fake excuse”. Representative narratives allege that the pandemic was orchestrated to depopulate the earth or implant microchips into the global population. These narratives frequently invoke Gates as a central figure in broader conspiracy theories about global control.
Topic 5: COVID-19 and Humanitarian Aid. This topic is characterized by phrases like “humanitarian aid” and “cases of COVID”. Key narratives include claims that the United Nations imposed conditions, such as the legalization of abortion, on humanitarian aid packages to Ecuador. Other messages propagate pseudoscientific claims about asymptomatic individuals being healthy and spreading immunity rather than the virus.
Topic 6: Controversies around Masks. This topic addresses the ongoing debates surrounding “masks” and their effectiveness. Representative documents include claims about government restrictions on mask production in France and allegations that the WHO changed its guidance on masks to state they increase infection risk.
Topic 7: Spain-Specific Narratives. Focusing on “Spain” and its pandemic response, this topic includes claims about lockdown enforcement, such as the need for certificates to justify movement and allegations of political leaders violating restrictions. Representative documents also discuss rumors of Madrid’s complete closure, which were later debunked.
5. Discussion The results of this study demonstrate the high efficiency and robustness of the proposed methodology for misinformation detection and analysis. The classification model achieved excellent performance metrics, with an accuracy of 99.47% and precision, recall, and F1-score at similar levels. The low test loss of 0.01698 highlights the model’s capacity for accurate predictions and minimal error in classification tasks. These outcomes reflect the effectiveness of integrating advanced natural language processing techniques, such as BERT embeddings and Bi-LSTM architecture, with attention mechanisms for enhanced context understanding.
The analysis of misinformation topics using the clustering model revealed well-defined and distinct thematic groups, offering valuable insights into the narratives and misinformation trends present in the dataset. The topic modeling results demonstrated the methodology’s ability to uncover hidden patterns and key themes, enabling a deeper understanding of the structure and spread of misinformation.
Three other studies validated their classification models on the same dataset. Vinay et al. [34] employed a feature-engineered approach using different machine learning classifiers. Their best model achieved an accuracy of 99.20% and an F1-score of 0.9920, emphasizing the importance of linguistic and social contexts and statistical features. While their approach demonstrates high effectiveness, our methodology surpasses it by leveraging deep contextual embeddings and attention mechanisms, enhancing accuracy and interpretability. Furthermore, our model’s architecture allows for better adaptability to evolving misinformation narratives.
The paper by Sikosana et al. [35] evaluated various machine learning and deep learning models, including hybrid CNN-LSTM architectures, achieving accuracy metrics exceeding 99% on the FNIR dataset. While these models demonstrate strong performance, they rely heavily on complex neural architectures that may face challenges in scalability and real-time applications. In contrast, our model maintains high accuracy while ensuring computational efficiency, making it suitable for large-scale deployments. While the accuracy of both models is identical at 99.47%, our model demonstrates superior performance when considering the F1-score, which is marginally higher at 0.9947 compared to 0.9945 for the model presented in [35]. This seemingly small difference is significant in academic evaluations, as the F1-score reflects a harmonic mean of precision and recall, providing a more comprehensive measure of a model’s performance in handling imbalanced data. The higher F1-score of our model indicates that it achieves better precision-recall balance, which is particularly critical in misinformation detection, where minimizing false positives and false negatives is essential.
Qadees and Hannan [36] explored traditional machine learning models, reporting the best accuracy of 91.6% and 0.92 F1-score for Random Forest. While these models are computationally efficient, their limited ability to capture contextual and sequential information within the text restricts their generalizability. By incorporating attention-based mechanisms, our approach overcomes these limitations, providing a more robust solution for misinformation detection.

The comparative analysis underscores the effectiveness of integrating advanced NLP techniques with traditional classification methodologies. While feature-based models excel in simplicity and computational efficiency, they cannot capture deeper semantic relationships. Hybrid deep learning models offer robust performance but may introduce challenges related to interpretability and resource demands.
Our approach strikes a balance by combining high accuracy with computational feasibility and adaptability.
These findings validate the proposed pipeline as a comprehensive tool for detecting and analyzing health-related misinformation. The approach proves to be adaptable for various datasets and domains by maintaining high classification accuracy and providing detailed thematic insights. Moreover, the methodology’s scalability and modular design allow for its application beyond the specific context of COVID-19, making it a versatile solution for addressing misinformation in diverse areas of public health and beyond.
A limitation of our research is its focus solely on textual data, excluding the increasingly prevalent multimodal misinformation that combines text with images, videos, or other media formats. As digital misinformation evolves, addressing such complex content is essential for ensuring detection models’ broader applicability and robustness. Future work should extend the approach to handle multimodal data effectively for comprehensive misinformation detection.
6. Conclusions This study presents a comprehensive framework for detecting and analyzing COVID-19-related misinformation using advanced machine learning and NLP techniques. The proposed classification model achieved exceptional performance, with an accuracy of 99.47% and an F1-score of 0.9947, demonstrating its ability to identify false information reliably. Furthermore, the integration of topic modeling revealed well-defined thematic clusters, offering insights into misinformation narratives and facilitating targeted public health interventions.
The scientific novelty of this research lies in its integration of deep contextual embeddings with attention-enhanced sequential learning, significantly improving classification accuracy and interpretability. By combining BERT embeddings with Bi-LSTM and an optimized attention mechanism, the study advances misinformation detection beyond existing approaches, achieving state-of-the-art performance while ensuring contextual depth in model predictions. Additionally, incorporating advanced topic modeling with BERTopic, fine-tuned UMAP dimensionality reduction, and hierarchical clustering enables a dual-layer analysis, identifying misinformation with high precision and unravelling its thematic structures and dissemination patterns. This holistic framework sets a new benchmark for misinformation detection systems, emphasizing the critical need for interpretability and narrative analysis in combating digital misinformation at scale.
Practically, the proposed methodology provides a scalable and adaptable solution for analyzing misinformation across various domains and datasets, with potential applications in public health communication and digital information governance. The modular design ensures flexibility, extending the framework to other contexts beyond the COVID-19 pandemic.
Future research should address the limitation of focusing solely on textual data by exploring the integration of multimodal inputs, such as images and videos, to enhance the framework’s capability to detect complex forms of misinformation. Expanding the methodology to multilingual and cross-cultural datasets will further improve its applicability in diverse settings, ensuring its utility as a comprehensive tool for combating misinformation.
