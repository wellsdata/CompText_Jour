1. Introduction Truth is the lifeblood of journalism and the cornerstone of media credibility [1]. However, with the rapid development of information technology, fake news has become a serious threat that distorts public cognition, misleads opinions, and undermines social stability, ranging from influencing election outcomes to fuelling distrust and panic during pandemics [2,3]. These harms are self-evident, making fake news detection an increasingly prominent and critical area of research [4,5].
Recent studies have analysed the textual content, focusing on lexical and semantic features. Moorthy et al. proposed a dual-stream graph-enhanced transformer model that integrates RoBERTa with graph neural networks [6]. By introducing propagation structure features in addition to semantic representations, their method achieved joint modelling of content and context, thereby improving detection accuracy. Moreover, Kuntur et al. conducted systematic comparisons between graph neural networks and transformer models and reported that transformer-based textual models perform remarkably well across multiple public datasets, highlighting the strong ability of transformers to detect single-text fake news [7]. In addition, Rout proposed an attention-enhanced transformer variant that further optimized semantic modelling and demonstrated strong stability and generalizability in multitask scenarios [8]. Nevertheless, despite substantial progress in terms of accuracy and robustness, most existing studies have focused on semantic optimization and context fusion while paying inadequate attention to narrative frames and organizational structures inherent in news writing as a distinct genre.
Indeed, the deceptive influence of fake news often stems not from fabrication but from the manipulation of information organization, narrative logic, and structural arrangement [9]. Entman’s case studies demonstrated that framing shapes readers’ perception, comprehension, memory, evaluation, and decision-making. Frame theory emphasizes how media selectively present and constrain narratives [10], and both scholars and journalists recognize framing choices as integral to news production. Each structural pattern reflects a particular mode of information organization and communicative intent [11]. The incorporation of such structural labels allows models to capture the underlying rhetorical strategies of news writing [12,13]. Consequently, models can learn to discern organizational tactics and textual coherence. A literature review further reveals that six structural frames have been identified for Chinese and ten for English [14]. Moreover, integrating structure into fake news detection offers a promising path to complement existing semantic approaches, which can enhance performance and interpretability [15].
In this paper, we propose structure-guided iterative fusion with a transformer network (SIFTNet). The contributions of SIFTNet are summarized as follows: (1) . We propose a structural label annotator based on prompt engineering. Qwen2.5 is used to construct prompts for the Chinese and English datasets, generating frame labels for both languages. Manual corrections are applied to these labels to ensure data quality.
(2) . We propose a structure-guided iterative fusion mechanism based on frame theory. Our new frame integrates structural information and fine-grained labelling for information architecture into traditional semantic modules, improving the modelling of potential structural features. This enables the encoder to adapt to various structures dynamically, enhancing its ability to capture global narrative organization.
(3) . Experiments on Twitter (X) and Weibo21 demonstrate the effectiveness of SIFTNet and validate the efficacy of applying framing theory to improve the detection of fake news.

2. Related Theories 2.1. Traditional Fake News Detection With the rapid development of social media, the spread of fake news has become a major research concern for experts and scholars, especially in the field of information content detection, where substantial progress has been made. Early research has focused mainly on text-based detection methods.
With the advancement of deep learning technology, methods based on pretrained language models (PLMs) have made remarkable progress in the field of fake news detection [16]. Among them, Bidirectional Encoder Representations from Transformers (BERT) is widely used for various text classification tasks, including true and fake news recognition, because of its strong semantic modelling ability [17,18]. The BERT architecture models context through a bidirectional transformer structure [19], which enables it to capture semantic relationships and improve the accuracy of text representation, thereby achieving superior classification performance on multiple datasets.
Overall, in recent years, research on fake news detection based on deep learning, especially pretrained language models [20], has developed rapidly, with research hotspots focused on transfer learning, small sample modelling, multimodal fusion, knowledge enhancement, and model lightweighting [21]. These achievements have collectively advanced the practical development of fake news detection technology and laid a solid foundation for subsequent research. Although existing methods have made major breakthroughs in semantic features, systematic modelling of the unique frame structure and writing logic of news as a narrative genre is still lacking. Therefore, future research should explore how to integrate discourse structural features with deep semantic representations from the perspective of news frames to further improve the accuracy and interpretability of fake news detection.
2.2. Framing Theory Entman’s research case shows that frames can influence readers’ perception, understanding, memory, evaluations, and decisions regarding the presented information. Framing theory focuses on the selective and limited narrative of media through the presentation of reports [22]. Scholars and journalists believe that frame selection is both a critical component of news work and an inevitable fact in the public communication process. Multiple methods have been developed for detecting frames, such as Liu et al.’s fine-tuning of the BERT large language model to recognize frames based on news headlines [23]. The news structure in terms of content includes six commonly used content structures in news reporting: the inverted pyramid, pyramid, a combination of inverted pyramid and pyramid, story style, prose style, and three-dimensional style [24]. Each news structure reflects an intent behind information organization and dissemination [25]. Introducing these structural labels enables the model to capture the writing motivation behind the news, allowing it to learn organizational strategies and use structural integrity to distinguish information.
2.3. Iterative Fusion Mechanism Guided by Structure In the task of identifying fake news, the transformer architecture has been widely proven to have strong text modelling capabilities [26], especially in capturing local and global contexts [27]. However, this architecture often lacks the ability to model the organizational form of high-level text structures. As a highly structured genre, news texts often follow a frame structure that reflects writing motivation and information organization logic, which are highly important for discerning the authenticity of news. Therefore, this study introduces a structure-guided iterative fusion mechanism that encodes structural labels as embedded vectors, guiding the language model to iteratively integrate its internal structure and ultimately form a semantic representation with organizational structure awareness. The core idea of this mechanism is to use explicit news structure types as auxiliary information and enable deep interaction with the semantic representations produced by the DeBERTa encoder [28], thereby guiding the model to focus on discriminative semantic segments in the text in a structure-sensitive manner, thus addressing the limitations of the existing model’s structural perception ability.
3. Research Methodology To improve the performance of fake news detection by combining the news structure, we propose SIFTNet. As shown in Figure 1, we decompose SIFTNet into four modules: (1) The prompt-driven structural label generator automatically generates the corresponding structural frame label for each news text. (2) The information architecture representation module provides fine-grained labels for the news frame across four dimensions. (3) The structure-enhanced representation module treats the structural type and fine-grained labelling for the information architecture as a global guiding signal. (4) The structure-guided iterative fusion module integrates structural label information into the token-level representations produced by the backbone semantic encoder. The final representation is used for classification, yielding a prediction of whether the news is real or fake.
3.1. Prompt-Driven Structural Label Generator In this module, we design high-quality prompts for automatically assigning structural labels to news. On the basis of existing research, we identify 10 structural types in English and 6 in Chinese. In English, the structural typology includes inverted pyramids, pyramids, chronological structures, hourglass structures, kebab structures, accordion structures, nut graph structures, Christmas tree structures, organic structures, and five-box frameworks. Chinese structures include inverted pyramids, pyramids, combined inverted pyramids and pyramids, three-dimensional structures, story structures, and prose structures. This difference reflects established journalism research traditions: English news writing has a richer structural typology developed through Western journalism education, while Chinese journalism research identifies fewer but broader structural categories aligned with Chinese news writing conventions.
The structural label generation process consists of two stages: automated LLM-based labeling and manual correction. For automated labeling, we use Qwen2.5 with decoding parameters set to t e m p e r a t u r e = 0.1 , t o p p = 0.9 , and m a x t o k e n s = 512 . The low-temperature setting ensures deterministic and consistent label outputs.
The English prompt is as follows:
“You are an expert in news structure analysis. Based on framing theory, identify the structural type of the following news article. Choose from these 10 categories: inverted pyramid, pyramid, chronological structure, hourglass structure, kebab structure, accordion structure, nut graph structure, Christmas tree structure, organic structure, or five-box framework. Provide only the structural type name. News article: [text].”
The Chinese prompt is as follows:
“你是一位新闻结构分析专家。请根据框架理论, 识别以下新闻文章的结构类型。从以下6种类型中选择: 倒金字塔、金字塔、倒金字塔与金字塔结合式、立体交叉式、故事式、散文式。仅输出结构类型名称。新闻文章: [text].”
For manual correction, three graduate students majoring in journalism and communication served as annotators. All annotators received training on framing theory and the annotation guidelines prior to the task. The correction followed a three-step protocol: (a) each annotator independently reviewed all LLM-generated labels; (b) labels unanimously agreed upon by all three annotators were accepted; (c) labels with disagreement were resolved through group discussion to reach consensus. Fleiss’ Kappa was computed across the three annotators, yielding κ = 0.847 for the English dataset and κ = 0.863 for the Chinese dataset, indicating strong agreement. Among the LLM-generated labels, 12.3% on the English dataset and 8.7% on the Chinese dataset were manually corrected. The lower correction rate for Chinese is likely due to the fewer number of structural categories, which simplifies the classification task.

This ensures that the model can operate in a fully automated manner for real-time fake news detection.
3.2. Information Architecture Representation Module This module is designed with a coarse-to-fine hierarchical strategy, which progressively refines high-level discourse structure labels by incorporating four dimensions of information architecture features. Specifically, these features include hierarchical complexity, redundancy, positional novelty, and structural integrity.
Semantic unit segmentation: The text is partitioned into a set of paragraphs or semantic units denoted as P = { p 1 , p 2 , … , p m } . Semantic units are defined using a sentence-level sliding window with a window size of 3 sentences and a stride of 2 sentences. Each segment is treated as a semantic unit p j .
The corresponding probability distribution of information flow across these units is defined as follows: (3) P p j = f r e q ( p j ) ∑ k = 1 m f r e q ( p k )
where f r e q ( p j ) denotes the occurrence frequency of salient information fragments within unit p j . Salient information fragments are defined as named entities, numerical expressions, and domain-specific keywords extracted using the spaCy v3.5 natural language processing toolkit.
Complexity H : Entropy serves as a quantitative measure of the uncertainty and complexity inherent in the overall content: (4) H X = − ∑ J = 1 M P ( p j ) log P ( p j )

Higher entropy values indicate a more uniform distribution of information across the text, suggesting complex and diverse content organization.
In addition, we further compute the structural features of redundancy R ( X ) , positional novelty F ( X ) , and integrity I ( X ) as follows: (5) R X = 1 − H X log m ,   F X = 1 m ∑ j = 1 m exp − ∆ t j / τ ,   I X = p j ∈ P : c o r e p j = 1 m
where ∆ t j denotes the temporal difference between unit p j and the news publication time, τ is a temporal decay hyperparameter, and c o r e p j = 1 indicates whether the unit contains core facts.
Redundancy R : A larger R signifies greater redundancy or information overlap across units.
Positional novelty F : A higher F value reflects a greater concentration of novel information in earlier positions.
Integrity I : The indicator I specifies whether the unit contains core facts. Core facts are defined as sentences containing at least one named entity and one factual claim.
Normalization: All features are normalized to the [0, 1] range using min-max normalization before concatenation.

4. Experiments 4.1. Experimental Design 4.1.1. Datasets and Evaluation Metrics To evaluate the effectiveness of SIFTNet, we used Twitter (X) and Weibo21 datasets. The Twitter (X) dataset contains 11,766 news items, with 7207 labelled as fake and 4559 labelled as real. The Weibo21 dataset consists of 9128 news items, including 4640 fake and 4488 real news items. Following established protocols [29], we randomly split each dataset into 80% for training and 20% for testing and reported the average results for accuracy (Acc.), precision (P.), recall (R.), and F1 score (F1).
To ensure statistical reliability, all experiments were repeated across five different random seeds (42, 123, 456, 789, 2024). We report the mean values with standard deviations for accuracy (Acc.), precision (P.), recall (R.), and F1 score (F1). Paired t -tests were conducted between SIFTNet and each baseline to assess statistical significance at p < 0.05.
To mitigate potential data leakage from topic overlap, we also designed a category-stratified split for the Weibo21 dataset, ensuring proportional representation of all nine news categories (politics, military, education, technology, finance, health, entertainment, disaster, social life) in both training and test sets. We acknowledge that this does not fully eliminate event-level overlap, which we note as a limitation for future work.
In this study, to construct a high-quality training dataset with news structural labels, a semiautomatic annotation method combining large language model-driven prompt-based learning and manual fine-tuning was employed to generate structural labels for the Weibo21 dataset of Chinese news and the Twitter (X) dataset of English news. This approach exhibits zero-shot generalization capabilities, enabling adaptation to news texts of diverse types, genres, and styles while providing reliable label support for introducing structural hierarchical features in subsequent fake news modelling.
4.1.2. Experimental Settings We employed DeBERTa-v3 to extract 1024-dimensional semantic features. Structural and semantic features were fused using a 4-head attention mechanism (hidden size of 1024) with layer normalization. The model was trained for 10 epochs, with a batch size of 16 and an initial learning rate of 3 × 10 −5 . We applied a 10% warm-up proportion, evaluated the model every 50 steps, and enabled FP16 mixed-precision training. The experiments were conducted using five random seeds (42, 123, 456, 789, 2024), and the mean and standard deviation are reported.
4.1.3. Baselines To verify the effectiveness of SIFTNet, we choose models involving different features as baselines. These include the SVM-TS [30], GRU-based model [31], CNN-based model [32], FCN-LP [33], MDG [34,35], DG [35] and a DeBERTa-v3 text-only baseline.
The DeBERTa-v3 baseline uses the identical encoder under the same training conditions (same hyperparameters, same data splits, same seeds) but without any structural modules, serving to isolate the contribution of the proposed structure-guided iterative fusion from the backbone encoder’s inherent capability.
4.1.4. Experimental Results As shown in Table 1, the experimental results on Twitter (X) and Weibo21 demonstrate that our proposed SIFTNet achieves substantial improvements on both datasets (all values reported as mean ± std over five seeds). Notably, SIFTNet outperforms the DeBERTa-v3 text-only baseline by a mean improvement of 3.20% (95.87 ± 0.24 vs. 92.67 ± 0.31) in accuracy on Twitter (X) and 1.98% (94.52 ± 0.22 vs. 92.54 ± 0.28) on Weibo21, confirming that the performance gains are attributable to the proposed structure-guided iterative fusion module rather than merely the DeBERTa-v3 encoder. All improvements over baselines are statistically significant at p < 0.05 based on paired t -tests.
The results reveal that structural patterns in news texts significantly affect detection performance. By effectively leveraging latent propagation and narrative structures, SIFTNet dynamically adapts its fusion strategies to varying news types, thereby enhancing its detection effectiveness. Overall, SIFTNet increases its detection capability by effectively modelling framing pattern features that distinguish fake news from real news.
4.2. Ablation Experiment To validate the effectiveness of the SIFTNet model, we conducted independent ablation experiments to evaluate the specific contribution of the structure-guided iterative fusion mechanism to the performance improvement of the SIFTNet method. In each experiment, we removed news structure labels, assigned random structure labels, shuffled real structure labels, assigned a unified structure label, and restarted model training.
We evaluated each component by sequentially removing structural, complexity, redundancy, novelty, and integrity labels. As shown in Table 2, the main findings were as follows: (1) . Structural features enhance fake news detection, yielding mean accuracy gains of approximately 2.3% on Weibo21 and 4.3% on Twitter (X) over five seeds.
(2) . SIFTNet is sensitive to the quality of structural labels. Incorrect labels lead to performance degradation, especially on the English dataset.
(3) . The absence of structural features is more detrimental than noisy structural information.
(4) . A comparison of different language datasets further indicates that structural features contribute more to English news detection because of the more standardized organization in English news writing style.
(5) . The label validity ablation reveals a clear performance hierarchy: real labels > shuffled labels ≈ unified labels > random labels. Random structural labels produce the largest performance drop, confirming that the model leverages meaningful structural information rather than label artifacts. Shuffled labels (real labels randomly reassigned) and unified labels (all items receive the same label) produce similar moderate degradation, suggesting that the model benefits from both the correspondence between text and structure and the discriminative power of diverse structural types. These results validate that structural labels provide genuine semantic guidance rather than serving as mere additional parameters.

4.3. Robustness Testing We evaluated the ability of SIFTNet to resist interference and adversarial manipulation in social media platforms. Specifically, we injected Gaussian noise. As shown in Figure 2, these results demonstrate the stability and reliability of SIFTNet in complex social media contexts and the potential of structural-aware frames for the strong detection of fake news.
In addition to Gaussian noise injection, we designed seven realistic perturbation strategies that better reflect the ways in which fake news may be manipulated: (1) . Paraphrasing: Using a T5-based paraphrase model, each sentence in the news text was rewritten while preserving its original semantics.
(2) . Sentence reordering: The order of sentences within each news article was randomly shuffled to disrupt the structural coherence.
(3) . Lead sentence/paragraph removal: The first sentence (for Twitter/X tweets, which are typically single-paragraph posts) or the first paragraph (for Weibo21 articles) was removed, which is particularly detrimental to inverted pyramid and nut graph structures where the most critical information appears at the beginning.
(4) . Title–body mismatch: The original title was replaced with a misleading title that contradicts the body text. Note: This perturbation applies only to Weibo21 articles, as Twitter/X tweets do not have separate titles.
(5) . Emotional sentence insertion: Emotionally charged sentences were injected at random positions within the text to test whether the model can be distracted by emotional content.
(6) . Typographical noise: Random character-level perturbations (substitution, deletion, and insertion) were introduced at a 5% rate to simulate typographical errors.
(7) . Adversarial framing change: The structural label was replaced with an incorrect label during inference to simulate adversarial attacks on the structural guidance mechanism.

As shown in Table 3, SIFTNet maintains robust performance under most perturbations (all values reported as mean ± std over five seeds). The largest mean degradation was observed under lead sentence/paragraph removal (−3.10 ± 0.11% accuracy on Twitter/X, −2.22 ± 0.09% on Weibo21) and adversarial framing changes (−2.78 ± 0.10% accuracy on Twitter/X), which is expected given the model’s reliance on structural cues. Paraphrasing and typographical noise produced minimal impact (<1% mean accuracy drop), demonstrating the model’s resilience to surface-level text modifications. Sentence reordering and emotional sentence insertion caused moderate degradation (1.5–2.0%), confirming that structural coherence and emotional neutrality are important but not sole determinants of the model’s performance.
4.4. Qualitative Analysis To gain a deeper understanding of the impact mechanism of structural guidance strategies on fake news detection, we conducted a qualitative analysis from multiple dimensions, including concrete case studies and attention visualizations. Due to the tweet-length nature of the Twitter (X) dataset, the English case study presents a simplified attention analysis, whereas the Chinese Weibo21 dataset allows for multi-sentence analysis.
Case Study 1: Correct prediction—English fake tweet with pyramid structure. Consider the following fake tweet from the Twitter (X) dataset (ID: 383708778954113024): “Bush reading ‘Pet Goat’ on 9/11—Notice: book is UPSIDE DOWN!?—ALSO SEE this picture.” Despite its brevity, the tweet follows a pyramid structure, building from an observation to an insinuating conclusion that implies conspiracy. SIFTNet correctly classified this tweet as fake news (confidence: 0.93), while the DeBERTa-v3 text-only baseline misclassified it as real (confidence: 0.61). The attention heatmap (Figure 3a) reveals that SIFTNet’s attention builds progressively toward the concluding insinuation, guided by the pyramid structural label. In contrast, the text-only baseline distributes attention uniformly and fails to capture the escalating rhetorical structure.
Case Study 2: Correct prediction—Chinese fake news with story structure. A Chinese fake news article from the Weibo21 dataset (ID: 4609614811500400, category: Education) with a story structure was correctly identified. The article follows a narrative arc of “beginning, development, turn, conclusion”: a sponsor describes feeling heavyhearted that a student they funded for four years blocked them (beginning), explains the context of the student’s graduation and job search (development), reveals the emotional turning point of being blocked (turn), and reflects on the unpredictability of human nature (conclusion). As shown in Figure 3b, SIFTNet’s attention pattern exhibits a characteristic hill-shaped trajectory—attention is low during the opening, rises through development, peaks at the narrative turn, and descends during the conclusion. This aligns with the observation that fake news employing story structures tends to introduce emotionally manipulative claims at the narrative turning point.
Case Study 3: Incorrect prediction—Error propagation through structural mislabeling. We also analyzed a case where SIFTNet fails. A real Chinese news article from the Weibo21 dataset (ID: 4553685051379152, category: Military) with an inverted pyramid structure was misclassified as fake news. The article opens with its strongest claim about political provocation, then progressively provides supporting arguments, evidence, and conclusions—following the classic inverted pyramid pattern of decreasing information importance. As shown in Figure 3c, error analysis reveals that the structural label generator incorrectly assigned a “story” label instead of “inverted pyramid.” This mislabeling caused the model to seek a narrative turning point that does not exist, producing a misplaced attention peak at mid-text while neglecting the strong opening claim. This case illustrates the importance of accurate structural labeling and highlights a limitation of the current approach.
Attention Visualization: Figure 3 presents attention heatmaps for all three case studies—Figure 3a (Twitter pyramid), Figure 3b (Weibo21 story), and Figure 3c (Weibo21 mislabeling error)—each comparing SIFTNet with the DeBERTa-v3 text-only baseline. The visualizations clearly show that structural guidance leads to more focused and interpretable attention patterns: SIFTNet’s attention concentrates on structurally important segments (concluding claims, turning points, core arguments), while the baseline model’s attention is more diffuse and susceptible to distraction by surface-level features.
These qualitative findings and quantitative results corroborate each other, collectively indicating that: (1) the structural guidance strategy enables the model to capture the deep organizational logic of news rather than only surface language features; (2) the mechanism of structural information varies depending on the type of news and language and cultural background; and (3) incorrect structural information can generate systematic bias through attention mechanisms. These insights provide valuable perspectives on the model’s decision-making process, pointing to ways for improving structure-aware fake news detection systems in the future.
4.5. Cross Language Discussion Cross-lingual Structural Differences. A notable finding is the difference in the number of structural types between English (10 types) and Chinese (6 types) datasets. This discrepancy arises from distinct journalism research traditions: English news writing has developed a richer structural typology (including hourglass, kebab, accordion, nut graph, Christmas tree, and five-box frameworks) through Western journalism education, while Chinese journalism research identifies broader structural categories—inverted pyramid, pyramid, combined inverted pyramid and pyramid, three-dimensional, story, prose—that align with Chinese news writing conventions.
The structural embedding matrix E s has dimensions × d , where K is the number of structural types. Since K differs across languages, the embedding spaces are language-specific and not directly aligned. This is by design: each language has its own structural typology, and the model is trained separately for each language. The model architecture itself is language-agnostic—only the structural labels and their embeddings are language-specific. For future cross-lingual transfer, one could explore mapping structural types from different languages into a shared conceptual space based on functional similarity (e.g., the English and Chinese share similar functional roles under “inverted pyramid”).
Contribution of Structural Features. The experimental results consistently show that structural features contribute more to English news detection (+4.3% mean accuracy) than to Chinese news detection (+2.3%). We attribute this to the more standardized and rigid organizational patterns in English news writing, which makes structural deviations more detectable. Chinese news writing tends to be more flexible in structure, reducing the discriminative power of structural features. Nevertheless, the consistent improvements on both datasets validate the universal applicability of framing theory in fake news detection.
5. Conclusions In this article, a model called the SIFTNet is proposed. Its core idea is to encode structural labels as embedding vectors, guiding the internal structure iteration and fusion of the language model and ultimately forming a semantic representation with organizational structure awareness, thereby filling the gap in the existing model’s structural perception ability. Multiple experiments conducted on two publicly available datasets, Twitter (X) and Weibo21, with the addition of news frame tags, showed that the model is highly effective. The SIFTNet frame is used for fake news detection and can dynamically embed structures from different languages. Guided by framing theory, SIFTNet captures semantics at both the local sentence level and global structure level, demonstrating excellent accuracy and robustness.
We acknowledge several limitations of the current work. First, the manual correction step in structural label annotation, while ensuring label quality, introduces a scalability concern for real-time applications. Our preliminary analysis suggests that uncorrected LLM-generated labels produce only a modest accuracy drop (approximately 0.9%), indicating that advances in LLM capabilities may progressively reduce the need for manual correction. Second, the current evaluation is limited to two datasets; future work should extend to additional languages and datasets. Third, the structural typology is language-specific, and cross-lingual structural transfer remains an open challenge. Fourth, adversarial framing changes (incorrect structural labels during inference) cause notable performance degradation, indicating that the model’s robustness to structural label noise can be further improved.
