WHAT IS ALREADY KNOWN ON THIS TOPIC
- Demand for a future HIV vaccine may be susceptible to misinformation, as seen in previous vaccine rollouts, yet few studies have systematically examined the specific concerns that influence vaccine confidence and uptake.


WHAT THIS STUDY ADDS
- This structured review identified key concerns that could shape future HIV vaccine demand and confidence, including safety, fear of infection, stigma, risk compensation, conspiracy theories and access. These findings characterise emerging narratives that may fuel misinformation and influence the success of a future vaccine rollout.


HOW THIS STUDY MIGHT AFFECT RESEARCH, PRACTICE OR POLICY
- Understanding the specific concerns and misinformation surrounding an HIV vaccine can guide research priorities, inform public health communication and support policies that strengthen vaccine confidence.


Introduction
The development of an effective HIV vaccine would be a major breakthrough in global efforts to curb the HIV epidemic, particularly in high-prevalence regions and for high-risk groups. 1 Despite large-scale investment in prevention and treatment, the persistent burden of HIV suggests that new biomedical innovations, including an HIV vaccine, will be essential in reducing new infections. 2 Advances in gene-editing technologies illustrate the rapidly expanding frontiers of HIV prevention and treatment. 3 In parallel, mRNA vaccine platforms, successfully applied during the COVID-19 pandemic, are being explored for HIV, offering the potential for scalable, rapid and highly adaptable vaccine design. Persistent challenges with highly active antiretroviral therapy, including drug resistance, long-term toxicity and treatment fatigue, further reinforce the urgency of preventive strategies like vaccines to achieve meaningful and sustained reductions in HIV incidence. 4 5
However, the impact of an HIV vaccine will depend on widespread uptake, particularly among groups that have the highest risk of acquiring HIV. Prior studies have shown that low uptake of vaccines and biomedical prevention, ranging from influenza and COVID-19 vaccines to pre-exposure prophylaxis (PrEP) for HIV prevention, can limit the effectiveness of promising health innovations. 6 7 For example, despite offering greater than 90% protection from HIV, PrEP uptake and continuation among adolescent girls and young women in sub-Saharan Africa has been low due to myriad barriers ranging from stigma to pill-taking burden to misinformation, thus limiting its population health impact. 8 The same challenges are likely to apply to a future HIV vaccine, making it critical to anticipate key factors that could undermine confidence in and demand for the vaccine. Following the literature, we define vaccine demand as a person’s willingness to get a specific vaccine, along with their ability and intention to do so, based on the vaccine’s characteristics, availability, reputation and cost. 9 We define vaccine confidence as trust in the importance, safety and effectiveness of vaccines, as well as compatibility of vaccines with religious and personal beliefs and identities. 10
A key driver of poor vaccine demand and poor confidence is misinformation that individuals are exposed to while making vaccination decisions. The COVID-19 pandemic highlighted how misinformation about vaccine safety, effectiveness and importance can significantly reduce vaccine uptake. 11 Early research suggests that demand for a future HIV vaccine may be similarly vulnerable to misinformation. Given the historical and sociocultural factors that shape perceptions of health interventions, it is imperative to understand how vaccine concerns generally, and misinformation specifically, may affect future HIV vaccine demand.
Several studies have explored general attitudes toward HIV vaccines, primarily among research participants in HIV vaccine trials. 12 13 The biomedical attributes of an HIV vaccine, such as potential level of efficacy, duration of effect or side effects, may shape public perceptions in ways that are not yet fully understood. Furthermore, while past research on HIV vaccine trial participation provides some insights into emerging concerns about vaccine safety, these findings may not reflect the full range of concerns that exist outside of trial settings.
To address this knowledge gap, we searched the scientific and grey literature for beliefs and claims, which we refer to as ‘concerns’, that may shape demand for and confidence in a future HIV vaccine. We distinguish between concerns and misinformation in this paper, though the boundaries are not always clear and there is often overlap in how they are expressed in literature. Concerns reflect genuine questions or hesitations shaped by lived experiences, past interactions with health services or associations with previous vaccines. Whereas, misinformation refers to false claims about vaccines, such as incorrect statements about safety, efficacy or intent. While concerns highlight gaps in trust and communication which may potentially turn to misinformation if unaddressed, misinformation requires active correction to prevent negative impacts on vaccine uptake.
Many studies have examined HIV vaccine acceptability and demand, but few have systematically explored the specific concerns and misinformation that shape these attitudes. In characterising vaccine concerns, we sought to identify early signals that may be amplified in public discourse and on social media in ways that influence HIV vaccine demand and confidence. We move beyond general acceptability to map emerging concerns, highlighting patterns that could undermine vaccine uptake. Our results can inform public health communication to reduce the impact of misinformation on HIV vaccine uptake and provide actionable insights to pre-emptively address misinformation and strengthen vaccine confidence.
Methods
Literature search
We conducted a structured search of peer-reviewed and grey literature to identify beliefs and concerns about the HIV vaccine. For the peer-reviewed literature, we used a comprehensive set of search terms, including “HIV vaccine knowledge”, “HIV vaccine trials”, “HIV vaccine perceptions”, “HIV vaccine myths”, “HIV vaccine fake news”, “HIV vaccine misinformation”, “HIV vaccine hesitancy”, “attitude toward HIV vaccine”, “conspiracy beliefs”, “infodemic” and “infodemiology”. To capture relevant grey literature, we conducted general searches using terms such as “HIV vaccine concerns”, “HIV vaccine myths” and “HIV vaccine fake information” on Google. We then categorised concerns into topics and subtopics. The sample included all relevant scientific and grey literature in English, not limited by region or year of publication. We included empirical studies, literature reviews, theoretical articles, fact sheets and unpublished educational and policy materials. Our search followed the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta Analyses checklist and reporting guideline. 14 We used the Sample, Phenomenon of Interest, Design, Evaluation, Research type methodology to select relevant sources of information, including published and grey literature, to meet the objectives of this analysis. The topic of interest was HIV vaccine concerns that would be expected to reduce vaccine demand and vaccine confidence.
The literature search was conducted across multiple electronic databases, including PubMed, Scopus and Google Scholar, to ensure a broad coverage of relevant studies. We also conducted a Google search to identify grey literature and unpublished sources such as fact sheets, reports and HIV vaccine trial news. The title and abstract of articles and documents identified through the search were initially screened for relevance by one author (SP-W). Sources were included if they mentioned HIV vaccine concerns.
Data extraction
A second author (SB) reviewed the full text of included sources to identify and extract concerns related to the HIV vaccine. These concerns were entered in a structured database. For each concern, we recorded the source; publication date and the content—either as a verbatim quote or summarised in the reviewer’s own words. Where available, demographic or regional details were also recorded (eg, whether the concern was specific to a gender, age group or country). All the concerns were included in the database, including duplicates or near-duplicates, to ensure a comprehensive inventory and to reduce bias in the extraction or selection of claims.
Content analysis
A codebook of a priori codes was developed by the first author based on the research team’s expertise in HIV prevention and vaccine acceptance. The first author (SB) coded the concerns database using NVivo V.12, with flexibility to allow for new codes to emerge during the process. Following the initial coding process, the research team iteratively reviewed coded content, developing and refining content topics and subtopics. The final content schema was summarised by the number of concerns, and illustrative concerns were selected for each topic.
Patient and public involvement
Patients or the public were not involved in the design, or conduct, or reporting, or dissemination plans of our research.
Results
Our search identified 594 peer-reviewed articles. Although we did not record specific details from the grey literature search, this search yielded eight additional reports or fact sheets that potentially contained concerns about the HIV vaccine that might shape demand or confidence. Title and abstract screening followed by full-text review left 78 articles and 8 reports containing HIV vaccine concerns ( figure 1 ).

From these sources, we extracted 333 concerns, reflecting issues raised globally rather than being confined to any specific region. Content analysis of coded concerns revealed seven key topics ( table 1 ): (1) HIV vaccine safety, focusing on concerns about potential side effects; (2) Concerns about HIV-positive status, specifically the belief that the vaccine could result in a positive HIV diagnosis; (3) Stigma and social concerns, highlighting fears of judgement or discrimination associated with getting vaccinated; (4) Behavioural implications, including worries that the HIV vaccine might encourage unsafe or risky behaviours; (5) Distrust and conspiracy theories, encompassing scepticism toward governments and scientists as well as vaccine-related conspiracies; (6) Efficacy and effectiveness, questioning whether the HIV vaccine would be beneficial or ‘worth it’ and (7) Access and availability, addressing issues around eligibility, equitable distribution and ease of access to the vaccine. The topics are described in more detail below.

We further examined the year of publication for all claims included in the analysis, and found that most (283 claims) originated from sources published before the COVID-19 pandemic (2000–2018), while comparatively fewer (50) were drawn from 2019 to 2023. Despite this difference in distribution, all seven misinformation topics identified were represented across both periods, and no new topics were unique to either time period.
HIV vaccine safety
We identified 92 concerns related to the safety of the HIV vaccine, including doubts about the vaccine’s unproven track record and scepticism about clinical trials that ‘use human beings as guinea pigs’ to test vaccine safety. 15 16 Safety concerns extended to worries that the HIV vaccine could be lethal. 17–19 These concerns were exacerbated by the fact that there is no approved HIV vaccine yet, implying that an ‘experimental’ vaccine is by definition unsafe.
Other concerns related to vaccine safety centred on potential side effects, adverse reactions and long-term health risks associated with vaccination. 19–21 Key side effects included concerns that the HIV vaccine: (1) causes autism; (2) is associated with reproductive harm among women and (3) causes general side effects including pain at the injection site, skin discolouration and weakening of the immune system.
HIV vaccine causing HIV infection
While concerns about HIV vaccines causing HIV infection are, by definition, safety concerns, we treated them as a separate topic due to their prevalence in the literature. Multiple sources reported fears that the HIV vaccine could lead to HIV infection or a positive HIV test, often linked to perceived exposure to virus particles within the vaccine. 20 22 23 Studies conducted among potential HIV vaccine trial participants and qualitative studies reporting on individuals’ vaccine perceptions showed that some had a general awareness that vaccines might involve exposure to a virus or viral components. However, the perception that the vaccine could directly cause HIV infection was a distinct concern common in studies of HIV vaccine trial participation. 24 25 While some concerns were related to the safety of vaccines, there were additional concerns that related to seropositivity (seropositivity refers to the presence of vaccine-induced HIV antibodies that may cause a positive result on rapid HIV tests despite the absence of actual infection) highlighting the irreversibility of a confirmed HIV-positive test result and what it would mean socially and sexually to live with an HIV-positive diagnosis, even if caused by vaccination.
Vaccine-related stigma and social concerns
Concerns about vaccine-related stigma included fears that individuals who receive the vaccine are HIV-positive. 26 27 Some concerns were expressed that getting vaccinated would be associated with engagement in high-risk sexual behaviours. 28 29 Additionally, some individuals feared that receiving the vaccine might lead to assumptions about their sexual orientation or their belonging to populations that face a high risk of HIV acquisition. This included concerns that they would be perceived as gay men or other men who have sex with men, sex workers or people who inject drugs. 16 30
HIV vaccine behavioural implications
Concerns were identified that the HIV vaccine would promote risky sexual behaviours or alter recipients’ engagement in other healthy behaviours. For example, some feared it may reduce utilisation of HIV testing services. 24 31 32 There were also concerns that the vaccine might weaken women’s ability to negotiate safe sex practices with male partners, potentially limiting their control over condom use and other protective measures. 29
Distrust and conspiracy theories
Several concerns highlighted distrust of governments and scientists that shape perceptions about a future HIV vaccine. There was scepticism about the intentions behind vaccine trials, especially reasons for conducting trials in low-income and middle-income countries rather than in wealthy Western countries. 15 33 34 Concerns were raised that vaccine trials were part of a larger plan to target or harm specific individuals or groups (eg, gay men, Black women), or as a tool for population control, rather than to advance prevention science. 20 35 Separate concerns included the belief that the vaccine and vaccine trials are merely a money-making mechanism for rich countries and pharmaceutical companies. 16 36
HIV vaccine efficacy and comparative effectiveness
Several concerns emerged regarding vaccine effectiveness and efficacy, including suspicion that the HIV vaccine would provide only partial protection from HIV infection. 21 28 Specifically, studies reported frustration with scientists’ inability to confirm that the vaccine would be ‘100% effective’, leading to doubts about its reliability or usefulness as a preventive measure. 22 Other concerns related to whether the HIV virus’s ability to mutate would make it difficult to develop a vaccine that remained effective over time and across different variants. 36 37 Some studies also reported perceptions that an HIV vaccine might not be necessary, as there are already established and effective prevention methods such as PrEP. 38
HIV vaccine access
The final topic identified in the literature focused on the accessibility, availability and eligibility criteria for a future HIV vaccine. 37 39 Concerns centred on the cost of obtaining the vaccine, including claims that the vaccine exists already but is only accessible to the very wealthy. Related concerns included equitable distribution and availability. Questions about eligibility to receive the vaccine or to participate in vaccine trials revealed confusion about whether HIV-positive individuals would be eligible for the vaccine. 39 The role of HIV risk in determining HIV vaccine eligibility was also a source of confusion and concern, reflecting broader uncertainties about the vaccine distribution, targeting and accessibility.
Discussion
Our structured literature review, encompassing both published and grey literature, identified multiple concerns related to the HIV vaccine that we might expect to shape future demand and confidence. Our analysis focused on concerns emerging from studies of HIV vaccine clinical trial recruitment and participation globally, as well as more general perceptions about a potential HIV vaccine. The key concerns identified included apprehensions about vaccine safety, fears of HIV infection from the vaccine, vaccine-related stigma, concerns that vaccination might lead to increasingly risky sexual behaviours and reduce motivation to engage in HIV testing services, the influence of conspiracy theories, and overall vaccine access. These topics help characterise the emerging narratives surrounding the HIV vaccine that might fuel misinformation and affect the success of a future rollout when an effective vaccine is developed.
Previous research on vaccine hesitancy showed that public perceptions of safety play a crucial role in vaccine uptake. 40 Worries that the HIV vaccine could cause severe side effects, or that vaccine safety is insufficiently assessed in vaccine trials, contribute to reluctance to vaccinate. The specific concern that the HIV vaccine could induce seropositivity as measured by an HIV test is a telling example of how false or misleading information can be built up around a small nugget of correct information and can also be propelled by the public’s mental models about vaccines. We also identified a number of concerns related to scepticism about clinical trials and distrust of pharmaceutical companies.
Additionally, a recurring concern in the literature is that an HIV vaccine might encourage risky sexual behaviours by creating a false sense of protection, a phenomenon known as risk compensation. This concern has been observed in other public health interventions, including COVID-19 vaccines and PrEP. 41 42 While some studies suggest that expanding prevention options does not necessarily lead to increased risk-taking behaviours, 43 44 these concerns should be carefully considered in future communication campaigns that promote HIV vaccines.
Insights from the COVID-19 vaccine and other vaccines suggest that hesitancy is more pronounced among populations experiencing health disparities. This raises concerns about low vaccine confidence in the very communities that stand to benefit most from an HIV vaccine. 45 While credible communication is essential in addressing hesitancy, it is equally important to consider the sociocultural and structural barriers that influence vaccine uptake. Factors such as perceived safety, trust in healthcare systems, experiences of discrimination, health literacy and access to healthcare play a critical role in shaping the use of health services or products, highlighting the need for comprehensive strategies to ensure vaccine confidence for all. Proactive efforts to counter misinformation must begin well before an HIV vaccine is rolled out to ensure widespread public acceptance and uptake.
The comparison of claims published before and after 2019 showed a consistent pattern in the nature of the HIV vaccine misinformation over time. Although the number of post-2019 sources was smaller, all seven categories of misinformation were represented in both periods, and no new themes emerged that were unique to the post-COVID-19 era. This continuity may suggest that long-standing narratives surrounding vaccines have persisted despite shifts in the global health context. It may also indicate that misinformation tends to recycle familiar concerns such as safety, trust and morality, rather than introduce entirely new topics in response to emerging vaccines. 46 47
In the absence of clear and accessible information about the HIV vaccine, concerns are likely to arise, some of which may represent legitimate uncertainties rather than outright misinformation. The distinction between such concerns and misinformation is often subtle, yet critical, as unaddressed uncertainties can contribute to the spread of misleading narratives. Safeguarding the acceptance and potential impact of a future HIV vaccine requires innovative approaches to countering misinformation effectively. One promising strategy is psychological inoculation, which works in the same way as a vaccine by exposing individuals to weakened forms of misinformation and equipping them with the tools to recognise and resist false claims. 48 49 By pre-emptively addressing misinformation before it takes hold, such approaches have the potential to strengthen public resilience against misleading narratives and support informed decision-making regarding HIV vaccination in the future.
This structured review of HIV vaccine misinformation and concerns comes at a critical time when several HIV vaccine trials have faced setbacks, potentially increasing public scepticism toward a future vaccine. 50 These disappointments may erode confidence in future vaccines more broadly. Yet, promising candidates remain in the pipeline, and an effective HIV vaccine could significantly reduce infections, especially among vulnerable populations. Understanding and addressing misinformation early is therefore crucial. This research provides a foundation for studying HIV vaccine misinformation and exploring strategies, such as ‘inoculation’, to counter false narratives pre-emptively and enhance public readiness for future breakthroughs.
This review has several limitations. First, misinformation evolves rapidly, making it challenging to capture a comprehensive and up-to-date representation of emerging narratives. The dynamic nature of misinformation means that new concerns may arise, potentially limiting the long-term applicability of our findings. We did not restrict our search by publication year; therefore, some concerns reflect older perceptions that may have shifted with advances in HIV prevention and recent vaccine experiences. Nonetheless, in the absence of an approved HIV vaccine, these concerns likely still hold relevance in certain populations. Second, while lessons from COVID-19 and other vaccines provide valuable insights into vaccine misinformation, the unique social and scientific context of an HIV vaccine may present distinct challenges that require tailored strategies. Third, the rapid spread and adaptation of misinformation necessitate continuous monitoring and flexible approaches to address emerging concerns effectively. Fourth, article screening was conducted by a single author, and a second author reviewed the full texts of included sources to extract concerns related to the HIV vaccine. This process may have introduced bias; however, eligibility criteria were applied systematically and results were reviewed with the broader research team. Lastly, this review did not include misinformation or concerns expressed on social media platforms, which play a significant role in shaping individual perceptions. A separate search by our team is exploring these misinformation trends in online public discourse to provide a more comprehensive understanding of their impact on HIV vaccine attitudes.
Conclusions
Addressing misinformation and concerns about an HIV vaccine is crucial to ensuring its future acceptance and effectiveness, particularly given past vaccine trial setbacks and the broader challenges of vaccine hesitancy. This structured review highlights key misinformation topics, all of which can undermine confidence in a future HIV vaccine. The evolving nature of misinformation necessitates proactive strategies, such as inoculation, to build public resilience against false claims. By compiling a comprehensive repository of misinformation and concerns, this study lays important groundwork for developing and testing interventions to counter misinformation and support public readiness. Ultimately, these efforts are essential to realising the full potential of a future HIV vaccine in reducing infections and protecting vulnerable populations.
