Introduction Definition of Fake News Fake news is misinformation or disinformation communicated through word of mouth and traditional media, more recently through digital modes of communication such as videos, memes, unsubstantiated adverts, and social media spread rumors [1]. Fake news propagated through social media has become a serious concern, potentially leading to mob violence and community discord [2, 3]. Although social media platforms have become the most common means of disseminating news and information, they are increasingly being used to spread rumors, fabricated content, and false information, either to ruin someone’s reputation or simply for amusement [4, 5]. “As a senator, Hillary Clinton paid women less than the men in her office.”; “An anonymous e-mail says that Hillary Clinton advocates communism.”; “Hillary Clinton and Barack Obama want to raise taxes on all income brackets” . Such false accusations and hoaxes were intended to tarnish the reputation of well-known political figures during the election season, either for personal benefit or amusement.
Role of Social Media in the Dissemination of Fake News A scientific study published in the peer-reviewed Journal of Medical Internet Research by doctors from Rochester, New York, and Pune, India, seems to provide some insight into the behavior of Indian internet users during the pandemic on WhatsApp, one of the primary sources of COVID-19 misinformation in the country [6]. According to the survey, roughly 30% of Indians accessed WhatsApp for COVID-19 information, and only about half of those who used WhatsApp for COVID-19 information fact-checked fewer than 50% of messages before forwarding them. Even more shocking, 13% of those surveyed admitted that they never fact-checked messages before forwarding them on WhatsApp. However, the analysis showed that a small group was responsible for the majority of forwarding. Only 14% forwarded three or more messages every day and only 5% forwarded nine or more per day. The survey also looked at age groups, finding that those over 65 were more likely to receive misinformation, believe it, and act on it, while those under 25 were the least likely [6]. The survey also indicated that 24–27% of the respondents had considered adopting COVID-19 herbal, ayurvedic, or homeopathic therapies. Approximately 7–8% stated that they had tried them, while 12% said they had considered home cures. Even though an attached link or source reference does not necessarily make a claim authentic, three-quarters of Indians believe that an attachment makes a message more trustworthy. Only one-third of Indians said they trusted messages from known senders more than from unknown senders [6]. During the pandemic, many false claims and rumors about fake remedies, lockdown rumors, and conspiracy theories regarding the virus’s origins were purposely propagated among the already stressed out innocent populace to cause chaos in society [7]. Examples may include: “Mosquitoes can transfer COVID-19 from person to person”; “Japanese doctors claim drinking water every 15 min prevents COVID-19”; “The common flu kills 60 times more people than the coronavirus every year”; “The Chinese government announced that garlic is a preventative food for the novel coronavirus” . Such false claims misled many, causing trouble for the innocent masses. This shows how this malice made the situation worse during the COVID-19 pandemic [8].
During the 2016 US presidential election, an increasing amount of so-called “fake news” disseminating deceptive content or excessively distorting the true news was posted on social media platforms [9, 10]. While misinformation and propaganda have existed since the beginning of time, their impact and power in the age of social media are yet unknown. A thorough look at how real news and fake news spread on Twitter showed that fake news spreads faster and farther than real news. The intake of news, in general, shows a polarization in communities, which corresponds to political affiliation [11]. Recent studies have also demonstrated the significance of bots, or automated accounts, in the dissemination of disinformation [12]. As a result, the propagation of fake news has become much easier, and the problem has gotten worse because many people believe what they read, and many people who are unfamiliar with technology may be easily duped. Shao et al. [13] discovered that during the 2016 US presidential election on Twitter, bots were responsible for the early promotion of misinformation as they targeted influential users through replies and mentions. The sharing of fact-checking articles nearly vanished in the network’s core while social bots proliferated.
Artificial intelligence, deep learning, and machine learning approaches are being investigated to combat the spread of fake news and hoaxes. Transfer learning, which allows us to leverage a pre-trained model and then fine-tune it for downstream tasks, has recently gained popularity in deep learning [14]. Because they are pre-trained on large corpora, these models are significantly more accurate than those constructed from scratch. So, models based on transfer learning that has already been trained, like BERT, can be tweaked to find fake news. Deep learning models can be paired with explainable AI to unwind the black box of models trained with millions or billions of parameters, providing insight into which phrases contribute to claiming a news instance as fake or authentic [15].

Related Research Work Fake News Detection Approaches The tools and approaches for detecting fake news fall into one of two categories: network-based or linguistic-based [16, 17, 18–19]. By evaluating the source’s reliability, network-based algorithms can estimate the truthfulness of news. They focus on either heterogeneous or homogeneous networks and use network features such as authors, timestamps, or included links [20]. Unlike network-based approaches, linguistic-based methods concentrate on the news content under investigation [21]. They look for abnormalities in the text to validate its authenticity, based on the theory that fake news follows certain patterns. For example, an unusually high frequency of specific terms may be a clue to the abnormality of a text under investigation [22]. In linguistic-based approaches, the three most common techniques are machine learning (ML), primitive deep learning, and advanced deep learning-based language models. Algorithms such as logistic regression, support vector machines (SVM), and decision trees are examples of machine learning-based techniques [23, 24]. Algorithms like recurrent neural networks (RNN), convolutional neural networks (CNN), and long short-term memory (LSTM) are primitive deep learning-based approaches [7, 25, 26–27], while advanced deep learning based language models include bidirectional encoder representations from transformers (BERT), distilled version of BERT (DistilBERT), Embeddings from Language Model (ELMo), etc. [8, 14, 22, 28].
Pathwar and Gill [7] worked on a binary classification problem (real or fake). They used the COVID-19 news dataset, which they obtained from several fact-checking websites, such as Politifact, NewsChecker, and Boomlive. They preprocessed it by removing punctuation and stopwords, stemming, lowercase comments, lemmatization, and tokenization. They used Term Frequency-Inverse Document Frequency (TF-IDF) and Global Vectors for Word Representation (GloVe) word embeddings, as well as conventional and deep learning models like CNN, RNN using Gated Recurrent Units (GRU), LSTM, Deep Neural Networks (DNN), and Random Multimodel Deep Learning (RMDL) which is an ensemble of CNN, RNN, and DNN. The best accuracy for detecting fake news was 93.92% using CNN with GloVe word embeddings.
Flores and Hao [29] worked on a binary (real or fake) classification problem. They fine-tuned BERT classifiers using the LIAR and Kaggle Fake-News dataset, then put them through three adversarial attacks, namely a negation attack, party reversal attack, and adverb intensity attack. To model the binary classification problem, they mapped the LIAR dataset’s True, Mostly True, and Half True labels into a single Real class and the Barely True, False, and Pants on Fire labels into a single Fake class. They achieved an accuracy of 57.5% and 98.8% over the LIAR and Kaggle Fake-News datasets, respectively. They also computed saliency maps for the Kaggle Fake-News model using the Gradient × Input method (G × I), to measure how individual words impact the models’ classifications. They discovered that BERT-based models are vulnerable to negation and party reversal attacks but not to adverb intensity attacks.
Szczepanski et al. [22] used two datasets from Kaggle to solve a binary classification problem (real or fake). Experiments on two independent models using bidirectional LSTM and BERT-based fake news detectors were conducted to test the proposed approach’s applicability. In addition, each of them was trained on a different dataset. The accuracy of the BERT-based fake news detector was 98%, the precision was 97% for real news and 99% for fake news, the recall was 99% for real news and 97% for fake news, and the F1-score for both classes was 98%. The accuracy of bidirectional LSTM was 92%. Real news had a precision of 94%, whereas fake news had a precision of 90%. Both categories had the same recall rate of 92%. The real news classes received an F1-score of 93%, while fake news received an F1-score of 91%. They also examined the interpretability of their proposed model using two Explainable Artificial Intelligence (XAI) techniques: Local Interpretable Model-Agnostic Explanations (LIME) and Anchors.
Nasir et al. [26] worked on a binary (real or fake) classification problem (real or fake). They used the FA-KES dataset and ISOT dataset (obtained from the University of Victoria, Canada), and re python and the NLTK package for preprocessing. They employed pre-trained GloVe word embeddings and a Hybrid CNN-RNN model that utilized the Keras library’s sequential model, Conv1D, Maxpooling1D, and LSTM. The suggested hybrid CNN-RNN model outperformed all previous techniques with an F1-score of 59% and 99% on FA-KES and ISOT datasets, respectively. However, cross-dataset validation results revealed poor generalization despite the enormous corpus used for training.
Khan et al. [28] worked on a binary (real or fake) classification problem on three datasets: LIAR, Fake or Real News, developed by George McIntire, and combined corpus incorporating a wide range of topics. They performed preprocessing such as eliminating unnecessary IP and URL addresses, removing stop words, and removing suffices from words by performing stemming on the datasets. They used 100-dimensional pre-trained embeddings from GloVe for feature extraction. Six machine learning algorithms like SVM, Logistic Regression, Decision Tree, AdaBoost, Naive Bayes, and k-Nearest Neighbor (kNN) while six deep learning models including CNN, LSTM, Bi-LSTM, C-LSTM, Hierarchical Attention Networks (HAN), and Convolutional HAN, were also employed. Moreover, five advanced language models like BERT, Robustly Optimized BERT Pre-training Approach (RoBERTa), DistilBERT, Efficiently Learning an Encoder that Classifies Token Replacements Accurately (ELECTRA), and ELMo were also used in this work to detect fake news. It was observed that among traditional machine learning models, Naive Bayes with n-gram features performed the best with 93% accuracy. Among the deep learning models, Bi-LSTM and C-LSTM showed great promise with 95% accuracy, and advanced language models outperformed traditional and deep learning ones. The pre-trained RoBERTa achieved 96% accuracy on the combined corpus, while the BERT model performed best on the LIAR dataset, achieving accuracy and F1-score of 62% and 62%, respectively.
Ayoub et al. [8] worked on a binary (real or fake) classification problem using their dataset of 984 COVID-19 claims and COVID-19 Fake News Detection Shared Task to test their suggested model. Tokenization, lemmatization, removing stop words and punctuation, and transforming the textual representation into a vector space model using TF-IDF were all done as a preprocessing step. For detecting misinformation about COVID-19, traditional machine learning algorithms such as classification tree, logistic regression, random forest, and natural language processing model based on BERT, DistilBERT were used with Shapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) to explain the predictions locally. It was discovered that the DistilBERT model outperformed typical machine learning models on both datasets, achieving an accuracy of 97.2% on their dataset and an accuracy of 93.8% on the test dataset.
Sastrawan et al. [27] worked on a binary (real or fake) classification problem on four different datasets: ISOT, Fake News, Fake or Real News, and Fake News Detection. As part of the preprocessing step, tokenization, lemmatization, the removal of stop words and non-alphabetic characters, and the conversion of each word token to lowercase were performed. For feature extraction, they employed pre-trained word embeddings such as Word2Vec, GloVe, and FastText. This study used a variety of deep learning approaches to detect fake news, including CNN, bidirectional LSTM, and Residual Network (ResNet). The results revealed that the Bidirectional LSTM architecture outperformed CNN and ResNet on all datasets evaluated, achieving an accuracy of 99.95% on ISOT, 98.65% on Fake News, 94.6% on Fake or Real News, and 99.24% on the Fake News Detection Dataset.
Kaliyar et al. [14] worked on a binary (real or fake) classification problem on a Real-world fake news dataset. They introduced FakeBERT, a BERT-based deep convolutional technique for detecting bogus news. For better learning, the model combined BERT with three simultaneous blocks of 1D-CNN with varying kernel-sized convolutional layers and distinct filters. The model was created on top of a pre-trained word embedding model based on BERT. FakeBERT produced more accurate findings, with an accuracy of 98.90%.
Xu et al. [30] used two English datasets, Pheme and LIAR, and two Chinese datasets, Weibo and DataFountain, to solve a binary classification problem. For the LIAR dataset, they mapped pants on fire, false and barely true to FALSE and half true, mostly true and true to TRUE. They developed a new topic-driven rumor detection (TDRD) framework to figure out if a post is a rumor based only on its source microblog. They used CNN for English datasets and FastText for Chinese datasets to classify the topic of the source microblogs, then combined this predicted topic vector with word embeddings of the source microblogs for rumor detection, achieving an accuracy of 82.66%, 62.24%, 86.71%, and 96.51% on the Pheme, LIAR, Weibo, and DataFountain datasets, respectively.
Bhutani et al. [23] worked on a binary (real or fake) classification problem on the LIAR dataset and the George McIntire dataset. They offered a strategy for detecting fake news by adding sentiment as a new column to the dataset. This would allow them to detect fake news with greater precision. They used the cosine-similarity measure, TF-IDF vectors, and sentiment analysis scores as preprocessing tools. Naive Bayes and Random Forests were utilized as training models. Random Forests had a higher accuracy of 35.6% using TF-IDF with cosine-similarity for the LIAR dataset. In comparison, Naive Bayes had a higher accuracy of 84.3% using TF-IDF with cosine-similarity for the George McIntire dataset.
Aldwairi and Alwahedi [24] worked on a binary (real or fake) classification problem. They crawled the social media websites that had more fake news or clickbait ads or articles, such as Facebook, Forex, and Reddit, to collect URLs for the clickbait, thereafter, they extracted the features from the title and the content of the web pages. They ranked the attributes based on information gain and correlation attributes to improve accuracy and reduce training time. Further, they used machine learning algorithms such as BayesNet (Bayes Network), Logistic Regression, Random Tree, and Naive Bayes on the Weka tool to detect bogus news. It was discovered that the best precision achieved for detecting fake news in social media networks was 99.4% using logistic regression.
Trueman et al. [25] worked on a multi-class classification problem with six labeled categories: true, half-true, mostly-true, barely-true, false, and pants-on-fire from the LIAR dataset. As a preprocessing step, they leveraged case conversion, tokenization, and the removal of special symbols. They used pre-trained GloVe word vectors, CNN with Max-pooling, and a Bidirectional LSTM (Bi-LSTM) Attention mechanism. They suggested an attention-based convolutional bidirectional long short-term memory (AC-BiLSTM) technique detect bogus news. Further, an attention mechanism was used to aid in the memorization of lengthy input sequences after C-BiLSTM was used to determine the local, global, and temporal meaning of a sentence. As a result, the proposed hybrid model outperformed other existing models in terms of accuracy (35.1%) and micro F1-score (39%).
Jwa et al. [31] worked on a four-label multi-class classification problem: Agrees (AGR), Disagrees (DSG), Discusses (DSC), and Unrelated (UNR). The Fake News Challenge (FNC-1) dataset was used for testing, while Cable News Network (CNN) and Daily Mail news data were used for training. BAKE and exBAKE were proposed as two models which outperformed BERT by addressing the issue of data imbalance. In the pre-training process of exBAKE, additional CNN and Daily Mail news data were added to the BAKE model. The models were tested against the FNC-1 dataset, and BAKE obtained an F1-score of 73.4% while exBAKE obtained an F1-score of 74.6%.
To recap, Fake News Detection can be modeled as either a binary or a multi-class classification problem. Linguistic data is first preprocessed using techniques such as lower-casing, stopwords removal, lemmatization, and so on, then a model is trained using machine learning, deep learning, or an advanced language model, followed by evaluating the model on test data. Research Motivation and Contribution Fake or misleading news can have a significant influence on targeted individuals [32]. This was especially bad during the coronavirus pandemic in India when highly dubious information on the internet often swept away reliable news sources. False information has had major ramifications for minority groups, and some corporate sectors [11, 33]. Apart from the pandemic, this malice has caused massive losses to numerous companies by spreading fraudulent remarks about their accounting processes and stock prices on social media [2]. As a result, many companies lost their market value in a single day. Many enterprises and businesses have lost their trust and reputation in the past as a result of the widespread dissemination of false news [34].
Several research groups are still looking into the problem of fake news detection because it is widespread with challenges. A few of the challenges include:
- Scarcity of generalized datasets: The majority of the datasets accessible are focused on specific facets of society, such as politics or pandemics [35, 36]. As a result, datasets containing news from various spheres of society, such as sports, crime, education, and technology, are scarce, resulting in models that perform well on instances from the same domain as the training data but fail to deliver satisfactory results when tested on data instances from different domains [26].

- Lack of interpretability in results of complex neural networks: Unboxing the black box of the complicated neural network, where explainable AI comes into play, is required to understand why a model is categorizing or misclassifying an instance as fake or real [14, 25, 28]. It enables one to fully comprehend the model and its flaws to develop it further.

- Imbalanced class problem: Because actual news instances nearly always outweigh fake news instances, detecting fake news is essentially an imbalanced class problem [22, 26].


Fake news can take the shape of text, images, videos, memes, advertisements, posters, and other sorts of data. So, it is a multimodal classification problem that is hard to solve because different kinds of data need to be processed and handled in different ways. We only used textual data because most of the research we looked at was only based on linguistic data.
To combat the problem of fake news classification, we propose I-FLASH, Interpretable Fake News Detector using LIME And SHAP that employed three different models, namely Logistic Regression with TF-IDF, Bidirectional LSTM with GloVe, and pre-trained BERT, allowing comparison of different models on the same datasets. The models were trained and tested using two publicly available datasets: the COVID-19 dataset [36], which contains mostly pandemic-related news, and the benchmark LIAR dataset [35], which primarily contains news instances related to US politics. Both of these datasets cover two different domains, namely, health and politics.
In this paper, we intend to resolve the above-mentioned issues. The contribution of the work is as follows:
- To address the issue of generalized dataset scarcity, two new datasets have been constructed, one of which is an extension of the other. The datasets were culled from the official Twitter accounts of various well-known media outlets as well as certain fact-checking websites hosted by these outlets. The news items in the datasets cover a range of topics, including politics, education, technology, crime, and sports.

- Explainable AI (XAI) was utilized to explain the predictions of different models on individual instances to get insight into which phrases contribute to determining whether the news is fake. By comparing the probability of identifying an instance as fake, it was also possible to see how confident the models were in classifying a certain news instance as fake.

- Cross-dataset validation was performed by training models over the COVID-19 and LIAR datasets and testing them on the FactCheck2 dataset to see how efficient domain-specific datasets are in predicting fake news concerned with other aspects of society.


The F1-score classification metric was used in conjunction with the accuracy metric to address the imbalanced class problem. This would emphasize the necessity of appropriately classifying instances of fake news. It can be shown that, despite their high accuracy, some algorithms perform poorly when it comes to classifying fake news.

Materials Using Publicly Available Datasets The work used two datasets for training and testing the models–the COVID-19 dataset [36], and the benchmark LIAR dataset [35].
COVID-19 dataset [36] is a COVID-19 fake news dataset with 10,700 news instances collected during the pandemic. The dataset is already split into the train (6420 instances), validation (2140 instances), and test (2140 instances) sets. The dataset is class-wise balanced, with rełnews accounting for 52.34% and fake news accounting for 47.66% of total samples.
The LIAR dataset [35], is used comprising 12,791 human-labeled short statements taken from PolitiFact.com. The dataset is available in three partitions—train (10,240 instances), validation (1284 instances), and test (1267 instances). pants-fire, false, barely-true, half-true, mostly-true, and true are the six possible values for the class label in this dataset. To model the binary classification problem, pants-fire, false, and barely-true are mapped to ‘fake’ while half-true, mostly-true, and true are mapped to ‘real’. The modified dataset now contains 56% true statements and 44% false assertions.
Curation of New Datasets FactCheck and FactCheck2 are two new tiny datasets proposed in the paper.1
The FactCheck dataset consists of two CSV files. The first file, named ‘realNews.csv’ contains 100 real news samples gathered from official Twitter accounts of well-known news media outlets such as Times Now, India Today, Mirror Now, and others. ‘Link’, ‘Source’, ‘Tweet’, ‘Category’, and ‘Label’ are the five columns in this file. The ‘Link’ field contains the appropriate Twitter link for that tweet, the ‘Source’ field contains the name of the media firm whose Twitter handle the tweet was retrieved from, the ‘Tweet’ field contains the tweet itself, and the ‘Category’ field contains the news domain. The real news stories are divided into three categories: 33 are linked to ‘Crime’, 33 are related to ‘Health’, and the remaining 34 are related to ‘Politics’. Finally, all real instances have a ‘Label’ of ‘0’, indicating that this is a genuine news instance. The second file named ‘fakeNews.csv’ contains 100 fake news samples gathered from the India Today Fact Check website.2 This file has four columns: ‘Link’, which contains the article’s URL, ‘Claim’, which has the actual text, ‘Explanation’ which explains why the claim is wrong, and ‘Label’, which has three values–Half true, Mostly lies, and Absolutely false, which would all be mapped to fake.
The FactCheck2 dataset is merely a supplement to the FactCheck dataset, with the sole difference being the number of actual and fraudulent news instances. It also consists of two CSV files. The first file, named ‘realNews2.csv’ contains 600 examples of legitimate news, equally divided into six categories—‘Crime’, ‘Education’, ‘Health’, ‘Politics’, ‘Sports’, and ‘Technology’. The second file, named ‘fakeNews2.csv’ contains 120 examples of fraudulent news. It is essentially an imbalanced class dataset, similar to what we find in real life. The remaining details are identical to those that FactCheck provided. Tables 1 and 2 show a few Real and Fake samples from FactCheck2, respectively.
All the datasets were preprocessed to include just two attributes—the ‘text’ and the ‘label’, which takes the value of ‘0’ for all real news occurrences and ‘1’ for all fake news instances because the goal of this work is to categorize fake and real news alone.

Background Term Frequency-Inverse Document Frequency (TF-IDF) Word Vectors It is a numerical statistic that determines the significance of a word in a collection or corpus. The number of times a word appears in the document increases the TF-IDF value correspondingly [37]. TF-IDF generates values for each word in a document using the inverse proportion of the frequency of a word in a given document to the percentage of documents the word appears in. Words with high TF-IDF numbers have a strong association with the document in which they occur, implying that if that term appeared in a query, the document could be of interest to the user [38]. Term frequency, tf ( t ,  d ) is the relative frequency of term t within document d , t f ( t , d ) = f t , d ∑ t ′ ∈ d f t ′ , d where f t , d is the raw count of term t in document d . The denominator is the total number of terms in the document d . The inverse document frequency is a measure of how common or rare a word is, across all documents. i d f ( t , D ) = log N | { d ∈ D : t ∈ d } | where N is the total number of documents in the corpus, and the denominator indicates the number of documents that contain the term t . Then TF-IDF is calculated as: t f i d f ( t , d , D ) = t f ( t , d ) · i d f ( t , D )
Global Vectors for Word Representation (Glove) Word Embeddings GloVe is a global log-bilinear regression model for unsupervised learning of word representations, which is used for tasks such as word analogies, word similarity, and named entity recognition [39]. It is a model for distributed word representation. The model is an unsupervised learning approach for obtaining vector representations for words. This is achieved by mapping words into a meaningful space in which the distance between words is proportional to their semantic similarity. The resulting representations, which are trained using aggregated global word-word co-occurrence information from a corpus, indicate fascinating linear substructures of the word vector space [40].
Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) Networks It is a form of RNN that is capable of learning long-term dependencies and is a particularly effective solution to the vanishing gradient problem. The LSTM design was inspired by a study of error flow in existing RNNs, which revealed that existing architectures could not handle extended time lags because backpropagated error either explodes or decays exponentially. An LSTM cell is made up of weights and three distinct learning gates. There is an input gate for the current input, an output gate for predicting values, and a forget gate for discarding useless information at each time step. The input to the cells is multiplied by the input gate’s activation, the output of the net is multiplied by the output gate’s activation, and the prior cell values are multiplied by the forget gate’s activation. Only the gates allow the net to interact with the cells [41]. Bidirectional Recurrent Neural Networks (BRNN) When an RNN model processes a sequence not just from beginning to end but also from end to beginning, it performs better. It is helpful to know the context around a word, not just the words that came before it when predicting the following word in a phrase. The bidirectional RNN model is used to do this. A bidirectional RNN is created by extending a standard RNN. A BRNN can be trained without being limited to only using input data up to a predetermined future frame. This is accomplished by training it in both positive and negative time directions simultaneously. We achieve the bidirectional LSTM model by replacing the RNN units with LSTM cells, which is considerably superior to the unidirectional counterpart [42]. Transformers It is a type of attention mechanism that learns the context of words (or sub-words) in a text. The transformer has two different mechanisms in its basic form: an encoder that reads the text input and a decoder that generates a prediction for the task. The transformer encoder reads the complete sequence of words simultaneously, unlike directional models that read the text input sequentially (left-to-right or right-to-left). This property enables the model to deduce the context of a word from its surrounds (left and right of the word) [43, 44].
Bidirectional Encoder Representations from Transformers (BERT) It is a pre-trained model that was designed to learn contextual word representations of unlabeled texts [45]. It is a self-supervised transformers model that was pre-trained on a big corpus of English data. This implies it was pre-trained on simply the raw texts, with no human labeling (which is why it can use so much publicly available data), and it used an automatic mechanism to generate inputs and labels from those texts. This method allows the model to learn an inner representation of the English language, which may subsequently be used to extract features relevant to downstream tasks [45, 46]. Out of the two versions of BERT (i.e., BERT-Base and BERT-Large). we chose BERT-Base over BERT-Large for this study due to the large time and memory needs of the BERT-Large model. Interpretability Interpretability is the degree to which a human can comprehend the reason for a decision [47] or the degree to which a person can reliably predict the model’s outcome [48]. Because it is evident that we must construct ‘transparent’ models that can explain why they anticipate what they predict in order to establish confidence in intelligent machines and proceed towards their meaningful incorporation into our daily lives, interpretability is important [49]. There is usually a trade-off between accuracy and comprehensibility or simplicity [50]. The relative relevance of each feature to the mapping can be determined by comparing covariate weights in a regression model. Insofar as data classes are determined by their placement relative to the decision boundaries, SVMs, and other linear classifiers are interpretable. However, the behavior of deep neural networks, in which input features are automatically learned and modified via non-linearities, is unlikely to be understood [51].
Explainable Artificial Intelligence (XAI) Explainable AI is a subfield of machine learning that aims to add human-interpretable explanations to the training process, learned representations, and judgments. Before, many scientific applications used linear models because they were easy to understand and could be used to make predictions. But fast progress in the field of XAI has made it possible for users to understand almost any complicated model, whether it is supervised or unsupervised. As a result, we no longer need to sacrifice predictability for interpretability, and we can use strong nonlinear ML to its full potential in real-world applications [52]. Explanations are often categorized along two main aspects. The first determines if the explanation is for a single prediction (local) or for the entire model’s prediction process (global). The second distinguishes between explanations that emerge immediately from the prediction process (self-explaining) and those that require further processing (post-hoc) [53].
Local Versus Global Explanations A local explanation gives information or justification for a model’s prediction based on a single input, but a global explanation provides equivalent justification by explaining how the model’s predictive process works regardless of the input [53].
Self-explaining Versus Post-Hoc A self-explaining technique, also known as directly interpretable, provides the explanation at the same time as the prediction based on information emitted by the model as a result of the prediction process. LIME is an example of a local explanation employing a surrogate model applied after the predictor has completed its work [53].
Local Interpretable Model-Agnostic Explanations (LIME) Local surrogate models are models that can be understood and are used to explain how black-box machine learning models arrive at their predictions. LIME is a concrete example of local surrogate models that learns an interpretable model locally around the prediction to explain any classifier’s predictions in an interpretable and faithful manner. The overall purpose of LIME is to find an interpretable model that is locally faithful to the classifier over the interpretable representation [15]. Surrogate models are trained to approximate the predictions of the underlying black box model, and LIME focuses on training local surrogate models to explain individual predictions instead of the global surrogate model. LIME generates a new dataset containing the modified samples as well as the black box model’s predictions. LIME then uses this additional dataset to build an interpretable model that is weighted by the sampled instances’ proximity to the instance of interest and this interpretable model should be a good local approximation of the machine learning model’s predictions [50]. Mathematically, local surrogate models with interpretability constraints can be expressed as follows: e x p l a n a t i o n ( x ) = a r g min g ∈ G L ( f , g , π x ) + Ω ( g ) The model g that minimizes loss L is the explanatory model for instance x . While the model complexity Ω ( g ) is kept low (e.g. prefer fewer features), L (e.g. mean squared error) assesses how near the explanation is to the forecast of the original model f . G stands for the family of plausible explanations, such as all linear regression models. The proximity measure, π x specifies the size of the neighborhood that we evaluate for the explanation around instance x [15].
SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) SHAP is a technique for explaining individual predictions. It dissects a prediction to explain how each feature affects it. The purpose of SHAP is to compute each feature’s contribution to the prediction of an instance x in order to explain it. The SHAP explanation technique, which is based on coalitional game theory, is used to calculate Shapley values. The Shapley values show us how to distribute the ‘payout’ (= prediction) across the features in a fair manner [50]. Mathematically, the SHAP explanation can be expressed as follows: g ( z ′ ) = ϕ 0 + ∑ j = 1 M ϕ j z j ′ where g is the explanation model, z ′ ∈ { 0 , 1 } M is the coalition vector, M is the maximum coalition size and ϕ j ∈ R is the feature attribution for a feature j , the Shapley values [50].
Methodology Preprocessing URLs, Twitter links, special characters, punctuation marks, and additional white spaces were removed from the raw data first. After that, the text was converted to lowercase. The empty rows were removed from the dataset, and a new column named Class was added, which is a categorical variable that can take just two values: ‘0’ for real and ‘1’ for fake. Subsequently, the data were broken up into small pieces called "tokens", and the maximum length of input for each dataset was found by drawing the histograms for that dataset. The instances that were longer than this maximum input length were then truncated to this maximum length, while those that were shorter were padded to this maximum length. In the LIAR dataset [35], for example, 12,786 data instances out of a total of 12,791 had a number of words less than or equal to 50 (please see Fig. 5 for the histogram of the word frequency in the news instances). As a result, the maximum input length was set to 50 words. Figure 6 shows the block diagram of the steps involved in the preprocessing of the dataset. The output of the preprocessing step is the vector representation of words to be used as the dataset for model training and validation. Fig. 5 [Images not available. See PDF.]

Model Training and Validation The proposed framework used the vectorized dataset obtained after preprocessing phase to train a machine learning algorithm—Logistic Regression with TF-IDF vectors, a deep learning algorithm—Bidirectional LSTM with GloVe word vectors, and an advanced language model—pre-trained BERT (please see Fig. 7). Each model was trained and evaluated using a stratified 10-fold cross-validation procedure to obtain a reliable assessment of model accuracy and an F1-score at 95% confidence level. To get the best possible accuracy with minimal loss, extensive hyperparameter tuning was performed. Table 4 shows the values of all the hyperparameters used for the bidirectional LSTM model for each of the datasets. The activation function is Sigmoid at the output layer and the Adam optimizer is utilized to update the network weights. The Binary Cross Entropy served as the loss function. The dropout regularization method was used to prevent the models from overfitting, wherever needed. The pre-trained BERT model used Adam as the optimizer, while Sparse Categorical Cross Entropy served as the loss function. The batch size was 32. Tests proved that two epochs were enough for the COVID-19 and LIAR datasets, while four epochs sufficed for the FactCheck and FactCheck2 datasets. The learning rate employed was 3e–5. Evaluation Metrics The evaluation metrics used in the work are accuracy and F1-score. In our experiment, we considered fake news as the ‘positive class’, and real news as the ‘negative class’. Hence, True Positive (TP) means the news is actually fake, and also predicted as fake while False Positive (FP) indicates that the news is actually real, but predicted as fake. True Negative (TN) and False Negative (FN) imply accordingly. Accuracy is the number of correctly predicted instances out of all instances. The F1-score combines the precision and recall of a classifier into a single metric by taking their harmonic mean. F1-score was used here because the positive class was more important than the negative one. So, more importance had to be given to the detection of fake instances. Also, it is an imbalanced class problem, so accuracy may sometimes give misleading results due to which F1-score was more appropriate to work with.

Result Analysis As evident from Table 5, except for the LIAR dataset, where Logistic Regression with TF-IDF produces the best results, the BERT model surpasses the other two models in terms of accuracy. However, the difference in accuracy between the BERT model and the Logistic Regression model is quite modest. It can also be observed from Table 5 (please see Eq. (2) for its computation), the BERT model outperforms the other two, in terms of F1-score also, and provides the best F1-score for all the datasets so far. For COVID-19, LIAR, and FactCheck datasets, Logistic Regression using TF-IDF outperforms the Bidirectional LSTM model. Also, when compared to the other two datasets, the variation of 10-fold cross-validation accuracy and 10-fold cross-validation F1-score for FactCheck and FactCheck2 is larger. This is owing to the tiny size of these two datasets, which made model overfitting a possibility.
Imbalanced Dataset Problem Another important point to observe is that the F1-score of Logistic Regression with TF-IDF for the FactCheck2 dataset has a value of zero. This demonstrates how Logistic Regression fails to perform well on a dataset that is imbalanced. Only the FactCheck2 dataset has a significant imbalance, with just 16.67% fake occurrences. It has an F1-score of zero despite having a good accuracy of 83.33 ± 0.00%. This demonstrates that the model is mistakenly categorizing all fake occurrences as real while still achieving a satisfactory level of accuracy. This can also be observed in the corresponding confusion matrix of Logistic Regression in Fig. 9a. This highlights the necessity of utilizing the F1-score metric when dealing with imbalanced datasets, as accuracy can often produce deceptive results, and thus it is not a sufficient metric to evaluate any model, especially when dealing with skewed datasets.
Cross Dataset Validation Cross dataset validation means training a model on one dataset and testing it on another dataset. First, we trained a BERT model over the COVID-19 dataset and tested it on the FactCheck2 dataset, obtaining an accuracy and F1-score of 47.92% and 38.22%, respectively. Next, we trained a BERT model on the LIAR dataset and then tested it on the FactCheck2 dataset, obtaining an accuracy and F1-score of 27.78% and 27.98%, respectively. It can be observed that the results are not that satisfactory which shows the incompetence of domain-specific publicly available datasets like COVID-19 and LIAR, to perform well on generalized datasets like FactCheck2 that cover different domains of society.
Further, we trained a BERT model on the FactCheck2 dataset and tested it on the COVID-19 dataset, obtaining an accuracy and F1-score of 64.25% and 42.76%. We next trained a BERT model on the FactCheck2 dataset and tested it on the LIAR dataset, obtaining an accuracy and F1-score of 54.22% and 49.53%. We can observe that the model trained on a generalized dataset like FactCheck2 is able to perform well on domain-specific datasets like COVID-19 and LIAR. This proves the need for the curation of new datasets like FactCheck and FactCheck2 which are able to cater to several domains of society. Table 6 summarizes the cross-dataset validation results. None of the works have used explainable AI to unwind the black box working of models, except Flores and Hao [29] who computed saliency maps but not for the LIAR dataset.

It also employs the model-agnostic approaches of explainable AI such as LIME and SHAP to restore trust in the models built. Conclusions I-FLASH employed LIME and SHAP to explain the output of three proposed models based on Logistic regression, Bidirectional LSTM, and BERT. Two new self-curated generalized datasets—FactCheck and FactCheck2, are constructed spanning news from a variety of societal domains. Using stratified 10-fold cross-validation at 95% confidence level, the pre-trained BERT model outperformed Logistic Regression with TF-IDF vectors and Bidirectional LSTM with GloVe word embeddings with an accuracy of 94.41 ± 0.38%, 87.25 ± 2.45%, and 92.91 ± 2.07% on COVID-19, FactCheck, and FactCheck2 datasets, respectively, with one exception on the LIAR dataset, where Logistic Regression with TF-IDF produces the better results, with an accuracy of 61.18 ± 0.55%. Moreover, the BERT model outperformed the other two models in terms of F1-score, with stratified 10-fold cross-validation F1-score of 94.10 ± 0.47%, 51.54 ± 3.21%, 87.78 ± 2.38%, and 80.00 ± 3.37% on COVID-19, LIAR, FactCheck, and FactCheck2 datasets, respectively, at 95% confidence level.
For the FactCheck2 dataset, despite having a good accuracy of 83.33 ± 0.00%, Logistic Regression misclassifies all the fake instances as real ones, resulting in an F1-score of zero. Furthermore, the variance of stratified 10-fold cross-validation accuracy and F1-score for FactCheck and FactCheck2 datasets is significantly larger than for COVID-19 and LIAR datasets, owing to the small size of these datasets, which made the models vulnerable to overfitting.
Cross-dataset validation has also been performed to prove the incompetency of domain-specific datasets compared to generalized datasets. Further, LIME and SHAP were employed to unwind the black box models which appropriately phrased the words claiming an instance as Fake or Real . Additionally, the LIME and SHAP explanations were compared for some instances of the dataset, and it was found that similar words were typically deemed relevant by both of these XAI methods.
Fake news detection is an important and rapidly evolving field. There are several promising avenues that can be explored in the future. For instance, multimodal datasets comprising the news in the form of images and videos can also be examined to classify fake and real news. In addition, meta-information (such as social context) can also be combined with news content to effectively detect fake news. As a future scope, we intend to expand the newly created datasets to capture more diversified news across all possible domains, thereby improving cross-dataset validation results. Finally, for model explainability, we aim to explore the applicability of other explainable AI methods in the proposed domain.
