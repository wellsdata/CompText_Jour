Headnote Abstract: This paper explores an Islamic framework for news reporting and dissemination, as applicable to both reporters and transmitters. Drawing on specific Quranic verses and their exegeses, as well as Hadith methodology, the paper aims to extrapolate Islamic guidelines in relation to practices such as source-checking, content-verification, and accountability. Quranic injunctions and case studies reveal that the highest standards of authentication must be applied when reporting or sharing news and acting upon it. In addition, the dissemination of known corruption or misconduct is allowed for the purpose of redressing wrongs or benefiting the community.
Key words: Communication ethics, dissemination ethics, htisba, 'ilm, amana
Introduction
Much of the scholarly literature addressing the intersection of Islam or Muslims and the media has to do with the depiction of Islam or Muslims in the media,1 or the emergence of new Muslim media such as the television networks Al-Jazeera and Al-Arabiya in the past two decades.2 However, when it comes to the theoretical understanding of news reporting and dissemination based on Islamic principles, current research, although not completely absent, is considerably lacking. This is interesting given that other fields of the social sciences, such as economics, politics, and education, have been explored extensively from an Islamic theoretical perspective; however, journalism and news transmission, for the most part, have not received the same attention. This lacuna becomes even more salient in today's age of "fake news" and "retweets" where anyone, even if not an actual news reporter, can become a disseminator via the sharing and reposting of news.
What does Islam have to contribute to the ongoing debate about the reporting and dissemination of news? This is the question with which the current article is concerned, drawing on Quranic and Hadith sources to determine the Islamic ethical guidelines around which individuals and groups can operate with respect to news reporting and news sharing. Following a brief literature review, this article is divided into three parts: the first part sets the groundwork by examining relevant Islamic concepts as well as introducing Hadith methodology; the second part looks at certain Quranic verses,3 applying textual methodology to the issue of communication ethics using Quranic exegesis (tafsīr), in order to establish guidelines for news content and reporting; the third part looks at case studies in the Qur'an to add a practical historical element. Although the works of several exegetes are examined, those of Sayyid Qutb (d. 1966)4 and Abū al-Alā Al-Mawdūdī (d. 1979),5 are particularly pertinent.
Literature Review
In 1980, Mughees-ud-Din very briefly explored an "Islamic Concept of Mass Communication"6 by comparing Fred Siebert, Theodore Peterson, and Wilbur Schramm's "Four Theories of the Press"7 with principles meant to guide an "Islamic press." A few years later, Syeed Al Seini discussed "An Islamic Concept of News," including newsworthiness and the need for verification.8 Both articles, however, were neither very detailed nor comprehensive. More recently, Hamid Mowlana discussed communication ethics using Qur'anic concepts such as tawhîd (monotheism), taqwā (God-consciousness), and amāna (trusteeship) as applicable to journalism.9 Similarly, Mohammad Ayish also attempted to construct a normative communications perspective using secular and religious values from both Arab and Muslim cultures.10 Although these sources are helpful in creating a general understanding of what a Muslim perspective of communications might entail, perhaps the most relevant and detailed works have been Syed Pasha's discussion of the nature of news in Muslim cultural theory11 and Mughees-ud-Din's later work aimed at developing a Muslim understanding of media ethics.12 With the internet boom, works such as those by Muhammad Amanullah have also explored Islamic ethical guidelines for social media usage.13
In terms of monographs, Freedom of Expression in Islam by Mohammad Hashim Kamali is also a valuable reference for understanding legal issues of news reporting such as libel, slander and defamation, as well as ethical issues such as freedom of speech and victim's grievances.14 Nevertheless, there is an evident lack of literature in this area as most of the above sources have only examined the issues of news reporting and communication ethics on a peripheral level. This gap in knowledge has mostly to do with exploring these issues in depth, such as the Islamic concepts discussed by Mowlana and Ayish, or the Quranic verses examined by Pasha. Furthermore, in discussing relevant Quranic verses, these authors have not examined exegeses of the Qur'an to determine the reasons for revelation, their context, and implications for the future. This article thus primarily employs exegetical methodology in attempting to determine Islamic guidelines for news reporting and dissemination.
Basic Concepts: Tawhîd, Hisba, and 'Ilm
The most fundamental concept in Islamic theology is tawhîd or monotheism.15 Put simply, this concept asserts the Oneness of God-He being the Sole Creator and the Only One worthy of worship. As Ismā'īl al-Farūqī has noted, tawhîd results in a worldview which asserts that "the nature of the cosmos is teleological; that is, purposive, serving a purpose for its Creator, and doing so out of design."16 Additionally, according to Farūqī the central position of such a belief in the Unity of God is manifested in all realms of knowledge, including "the realm of the arts and sciences, in which Islam cannot remain neutral vis-avis any form of knowledge."17
Taking this as a point of departure, as well as the idea that human beings are God's vicegerents on earth,18 one may develop a sense of ethics, responsibility and accountability toward God. As in economics, this sense of responsibility and accountability also establishes a trusteeship of resources.19 So too in communication, which has similar implications in terms of the trusteeship of information:
A journalist who uses his/her faculty of observation, reason, consciousness, reflection, insight, understanding, and wisdom must realize that these are the amānah (trust) of God and must not be used to injure a human soul for the sake of self-promotion or for selling the news, rather . . . these are to be used in arriving at truth.20
Societal relationships and responsibilities are further guided by the fundamental Islamic principle of enjoining good and forbidding evil (al-amr bi-l-marūf wa-lnahy 'an al-munkar). This is also often known as the principle of hisba, which literally means "accountability." Qur'an 3:104 forms the locus classicus of this concept: "Let there arise out of you a band of people inviting to all that is good, enjoining what is right, and forbidding what is wrong: They are the ones to attain felicity."
Commenting on this verse, Mohammad Hashim Kamali observes that the verse begins with a command ("Let there arise out of you . . "), which implies a collective obligation on the community (fard kifāya).21 Thus, as Mowlana notes, the obligation to enjoin good and forbid evil "includes all of the institutions of social communication such as the press, radio, television, and cinema."22 By extension, we can also update this interpretation to include social media platforms such as Facebook, Twitter, and Snapchat.
Also relevant is the following hadīth of the Prophet Muhammad: "If any of you sees something evil, he should set it right with his hand; if he is unable to do so, then with his tongue, and if he is unable to do even that, then (let him denounce it) in his heart. But this is the weakest form of faith"23 Inferring from this tradition, there are three varieties of hisba, as Kamali points out: (l) acting against evil by physical action; (2) acting against evil by verbal admonition; (3) and acting against evil by disapproval.24
While these three alternatives are applicable for most individuals, one may argue that h. isba is even more appropriate for news reporting and dissemination ethics because journalists and social media users are in a position to reach a wider audience; they also have the power to address grievances and rectify social evils, at least by means of the verbal variety of hisba mentioned above. However, certain conditions must be met when fulfilling this duty, namely possessing sufficient knowledge, avoiding a greater evil, and acting on "the basis of an overwhelming probability (al-zann al-ghālib) that the attempt to enjoin good or to forbid evil is likely to achieve the desired result"25 For reporters and news transmitters, this implies a duty to be in a position of knowledge; i.e., educating oneself through reliable sources about the matter under consideration. They must also ensure or be reasonably certain that reporting on the matter results in more benefit than harm.
Finally, the Qur'ānic notion of 'ilm, or knowledge, is pertinent because "communication in Islam is intrinsically related to [it]" and "as a defining concept of the worldview of Islam, its influence permeates all aspects of Muslim individual and societal behaviour."26 The acquisition of knowledge is given utmost importance in the Quran and, therefore, in Islamic thought, where it is categorised as a human need without which survival and success are unlikely.27
The concept of 'ilm in Islam implies that people have a right to know information that is beneficial and relevant to them and their lives, with the key terms being "beneficial" and "relevant." For those reporting or sharing news, this means that they must "shoulder the responsibility of being society's watchdogs, on the alert to provide information on marūf and support it, and to identify munkar and oppose it."28 Syed Pasha also notes that 'ilm is the "polar opposite" of zann, which means "speculation" or "conjecture." Furthermore, zann "itself is contrasted with Haq (Truth)" in Quran 53:28, which states, "conjecture avails nothing against Truth."29 The distinction between 'ilm and zann is vital because reporting unverified news based on mere suspicion or conjecture, without any solid basis in truth (i.e., "fake news") can be both unethical and potentially detrimental to society. We shall return to this point later.
Theory in Practice: Hadith Methodology
The basic theoretical concepts of tawhīd, hisba, and 'ilm can be combined with the practical example of their implementation in the form of Hadith methodology, from which we can draw parallels for news reporting and communication ethics today. A hadīth has two main parts: the isnād, the chain of narrators; and the matn, the actual content. Hadith scholars developed intricate systems to verify both in order to ascertain the narration (riwāya) and the meaning (dirāya) of Hadith. As Kamali notes, "The former is concerned with ensuring authenticity in source and accuracy in transmission . . . [the latter] with the accuracy of the text of the hadīth by paying attention to all the nuances of language and the purpose of the text as well as the ruling it might contain."30
With regard to the isnād, a major source of authentication for chains of narration are the biographies of Hadith transmitters ('ilm tā'rīkh al-ruwāt)d These deal primarily with the biographical data, chronology and life histories of narrators, as well as "their academic achievements, their teachers, their students, school or following, political leaning and views of other people concerning them."32 This information helps determine, for example, if a particular narrator would have been alive and present at a particular time and place to meet a previous narrator in the chain. In addition, biographies are detailed to the extent that it is possible to determine whether a narrator may have been reliable at one point but changed over time due to circumstances such as the adoption of controversial views, loss of memory, etc.33
This ancillary branch of Hadith studies also contains the science of al-jarh. wa-l-ta'dü -"criticism and validation"-which primarily deals with the reliability of narrators. For example, grounds for declaring a hadīth unreliable include the narrator's indulgence in deeming actions permissible (mubahāt); acceptance of unwarranted innovations (bida); or indulgence in unsubstantiated opinion (hawā). By contrast, grounds for declaring reliability include traits such as truthfulness, a critical approach to the authentication of Hadith, and the avoidance of bida and grave sins. Based on these factors, narrators were given grades of acceptability or unacceptability.34
Likewise, with regard to the text of a hadīth (matn), scholars adopted similar measures to ensure authenticity. Zubayr Siddiqui writes, "According to traditionists, even if the isnād is completely without fault, the text should still be analysed before the genuineness of its attribution can be established."35 Forged texts, for example, might be determined by factors such as the use of Arabic grammar and stylistics, since the Prophet Muhammad was known to have been eloquent and sophisticated in his Arabic usage. Other determinants might include corruption in the purpose and meaning of the text; clear opposition to a Quranic text or command; unhistorical statements; a text that is reflective of scholastic sectarianism, or one that advocates a ruling that is simply unreasonable.36 In this way, Hadith methodology applied a rigorous authentication process to ensure reliability of the highest degree.37
With respect to Islamic communications ethics and news reporting, Hadith methodology has important repercussions. Abdullah Schleifer discusses this extensively in the article, "Media and Religion in the Arab-Islamic World." He notes that neglect of accuracy in news reporting is un-Islamic precisely because of the mechanisms employed by early Muslims to retain accuracy in the transmission of Hadith texts, which can be considered "sacred news."38 Linking Hadith methodology to the modern journalistic practice of "sourcing," Schleifer notes that these mechanisms were necessary because "political struggles among the Muslims were inevitably colored by religious justification, and the temptation to forgery, to invent politically useful hadīth must have been great."39
Following Schleifer's advice, reporters and news transmitters can derive guidelines such as thoroughly checking the reliability of sources before reporting them or acting upon information provided by them. This is especially necessary for controversial political events or internal disputes within the Muslim community. Just as Hadith scholars were cautious in accepting narrations "from unknown people and persons of obscure identity and character," so too reporters and social media users today must think twice before accepting and sharing news from dubious sources.40 In addition, a journalist reporting the news should confirm her information through multiple sources, especially if the original source is questionable.
With regard to the content of news, reporters can take a lesson from matn criticism in that if the reported information is unusual or goes against the norm, it warrants further investigation and confirmation before being reported. Although the unusual or bizarre is considered newsworthy by conventional standards, Islamic values dictate that such information, especially if it contains an accusation or is otherwise controversial, demands thorough investigation and confirmation.41 In general, the reporter must take any biases into consideration and be vigilant of the source's motive for making a claim, just as Hadith scholars were careful when considering a narration that reflected scholastic sectarianism or furthered a political agenda.
News Content and Dissemination Ethics in the Qur'an
Inevitably, the central aspect of news reporting is the news content itself, which may be determined by a number of factors. According to the Qur'ān, God has delineated certain hudūd (limits or boundaries) that should not be crossed (Q 2:229) or even approached (Q 2:187).42 The question thus becomes, what are these limits or boundaries?
1.(Qur'an 4:148, 6:151 and 7:33)
Quran 4:148 states: "God does not publicly mention evil except for one who has been wrongedMohammad Hashim Kamali writes that the key Arabic term in this verse, al-jahr, means "publicising or broadcasting," and that the term su, means "evil" (or "evil speech"). Thus, the verse warns against "the publicising of evil."43 In this context, exegetes have taken "evil speech" to refer to that which accuses another of something negative. With minor variations, several commentators note that this verse was revealed when a man complained about some people who denied him hospitality; for this reason, he was given the dispensation to complain.
Similar injunctions are found in Q 6:151 ("Stay well away from committing indecent deeds, whether openly or in secret . . .") and Q 7:33 ("Say [Oh Prophet], 'My Lord only forbids disgraceful deeds-whether they be open or hidden ..."). The Arabic term used for "indecent/disgraceful deeds" in both of these verses is fawahish. Sayyid Qutb writes that linguistically, fawahish refers to "everything that goes beyond its proper limits," although in this context it most commonly denotes adultery and fornication.44 Abū al-Alā Al-Mawdūdī applies this term "to all kinds of impure acts, whose obscenity is absolutely obvious."45 Additionally, in relation to these verses, Kamali mentions the legal principle of sadd al-dharai', which translates as "blocking the means to evil."46 According to the logic of this ruling, the means to doing something that is forbidden should also be forbidden. Therefore, in the present case, the means to obscenity and indecency would also fall under the rubric of the Quranic prohibition.
Among the rulings to be derived from these verses is the prohibition of "foul language." This ruling helps maintain social morality, as "a word said unwittingly and a rumour intended against only one person may have highly detrimental effects on society as a whole, its traditions, and values," especially with regard to the danger of an evil becoming commonplace once it is brought into the open. Further, hateful speech and false accusations may damage not only the accused but also others as well, since often no distinction is made between the innocent and guilty parties and restraining people becomes difficult.47
The only exception, which is an important one, as the Qur'ān notes, is for the victim "who has been wronged;" for such a person there is leeway for complaint. Thus, as long as the aim is to redress a wrong committed by someone against another person, speaking ill about the malefactor is permitted. In the broader sense, this is in the interest of the community at large because it "works as a deterrent"48 However, this exception is not a license to malign or shame the alleged wrongdoer uncontrollably, which often occurs today on social media. Thus, if the source and content of the news of a crime is known to be authentic, redressing the wrong must always be done with the intention of benefiting the community as well as the parties concerned, without acting excessively.
2.(Qur'an 24:27-29)
Believers, do not enter other people's houses until you have asked permission to do so and have greeted those inside; that is best for you: perhaps you will bear this in mind. If you find no one inside, do not enter unless you have been given permission to do so. If you are told, "Go away," then do so; that is more proper for you. For God knows well what you do.
The above verses from Sūrat Nūr are relevant in exploring the issue of invasion of privacy. According to Sayyid Qutb, they were revealed to establish a protocol for visiting people, because in pre-Islamic Arabia, people used to first enter a house and then announce themselves. This often led to invasion of privacy by compromising people's sense of security in their own homes. Thus, "God laid down the requirement to observe fine manners, making it necessary for a Muslim to announce himself and greet the people inside before entering. This establishes a friendly atmosphere right from the first moment"49
On this verse, the medieval exegete Ismā'īl ibn Kathīr (d. 1373 CE) offers a similar interpretation, noting that one may seek permission three times before entering a person's house; if after the third time, permission is not granted (or no response is given), then the individual should leave. He cites the time when Abū Mūsā went to visit 'Umar ibn al-Khattāb and sought permission to enter thrice. When no one answered, he left. Upon learning that Abū Mūsā had visited, 'Umar sent for him and inquired why he had left, to which Abū Mūsā replied, "The Prophet (peace be upon him) ordered that if permission is not given after requesting it three times, then return. I sought permission [from you] three times and when no response was given, I acted upon this hadīth and left"50
Abū al-Alā Al-Mawdūdī extends the prohibition to peeping into houses, glancing from outside, or reading someone's letter without permission. He writes that jurists also consider listening to anyone's conversations without their knowledge or approval under this ruling. Further, entering even an empty house is not allowed unless permission from the owner has already been granted. Moreover, once permission has been refused, it is obligatory upon the caller to leave. As Mawdudi writes, "Nobody has any right to compel the other person for a meeting or to embarrass him by standing obstinately at his door."51
3.(Qur'an 49:11-12)
Believers, no one group of men should jeer at another, who may after all be better than them; no one group of women should jeer at another, who may after all be better than them; do not speak ill of one another; do not use offensive nicknames for another. How bad it is to be called a mischief-maker after accepting faith! Those who do not repent of this behaviour are evildoers. O ye who believe! Avoid suspicion as much (as possible): for suspicion in some cases is a sin: and spy not on each other, nor speak ill of each other behind their backs. Would any of you like to eat the flesh of his dead brother? Nay, ye would abhor it... But fear Allah: for Allah is Oft-Returning, Most Merciful.
The above verses establish guidelines for communication about others in general, which also apply to reporters and social media users. Ibn Kathir writes that in verse 49:11, God is preventing Muslims from abusing, insulting, or defaming others, because the one being abused or insulted may be a better person in the sight of God.52 In addition, verse 49:12 exhorts Muslims to abstain from spying on and backbiting against each other. This is affirmed in the hadīth in which the Prophet is reported to have said:
Beware of suspicion, for suspicion is the worst of false tales; do not look for others' faults and do not spy, do not be jealous of one another, do not desert (cut your relation with) one another, and do not hate one another; and Oh Allah's worshipers! Be brothers (as Allah has ordered you!)53
Also relevant is the hadīth that states, "A Muslim is one from whose tongue and hand other Muslims are safe."54 Ibn Kathir cites several other narrations that also establish the prohibition on backbiting, suspicion, and insults.55 Note also that the Arabic command is: wa lā talmizū anfusakum, i.e., "do not taunt/defame yourselves," which implies that defaming a person hurts both the victim and the perpetrator.
In relation to the guidelines to be drawn from these verses, Mawdudi's contemporary exegesis is very well extrapolated and is thus especially constructive. According to him, one may derive from these verses "a detailed law of libel . . . [which] recognises a basic honour for every person."56 As per this implied law, mocking is forbidden, which includes "mimicking somebody, making pointed references to him, laughing at his words, or his works, or his appearance, or his dress, or calling people's attention to some defect or blemish in him so that others also may laugh at him."57 He also notes that the Arabic noun lamz is "very comprehensive and applies to ridiculing, reviling, deriding, jeering, charging somebody or finding fault with him, and making him the target of reproach and blame by open or tacit references."58 As such, assigning offensive or humiliating titles-such as "sinner" or "hypocrite"-to a person is also forbidden, as is personal identification by an inherent defect or blemish ("lame" or "one-eyed"). The only exemption for debasing others in this way is when the attribution has some legal grounds, as mentioned in the discussion of Q 4:148 above.
Commenting on verse 49:12, Mawdūdī lists four kinds of conjecture or speculation:
1. Praiseworthy: holding a good opinion of God, His Messenger and believers.
2. Unavoidable: such as for a judge to weigh the evidence and make the best decision possible based on "probable conjecture," since a direct insight into the facts is impossible.
3. Suspicion but not sinful: for example, when it becomes evident that someone's character or conduct is blameworthy, in which case the law does not require one to keep a good opinion about him/her.
4. Sinful: when one holds or spreads a suspicion about people without any basis at all, especially when they are apparently of righteous character.
This analysis is logical because, as is stated in the verse, ". . . suspicion in some cases is a sin," implying that in other cases it is not. Thus, as Mawdūdī rightfully notes, "Conjecture by itself is not anything forbidden; rather in some cases and situations it is commendable, in some situations inevitable, in some permissible up to a certain extent and un-permissible beyond it, and in some cases absolutely unlawful."59
On the other part of the verse, ". . . and spy not on each other," Mawdūdī writes that this implies not prying into people's secrets or looking for their defects and weaknesses. This is forbidden regardless of whether it is done "because of suspicion, or for causing harm to somebody with an evil intention, or for satisfying one's own curiosity."60 With respect to spying, Mawdūdī also includes reading people's letters, eavesdropping, glancing into people's houses, or trying to get information in various ways about the domestic life or private affairs of others. According to him, "This is a grave immorality which causes serious mischief in society."61
This command can be understood in conjunction with verses Q24:27-29, whereby it is forbidden to enter people's homes without permission. In relation to this, there are also hadīths which affirm an individual's right to privacy and concealment of faults, such as:
Oh people, who have professed belief verbally, but faith has not yet entered your hearts: Do not pry into the affairs of the Muslims, for he who will pry into the affairs of the Muslims, God will pry into his affairs, and he whom God follows inquisitively, is disgraced by Him in his own house.62
If a person conceals [the weakness of] another in this world, God will conceal their [weakness] in the Hereafter.63
Again, the exceptions to these injunctions include situations such as a person showing unmistakable signs of corruption and the intention to commit a crime or abuse; when there is a marriage proposal; or when one is about to embark on a business venture with someone. In such cases one may investigate the integrity of the parties concerned. Furthermore, as Jonathan A. C. Brown has noted, "Private misconduct that demonstrably harms or victimizes other people is not private misconduct," which acts as a clear limitation on calls for the concealment of people's faults.64 In other words, the injunction to ignore people's faults does not apply when the sin or misconduct in question involves harming others.
Implications for News Reporting and Dissemination
The above verses and their exegeses have implications for ethical news reporting and transmission. These can broadly be categorised under the following categories: (a) limitation of news content; (b) consideration of privacy; (c) promotion of justice; and (d) freedom of criticism.
a.Limitation of News Content
To begin with, Quran 4:148 sets a clear restraint on false or unverified accusations, hateful speech, and foul language. This is further reinforced in Q49:11-12, where the restriction is "not lifted just because the person doing it is a columnist and can display his skills in front of a much larger audience and with more polish."65 By the same token, making false allegations and exposing the sins of others requires more serious consideration for a news reporter because of the wider reach of modern media, unless in case of the exceptions discussed above. However, these exceptions also require judiciousness with respect to verification, the authenticity of sources, and weight of the potential benefit to the community with respect to the harm that may be caused by dissemination. It is also important to note that these limitations do not imply an acceptance of censorship (see also, "Tools for Justice" and "Freedom of Criticism" below).
b.Consideration of Privacy
The ruling against violating an individual's right to privacy applies as much to a reporter as it does to the layman. Thus, inferring from 24:27-29 and 49:12, the primary relevant command is that it is not permissible to intrude into people's homes and private lives without their consent. As Baig writes, "the right to privacy is a sacred human right which nobody (including the journalist) can violate . . . [and] in the pursuit of good, Islam does not permit, not even to a journalist, to spy and seek to confirm suspicions, slander, circulation of rumour, and name calling."66
Consideration of privacy is also relevant because it relates to the issue of sensitivity in news reporting, especially when a tragedy has occurred and the reporter tries to get a comment from the family. Sayyid Qutb notes that few people would like "to be seen in a situation of weakness, crying or angry, or in pain or distress."67 Such a consideration is also consistent with the Society of Professional Journalists (SPJ) Code of Ethics, which states that "ethical journalists treat sources, subjects and colleagues as human beings deserving of respect."68 Under this category of minimizing harm, journalists are required to treat those affected by tragedy or grief with compassion and sensitivity.
When it comes to investigative reporting, the SPJ code exhorts journalists to respect people's right to privacy and "avoid pandering to lurid curiosity."69 Similarly, commenting on Q49:12, Qutb argues that spying occurs as a result of zann (conjecture), which is often a prelude to divulging people's secrets. Thus, the Quran warns against this from a moral point of view, because mere curiosity or suspicion does not provide a warrant to violate the sanctity of people's homes and lives.
c.Promotion of Justice
In addressing limitations on content and reporting strategies, Islamic guidelines do not ignore the fact that media and reporters can also be tools for justice. This can be inferred from the exemptions provided in Q 4:148 and Q 49:11-12. Publicising the faults of others, whether or not it invades their privacy, is permitted if it serves the larger purpose of attaining justice. As Kamali writes, ". . . for those who are notorious, it is recommended that their evil is not concealed and that the matter is reported to the authorities. For satr [concealment] in such cases will only encourage further corruption and lawlessness."70
For reporters, these exceptions mean that they should function as promoters of justice within a society by acting as mediators for those who have been wronged and providing a platform for them to voice their grievances. In the context of Islamic ethics, this falls under the hisba principle of "commanding good and forbidding evil," as it aids in deterring further evil. In conventional media theory, this also fits well within the social responsibility function of the press.
For this reason, Mawdūdī writes that spying, backbiting and name-calling, while generally forbidden in Islam, are allowed in some cases, including (but not limited to) when a victim complains against his/her oppressor to seek justice; or when those spreading evil and immorality are criticized; or when the intention is to warn people of the same in order to bring about reform.71 This is because such conditions necessitate speaking about others in a manner they may not like. At the same time, however, it must be remembered that:
. . . those evil words which are allowed to be spoken openly originate with a particular person, who has been wronged, for a particular reason, and are levelled at one person in particular, the wrongdoer. The end purpose justifies the exemption given in this verse, namely, to restore justice to a person who has been wronged. To be sure, Islam does not allow libel in any form. It protects people's reputation and integrity as long as they do not wrong others. If they do so, however, they relinquish their rights of protection [emphasis added].72
For reporters in their pursuit of justice, the practical challenge is to balance the victim's right to complain with the accused's right to the presumption of innocence. Once the accused is proven guilty, there is no barrier to publishing a report with "the explicit purpose of discouraging crimes" and "would-be criminals."73
d.Freedom of Criticism
Within the objective of attaining justice, one may also include the "fourth estate"74 function of the media-namely fair and constructive criticism of the government. In other words, the above-mentioned limits on freedom of expression (in cases of defamation, libel, invasion of privacy) should not be construed to mean that there is no room for criticism. The freedom to criticise (Ar. hurrîyat al-muārada') is granted by the the Sharī'a, whereby reporters may keep tabs on the government "by means of sincere advice, constructive criticism, or even ultimately by a refusal to obey the government if it is guilty of violating the law."75
The reason this freeedom is key is because it falls under the principle of hisba. Kamali notes the centrality of this principle in condemning transgression, regardless of the perpetrator. It also falls under the category of muārada, or criticism, which has a precedent in the practice of the Prophet and his companions, and is also affirmed by the following hadīth: "The best form of jihad is to utter a word of truth to a tyrannical ruler."76 There were also occurrences where regular citizens, including women, questioned the reasoning and decision of the caliphs.77
Thus, we may infer that reporters should fulfil this principle of hisba by holding leaders and those in a position of power accountable. In this category, one may include not just public officials or leaders but also the officers of corporations, and the heads of educational, social, and financial institutions. In other words, this freedom applies with respect to any entity whose policies and actions have repercussions for society at large. However, the freedom to criticise should not be abused or based simply on suspicion; instead, it should be used "to search for the right course of conduct in order to benefit the community as a whole."78 In this regard the following rules should apply:
* The basis for criticism must be solid and the sources verified. If there are sufficient grounds for suspicion, then such suspicion would fall within the permissible category of conjecture, referred to in Arabic as zann mubahı.
* Critics must be sincere and convinced of the correctness of their own opinion, without being hypocritical or overtly political. However, even if the critic's accusations/criticisms turn out to be baseless, the critic is considered blame-free provided his/her effort was sincere.
* Finally, "criticism must be proportionate to its object . . . words used and the manner should suit the occasion and must be neither too harsh nor too feeble; however, it must also be courteous and effective."79
Case Studies in the Qur'an
Having examined the above issues, we may further elicit journalistic ethics using two historical incidents, about which Quranic verses were revealed.
1. The Ifk Incident-Qur'an 24:11-20
The first incident is the case of false accusations propagated against 'A'isha, the Prophet's wife. This is known as the "Ifk Incident,"as ifk refers to covering up the truth with absolute falsity. When used in the context of an accusation, it implies slander.80 The incident is addressed in verses Q24:11-20, which were revealed around 5 or 6 A.H., and reported in extreme detail by 'A'isha herself.81
The condensed version of the story is that A'isha accompanied the Prophet during the expedition to Banū al-Mustalaq. When returning to her camel litter (hawdaj), she realized that her bead necklace was missing so she went back for it on foot. Meanwhile, the people took her camel litter with them as they left, thinking that she was in it. She found her necklace and returned to the camp, only to discover that the army had left without her. She decided to return to her original place, hoping that the others would come back for her once they realised she was missing. While there, she fell asleep. In the morning, she was discovered by safwan ibn al-Mu'attal, who was delayed on some business. He recognized her, and took her back to the army on his camel.
Sayyid Qutb writes that Abdullah ibn Ubayy ibn Salūl, the leader of the hypocrites in Madīna, saw them approaching and upon discovering that 'A'isha was on safwan's camel, began the false accusation by saying, "Your Prophet's wife has spent the whole night with a man, and now she turns up with him leading her camel!"82 Later, back in Madīna, the accusation spread rapidly, and, as the Prophet had not received any revelation concerning the accusation, it was "the subject of conversation in Madīnah for a whole month, and he had no means to put an end to it."83 However, soon after this, the Quranic verses 24:11-20 were revealed, confirming A'isha's innocence and reprimanding the believers for not recognizing the concocted story as a lie from the outset.
Following this revelation, the principal figures involved in the slander, namely Mistah ibn Athātha, Hassān ibn Thābit and Hamna bint Jahsh, were flogged with eighty stripes, in accordance with the punishment for false accusation of slander. Abdullah ibn Ubayy ibn Salūl, who was originally responsible for the fabrication, became the butt of reproach and humiliation amongst his people after his real intentions had been unequivocally exposed to the public.84
The story circulated because Abdullah ibn Ubayy repeated it in various ways to others, without incriminating himself. Thus, while it was initially propagated by the band of hypocrites, it was also picked up and talked about by some righteous Muslims. Additionally, the dissemination became widespread because as the revelation notes, the believers failed to act appropriately. First, they did not immediately reject the story as a "blatant falsehood;"85 had they done so, the lie would have been stopped from the moment it was vocalised. Second, they did not take the steps necessary for proof in such cases, namely producing four witnesses who could confirm the accusation.86 Finally, without having any proof, they spread a story about which they had "no knowledge," thinking that it was "a light matter" when in fact it was a serious matter with harmful repercussions.87
The primary journalistic ethical consideration to be derived from this incident has to do with the verification of information. In this regard, the two most important aspects to be considered are the source of news and the content of the news.
a.Sources, Consensus, and Dissemination
Considering that the source of the accusation was a hypocrite, the first principle to be derived from it is, at the very least, to verify the report with other sources of information. However, Abdullah ibn 'Ubayy was clever enough to never publicly proclaim it himself but had others propagate it. Thus, it is possible that many of those who circulated the story did not realize where it originated. For a reporter then, it is imperative to reach the original source to determine the credibility of a story instead of circulating the news without verification purely on the basis of a seeming consensus. Similarly, for those sharing the news, the same guidelines would apply. Taking from Hadith methodology, every narrator in the chain of transmission must be identified and have adequate credibility. Further, although a mutawātir hadīth (reported by multiple people at each stage of transmission) is accepted as valid (assuming the text is valid as well), today the issue of consensus has become more problematic. Syeed Al Seini alludes to this when he writes:
When radio and television stations and the print media report similar versions of the same story, it is easy to believe that there is a consensus. If we just have a look at the origin of these similar versions, we may find only a single wire service, a single reporter, or a single public relations man. And because of the overwhelming effect of the mass media, some people who want to believe in a particular item of news tend to imagine or pretend that they have heard it from a different source.88
In A'isha's case, this is exactly what happened. One source ('Abdullah ibn Ubayy) initiated the fabrication, which was then repeated by many others, including three righteous Muslims who were deceived. Since the verses in question call for the complete rejection of "fake news," the implication for contemporary times is that a seeming consensus or widespread dissemination of a report is not enough by itself for a news item to be considered true. Additionally, Hadith methodology teaches us that while multiple sources and consensus is to be considered seriously when assessing a report's authenticity, none of the sources should be anonymous and multiple reports should be sourced independently of each other.89
b.News Content
This brings us to the actual content of the news. This story concerned the Prophet's wife, presenting a serious charge not only against the Prophet's household but also against a Companion known to be righteous (Safwan ibn Muattal). One may argue that from the perspective of content, such a report is newsworthy because it fulfils many news determinants, such as novelty, proximity, timeliness, and even conflict.90 However, Islamic values do not condone the uncontrolled utterance of information, whether in private or in public. In this case the news contained a charge of adultery against a chaste woman, which was unproven by the Qur'anically prescribed methods of proof. Thus, making it public and repeating it falls under the categories of character assassination and slander. Another important principle to be considered when reporting salacious reports are those of context and the preponderance of evidence. 'Ā'isha and Safwan openly arrived at the camp at midday to rejoin the army, and both were of righteous character, neither with any history of immoral acts or of hypocrisy or rebellion. Thus, believers were supposed to apply the principle of husn al-zann, to "think best of others," as the Quranic verses note. In this case, the evidence was not sufficient to suspect Safwän and 'Ā'isha of adultery.
Based on these considerations, when the content of the information itself remains unverified, it is not permissible to repeat it even if seemingly there was a consensus. This is consistent with the techniques used in Hadith methodology, whereby both the isnād (chain of narrators, i.e., sources) and matn (kadīth text) need to be authenticated. For reporters then, both source-checking and content-verification is necessary before circulating any story, especially if it involves an accusation against others. For individuals who disseminate the news under the pretext of preventing harm, the condition is that it must be verified as truthful; otherwise, when wrongdoing is suspected, one must make a reasonable judgment -applying the principles of benefit and relevance-on whether it is beneficial to repeat it.91
2. The Story of al-Harith and Band al-Mustalaq-Qur'an 49:6
The second case is the incident in relation to which the following verse was revealed: Oh ye who believe! If a wicked person (fasiq) comes to you with any news, ascertain the truth, lest ye harm people unwittingly, and afterwards become full of repentance for what ye have done (Qur'an 49:6).
According to the majority of exegetes, the verse was revealed about Walīd ibn 'Uqba ibn Abī Mu'īt. Ibn Kathīr writes that the Prophet Muhammad sent Walīd to collect Zakat from Banu al-Mustalaq, whose leader, al-Hārith ibn Abī Dirar al-Khuzā'ī, had recently embraced Islam. On his way, Walīd became frightened, so he went back and reported to the Prophet that al-H. arith had refused to pay the Zakāt and wanted to kill him. The Prophet got upset at this news and sent an armed force against al-Hārith, who then revealed the truth, saying that Walīd never came to him. Later, Walīd also clarified this to the Prophet, at which point this verse was revealed.92
The accounts provided by al-Qurtubī,93 al-Zamakhsharī,94 and al-Wāhidī95 are also similar except for the addition that there was an old feud or enmity (ihnā) between Walīd and the Banū al-Mustalaq dating back to the pre-Islamic period. Al-Tabarī's (1999) version of the account states that the Prophet first sent Khālid bin al-Walīd to confirm the news, which was then clarified.
What remains evident, however, is that Walīd ibn 'Uqba did report false news to the Prophet. From the story and the exegetes' inferences, we may derive the following journalistic implications in terms of source-checking, cross-checking, bias, and accountability.
a. Source-Checking
As al-Zamakhsharī notes in his commentary, the use of the indefinite article instead of the definite article for the deviant/wicked person (fāsiq) and for the piece of news (nabā') is indicative that the command to verify news in this verse is generalized to any fāsiq and any piece of news.96 Sayyid Qutb notes that the two entities involved are the receivers of the news (i.e., the believers) and the senders and/or sources of the news (i.e., thefāsiq). The latter, he writes, are more in control of the dissemination of news and thus have more responsibility for news verification. In modern terms, this group would also include journalists, as they are the first to receive any piece of news from its source.97
Therefore, the first guideline to be derived from this example once again has to do with ensuring source reliability-the verse cautions people against accepting news from unreliable sources98 because they may be prone to lying or being mistaken.99 As Mawdūdī also notes, from this verse emerges a "Shar'ī rule which has a very broad application."100 Ibn Kathīr makes the same observation, noting that God ordered people to first confirm news that comes from a fāsiq so that one may not wrongly judge a report based on insufficient evidence (as the Prophet was about to do) and cause undue harm. He also explains that this was why "groups of scholars refused to accept the narrations from an anonymous source, because there was a possibility for him to be afāsiq."101
In addition, the medieval commentator al-Qurtubī notes that in this verse, "there is evidence for accepting news coming from someone who is righteous because the order of verification is only for news from afāsiq."102 In other words, if the source of a news report has demonstrated unquestionable integrity and sound character, then it would be permissible to accept a piece of news from him/ her. Since news is a trust (amāna), its truth-value becomes "so paramount and overriding in Muslim cultural theory that the question of character, competence, and integrity of journalists and their sources in the newsgathering and reporting processes assumes critical importance."103
b. Cross-Checking
However, if the original source is questionable, the command in the verse is to verify the news before disseminating it. It is worthy of note that the injunction in this example is not to reject the piece of news altogether (as in 'A'isha's case); rather, it is to verify it, presumably with other sources. We may derive this principle of cross-checking from the story as narrated by al-Tabarī, in which the Prophet sent Khalid ibn al-Walīd to verify that the Banu Mustalaq had indeed refused to pay Zakāt and were planning to attack Walīd; which was then denied by al-H. ārith.
Thus, a judicious reporter should confirm a piece of news received from a questionable source by consulting other reliable sources. In the book Gathering and Writing News, John Paul Jones notes that "the newspaper business is no place for the gullible or naive reporter who accepts facts, figures, and opinions from speakers, interviewees, and government and business officials without question."104 Furthermore, the SPJ Code of Ethics also exhorts reporters to "test the accuracy of information from all sources and exercise care to avoid inadvertent error."105
c. The Bias Factor
Walīd's story, his reporting of false news, and the possible existence of an old enmity or feud between him and the Banū al-Mustalaq are also indicative that it is possible for a source of news to have ulterior motives behind his/her statement. Therefore, the reporter must take into consideration a possible bias factor when interviewing a source.
In this regard, if a piece of news involves another party (as Walīd's news involved al-Hārith and the Banū al-Mustalaq), then the reporter should make every effort to contact all of the individuals concerned, not merely to verify the report (as in al-Hārith's case) but also to incorporate other sides of the story. This falls under the "fairness" or "balance" principle set forth in contemporary journalism guidelines according to which, depending on the nature of the news, a journalist should get in touch with the primary sources involved "instead of quoting second-hand sources."106
d. Accountability
Finally, the verse and its reasons for revelation (asbāb al-nuzūl) make it evident that whoever receives news and then acts upon it should be accountable for his/her actions. It is clear in the verse that news reports are to be verified lest unwarranted harm be done to another and then regretted. In this example, had the false news about the refusal to pay Zakāt not been crosschecked and corrected, many lives might have been lost for no good cause. Thus, it is important to remember that "news (knowledge or information in general) has consequences, which may adversely affect individuals, groups or societies, and as a result, it must display a strong sense of responsibility and accountability on the part of the journalists and their organisations as well as the entire society."107
Similarly, the SPJ Code of Ethics states that journalists are accountable to their audiences, which involves admitting mistakes and correcting them promptly.108 News is a trust that must be handled with care, implying that if a reporter receives a piece of news that turns out to be false after one has disseminated it, then one should be accountable for the responsibility to verify it. Accountability also relates directly to the concepts discussed earlier in this article, namely that of God-consciousness (taqwā) and trust (amāna), whereby a journalist must be conscious of his/her abilities and use them ethically.
The previous example of 'Ā'isha is similar to that of al-Hārith in some respects. Chronologically, the incident involving Ā'isha occurred first, and the verses concerning it were revealed in 5 or 6 A.H. Al-Harith's incident occurred in 9 A.H. during the Year of Deputations. In both cases, the source of news was questionable: Walīd ibn 'Uqba had a reputation for being untrustworthy while 'Abdullah ibn Ubayy ibn Salūl was a known munäfiq-the leader of the hypocrites.
Comparing the two cases, we might conclude that while the sources in both cases were unreliable, the piece of news in the latter example was relatively more likely to be true since the Banū al-Mustalaq had recently accepted Islam and thus it could have been possible for them to renounce their Islam and refuse to pay Zakāt. Hence the Quranic injunction was to verify the news, whereas in the first case it was to reject the news outright.
Conclusion
In this article, I have attempted to extrapolate some Islamic guidelines for news reporting and dissemination based on Quranic and Hadith sources. Many of these guidelines align with long-established best practices in the profession of journalism. However, in an age when most people have access to public platforms via social media and the Internet, these guidelines can be applied more broadly.
In summary, reporters are first to be guided with the highest level of taqwā or God-consciousness, and should consider their vocation as a trust from God. Regarding specific practices, news content should not be offensive, defamatory, or intrusive upon people's private lives. Thus, libel and slander are explicitly forbidden, publicizing the faults of others should be done only when reporting fulfills the ethical principle of forbidding evil or redressing a wrong. Even in this case, however, the news should be confirmed as true and unnecessary defamation should be avoided. Moreover, reporters and news disseminators should thoroughly examine the credibility of their sources, including consulting multiple sources if there is any reasonable doubt. However, the call for thorough investigation and verification does not imply censorship or the inability to exercise freedom of criticism. Rather, calling out wrongdoing committed by governments, institutions, and individuals is considered an important function of reporters, whether they are journalists in the traditional sense or social media commentators in the modern sense. These guidelines simply aim to ensure that high ethical standards are considered when engaging in reporting or sharing information.
Finally, it is worthwhile to point out that this research has set forth a theoretical understanding of news and information dissemination building on exegetical interpretations. In today's reality, ethical issues and specific cases may be more complex with multiple factors at play, and a dichotomy between theory and practice may often result. Thus, it is useful to examine more specific case studies-both historical and contemporary-to see how the theoretical issues discussed in this article have been applied to practice.
