Introduction
Disinformation has emerged as a global challenge, amplified by the rapid circulation of manipulated narratives on digital platforms. Events like COVID- 19 pandemic (Baines & Elliot, 2020), Brexit, and recent armed conflicts illustrate how disinformation can erode public trust and deepen societal polarization. Despite growing academic interest in this phenomenon, empirical research on Portugal’s specific disinformation dynamics – including both domestic and foreign influences, societal vulnerabilities, and regulatory frameworks – remains limited. This study addresses this gap by investigating expert perceptions of disinformation in the Portuguese context, focusing on how it manifests, the vulnerabilities it exploits, and the challenges of regulation. The central research question guiding this study is: how do specialists in Portugal perceive and interpret the dynamics of disinformation, particularly in relation to its political dimensions, social vulnerabilities, and regulatory responses?
While Portugal shares many features common to other European countries facing disinformation challenges (e.g., the emergence of populist right-wing movements), it also shows several specific attributes: it presents a small electorate of around 9.3 million (PORDATA, 2022); it has experienced nearly five decades of dictatorship, ending in 1974; it ranks among the higher-trust countries in terms of mainstream media consumption and trust in the news in general (Newman et al., 2023); and low political polarization. These characteristics might interplay with the disinformation dynamics and vulnerabilities, as will be further developed.
Disinformation typically involves three defining elements: 1) the falsity or misleading nature of the information, 2) the potential for social harm, and 3) the deliberate intent of the actor (Ó Fathaigh et al., 2021). Across the European Union, approaches to regulating disinformation vary considerably, in some cases overlapping with criminal provisions, thereby creating uncertainties in enforcement and governance. The literature has been progressively identifying and documenting multiple harmful impacts of disinformation. One of the most pressing threats is the erosion of democratic processes, including diminished trust in institutions and interference in elections (Chenzi, 2021; Colomina et al., 2021). Additionally, counter-disinformation measures can pose their own challenges, potentially constraining freedoms of expression and information (Miró-Llinares & Aguerri, 2023). Disinformation can also have repercussions in other domains, including public health (Wang et al., 2019), and it heightens social polarization (Colomina et al., 2021; Johnson, 2018). Consequently, gives rise to a range of negative attitudes and behaviours, fostering strong animosities, demonisation, hate speech and violence towards opposing political parties (Chenzi, 2021; Fokou et al., 2024; Iyengar et al., 2019; McLaughlin, 2020), increasing domestic terrorism (Piazza, 2022). For Criminology, information disorders are an appealing topic due to its connections with transgressive behaviors and the modalities of social control (Innes & Innes, 2023).
To address these challenges, this study employs a qualitative approach grounded in expert interviews, aiming to capture insights into disinformation’s underlying mechanism, associated risks, and existing regulatory frameworks. Interviews offer in‐depth perspectives on how disinformation is perceived, confronted, and shaped by those with deep knowledge of the phenomenon. By engaging specialists from diverse fields – such as academia, media and journalism, cybersecurity and policy, we sought to map the core attributes of disinformation in Portugal, identify its key problematizations, locate the most pressing concerns, and to obtain perspectives that might remain obscured by quantitative or documentary analyses.
The article is structured as follows: the first section presents the literature review, focusing on the conceptualization and nuances of disinformation, the vulnerabilities of the phenomenon, the challenges of regulation and, finally, the analysis of disinformation through a criminological lens. The second describes the objectives and outlines the qualitative methodology and expert interview process. The third section details the key findings, organized into four themes: internal disinformation, external disinformation, vulnerabilities, and regulatory perceptions. Lastly, we will discuss the results and its implications.
Theoretical Background
Understanding Information Pollution: Disinformation and Related Terms
Information pollution, an umbrella concept encompassing disinformation, misinformation, malinformation and similar problematics forms of speech – has intensified in the digital era (Kalsnes, 2018). Often referred to as an “information disorder” (Gelfert, 2018) it has been at the forefront of the European security agenda for nearly a decade, particularly since the 2016 U.S. elections popularized the term “fake news” (Allcott et al., 2019; Marwick & Lewis, 2017; Petratos, 2021). Despite growing attention, conceptual debates persist, especially regarding tensions between regulation and free speech (Wardle, 2024).
Disinformation refers to the deliberate spread of false, misleading, or decontextualized information with the intent to deceive (Wardle & Derakhshan, 2017; Oremus, 2017). It contrasts with misinformation, which is the unintentional dissemination of inaccurate or misleading information (Kavanagh & Rich, 2018). A third category, malinformation, consists of genuine information repurposed to inflict harm, such as leaks, harassment and hate speech (Baines & Elliott, 2020; Wardle & Derakshan, 2017). Although distinct, all can be challenging to regulate, not least because they often fall within the scope of legally protected speech ( Wardle, 2024; Wardle & Derakshan, 2017 ).
Disinformation can range from isolated, low-impact incidents to coordinated, wide-reaching campaigns, whether orchestrated internally or by foreign ( Caramancion, 2020; Jack, 2017; Rubin, 2019 ) . External disinformation, specifically a form of hybrid warfare consisting of highly organized campaigns by foreign actors that aim to destabilize their target State is the highest danger by governing institutions and of the cornerstones of European defense strategy (European Commission, 2016). A 2023 analysis by the European External Action Services (EEAS, 2023), found that over 40% of disinformation)incidents across Europe originated from Russian or Chinese State-related sources, frequently amplifying each other’s narratives. Similar findings are evidenced in an Iberifier (IBERIFIER, 2023) report of Portuguese disinformation from 2020 to 2023, in which the majority of content was exogenously produced, consisting of translations permeated with known Russian terminology.
Disinformation Vulnerabilities: Explaining Factors
With a growing reliance on social media as a primary source of news, the"polluted"digital (Wardle & Derakhshan, 2017) ecosystem poses significant challenges to political communication, particularly within democratic societies. It is then crucial to understand which conditions foster the diffusion and consumption of disinformation.
Several authors (e.g., Dragomir et al., 2024; Humprecht et al., 2020; Narciso & Costa, 2021) have identified a set of conditions that can weaken the resilience of countries to problems of online disinformation, such as high political polarization (e.g., Allcott & Gentzkow, 2017), low public trust in news media (Boulianne et al., 2022), reliance on social media for news (Shehata & Stomback, 2021) and highly fragmented media ecosystems (e.g., Shin & Thorson, 2017).
Results have been showing that the use of social media for news increases exposure to and sharing of disinformation (Meraz & Papacharissi, 2013; Shin & Thorson, 2017). For instance, Boulianne et al., (2022) found that social media use was associated with awareness, exposure and sharing of disinformation, indicators of lack of resilience. Accordingly, Roozenbeek et al., (2020) showed that being exposed to information about the COVID- 19 on social media was associated with higher susceptibility to misinformation. More recently, Kont et al., (2024) observed that out of the 12 studies that investigated social media use, 50% found a positive correlation between this factor and disinformation believe or sharing. These results were interpreted as the amplifying nature of social media.
Polarization of society is an important factor related to limited resilience (Humprecht et al., 2020; Alccott & Gentzkow, 2017; Shin & Thorson, 2017; Benkler et al., 2018). In polarized contexts, individuals face deviating representations of the reality, becoming harder to differentiate between false and accurate information, undermining their resilience to disinformation (Craft et al., 2017; Humprecht et al., 2020; Swire et al., 2017). Relatedly is the populist communication. Populism and disinformation are closely linked, as both involve spreading partisan information to support one group while discrediting others. Partisan disinformation aligns with populist narratives by creating a moral divide between"us"(truthful) and"them"(fake). Populist political actors often use in-group/out-group narratives and blame certain groups, such as elites or immigrants, for current problems (Engesser et al., 2017). Furthermore, they often blame the media for spreading"fake news"that misleads the public and advance their own agendas (Ross & Rivers, 2018).
Low trust in traditional news outlets often leads individuals to seek information from alternative sources, which may lack credibility and facilitate the dissemination of false content (e.g., Benkler et al., 2018; Humprecht et al., 2020; Tsfati & Cappella, 2003; Zimmermann & Kohring, 2020). Ognyanova et al., (2020) collecting data for 3000 respondents from the U.S., found that fake news exposure was associated with a decline in mainstream media trust among respondents. Also, Humprecht (2023), in a comparative study, observed that citizens with high trust in news on social media were most willing to like, share and comment on disinformation across all countries.
Trust in in political institutions has been linked to resilience against disinformation. In less polarized societies, political trust can have a protective effect against disinformation. For instance, Zimmermann and Kohring (2020) found that in Germany, political trust decreased the likelihood of believing in disinformation. Therefore, distrust toward democratic institutions and actors can make individuals more susceptible to disinformation (Stier et a., 2020). Humprecht (2023) found that trust in politicians and general attitudes toward democracy were more important than trust in the news media and that low satisfaction with democracy was related to the willingness to disseminate disinformation.
Advertising markets might also be relevant to the disinformation resilience. Large online advertising ecosystem triggers sensational and emotional content that captures user’s attention. Platforms like Google and Facebook profit from views and clicks, which encourages the spread of disinformation. In turn, this ecosystem allows smaller publishers to operate outside traditional ethical constraints, reinforcing the appeal of publishing such content (Tambini, 2017).
Regulating Disinformation
Efforts to combat disinformation have intensified at both national and supranational levels, with the EU playing a central role in shaping a multi-layered regulatory framework. Key initiatives include the Code of Practice on Disinformation (2018a, 2022), the Action Plan Against Disinformation (European Commission, 2018b ), and the Digital Services Act (DSA) (European Parliament & Council of the EU, 2022), which aim to mitigate disinformation’s impact by defining the responsibilities of digital platforms. A significant body of scholarly literature examines the evolution of these efforts, focusing on key milestones, governance models, and regulatory challenges. Central questions address what should be regulated, how, and by whom, considering both normative challenges—such as balancing disinformation control with fundamental rights—and instrumental challenges related to policy design and enforcement. This analysis will explore two interconnected themes: Compatibility with Human Rights and Disinformation Governance. These issues are particularly evident in content moderation, where tensions between regulatory control, platform governance, and fundamental rights emerge.
Disinformation can undermine freedom of expression, which encompasses media freedom, pluralism, and the right of citizens to hold opinions and receive and impart information. However, measures to combat disinformation may also restrict this freedom, which is safeguarded under European fundamental rights law, as established in the Charter of Fundamental Rights of the European Union (EU, 2012, Article 11) and the European Convention on Human Rights (Council of Europe, 1950, Article 10; hereinafter ‘ECHR’). While freedom of expression is a fundamental right, it is not absolute. Under Article 10(2) of the ECHR, it may be restricted for specific reasons, including “public safety, national security, public health, and morals,” with limited grounds for restriction in political speech and matters of public interest. The balance between combating disinformation and protecting freedom of expression varies across legal, political, and cultural contexts, influenced by differences in the interpretation of legality, necessity, and proportionality. As Helm and Nasu (2021) highlight, necessity concerns both the scope and extent of restrictions—defining what content should be restricted and determining the measures applied to false or misleading statements. Case law from the European Court of Human Rights (Mazur & Chochia, 2022; Vese, 2022; Helm et al., 2021; Hoboken et al., 2019) reveals the narrow path to justifying restrictions based solely on the falsity or misleading nature of information.
There is broad consensus that European efforts to combat disinformation depend on achieving a delicate balance: regulating the influence of digital platforms’ business models to reduce their potential to distort public opinion, while also safeguarding freedoms (e.g., Epstein, 2020; Kachelmann & Reiners, 2023; Hoboken et al., 2021). However, several challenges remain. One major difficulty is the lack of a consistent definition of disinformation, which complicates the implementation of regulatory measures across different jurisdictions, as already highlighted. While some definitional elements are largely agreed upon, their empirical operationalization and verification remain contingent and inconsistent. The types of communication that may fall under disinformation are diverse, each operating in distinct contexts with varying intentions, audiences, and regulatory challenges. Additionally, disinformation is problematized in multiple, often conflicting ways. It is framed as a threat to fundamental rights, a danger to liberal democracy—eroding trust in institutions and electoral processes—and as a security issue linked to foreign interference, hybrid threats, and even national stability. These competing narratives inevitably make the challenge not only one of definition but also one of governance, with significant implications for the protection of democratic values (e.g., Hoboken, Ó Fathaigh, & Appleman, 2021). Within the EU, two key issues are particularly relevant: the consistency of the disinformation definition across the EU framework and its relationship with regulatory responses, and the alignment of these efforts with definitions and legal provisions adopted by individual Member States. There is general consistency in the definitional elements found in EU regulatory instruments, that align with definitions proposed by Wardle and Derakhshan (2017), the European Commission (2018c), and the High-Level Expert Group on Fake News and Online Disinformation (Directorate-General for Communications Networks, Content and Technology, 2018). The conceptual boundaries are also delineated through exclusions such as “misleading advertising, reporting errors, satire and parody, or clearly identified partisan news and commentary” (European Commission, 2018a, Preamble). Crucial is the distinction consistently made between illegal content and disinformation. Disinformation is not illegal per se. If it includes illegal content, it falls under the legal frameworks for illicit material or behaviour at the EU or national level. The European Regulators Group for Audiovisual Media (ERGA, 2020) identified a ‘highly unharmonized’ approach to disinformation, both in definition and regulation, among Member States. This lack of consistency complicates cross-border services and presents challenges for achieving harmonization within the EU. Echoing concerns raised by the European Commission (2020b), the report highlights the risks to freedom of expression posed by legal provisions introduced during the COVID- 19 pandemic, including criminal measures with unclear definitions. Recently, concerns have been raised about what is seen an expansion and ambiguity in the EU’s definition of disinformation, particularly in the Strengthened Code of Practice. This includes ‘misinformation, disinformation, information influence operations, and foreign interference’ under the broader term ‘disinformation’ (Peukert, 2023). Furthermore, the DSA mentions disinformation only in its recitals, but it is addressed within the broader framework of"crisis"response and"systemic risk,"especially regarding obligations imposed on Very Large Online Platforms (VLOPs) (Ferreau, 2024; Ó Fathaigh, Helberger, & Appelman, 2021; Husovec, 2024; Peukert, 2023). This inclusion raises concerns about the potential conflation of disinformation with illegal content, which could lead platforms to over-remove or preemptively restrict content to avoid regulatory scrutiny.
The Strengthened Code, supported by the DSA, marks a significant shift from the (regulated) self-regulatory approach of the 2018 Code of Practice to a co-regulatory model (e.g. Kachelmann & Reiners, 2023). This transition reflects broader changes in the EU’s approach to disinformation, seeking to address the limitations of earlier frameworks. By moving from voluntary, industry-driven measures to a more structured system involving both public authorities and private platforms, the Code aims to enhance accountability and transparency. The combined framework emphasizes shared responsibility for content moderation and risk mitigation, while balancing the protection of fundamental rights, particularly freedom of expression.
The governance theoretical framework provides a flexible tool for understanding disinformation regulation, incorporating institutional, economic, and social dimensions of regulatory design. This perspective goes beyond static legal interpretations, emphasizing the negotiated nature of regulatory power and the complex interplay between state, market, and civil society actors. These features are essential to understanding the evolving mechanisms of disinformation control. A central focus is platform governance, where private companies enforce content moderation rules, often in response to state regulatory efforts. As Balkin (2018) points out, digital platforms have fundamentally altered the landscape of public discourse. Once perceived as open, democratic spaces for free expression, these platforms prioritize business models that focus on profit and engagement, often at the expense of content neutrality and democratic values. They curate what information is seen, by whom, and how it spreads. Similarly, Gillespie (2018a) argues that content moderation is not merely a tool for cleaning up the digital space but is integral to platforms’ business models—maximizing user engagement, minimizing harmful content, and complying with legal requirements. Content moderation shapes the online environment, making it essential to recognize as a core business strategy rather than just a regulatory or ethical issue. As Bayer (2024) highlights, the Digital Services Act (DSA) demonstrates the growing awareness of this issue, with increasing safeguards centered on transparency and informing users about changes to content made by platforms. However, as Rone (2021, p.188) emphasizes, the focus on platform governance should not overshadow “democratically negotiated regulation,” as legitimacy and efficiency in democratic states depend on “citizen participation in discussions on what we want to regulate, how, and why.
Towards a “criminology of Disinformation”: Moral Panics and Governance As Frameworks of Analysis
Although criminology has maintained a relatively understated role in disinformation research, the criminological literature on social control offers a rich and diverse field that could provide valuable insights into understanding disinformation. As Innes (2020,12023) demonstrates, the study of policing and crime management—particularly with respect to complex criminal problems—offers important lessons for combating influence operations, as well as for recognizing the unintended effects that arise from such control strategies. Perhaps the theoretical framework of “moral panics” (Cohen, 1972; Garland, 2008; Young, 1971) represents the most explored avenue by criminology scholars. This body of work has primarily focused on how social media platforms amplify moral panics (Lavorgna & Massa, 2024; Miró-Llinares & Aguerri, 2023; Walsh, 2020). By accelerating the spread of alarming narratives, social media fuel outrage and reinforce perceptions of social disorder. Their algorithmic structures prioritize sensational content, making certain issues appear more urgent or threatening than they are. In this way, social media act as producers of moral panic, shaping public discourse in forms that exaggerate fears and drive regulatory responses. However, an interesting dimension worth more exploring is the dual role of social media in the regulation of disinformation. Not only do social media platforms act as producers of moral panics, but they are also often cast as folk devils—scapegoats blamed for societal decline, misinformation, and manipulation. They are frequently portrayed as enablers of democratic erosion, the source of mass deception, and the primary culprits behind the spread of harmful content. This framing positions them as villains that must be controlled to protect the public good. This dual role creates a significant tension. This dual role creates a significant tension. If social media are both the engines of moral panic and key scapegoats within it, while also playing a central role in regulating disinformation, this perspective could offer an insightful framework for addressing the regulatory conundrum.
Another criminological perspective that could enrich our understanding of disinformation is the study of security and policing within frameworks of governance (Braithwaite, 2000; Crawford, 2006; Loader, 2000; Shearing & Wood, 2003) and governmentality (Rose, O’Malley & Valverde, 2006). In recent years, disinformation has increasingly been framed as a security issue, raising concerns about its impact on public order, democracy, and social cohesion.
As Loader (2000) notes, contemporary policing extends beyond traditional government structures, reflecting a plural, networked model of security governance. A similar lens could be applied to disinformation regulation, which involves a complex network of state agencies, private platforms, transnational bodies, and civil society actors. Each of these actors contributes to the definition, detection, and suppression of harmful content, creating a multifaceted approach to governance.
By drawing on criminological insights into the diffusion of security responsibilities, risk governance, and market-driven policing, scholars can better understand how power, legitimacy, and control operate in the regulation of disinformation. This approach offers a more nuanced view of how disinformation is governed within contemporary democracies, highlighting the complex interplay between various actors and the challenges of regulating such a pervasive phenomenon.
Current Study
This qualitative study aimed at examining the specialist’s perceptions about key aspects related to disinformation in Portugal. Concretely, it sought to explore: - How they describe and problematize the patterns of internal and external disinformation, particularly politically orientated, in the Portuguese context;

- Their knowledge and views about eventual efforts of foreign state targeting the country, detailing the evidence about specific disinformation tactics and techniques that have been deployed as part of the above efforts;

- Their perceptions of political and public vulnerabilities to disinformation, across a range of issues and sectors of society;

- How they perceive and evaluate the regulation of disinformation, both within Portugal and by the European Union.


Methods
The present study, funded by the Cardiff University, took place in the Portuguese context and aimed to understand experts’ views on the dynamics and regulation of disinformation through semi-structured interviews. Our research team consisted of six researchers, mainly from the University of Porto, all of them involved in the different phases of the study (preparation of the interview guide, data collection and analysis). Nevertheless, to ensure reliability, a single researcher conducted all the interviews, consistently applying the same interview guide throughout.
Participants
In total, 24 experts from across diverse domains in the Portuguese context were invited to participate, of whom 15 accepted. Participants (Table 1) were invited on an individual basis or through an invitation to relevant institutions for the topic under analysis. However, even in this latter case, their participation maintains an individual status, meaning they do not necessarily represent the interests of the institution. The interviewees for this study represent a diverse range of expertise, encompassing various fields relevant to the analysis of disinformation and information security in Portugal. Among them are specialists in international relations, cybersecurity, political science, journalism, and disinformation studies, providing a comprehensive perspective on the topic. Table 1. Instrument and Procedures
The interview guide was developed based on the objectives of the research project. To achieve an overview of disinformation, it comprised the following dimensions: i) the identification of patterns (internal and external; thematic areas of disinformation, the processes of production and dissemination, and its evolution), aiming also to compare Portugal’s experience with other countries; ii) characterization of the political disinformation (campaigns, evolution, and potential influence on elections and other societal impacts); iii) perceptions related to the actions by foreign states targeting countries of interest; iv) vulnerabilities to disinformation; v) perception and evaluation of the regulation of the phenomenon, specifically the national and EU-level frameworks.
This study took four months between the development of interview guide and completion of data analysis (starting in September 2023). Invitation email for the interviews was sent out between late September and early October. Upon accepting the invitation to participate in the project, experts were contacted directly by the interviewer to schedule the interview dates. All interviews, after consent, were audio recorded and transcribed for ease of data analysis. The interviews ranged between 45 min (E4) and 2 h (E1) and were conducted via Zoom or Teams, according to participants’ preferences. Data from the interview transcriptions were anonymized, and each participant was assigned a unique code. To analyze and interpret the data accurately and to avoid misinterpretations, the statements of the interviewees were contextualized within the discourses of the subjects and the depth of the issues they raised. This approach highlighted the complexity, nuances and uncertainties that the participants themselves expressed in their opinions and judgments. The results were analyzed through thematic analysis (Braun & Clarke, 2006).
Results
The results are organized and presented according to the following main themes: (i) Disinformation of internal origin, (ii) External disinformation, (iii) Vulnerability factors and (iv) Disinformation regulation.
Disinformation of Internal Origin: An Evolution
According to participants’ perceptions, even though the situation of disinformation to which the Portuguese are exposed is similar to that of other countries, its internal production remains at a"childish"level. In terms of both quantity and"sophistication,"it is still underdeveloped compared to other European Union countries, notably France and Germany.
The evolution and trends outlined by the interviewees share consistent elements. First,"there is a before and after COVID,"with the pandemic context intensifying the production and dissemination of disinformation, both external and internal. With the severity of events and isolation, an online battle over the pandemic emerged. There was also a highly globalized circulation through organized movements, and there was an immense wave of disinformation about the pandemic, which then evolved towards the vaccines. Furthermore, it evolved within the same groups and channels, originating from the same disinformation sources, towards the war in Ukraine. This also connects to conspiracy theories about the globalist agenda. (E12)

Although themes directly related to the pandemic were highlighted, participants emphasized the growth of disinformation specifically related to political nature or motivation. Among the interviewees, it is common to attribute this growth to the populist far-right represented by the Chega party, which, precisely in the 2019 legislative elections, gained parliamentary representation for the first time. Moreover, the electoral campaign, as some interviewees mentioned, had already been marked by an intensification of this trend, with obvious targeting in the content spread that aimed at prominent figures from left-wing parties. The post-election presence of the Chega leader became daily news, paving the way for an intensification (even a normalization, for some interviewees) of populist themes on social media , especially focusing on corruption, which tend to involve cases or cast suspicions on opposing political figures or journalists. From 2020 - 21 onwards, especially after the last legislative elections in Portugal, this political space started having a media voice; in other words, the members of parliament are interviewed on television, the leader gives interviews to newspapers, etc. That is an important qualitative change. (E8)

Some of the interviewees emphasize that Chega might have relied on the"know-how"of extreme right-wing political forces in Europe to implement propaganda/disinformation strategies, especially in a digital environment. Apart from Chega ’s official page (Folha Nacional), a series of other pages are mentioned as notable producers and disseminators in this trend, some of them with names and imagery inducing their identification as journalistic entities, fake profiles, and groups present on social media, with special prominence on Facebook.
The centrality assumed by the theme of corruption in this period is highlighted by the interviewed experts as a Portuguese specificity in the European context: unlike the prominence in Europe of xenophobic and racist content associated with immigration, Portugal is closer to the predominant themes in Latin America, notably Brazil. Two motivations are highlighted: i) political, with the main objective being"the discrediting of holders of public office, institutions, and public policies”, and ii) economic, fuelled by the apparent indifference of digital platforms to complaints. Unlike the other European countries, the prominent theme [of disinformation] in Portugal was corruption. This was not observed in any other European country. In Spain, the main issue was primarily the Catalan Independence movement and the nationalist response to it, greatly intensified by an extreme-right party, VOX. In the other European countries, especially in France, Germany, and Italy, it was related to religious issues, meaning anti-Islamism and anti-immigration discourse. (E8)

Therefore, disinformation in Portugal initially rotated around corruption rather than immigration. Part of this emphasis stems from the emergence of the populist far-right, which gained traction in recent years. In contrast, much of the European far-right had already been targeting immigration for some time. According to participants, the Portuguese context only recently began mirroring broader European narratives – particularly around immigration. In fact, over the last few years, not only the disinformation intensified, but also the disinformation themes have increasingly aligned with those prevalent in other European countries. Xenophobic and racist discourses have emerged strongly, fuelled by recent mutations in immigration, both in volume and in growing heterogeneity of countries of origin. The increasing visibility of these “new immigrants” in public spaces is associated to what several interviewees identify as a new trend on social media : the increase in content and reactions to content shared that are markedly xenophobic and racist.
As some interviewees highlight, however, it is not possible to reduce the intensification of disinformation and internal disinformation campaigns, notably organic in nature, as well as the thematic trends, solely to the growth of far-right movements and a certain"normalization"of their narratives in the political and media space. There are movies, messages that are obviously propagated on social media - and WhatsApp continues to be frankly the major disseminator – (…) they are also people who don’t have that concrete connection [to far-right parties], and they can be both young and old who are susceptible to this discourse. For example, if today there are no houses, it’s also because of"them,"although people know that they live in miserable conditions, renting a bed... (E13)

Moreover, as referred to by the interviewees, there is a thematic diversification in disinformation and the proliferation of content that, while not necessarily false, shows increasing"polarization"and heightened emotional charge. The most mentioned"fracturing micro-themes"are content about gender and race issues, where the woke inspiration is evident, as well as environmental and climate activism. The Ukraine war and especially the recent conflict in the Middle East, not only intensified disinformation, but also contributed to the growing polarization of the online"domestic"ecosystem.
External Disinformation
The participants’ approach regarding external disinformation focuses on campaigns conducted or supported by foreign States or external non-State entities that challenge the interests of the Portuguese State or its institution, with a political motivation. Throughout the interviews, participants presented a narrower or broader interpretations of what national interest entails. In the narrower sense , it refers to disinformation that may affect Portugal’s external image and consequently the pursuit of its specific national interests. The broader sense encompasses externally sourced content aimed at undermining citizens’ trust in national institutions, international institutions of which Portugal is a member (such as the European Union, NATO, or the United Nations), or the principles and values inherent to the democratic rule of law.
Russia is identified as the primary source of external disinformation, often targeting other states and aligning with far-right European movements. This alliance fosters distrust in national and European institutions, democracy, and the rule of law. Interviewees noted that Russia’s role was a key factor in the EU’s concerns about disinformation influencing the 2018 elections.
It is unanimous that Portugal is not considered a primary target for external disinformation campaigns. While part of the EU, making it indirectly targeted through broader campaigns against European institutions (e.g., Brexit, European elections, Ukraine conflict), its small size and limited electorate reduce its strategic appeal compared to larger nations like France or Germany, and it is not an Eastern European country where Russian disinformation finds more acceptance. Because we have a small electorate, we are a small country, and it is much more important, obviously for Russia, in this matter, to concentrate its resources, let us say, its tools, its instruments in France or Germany, in the United Kingdom, in the United States, etc. (E1)
Portugal fits into the vast international and Western spectrum (…), but considering (…) a lesser relevance compared to other nation-states, we are not as exposed to these information disorder campaigns. (E4)

As one international relations expert notes,"linguistic universes"play a key role in the cost–benefit analysis of external campaigns supported by States. While Portugal shares the Portuguese language with Brazil and several African countries, where disinformation campaigns, particularly from Russia, are rising, Latin America and Africa hold distinct geopolitical and economic interests for Russia and China. This mismatch between the"linguistic universe"and geopolitical interests diminishes Portugal’s relevance as a specific target of disinformation linked to these regions.
Participants highlight the Portugal’s historical unity, in contrast to Spain’s regional divisions, serving as a mitigating factor against disinformation risks. Additionally, Portugal maintains a political system characterized by the rotation of the two main parties of the democratic regime since the April Revolution ( Socialist Party and Social Democratic Party ), despite recent fragmentation on the right with the emergence of two new parties ( Chega and Iniciativa Liberal ).
In sum, in a broader sense, Portugal is a potential target since, as one of the interviewees says,"no NATO country is peripheral, no matter how small".
Vulnerability to (political) Disinformation
The participants discussed some elements influencing Portugal’s vulnerability to disinformation in general, and particularly to politically motivated disinformation. The factors that gather consensus regarding this alleged vulnerability are at the intersection between sociodemographic, sociocultural vectors, as well as a third factor related to the state of the media and journalism in Portugal.
Portugal is described as a small country marked by a pronounced aging of its population and significant asymmetries between the coastal and inland regions. The country’s population size is cited as one of the factors contributing to the relatively low level of internal disinformation production associated with the less sophisticated means used. In Portugal, the rhetoric of certain fringe groups that heavily dwell in disinformation does not yet have a dimension that could contaminate confidence in journalism and verified information. (E11)

The aging of the population is invoked as both a factor of greater and lesser vulnerability. On one hand, it contributes to lower population qualification levels, resulting in lower digital and media literacy, thus constituting a risk factor for the spread of disinformation and potentially intensifying post-exposure effects. On the other hand, it is associated with a more conservative media diet which tends to be faithful to mainstream media and resistant and skeptical toward innovations, therefore acting as a potential protective factor.
Regarding the media landscape in Portugal, participants explore two interconnected vectors: i) the relationship between citizens and the media and ii) the crisis of the media and journalism. Most interviewees highlight the higher relative trust by the Portuguese in content produced by news agencies alongside greater distrust regarding the quality of information conveyed on social media . Despite the increasing importance of social media , the preference for more conventional means remains evident, i.e., television. We still believe in what television says in the most well-known, more traditional institutional channels (…) But I don’t know if there is any traditionalism because we had fifty years of dictatorship that made us a bit more delayed, and our way of thinking might have been a bit different from other countries that did not go through this experience. (E2)

Yet, this does not mean that despite greater trust, they do not equally contribute to the dissemination of false information. In other words, they may be less vulnerable to its effects, especially in forming their opinions. Participants highlighted the COVID- 19 pandemic as an example where, despite widespread misinformation, citizens showed strong trust in authorities and health experts, leading to mass vaccination with minimal dissent. (…) 85% of the Portuguese interviewed said they made their decisions based fundamentally on information obtained through television channels. However, they had interacted with fake news, right? They admitted that, but I mean, many of them said it was much more of a game, entertainment, often to annoy other people (E1)

However, as some of the participants stress, it is necessary to contrast the attitude of greater trust in mainstream media with the fact that traditionally, the Portuguese present very low levels of consumption of journalistic content and reading material, in general. This must be understood through historical factors, namely the long dictatorship and the consequent educational delay of the currently older groups.
The second media related vulnerability factor is the severe structural crisis faced in media and journalism. While it is a global issue, Portugal feels it severely. The transition of mainstream media to the digital environment is understood to have been late, slow, and strained by a crucial mistake: the gratuity with which online news content was initially made available, which contributed to the devaluation of information as a good whose quality depends on considerable conditions and resources. The blame also lies with traditional media outlets that, especially in the first decade of this century, betted in not charging for their online content because they believed that by doing so, they would gain more buyers. They thought that the work being shown for free on the Internet would lead the new generations to subscribe to newspapers and buy them, etc. (E8)

The collapse of the traditional business model, notably with the drastic decline in advertising revenues that largely shifted to digital and social media , is pointed out as decisive factor for the great precariousness of working conditions in media and journalism, marked by very low salaries and unstable employment contracts (913EUR).
Increasingly, newsrooms are filled with newly graduates –"the rejuvenation of journalism"– who, relying less and less on the support of more experienced journalists, find themselves in a situation of increased vulnerability to the demands of the profession, especially regarding pressures of independence and integrity. Today, the media’s strong connection to economic, political, and socio-economic power is a direct consequence of the bankruptcy of the business model. Since the 2008 crisis, it has been plummeting tremendously... It’s a clickbait policy, the newsrooms are exhausted, smaller and full of younger and more precarious staff. (E13)

The erosion of journalism quality is linked to adopting social media-driven models, reducing fact-checking, contextualization, and ethical compliance. This shift intensifies disinformation and undermines the integrity and pluralism of news organizations.
Regulating Disinformation
The EU Governance of Disinformation
Efforts to govern disinformation are perceived and evaluated by participants through two thematic axes that serve as key organizing principles, shaping their discussions and engagement with the issues. The first axis explores the desirability and inevitability of the European Union’s leadership in this domain. The second highlights the tension between effectiveness and the protection of fundamental rights, particularly freedom of expression and information, which is central to the democratic governance of disinformation. This delicate balance often fosters a cautious—and at times sceptical—attitude toward legal controls.
EU Leadership: Inevitable and Desirable
The main arguments participants present in favour of EU leadership, as opposed to a fragmented, state-by-state approach, stem from the complex and global nature of the problem. A unified approach is seen as essential for developing a consistent and cohesive regulatory framework. This consistency is particularly critical for addressing cross-border disinformation campaigns sponsored by third-party states. In addition, the EU’s superior access to financial, technical, and expert resources, coupled with its political influence, provides significant leverage in negotiating with major digital platforms headquartered outside its borders. This enables the EU to establish more effective and globally relevant standards for combating disinformation—achievements that individual member states would struggle to attain on their own. Crucially, these standards are designed to uphold democratic principles, ensuring that regulation respects and reinforces fundamental values. Participants, particularly those well-versed in the EU’s regulatory agenda, identify its key pillars and connect them to the conditions—especially geopolitical ones—that shaped their evolution. These pillars include the Action Plan against Disinformation (European Comission, 2018b) and the voluntary Codes of Practice (European Comission 2018a, 2022). A central concern driving these initiatives is the threat posed by Russia and the need to protect the integrity of democratic values and processes, especially during elections. A defining feature of these initiatives is their multidimensional approach. Participants emphasize the importance of establishing standards to regulate how platforms address information disorders, ensuring greater transparency and scrutiny in their activities, particularly content moderation. Alongside this, they highlight measures to enhance institutional and public understanding of disinformation. These include promoting fact-checking and implementing educational initiatives such as media literacy. Together, these efforts are seen as fostering a more informed and resilient societal response to the pervasive challenges posed by disinformation. The components [of the EU Action Plan] are all really good; they were hugely successful... they created the rapid response system (…), and a new fact-checking support line was established. Suddenly, all the media outlets in Europe started having their fact-checkers and joined fact-checking networks. Another thing that was extremely popular was so-called media literacy. Everyone started investing in new initiatives to increase literacy, in schools, universities, among various layers of the population, especially the younger ones, but not only… (E1)

Legal vs. Illegal: The Red Line of Regulation
The highly heterogeneous, contingent, and open-ended landscape of information disorders is, to some extent, simplified by the"red line"participants draw when discussing the nature and limits of disinformation control. This distinction lies between legal and illegal content. While disinformation can sometimes take on an illegal or even criminal character, it is not inherently illegal. The first sentence, of the Action Plan against Disinformation from 2018, was, and this is very important:"Disinformation is not illegal."Disinformation in the political space, in the public space, that is, lying, is not illegal. We all know that some politicians lie more, others lie less, but generally speaking, they all lie a little bit, and we don’t go after them because they lied, right? (E1)
There is a red line that should not be crossed, which is the line of freedom of expression, as long as it does not enter the territory of defamation, violence, (...) or crime, as defined under Portuguese law and the laws of other countries. (…) It is a complex phenomenon, and it is difficult to regulate, as it is not through the mere definition of laws that this can be effectively combated. (E12)

From the participants’ perspective, the major challenge for regulation in democratic regimes arises when dealing with disinformation that is not illegal. For illegal content, particularly criminal content, the necessity and desirability of repressive regulation are clear. In such cases, member states need only enforce the sanctions outlined in their legal frameworks. However, censoring material solely because it is false or misleading is considered unacceptable. Participants broadly agree that alternative control measures are necessary. Otherwise, they caution, there is a risk of infringing upon fundamental rights in democratic states governed by the rule of law—such as the right to information and freedom of expression—rights that the fight against disinformation aims to protect. This tension helps explain why legal efforts by certain member states to regulate online disinformation are often met with reluctance and why participants support the EU’s strategy of engaging platforms for content moderation. By involving platforms in disinformation governance, the EU addresses harmful content without resorting to direct censorship. Content moderation applied to what was illegal, such as ‘denying the Holocaust,’ ‘inciting violence,’ (...) and therefore, platforms had to consider national legislation on content illegality. But this national legislation on illegal content covers only a very small spectrum. The major issue we face with disinformation is enormous, right? It doesn’t relate to those laws. And, of course, Western countries do not have laws that censor content. So, the way we circumvented this problem was by asking platforms to censor content. (E1)

From Self-Regulation to Co-Regulation, and Beyond
The European agenda’s emphasis on engaging major digital platforms is seen as inevitable, given their central role in disseminating and amplifying disinformation. While some reservations are expressed, participants generally support this strategy, recognizing that platform cooperation is crucial to the success of regulatory measures due to their unmatched expertise, tools, and understanding of the digital environment. Moreover, they largely view the EU’s focus on platform regulation as appropriate, aiming to enhance transparency, accountability, and procedural safeguards.
Although the EU’s regulatory approach is regarded by participants as consistent over time, they note a shift from a predominantly self-regulatory model to an enhanced self-regulation model, particularly through strengthened monitoring tools introduced in the 2022 Strengthened Code of Practice on Disinformation. More recently, this evolution has progressed toward a co-regulatory model under the Digital Services Act (DSA), representing, for participants, a marked shift in the EU’s strategy on disinformation. The DSA incorporates disinformation into a binding legal framework primarily designed to regulate illegal content, marking a departure from previous approaches that relied on voluntary commitments and maintained a clear distinction between disinformation and illegal content. On one hand, the DSA is seen as a significant step toward achieving greater effectiveness in efforts to combat information disorders and promote a safer, more transparent digital environment, notably by imposing sanctions on platforms, particularly VLOPS, that fail to meet their obligations. On the other hand, the Act has been criticized for blurring the lines between disinformation and other important, yet distinct, issues, such as consumer protection and the regulation of illegal content, raising concerns about the lack of clarity in its regulatory objectives.
A second action plan was not adopted; instead, other legislative instruments, namely the Digital Services Act, were chosen—something some participants view as a mistake. One participant stated:
This is a digital regulation, and therefore, including the Code of Practice against Disinformation within it is completely absurd. These are matters that have absolutely nothing to do with each othe. (E1)
As for the DSA, I raised this issue earlier this week with some members from the legal department. I did find references to disinformation in the initial considerations of the document. However, upon analysing the articles one by one, I realized that disinformation is never objectively addressed. (E7b)
This lack of clarity is one reason why the DSA has garnered less consensus and more reservations from participants. Some view it as part of a recent setback that obscures the relevance and distinct nature of disinformation and the challenges it poses, especially when compared to the more nuanced approach outlined in the Action Plan and the Codes of Practice. As one participant noted: "Disinformation is not a matter of digital services but a matter of sovereignty, of pure politics, which concerns the heart of our political system."
Additionally, participants raised concerns about the lack of clarity regarding the coordination between the DSA and the Strengthened Code of Practice. Finally, there were reservations about the direct involvement of national states. This approach raises concerns about the potential loss of consistency in the broader European agenda on disinformation, particularly given the awareness that some states have shown tendencies to limit freedom of information.
Main Challenges
Despite the generally positive evaluation of the measures implemented by the EU and those voluntarily adopted by digital platforms and technology companies, participants unanimously recognize that disinformation remains a persistent issue, and its regulation presents significant dilemmas and challenges in implementation.
Although the notion of"technological neutrality"is largely dismissed as outdated, widespread skepticism persists regarding the compatibility of digital platforms’ business models—rooted in"advertising and commercial expansion"strategies—with strict adherence to some of the proposed measures. These include ensuring transparency in political campaign financing and demonetizing disinformation. Algorithms and networks are fundamentally programmed to maximize profit, and the spread of disinformation is seen as a key strategy for sustaining revenue.
Is it sufficient? I don’t think so, because there is a fundamental problem with how the algorithm is programmed and how revenue is generated. The business model of social media is based on attention, and this creates negative dynamics (…). Not because it’s intentional, but because this is what keeps people most engaged with the content. This is the path to higher profits. So, it’s a matter of logic, financial rationale, and profit-seeking. (E12)
At the same time, interviewees express differing views on the extent to which demands can legitimately be placed on"collaborating"platforms. Strict compliance with regulatory measures might deter their participation in such initiatives, particularly given the uneven commitment among platforms. Some make notable efforts to comply, while others, like Telegram and Twitter (now X), openly distance themselves from EU regulatory efforts.
It reminds me of those priests at Mass who say, ‘Don’t forget to come to Mass every Sunday.’ So, this priest is telling the people who are already at Mass that they need to go to Mass, when his message is actually meant for those who aren’t attending Mass… In other words, the sanctions in various regulations will be applied to companies that are already making some effort to comply with the rules. (E6)
Additionally, some participants expressed concerns about the risks of over-reliance on digital platforms and private actors in the fight against disinformation. They emphasized the need for rigorous oversight of these entities’ operational standards and actions, particularly in content moderation. Platforms, they noted, are not neutral, and their private interests may collide with democratic principles, raising concerns about whether their actions genuinely protect public interests and values. Without such safeguards, the question"Who guards the guardians?"arises. This risk may be heightened by the increasing pressure on platforms, leading them to censor content with ambiguous illegality as a precaution against potential economic sanctions.
There is a serious problem too, which is leaving this issue to platforms’ discretion. Because they tend to protect themselves, probably incorporating more restrictive rules into their terms of use, which can often go beyond and attack freedom of expression in a way that is not proportional to what is being defended. Hence the usefulness of having a regulator as an appeals body to assess whether the terms and conditions of these platforms are in line with the Constitutions of the Member States and the existing instruments of International Law. (E7a)
The Lack of a National Political Agenda
Participants unanimously highlighted the absence of a consistent political agenda in Portugal for combating disinformation, pointing to several key issues. The first indicator is the absence of a National Action Plan. Another significant challenge is the lack of a legal definition of disinformation, which complicates efforts to address its spread and consequences. Moreover, participants pointed to the outdated and inconsistent nature of Portugal’s agenda for social communication, which has failed to keep pace with the challenges posed by the digital landscape. The delayed appointment of a national coordinator for the Digital Services Act (DSA) was also cited as emblematic of the insufficient prioritization of disinformation by public authorities, reflecting a broader lack of strategic focus.2
A National Plan: Absent but Not Missed
Portugal has not established a national action plan to address disinformation. Instead, it coordinates with the European Action Plan against Disinformation through Law No. 15/2022 of August 11 th, which amends Law No. 27/2021 of May 17 th—the law that approved the Portuguese Charter of Human Rights in the Digital Era.
The lack of a national plan did not feature prominently in participant discussions. This is partly because the European Union’s leadership in this area mitigates the need for national-level plans, and Member States are not obligated to adopt such plans. Additionally, participants pointed to the difficulty of defining a national entity capable of coordinating the implementation of such a plan under current conditions. Unlike many European countries, Portugal does not have a department for strategic communication—likely due to historical reasons—to take on this task.
Until we have those departments within public administration to deal with strategic communication—analysing both internal and external communication—it may not make sense to have an action plan. A central entity is needed to monitor and oversee such a plan. Despite ERC’s significant role, encompassing media literacy, fact-checking, and platform engagement, we lack a coordinating entity. Without it, perhaps an action plan doesn’t make sense, although we are moving forward under European Commission pressure. (E1)
While the Portuguese Regulatory Authority for the Media (ERC) could potentially take on some of this responsibility, participants noted that it lacks the necessary legal authority and resources. Article 6 of the Portuguese Charter of Human Rights in the Digital Era (later revoked) had tasked ERC with handling complaints about disinformation. However, legal challenges to exercising regulatory competencies in the digital environment persist. These challenges were echoed by ERC members, particularly regarding the delayed appointment of a national coordinator for digital services, a requirement under the DSA that Portugal has yet to fulfil.
Lack of a Legal Definition of Disinformation
One of the core difficulties in combating disinformation lies in the absence of a legally defined concept of disinformation, let alone sanctions for its practice (except where disinformation overlaps with existing legal offenses). The first and only attempt to establish a legal definition of disinformation in Portugal through Article 6 of Law No. 27/2021 did not go well. The President of the Republic raised concerns that the concept and its associated control mechanisms could infringe upon the constitutional right to freedom of expression. Although Article 6 was revoked following public dissent, the Constitutional Court later confirmed its unconstitutionality (Ruling No. 66/2023).
The definition proposed in Article 6 faced criticism for its ambiguity, particularly regarding the inclusion of terms such as"public policy-making processes"and"democratic political processes."These broad and imprecise categories raised concerns that legitimate criticism of government actions could be misclassified as disinformation, as one participant elaborated:
The definition of disinformation was not sufficiently precise, leaving room for interpretations that could lead to manipulative readings, particularly when referring to ‘democratic processes.’ The legislator was probably thinking of elections, but if that was the case, it should have been specified as ‘elections,’ because ‘democratic processes’ encompasses all aspects of democratic functioning, including the actions of the government. There was concern that criticism of the government, for example, could be seen as undermining democratic processes. (E7a)
Outdated Legal Framework for Media Regulation
Journalists and participants involved in media regulation highlighted the absence of a coherent political strategy for social communication, particularly the urgent need to update the Press Law. The ERC’s statutes do not grant it specific competence regarding the regulation of content published on digital platforms and social networks. As it stands, even the regulation of content produced by mainstream media in the digital environment remains ambiguous. The issue becomes more complex when it comes to"news"content produced by entities that do not hold the status of media outlets but seek to be perceived as such. The ERC’s regulatory power, as defined in its statutes, is limited to media outlets, a status that depends on their registration with the ERC.
Several episodes were cited as emblematic of this regulatory"grey area."As a result, both ERC members and interviewed journalists emphasized the need for a legal framework that clarifies the distinction between media outlets and other entities that produce news but do not meet journalistic standards. They advocated for amendments to the ERC’s statutes and the Press Law to establish tools for regulatory intervention.
The Press Law needs to be revised to distinguish journalistic outlets from others. A ‘sanitary barrier,’ in the best sense, is essential to protect journalistic outlets, which inherently do not produce disinformation. If journalistic outlets do convey disinformation, ERC must have the legal capacity to intervene. (E11)
We presented a proposal to amend the Press Law to the Assembly of the Republic about three months ago. It aimed to extend the law to electronic publications and allow them to classify themselves as either journalistic or non-journalistic entities, with an updated classification suited to the new context. However, we are still waiting for any signs from Parliament that this will move forward. We still lack a legal framework for both online media and services that produce content not considered media services. (E7a)
Beyond Legislation: Knowledge, Education, and Journalism Empowerment
Given the concerns about the current conditions for legal regulation of disinformation at the national level, the alignment with EU leadership on this issue, and the emphasis on the values inherent in a democratic rule of law, it is understandable that participants prioritize preventive and educational measures over legal ones.
I have great fear and scepticism about this, as well as regarding regulation and legislation, with the aggravating factor in Portugal that these initiatives are often poorly implemented and lack an understanding of realities on the ground. The essential approach is to address the issue upstream, for example, by heavily investing in media literacy in schools and literacy in general. This would help people better understand the online ecosystem, how to obtain good information from credible sources, and distinguish content coming from anonymous sites, blogs, or other types of pages. (E12)
Participants acknowledged national efforts to align with EU initiatives, such as the European Code of Conduct, highlighting media literacy programs led by the Ministries of Education, Culture, and the National Cybersecurity Centre. They also noted contributions from research centers like OberCOM and MediaLab/ISCTE, as well as international collaborations through IBERIFIER/EDMO.
A key concern, as mentioned earlier, is the structural crisis in journalism, threatening its integrity and pluralism. Participants stressed the need for better working conditions and the upholding of core journalistic values. They identified the lack of a sustainable financing model, especially public support, as crucial for safeguarding pluralism in Portugal. In this context, beyond expectations surrounding the European Freedom Act, most participants—both journalists and non-journalists—believe that public financial support should be explored, following the EU’s example. While recognizing the sensitivity of this issue, they argue that it should no longer be treated as a taboo but opened to broad public debate.
Discussion
This qualitative study, based on 13 in-depth interviews, explored 15 experts’ perceptions of foreign state-linked disinformation campaigns targeting Portugal, alongside internal disinformation trends, vulnerabilities, and regulatory challenges.
The findings underscore that Portugal is not a primary target for external disinformation campaigns. In fact, the first recognized campaign with external interferences against the two main parties (PS and PSD) occurred during the 2024 elections3 (MediaLab, 2024). Yet, Portugal is indirectly affected due to its membership in the EU. As already found by previous reports (e.g., Iberifier, 2023), Portugal is exposed to narratives that might undermine EU institutions, such as those observed during Brexit, Ukrainian conflict and hybrid wars. In any case, while participants expressed concerns that disinformation campaigns could undermine democratic values, they are also aware of the dangers of the restrictions of freedom of expression as a fundamental right. This is especially important as empirical data lacks regarding the extent and impact of disinformation (Miró-Llinares & Aguerri, 2023).
Internal disinformation in Portugal is perceived as less sophisticated and smaller in scale compared to other European countries. Nevertheless, it has grown significantly, particularly during the COVID- 19 pandemic. Over time, disinformation narratives have adapted to align with domestic issues, transitioning from pandemic-related topics to recurring themes such as corruption and immigration (EDMO, 2024). At the same time, the proliferation of disinformation in Portugal has been fueled by certain vulnerabilities. First, the increased political populism in Portugal prominently exercised by the far-right extremism movement, mirroring trends observed across Europe (Mudde, 2016). The populists’ direct and emotional communication on social media fosters disinformation, intensifying political and social polarization (Schultz et al., 2018) and promoting penal populist narratives (Lavorgna & Corda, 2024). The smaller and more radicalized parties lead the traffic in publications and shares on social network, such as Chega in Portugal. This party uses in their discourses on social media “a narrative emphasizing conflict between “us and them,” a spotlight on the dysfunctions within the democratic system (…) nativist discourse, and a prevailing tendency towards negative communication.” (Prior, 2024).
Relatedly, encrypted platforms like WhatsApp, are increasingly being used to disseminate disinformation, creates micro-communities, making it harder to access and combat such content (Narciso & Costa, 2021). Finally, the structural crisis in Portuguese journalism exacerbates vulnerability to disinformation (Iberifier, 2023). Declining revenues and precarious working conditions weaken the media’s ability to counter disinformation effectively. Our results resonate Humprecht et al., (2020) who inserted Portugal, along with Greece, Italy and Spain, in a cluster with comparatively high levels of societal polarization, populist communication, and social media use for news consumption. Nevertheless, compared with Spain, Portugal has a higher political unity and less fractures, such as the regionalisms that characterize the former. Furthermore, Portugal’s relatively high trust in traditional news (56% in 2024, Digital News Report) may act as protective factor (Benkler et al., 2018; Humprecht, 2023; Ognyanova et al., 2020). Moreover, television remains the primary news source, reducing exposure to user-generated content—a significant driver of disinformation (Newman et al., 2020).
In conclusion, Portugal’s resilience to disinformation is boosted by political unity and media trust but remains challenged by populism, journalism’s crisis, and social media’s influence.
Concerning the regulation of disinformation, the interviews reveal two central analytical axes that align with key themes in the existing literature on the subject. The first axis is the tension between power and legitimacy (Rone, 2021), while the second aligns with Gillespie’s (2018b) framework on the dual dimensions of platform governance: governance by platforms , which pertains to platforms’ self-regulation and content moderation practices, and governance of platforms , referring to legal mandates compelling platforms to act. These axes provide a lens through which critical regulatory challenges are analysed, including the definition of disinformation, its legal status, governance scope and modalities, and its perceived impacts.
As evident in participants’ discourse, a recurring challenge in disinformation regulation is the lack of conceptual clarity. According to the participants most knowledgeable about recent developments in the EU’s regulatory agenda, this uncertainty has been heightened by the DSA. The primary reason put forward is that it obscures the regulatory “red line” between illegal content and disinformation, which, in their discourse, appears to function as a “safe harbor” to safeguard legitimacy. Before addressing the distinction between disinformation and illegal content, the non-static nature of legal classification itself challenges the assumed consistency of the “red line”: a piece of content remains legal only until it is reclassified as illegal. As discussed by some scholars (Ferreau, 2024; Husovec, 2024), and despite the DSA’s definition of “illegal content,” which already seeks to address this issue, important questions remain about the compatibility of the regulatory framework with the legal provisions already adopted by some Member States to combat disinformation. Regarding the distinction between disinformation and illegal content, participants’ perceptions align with some of the concerns previously raised (Ó Fathaigh, Helberger & Appelman, S2021; Peukert, 2023; Husovec, 2024; Ferreau, 2024). These authors analyse the provisions on the obligation to conduct risk assessments (Article 34) and implement risk mitigation measures (Article 35), arguing that the concepts of ‘systemic risk’ and ‘crisis’ are insufficiently defined, which can potentially have implications for the measures adopted by platforms concerning ‘otherwise harmful’ disinformation. As Husovec (2024) aptly points out, the vagueness of the statutory language does not substantiate claims that the European Commission is at risk of becoming a “Ministry of Truth.” His analysis supports the view that the DSA does not seek to suppress incorrect or misleading -but otherwise lawful-information. Nevertheless, as he acknowledges, the enforcement of its risk-based approach raises concerns about its alignment with the principles of liberal democracy. This concern gains further relevance when viewed through the broader lens of securitization. Casero-Ripollés et al. (2023) argue that a process of securitization in the fight against disinformation is happening in the EU, highlighting a tension between two competing policy logics. The regulatory logic sees disinformation as a byproduct of digitalization and market dynamics, addressing it as a market failure through regulation, fact-checking, and digital literacy. After the war in Ukraine, however, the geopolitical logic has gained prominence, framing disinformation as a weapon wielded by foreign adversaries and justifying intelligence-driven interventions and increased surveillance measures. This distinction mirrors the divide between low policing, which focuses on public order and regulation, and high policing, which is centred on state security operations (Brodeur, 1983).
The measures most valued by the interviewees within the EU agenda—and in the Portuguese context—fall into two main dimensions. On one hand, there are initiatives that operate upstream of legal regulation, focusing on empowering citizens and strengthening journalism. On the other hand, there are EU legal provisions that establish procedures to enhance transparency in tackling disinformation, as well as measures aimed at reinforcing due process and appeal mechanisms for content moderation.
In this context, participants notably did not emphasize the absence of a coherent national agenda or dedicated legal instruments beyond those derived from the European framework. This omission is particularly revealing in a country with a traditionally state-centred approach. However, this is not due to a “denial” of the problem (Garland, 1996). Participants acknowledge the issue of disinformation but reject reactionary interventions, emphasizing the tension between effectiveness and fundamental rights, expressing reservations about stronger legal measures, and favouring approaches that distribute responsibility across multiple actors.
This perspective is likely shaped by Portugal’s relative lack of major disinformation-related crises or panics, as well as a pragmatic recognition that the phenomenon -here to stay -is too complex for strict regulatory solutions, which may be both difficult to enforce and normatively problematic. Ideally, as one interviewee (E8) reflects, companies and social media platforms should consciously acknowledge their growing role in the public sphere, ensuring that commercial and advertising interests never override the protection of the public good. Until then, as participants’ reflections on the challenges and shortcomings of regulatory efforts suggest—and as Rone (2021) and Gillespie (2018a) argue—self-regulation is not merely one policy choice among many. Rather, it is both the central problem and the key to all proposed solutions.
Despite these relevant insights, the present study has limitations. The qualitative method employed (semi-structured interviews) allowed us to obtain a rich understanding of the phenomenon, capturing diverse expert perspectives across different fields. In fact, we intentionally employed a maximum diversification sampling strategy, selecting participants from different fields (e.g., journalism, cybersecurity) to identify common problematizations across domains. Future research could conduct domain-specific studies that explore in greater depth the particularities of each field. Such studies would allow for a more detailed examination of how problematizations manifest in distinct professional and disciplinary contexts. Furthermore, it would be interesting to focus on specific aspects of the phenomenon (such as the regulation) to enable deeper exploration, something we deliberately did not pursue, given the comprehensive scope that guided the present study. Finally, the small sample size, yet higher comparing to other studies that explored interviewees’ perspectives on disinformation (e.g., Georgiadou et al., 2024) limits the generalizability of our findings. These are inherent limitations of the qualitative method that are widely recognized (e.g., Johnson & Onwuegbuzie, 2004; Noaks & Wincup, 2004).
Notwithstanding its limitations, this study offers valuable insights for research and policy. Future studies should adopt longitudinal designs to track how disinformation and European and national regulatory responses evolve, particularly during elections, revealing patterns and vulnerabilities. Comparative research across societies could reveal common patterns and national differences, while analysing disinformation within its political context is crucial to understanding its impact on public discourse and decision-making. Experimental studies are also vital for measuring its effects on trust, elections, and polarization. The interviews underscore the need for a comprehensive political agenda that enhances media literacy, supports civil society initiatives, and fosters academic research. Given the crisis in journalism, ensuring a pluralistic media landscape is crucial, along with strengthening media regulation to effectively address challenges in the online environment.
By integrating these recommendations, Portugal can strengthen its societal resilience to disinformation and contribute valuable insights to broader European discussions on the governance of information disorders.
