1. Introduction
Mobile communication, particularly Short Message Service (SMS), is one of the most widely used modes of interaction in Pakistan due to its affordability and compatibility with basic GSM devices [1]. SMS is employed for personal communication, banking, government notifications, and marketing campaigns. However, it has also become a medium for malicious activities, including fraud, phishing, identity theft, and disinformation [2, 3]. In Pakistan, scams such as impersonation of the Benazir Income Support Program (BISP), fake lottery schemes, and deceptive banking offers are prevalent [4]. These messages often combine Persian-Arabic–scripted Urdu, Roman Urdu, and English, enabling broad demographic reach and exacerbating risks to financial security and public trust [5, 6].
Recent studies such as [7, 8] highlight the growing scale, economic impact, and evolving technical challenges of telecom fraud worldwide. These works emphasize the need for localized and data-driven solutions, particularly in underrepresented regions.
From a national security perspective, SMS-based fraud is not merely a consumer protection issue but also a potential tool for social engineering, coordinated disinformation, and service disruption [9, 10]. Given Pakistan’s heavy reliance on mobile communication for both civilian and governmental operations, safeguarding this channel is critical for resilience against cyber threats.
Despite the severity of the issue, there is a lack of large-scale, publicly available SMS datasets that capture Pakistan’s linguistic and cultural diversity. Existing resources, often derived from developed countries, fail to account for local code-switching patterns, scam narratives, and phishing strategies [11]. To address this gap, we compiled a dataset of 5617 SMS messages, covering fraudulent, promotional, and legitimate texts from multiple regions in Pakistan, incorporating both Urdu and English scripts.
Using this dataset, we evaluate a range of supervised learning models, including logistic regression (LR), random forest (RF), support vector machines (SVM), extreme gradient boosting (XGB), multilayer perceptron (MLP), long short-term memory (LSTM), and an ensemble approach. Furthermore, recognizing that text classifiers are vulnerable to adversarial manipulations, a systematic evaluation was conducted to assess the resilience of the selected models to five attacks: synonym substitution, typo injection, noise injection, data poisoning, and label flipping. Beyond assessing technical performance, we examine the operational implications for national security intelligence, highlighting how artificial intelligence (AI)–driven SMS classification can provide proactive countermeasures against fraud and disinformation in Pakistan.
In addition to conventional machine learning (ML) and deep learning (DL) models, this study extends the evaluation by incorporating transformer-based multilingual architectures, specifically multilingual BERT (mBERT) and cross-lingual RoBERTa (XLM-R). These models are designed to capture contextual semantics and multilingual patterns, making them particularly suitable for the heterogeneous linguistic nature of SMS data in Pakistan. Furthermore, an extended robustness analysis is conducted to compare the behavior of traditional, neural, and transformer-based models under adversarial conditions, providing a more comprehensive assessment of real-world reliability.
2. Related Work
AI is increasingly recognized as a strategic enabler in national security and intelligence operations. Across leading intelligence agencies, AI has been deployed to enhance data fusion, predictive analytics, surveillance, and counterintelligence. For instance, the US National Security Agency (NSA) has established the Artificial Intelligence Security Center (AISC) to safeguard AI systems from adversarial threats and strengthen collaboration between government, academia, and industry [12]. Similarly, the US Department of Defense highlights AI’s role in securing military assets and defending industrial bases against cyber espionage, positioning AI as integral to the broader intelligence cycle. Moran et al. emphasize the transformation of AI within the US Intelligence Community—from a data-processing tool to a decision-support capability—yet they caution that regulatory constraints and institutional culture remain barriers to realizing its full potential [12]. Srivastava expands this perspective globally, underscoring AI’s adoption in the USA, China, India, and Canada for defense, cybersecurity, and strategic stability, while warning of ethical challenges, biases, and the Global South’s growing dependence on AI for internal security [13]. These insights align with broader debates on governance, oversight, and the ethical deployment of AI in sensitive intelligence contexts.
Within this broader security landscape, SMS fraud detection represents a critical operational domain, particularly in countries like Pakistan, where mobile communication underpins financial transactions, welfare programs, and political discourse. ML and natural language processing (NLP) have been widely applied to spam and fraud detection. Ayaz et al. achieved 99.42% accuracy in Roman Urdu SMS fraud detection using classical classifiers such as SVM and naive Bayes, highlighting the importance of dataset expansion and feature selection for improved performance [1]. Similarly, Maqsood et al. developed hybrid ML–DL frameworks for SMS and email spam, achieving accuracies of 99.6% and 95%, respectively, and demonstrating the value of robust text preprocessing pipelines [3]. Salman et al. advanced this work by incorporating adversarial resilience, examining character manipulation and URL obfuscation, and later by fine-tuning large language models (LLMs) to outperform traditional classifiers, achieving accuracies exceeding 98% [14, 15]. These studies confirm the promise of advanced architecture in countering evolving spam tactics.
Recent research has examined telecom spam and scam detection in the context of modern communication infrastructures and AI-driven threats. For instance, studies conducted by Pietri et al. [7] analyze economic implications and regulatory challenges, while another study [8] investigates existing defense mechanisms. However, these works largely focus on global trends and technical frameworks, with limited emphasis on multilingual datasets or region-specific SMS fraud detection, particularly in South Asian contexts.
Despite progress, regional and linguistic challenges persist. South Asian SMS fraud schemes differ significantly from those in Western spam datasets, given the prevalence of Roman Urdu, Urdu, and code-switching. Mehmood et al. addressed this gap by introducing a bilingual Urdu–Roman Urdu corpus, reporting 92.2% accuracy but underscoring difficulties related to spelling variation and linguistic ambiguity [16]. Pervaiz et al. identified 10 distinct SMS fraud schemes in Pakistan, primarily targeting rural and low-income populations, thus revealing how socioeconomic vulnerabilities intersect with linguistic ones [17]. Razaq et al. further explored the susceptibility of mobile users in Pakistan to social engineering attacks and recommended localized detection strategies tailored to literacy levels and cultural contexts [18]. Collectively, these works highlight the risks of applying Western-trained models directly to South Asian datasets without adaptation.
Recent research in text classification and fraud detection has increasingly focused on transformer-based architectures, which utilize contextual embeddings to capture semantic relationships across languages. Multilingual models such as mBERT [19, 20] and XLM-R [21] have demonstrated strong performance in handling code-switching and low-resource language settings. These approaches significantly outperform traditional feature engineering–based methods, particularly in noisy and adversarial environments, motivating their inclusion in this study for a more comprehensive comparative evaluation.
In summary, prior research demonstrates AI’s transformative role in intelligence and security operations and its effectiveness in detecting SMS fraud. However, gaps remain in three areas: (1) region-specific datasets that capture South Asian linguistic diversity; (2) adversarially robust models resilient to manipulative obfuscation tactics; and (3) governance frameworks that ensure privacy, accountability, and lawful deployment in sensitive national security contexts. Addressing these challenges is essential for operationalizing AI-powered SMS fraud detection in environments such as Pakistan, where mobile communication systems intersect directly with financial security, public trust, and national resilience.
3. Dataset Description
The dataset used in this study was specifically curated because no publicly available resource exists for SMS fraud in the Pakistani context. To address this gap, SMS messages were collected from multiple private sources through a snowball sampling strategy. Participants, including university students, friends, and family members, were invited to share any SMS messages they had received, covering fraudulent, promotional, and legitimate communications. The messages were transferred via WhatsApp and subsequently anonymized to remove personally identifiable information. Informed consent was obtained from all contributors, and data were used solely for research purposes in compliance with ethical guidelines.

3.2. Linguistic Characteristics
The dataset reflects the linguistic heterogeneity typical of SMS communication in Pakistan. Messages appear in English, Urdu (Perso-Arabic script), and Roman Urdu (Urdu written in Latin script). Code-switching, where multiple languages appear within the same message, is also frequent. This linguistic mixture introduces challenges such as spelling variation, phonetic transliteration, and nonstandard grammar. Such characteristics increase the complexity of automated classification but enhance the dataset’s representativeness of real-world fraud scenarios.

3.4. Novelty and Limitations
This dataset is, to the best of our knowledge, among the first curated resources for SMS fraud detection in Pakistan, incorporating English, Urdu, and Roman Urdu messages. It provides a valuable foundation for developing fraud detection models tailored to South Asian linguistic and socioeconomic contexts. However, certain limitations should be acknowledged. First, while the dataset includes diverse fraud schemes, it may not capture the full spectrum of evolving tactics. Second, the collection was limited to personal networks, which may introduce sampling bias. Future expansions could involve partnerships with mobile operators or regulatory authorities to obtain larger and more representative datasets.
4. Methodology
This section presents the methodological framework used to evaluate ML and DL models for SMS fraud detection. The framework consists of five main stages: data preprocessing, feature extraction, model development, adversarial robustness testing, and performance evaluation. This structured approach ensures not only benchmarking against established models but also a rigorous assessment of resilience under adversarial perturbations. 4.1. Preprocessing
Preprocessing was conducted to prepare the dataset for downstream modeling. First, language normalization was applied by converting all text to lowercase, thereby avoiding case-sensitive redundancy. Stopwords were then removed from both English and Urdu, using established Urdu stopword lists to ensure that uninformative words did not overshadow more discriminative terms. Finally, tokenization was performed with the nltk word_tokenize function, segmenting messages into word-level units. Together, these operations reflect standard practices in text mining and enable consistent representation of multilingual SMS data.
4.2. Feature Extraction
To transform raw text into structured input for the classifiers, term frequency–inverse document frequency (TF–IDF) was employed. This method captures the importance of words within each message while minimizing the weight of terms that appear frequently across the corpus. The dimensionality of the feature space was controlled by setting max_features to 5000, striking a balance between coverage of informative terms and computational efficiency. As a result, the dataset was represented in a high-dimensional but interpretable feature space well suited to classification.
4.3. Models Evaluated
To conduct a comprehensive evaluation, the study considered three families of models: traditional ML algorithms, DL architectures, and ensemble methods.
4.3.1. ML Models
The ML models comprised LR, RF, SVM, and XGB. LR provided a linear baseline for classification, while RF employed multiple decision trees to mitigate overfitting and enhance generalization. The SVM was selected for its effectiveness in high-dimensional spaces, where it constructs separating hyperplanes. Finally, XGB represented a strong gradient boosting framework, known for efficiency and high performance in structured text classification tasks.
4.3.2. DL Models
For DL, two architectures were considered. The MLP was used as a feedforward neural network baseline, with multiple dense layers designed for supervised classification. The LSTM model was employed to capture sequential dependencies in SMS text, making it particularly effective for code-switched and context-dependent data.
4.3.3. Ensemble Strategy
To combine the strengths of ML and DL models, a majority voting ensemble was implemented. This strategy integrated predictions from the MLP, RF, and XGB models, assigning the final label based on the majority-class prediction. The ensemble thus capitalized on the complementary characteristics of both approaches, enhancing robustness and accuracy.
4.3.4. Transformer-Based Models
In addition to traditional ML and DL approaches, transformer-based multilingual models were incorporated to enhance the classification framework. Specifically, mBERT and XLM-R were used for their ability to learn contextual representations across multiple languages. Unlike TF–IDF-based methods, these models process raw text directly and generate contextual embeddings that capture semantic meaning, word dependencies, and code-switching patterns. Fine-tuning was performed by adding a classification layer on top of the pretrained models, optimized using cross-entropy loss and the Adam optimizer. These models are particularly effective for multilingual environments such as Pakistan, where SMS messages frequently combine English, Urdu, and Roman Urdu. Transformer models were fine-tuned for 5 epochs using a batch size of 16 and a learning rate of 2e − 5. Early stopping was applied to prevent overfitting.
4.4. Adversarial Robustness Testing
Recognizing that text classifiers are vulnerable to adversarial manipulations, a systematic evaluation was conducted to assess the models’ resilience to a variety of attack strategies. Five representative attacks were designed to capture both natural and adversarial perturbations in real-world environments. For reproducibility, the perturbation intensity of each adversarial attack was explicitly controlled. In synonym substitution, words with available WordNet synonyms were replaced while preserving semantic meaning. Typo injection was applied with a probability of 10% per word by swapping adjacent characters. Noise injection appended three randomly generated punctuation characters to each message. For data poisoning, 10% of the training samples were modified by inserting a misleading token, while label flipping randomly altered 10% of the training labels. These settings were selected to simulate realistic adversarial manipulations while maintaining message readability and overall semantic consistency.
4.4.1. Synonym Substitution
In this attack, words within messages were replaced with their synonyms using WordNet. While the message’s semantic meaning remained intact, the lexical variation was intended to mislead the classifier by altering word distributions. This type of manipulation tests whether the model relies too heavily on surface-level lexical features rather than deeper semantic understanding.
4.4.2. Typo Injection
Typo injection introduced typographical errors into the text, mimicking user-generated mistakes or deliberate obfuscation by adversaries. For example, words such as “customer” could be altered to “custmoer.” The intent was to evaluate how robust the model remained when exposed to noisy or imperfectly formatted input.
4.4.3. Noise Injection
This attack inserted irrelevant tokens, such as “xyz” or “abc,” into otherwise legitimate or fraudulent messages. Such perturbations simulate adversarial attempts to confuse classifiers by increasing the dimensionality of the feature space with extraneous information. The test provided insights into how well the models could filter out noninformative signals.
4.4.4. Data Poisoning
Data poisoning targeted the training phase by deliberately introducing mislabeled samples. For instance, a fraction of fraudulent messages was intentionally mislabeled as normal, and vice versa. These simulated scenarios where attackers compromise the training pipeline. Evaluating this attack measured the model’s resilience to corrupted training data.
4.4.5. Label Flipping
Label flipping randomly altered the ground-truth labels in the dataset, mimicking annotation errors or malicious tampering during labeling. Unlike data poisoning, which is systematic, label flipping introduces stochastic noise into the dataset. This attack highlighted the extent to which the models depended on label quality and their ability to generalize in imperfect annotation conditions.
Collectively, these adversarial robustness tests provided a comprehensive assessment of the models’ performance under hostile conditions, ensuring that the evaluation framework reflects both operational and adversarial realities.
4.5. Performance Evaluation
Model performance was measured using four widely recognized metrics. Precision quantified the proportion of correctly identified fraud messages among all predicted fraudulent instances. Recall assessed the proportion of true fraudulent cases correctly detected. The F1-score, calculated as the harmonic mean of precision and recall, offered a balanced measure of classification effectiveness. Overall accuracy was also reported, representing the proportion of correctly classified instances across all message categories. By combining these traditional metrics with adversarial testing, the study delivered a holistic assessment of predictive accuracy and model robustness.

All experiments were conducted on a system equipped with an Intel Core i7 processor (2.6 GHz), 16 GB of RAM, and an NVIDIA GTX 1660 Ti GPU (6 GB). 6.4. Operational Implications in Pakistan’s Security Environment
The findings have direct implications for strengthening Pakistan’s cyber defense. Given the heavy reliance on SMS for financial transactions, welfare program notifications, and political communication, an AI-powered SMS fraud detection framework could significantly reduce the risk of exploitation by malicious actors. In particular, the resilience of ensemble models against adversarial perturbations ensures higher reliability when attackers attempt to obfuscate fraudulent content through linguistic manipulation, spelling variations, or targeted poisoning of detection systems.
From a national security perspective, this framework can assist intelligence agencies in detecting coordinated disinformation campaigns and fraudulent schemes that threaten sociopolitical stability. Telecom regulators and banks could also deploy these models in real-time SMS filtering pipelines, minimizing the financial and social impact of fraud.
6.5. Example Case Studies From the Dataset
The qualitative examination of the dataset highlights the practical risks of SMS fraud in Pakistan. For instance, a fraudulent message impersonated the BISP, promising financial aid and directing recipients to a phishing link. Another common scam involved fake bank notifications that requested account verification. Both examples illustrate how adversarial perturbations—such as replacing “account” with its Roman Urdu equivalent or adding benign words—could bypass traditional keyword-based systems.
Our experiments demonstrate that advanced models such as MLP and voting can detect such adversarially modified messages with high accuracy, thereby reducing the likelihood of successful fraud or disinformation campaigns.
7. Discussion
The results of this study demonstrate the viability of deploying ML and DL models, particularly transformer-based models for SMS fraud detection in Pakistan. For traditional models, feature importance was assessed using coefficient-based rankings, whereas for transformer models, attention weights were examined to identify influential tokens that contribute to classification decisions. The findings indicate that transformer models rely on semantically meaningful tokens rather than isolated keywords, thereby contributing to their improved robustness to adversarial manipulations. While the proposed framework demonstrates strong performance in SMS classification, its application to broader disinformation detection requires integration with additional data sources and modalities. Unlike prior global studies [7, 8], our work contributes a region-specific multilingual dataset, addressing the lack of localized resources highlighted in recent telecom fraud research. The current study focuses specifically on SMS-based fraud, promotional, and normal message classification, and future work will explore its extension to larger-scale intelligence and communication analysis systems.
7.1. Integration With Telecom Monitoring Systems
Pakistan’s telecom sector already operates centralized monitoring infrastructures under the Pakistan Telecommunication Authority (PTA), such as the Mobile Device Identification, Registration, and Blocking System (DIRBS) and centralized SMS gateways for filtering spam [22, 23]. The proposed framework could be integrated into these existing infrastructures as an additional AI-powered layer of protection. Real-time deployment at the SMS gateway level would allow fraudulent or adversarially manipulated messages to be flagged before delivery, thereby strengthening national resilience against fraud, phishing, and disinformation campaigns. Such integration, however, requires robust engineering for scalability, as Pakistan handles billions of SMS transactions monthly [22]. The integration of the proposed SMS spam detection model into the core message routing layer requires a highly scalable architecture capable of processing massive volumes of telecommunications traffic while minimizing screening-induced latency. Real-time operational deployment requires adherence to strict submillisecond service windows and regulatory compliance mandates, all while guaranteeing zero degradation to base traffic throughput.
7.2. Ethical Considerations
While the proposed framework has the potential to improve security, its use raises critical ethical concerns related to privacy and surveillance. Large-scale SMS monitoring could lead to the collection and storage of personal communication data, raising questions about user consent and proportionality. The balance between security and individual rights must therefore be carefully maintained. Techniques such as anonymization, on-device filtering, or privacy-preserving ML could mitigate the risk of overreach while still enabling effective fraud detection.
7.3. Legal and Regulatory Alignment
Any large-scale SMS monitoring or filtering initiative must align with the regulatory framework established by the PTA. Under the Pakistan Telecommunication (Re-organization) Act [24, 25], the PTA is mandated to regulate telecommunication services in a manner consistent with constitutional rights to privacy and freedom of expression. Therefore, the operationalization of this framework should include legal safeguards, transparent audit mechanisms, and compliance with national and international data protection principles. Collaboration with financial institutions and telecom operators would further ensure a lawful, standardized approach to fraud prevention.
7.4. Risk of Misuse
Finally, the potential for misuse of adversarial detection systems cannot be overlooked. While the framework is designed to safeguard citizens, the same tools could be weaponized by adversaries or authoritarian actors for censorship, political suppression, or mass surveillance. Moreover, sophisticated attackers may attempt to reverse-engineer the detection models to craft new forms of adversarial SMS attacks that evade filters. To mitigate these risks, the system should incorporate regular retraining using updated threat intelligence, red-teaming exercises, and strict governance mechanisms that limit access and ensure accountability.
7.5. Policy Recommendations
Based on the findings and contextual challenges, several policy directions are recommended for the PTA and telecom operators.
Adopt AI-powered SMS monitoring: PTA should mandate telecom operators to integrate AI-driven fraud detection models, enabling real-time filtering of fraudulent messages at the gateway level. This will strengthen proactive fraud prevention before such messages reach end users.
Privacy safeguards: To protect citizens’ rights, legally binding requirements must be enforced for anonymization, minimal data retention, and privacy-preserving analytics. These measures ensure that fraud detection does not compromise individual privacy.
Standardized incident reporting: A centralized reporting mechanism should be established to allow financial institutions, telecom operators, and consumers to submit fraud cases. Such a system would create a continuously updated knowledge base that supports effective model retraining.
Transparency and oversight: It is essential to introduce third-party audits and parliamentary oversight committees to guarantee that fraud detection systems are not misused for censorship or surveillance beyond their intended scope.
Capacity building: Long-term resilience requires investment in AI research and specialized training programs within Pakistan. Strengthening local expertise in cybersecurity and fraud prevention will reduce dependency on foreign vendors and promote national digital sovereignty.
In summary, while the technical contributions of this research demonstrate promising resilience against SMS fraud and adversarial perturbations, the successful deployment of such systems in Pakistan requires careful consideration of infrastructural integration, ethical safeguards, regulatory compliance, and robust governance mechanisms guided by effective policy. 8. Conclusion and Future Work
This study explored the use of adversarial attack simulations on SMS classification models to assess their robustness in the context of national security intelligence. By combining ensemble methods such as voting classifiers with RFs, XGB, and MLP, we demonstrated how ML can be applied to detect fraudulent, promotional, and legitimate SMS communications. The integration of multiple evaluation metrics, including precision, recall, F1-score, and ROC-AUC, provided a comprehensive understanding of the models’ performance under both normal and adversarial conditions.
From a national security perspective, these contributions highlight the vulnerability of communication channels to manipulation and exploitation by hostile actors. The findings emphasize the need to develop resilient AI systems that support counterintelligence operations, disinformation detection, and secure information flows in digital communication environments.
Nevertheless, several limitations remain. The dataset scope was restricted to SMS messages in a specific linguistic and operational context, which may not fully capture the diversity of adversarial strategies encountered in broader intelligence scenarios. Furthermore, the adversarial attacks tested, while extensive, do not encompass the full spectrum of potential manipulations that adversaries could deploy in real-world intelligence operations.
Future work will expand this research along three key directions. First, we will explore real-time deployment of adversarially robust classifiers in operational environments, ensuring that detection systems can adapt dynamically to emerging threats. Second, we aim to enhance multilingual robustness by incorporating datasets spanning multiple languages, dialects, and cultural contexts, thereby increasing the global applicability of AI-driven counterintelligence systems. Third, we will investigate the integration of SMS-based threat detection into OSINT pipelines, enabling the seamless fusion of communication intelligence with broader intelligence-gathering operations. In addition, explainability and transparency will be prioritized to address ethical and strategic oversight requirements, ensuring that deployed systems remain accountable and aligned with international policy frameworks.
The inclusion of transformer-based models further strengthens this study’s findings, demonstrating that contextual embedding approaches significantly outperform traditional feature-based methods in both performance and robustness. These results highlight the importance of adopting modern NLP architectures for security-critical applications such as SMS fraud detection. However, for certain types of attacks, the performance of the traditional models is better than the transformer-based models.
In summary, this research contributes to the growing body of work on adversarial resilience in AI for national security intelligence. By identifying vulnerabilities and proposing pathways to robust, transparent, and policy-aware AI systems, we lay the groundwork to advance secure and trustworthy intelligence operations in an era of growing algorithmic threats.
