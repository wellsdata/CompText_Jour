Headnote Terms like "fake news" and "alternative facts" are a part of everyday life now, but what do these terms mean to folklorists? In this paper, I consider types of fake news, where fake news occurs, and what motivates people to create fake news. I also address fake news by looking at alternative health belief sites, including anti-vaccination sites, as precursors to other types of fake news and as a way to understand the intersection of fake news and belief. Additionally, I ask that we, as folklorists and folk, consider our own belief systems and how they affect our research.
Keywords
AFS ethnographic thesaurus: Belief, belief systems, contemporary legend, health, narratives, rumors
let's start with some terminology. For the term "alternative facts," we'll stick with what Kellyanne Conway stated on Meet the Press, that they are "additional facts and alternative information" (2017). This is different from the term "fake news," which I'm defining as news perceived as untrue, which is a generous definition since I'm separating it from whether or not it is literally true. I recognize that in this definition, I'm allowing my bias as a belief scholar to show, and I think, at times, it is appropriate to use the term "fake news" as equal to "untrue news" or, as Russell Frank defines it, news that is intentionally false (2015:316; see also Mould 2018b). However, for now, I'll leave technical truth out of my definition, perhaps only to make my own point.1
Folklorists were not at all surprised when terms like "fake news" became popular; certainly we have seen these things before. When I first heard these terms, I immediately classified them as contemporary legends. If we consider some definitions of what makes a contemporary legend a legend, we can see that fake news and alternative facts fit. These stories are believable, even if they are not believed; they often establish credibility using FOAFs (Friend of a Friend), an authority figure or institution, or a source supposedly close to the story; they are often set in the recent or historic past; and they circulate by word of mouth and electronically. Additionally, fake news and alternative facts express fears and anxieties, warn of potentially dangerous situations, and often contain an element of social control. One of the few places where they do not align with legends is in their variability, since the texts themselves are often shared on social media as a set text, not rewritten. However, i believe that this is an issue specific to electronic transmission rather than a distinction between legends and fake news. These texts are often modified and reposted on other sites, and there is variation on the comments that proceed and follow their posting on social media.
When terms like fake news and alternative facts began to be used more and more, i saw many similarities to what i observed in vaccination narratives (Kitta 2012). Specifically, i've been interested in the changing nature of the measles, mumps, rubella (MMR)/autism debate. When i first began my research in 2003, the text was fully formed and certainly something that folklorists would have defined as a legend, with variants on the theme that ingredients in the MMR vaccines, typically thimerosal or aluminum, caused autism. As time went on, this legend underwent structural changes, morphing into more of a rumor, with the narrative simply being that ingredients in the MMR vaccine cause autism. Since the publication of my book, these narratives have lost even more specificity and have become the claim that the MMR vaccine causes autism, and finally that vaccines cause autism, operating more as a metonym or kernel narrative. Additionally, i think it is possible to categorize this as a dite, a short statement about belief, because it speaks to a larger belief system as well as a larger legend complex. Perhaps the term sage is better here, with a stronger emphasis on these narratives being things that people say over considering the structure of the narrative itself (Ellis 2017; Nicolaisen 1988). While i believe discussions on structure and definition are important, that is not the main point of this paper.
While attempting to understand how to categorize fake news, one text i found myself continually returning to was Paul Smith's 1992 article "'Read All About it! Elvis Killed by Drug-Crazed Alligators': Contemporary Legend and the Popular Press." in this article, Smith lists ways that the press uses legends (1992:45), all of which are still relevant today:
1. Reporting contemporary legends as "factual" news
2. Exposing oral or reported stories as "untrue" stories
3. Retracting, and thereby exposing, contemporary legends previously reported as factual "news"
4. Educating the reader via the presentation of contemporary legends
5. Entertaining the reader via the presentation of contemporary legends
6. Advertising products for sale using contemporary legends
As we can see, all of these are still in use to this day; they happen in all forms of media, including newspapers, news broadcasts, and on the internet, on both real and fake news sites. The current context suggests additions to this list, concerning the creators of news (for lack of better terms), be they official or unofficial:2
7. Monetizing viral news stories
8. Critiquing opponents or ideologies
9. Creating chaos for the sake of chaos
Monetizing Viral News Stories
While Smith certainly addresses the idea that legends can be used to make money via advertising using legends, I am specifically talking about "click bait" and the Macedonian fake news complex here. These sites make money through advertisements, so each click or share makes them a small amount of money, typically a few cents at a time. However, if you get enough traffic on a specific article, typically because of its sensational headline, it is possible to make $5,000 to $10,000 a month.3 The owners of these sites are frequently teenagers looking to make money fast; they are unconcerned with the ethical implications of spreading these stories. We also see this with alternative medicine websites. In fact, many of those interviewed about the Macedonian fake news complex specifically mentioned that before they got into creating political sites, they were (and still are) creating alternative health sites (Silverman and Alexander 2016).
As Frank points out, this does go beyond the realm of commercialism. He states, "Then, too, there is no way to know, without asking them, whether lesser-known purveyors of fake stories are hobbyists or professionals, or whether their labors of love also bring in a little money" (2015:316-7). This is a fair point, as not all creators of fake news make a lot of money (or are in it for the money); however, some certainly are.4 The Macedonian example, while one of the biggest fake news creators, is certainly not the only one, as we daily hear more about bots programmed to perform automated tasks, and other means of spreading intentional misinformation.
Critiquing Opponents or Ideologies
Frank also notes that some of the intentionally fake sites create stories that depict someone's version of an ideal world and that other stories are intended as satire (entertainment) (2015). In some cases, these satirical stories can be used to embarrass others on a large scale. For example, the Onions ongoing use of the headline "No Way to Prevent This, Says Only Nation Where This Regularly Happens," has been reprinted, almost word for word, at least seven times since 2014, following tragedies related to gun violence (Varagur 2017; see also Brodie 2018). While this satire is not directed at a single individual, the continual republishing of the same content is certainly intended to critique gun advocates and make a political statement.
This strategy can also be employed by audiences. Sharing links to snopes.com has become a common way to recognize legends even outside of the world of academic folkloristics. However, this sharing is often done in a context of condemnation as a way to shame others because of their beliefs in "fake news" or legends. Several groups exist on Facebook, for example, that are proponents of checking Snopes specifically, such as "It takes under a minute to check Snopes: Think before you post" and "Before you repost it, check it out on Snopes."5
Creating Chaos for the Sake of Chaos
I've considered including this in Smith's category "Entertainment," but since it does seem to be a specific type of entertainment with malicious intent, I've created a new category. Online groups such as 4Chan6 will often attack a specific individual or an ideology for the sake of creating chaos and ill will, and these events are frequently reported in the news (see Mast 2017; Johnston 2014). Unfortunately, news coverage can call attention to the issue and can cause the issue to intensify, for instance, through additional coverage on fake news sites.
Belief and Fake News
Many of the online discussions on social media about fake news have been interesting to read, as people are at times trying to discern the truth behind the story. While we as folklorists often find truth to be one of the least-interesting aspects of both legends and belief, we do recognize there is a difference between literal truth and other types of truth. As Smith says, "a contemporary legend may or may not, in whole or part, be true. This may not necessarily be literal truth, but perhaps truth which comes from typifying life in the twentieth century" (1999). So, while fake news is not literally true, it may contain ideas that seem true, as least to some people.
Smith expands on the motivating factors behind legends by considering their purpose, which is ultimately to find an answer (1997). While i do see this in certain circumstances, i'm not entirely convinced that fake news is shared as a way to find an answer. in the production of fake news, content is created for sites to make money, and there seems to be little interest in literal truth. Additionally, for many of these stories, as they are shared and discussed it seems as if the poster has already decided that they are true and is sharing them as a way of spreading his or her version of the truth.7 So, in at least some cases, it's not the production of the text, but the sharing of these texts that is crucial. However, we still don't entirely know the situational context of why these stories are shared. Sometimes context provides clues to the motivation of the sharer/teller, but sometimes fake news stories are posted and reposted without immediate context. in these cases, the context seems to be the larger body of work posted through social media, in particular on Facebook and Twitter. One only needs to look through the past posts to figure out their beliefs over time and to understand the motivation behind posting, whether it is to reaffirm, question, or mock a belief system.8
The teller and the audience share common cultural knowledge of legends and stories and can access a frame of reference that is shared, making the performance conversational (Smith 1998:494). This certainly happens on social media when these news stories are shared, or even in comments on forums after news stories are posted. Since many legends demonstrate the ambiguity, uncertainty, hopes, and fears of the teller, their texts stay "eternally unfinished, amplifiable, and fragmentary" (Dégh 1997:488), and, in this way, they're a lot like chaotic narratives (Goldstein 2012), at least in the way people process their narratives (but not in many of the other ways that chaotic narratives function).
However, it's not just about how legends are structured; it's also about what they do. Bill Ellis states that "legend telling is the communal exploration of social boundaries" (2001:11), so it is likely that some people are posting and reposting to understand where social boundaries lie, to challenge those boundaries, or to expand those boundaries. For example, while some people may have been surprised by the prevalence and open acknowledgment of white supremacy in American culture, most people were not surprised that it exists, since anti-immigrant legends are still common. Folklorists can point to countless examples of research throughout the history of the discipline that demonstrate misogyny, racism, ableism, and other ideas that are considered unacceptable to discuss in polite society, but we still know they exist (see also Ellis 2018; Mould 2018b). Perhaps we are in an era in which social boundaries are changing quickly, for better or worse, which has also led to an increase in fake news, alternative facts, and legends.
Considering fake news and alternative facts leads us to the larger topic of belief. While many forms of folklore can certainly cross multiple genres, considering fake news as a system of belief may be a useful way to discuss it. While individual fake news stories are often unique or involve variants, they express already established beliefs, and people share them because these stories already fit into their belief system9 either because they actually believe them or because they seem believable, demonstrating a form of confirmation bias.
Of course, the notion of legend and belief is hardly new, and folklorists have written about this topic for years.10 However, I wonder if approaching fake news as a system of belief is useful, at least as a way to curtail our own judgment. Does this idea, categorizing fake news as a system of belief, change how we approach these narratives, since as folklorists we're often trained to be more tolerant of beliefs over legends? I think it does, but it brings in a larger question of how it affects folklore scholarship. While folklorists are humans with their own belief systems, we are generally trained as ethnographers to not judge the belief systems of others. However, recently we have expressed more outrage at the belief systems in the news than we may have in the past. Why is this? Why do the stakes feel higher now than ever before? I wonder how much of it is a part of our training as folklorists that we may be more adamant because these beliefs clash with our own belief systems. certainly, we know that folklore and belief has been a factor in the election of a president or government and a justification for action, as Ellis has so eloquently pointed out, showing that legend (not intentional disinformation) was the impetus for political action (2017). Are we livid now because the fake news that spreads seems more outrageous than in the past (or that we perceive it to be more outrageous)? Is it because it's no longer marginal belief? I wonder where our reflexivity and cultural relativism comes into play at times like this. certainly, I'm not arguing that we should remain stoic or silent; I guess I'm just wondering where everyone was in 2008, 2011, 2013, 2014, and 2015, when measles epidemics were happening all over the United States and people were dying of preventable childhood diseases. As Ellis states, "legend telling is fundamentally a political act" (1990:2). So, if telling a legend is a political act, what is analyzing a legend (or choosing not to)? (Ellis 2001:243).
I am not attempting to shame others, but I am interested in why we study what we study, and I am as much to blame as anyone. For years, I kept my pro-vaccination stance out of the public eye, in part because I was worried that those who question vaccination would not want to talk to me if they knew I was pro-vaccine. While I initially thought this would be to my benefit, it was not. By not taking a stance, people often assumed that I opposed whatever stance they took, and I was berated by everyone involved in vaccine decision-making, including both those whom I agreed with and those whom I disagreed with. It wasn't until around 2009 that I began to make my pro-vaccination stance known on social media, in my sharing of articles and comments as well as by posting pictures of my own flu shots (under #FluShotSelfie). This was not without its own issues, including accusations that I had photoshopped the pictures or I was being injected with saline; a variety of comments indicated that I was a tool of the pharmaceutical industry. Somehow, by telling the truth, I became fake news.
All of these questions are about our place as folklorists, as citizens, as academics, as "experts," and whatever else you want to call us. This is a great time to think about these deeper questions, like why we are more able to accept some beliefs over others and why we feel justified in going back to arguments about truth here rather than in other times (see also Mould 2018a, 2018b). Are we falling into David Hufford's succinct summation of belief?: "What I know, I know, what you know, you only believe" (1982:47). While I'm certainly not arguing that we should stand by (#RESIST), we should ask ourselves these questions because it affects how we conduct our fieldwork. We should also learn from those who have already confronted these ethical issues,11 as this is a good opportunity to talk about reflexivity and fieldwork practices. We should also talk about how we decide what is interesting enough to study, if we're "studying down," and if any of this affects our relationship with other folk groups and their perceptions of folklorists.
We have the unique opportunity to engage the public via fake news and complicate their definitions of what that means (and what folklore is). We may even find this is an opportunity to solve some of the issues surrounding underemployment in folklore and academia at large. Engaging the public in discussion about fake news, legends, and belief is more important than ever. Valuing this type of scholarship is crucial to the discipline.
