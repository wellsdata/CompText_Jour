Introduction
Social media is regarded as the mainstream media through which the latest information or news is propagated. Though it can disseminate information earlier and quicker, it sometimes poses a severe threat to society by delivering false information to those who rely on them. Fake news or information is defined as fabricated news or storyline on an expansive scale to mislead readers. From the media point of view, fake information is described as twisted and misleading content in circulation as news via a communication medium such as print, electronic, and digital communication. Technological advancement has given more individuals access to digital media, making communication more effortless. It is evident from the literature that fake news propagates faster than real news [1]. Recently, we have seen fake news circulated connecting to COVID-19 vaccines and virus remedies during the pandemic.
Fake information shared on social media gives users more hints about the lack of quality of the information, and its source remains unclear. However, fake news spread in platforms that resemble authentic news outlets or appear even in trustworthy newspapers poses a significant threat [2]. The users trust these platforms and sources and are not doubtful about the information being shared. Dissemination of news in social media is easy and accessible, which makes it alarming to identify and check its trustworthiness [3]. The research community must be essential in developing automated systems to identify false claims, fake news, and unreliable information. Literature reveals various aspects influencing social media’s popularity and digital media platforms for news consumption. While considering the ease of access and expenses analogized to traditional newspapers and media, social media and online platforms are more accessible and less expensive. The information can be of different forms [4] in the propagation of unauthentic news, such as fake news, clickbait, propaganda, satire or parody, and rumors. Automatic fake news identification has been mainly dedicated to analyzing social media. The automated system should mitigate the time and human resources spent identifying fake news and the spreaders from the stream of constantly produced data. When we consider social media, the nature of the text is highly unstructured and noisy. Moreover, the texts can belong to various domains as users comment and share messages about different topics. Hence, identifying the domain of texts in which the information is circulated in social media is very important and can help design tools to detect fake news [5]. Generally, we can classify a fake news detection task as a binary text classification problem if there are only two classes, but it can be more fine-grained if modeled as a multi-class problem. The task becomes much more difficult if we have a multi-class fake news detection problem, as we address in this proposed work which uses a multi-class dataset consisting of labels fake, partially fake, true, and other. The main concern of this multi-class is that there can be a small amount of information in a document, making it partially false instead of false or any other class.
Artificial intelligence (AI) and machine learning (ML) have significantly impacted fake news detection. With the increasing spread of false information online, developing efficient methods to identify and flag fake news has become crucial [6]. Artificial intelligence and machine learning technologies provide powerful tools for this task, enabling the processing of vast amounts of data to identify patterns and trends indicative of fake news. One of the primary benefits of artificial intelligence for fake news detection is its ability to analyze language and content. These technologies can interpret the words used in a news article, the tone of the language, and even the sources cited in the article to determine whether it is credible or not. This analysis can be done at scale, allowing for the processing of thousands of articles in a short period of time. Another advantage is their ability to adapt and learn. They can be trained on large datasets of verified and fake news articles to develop algorithms that can identify the characteristics of each type of news.
When it comes to data collection, often it is retrieved from social media platforms or Wikipedia sources. When it comes to news articles, there have been fewer data collection that classifies into fake and truthful information. The study on fake news detection can be outlined back to 2014 when the purpose was to identify fake news in news articles, which led to the first and foremost fake news challenge organized by [7] to prevent the reach of fake information in the form of news articles. Since then, many techniques have been presented in the literature for detecting fake information from news articles. Human intervention is required to fact-check and validate the authenticity of these data as it produces a massive amount of data daily. Hence researchers and practitioners are investing substantial resources to devise an automated approach to help fact-checkers determine fake information promptly and reliably. Users can share their opinions concerning any information with friends or different users. Thus digital platforms pave a quick way to spread fake news among thousands of users.
News regarding SARS-Cov-2 origin, treatment, and spread can be taken as a sample of the accumulation of fake news on social media, which was prominent during the pandemic [8]. A solution to avoid the accumulation of misleading or false information on social networks, which substantially influence society, would be to depend on experts, such as newspeople, to ascertain the integrity of the news based on publicized facts in newspapers or authorized websites. However, the resolution is not feasible because it tends to be significantly slow and costly due to the vast amount of information disseminated on the networks.
As we see nowadays, large language models (LLMs) are significantly helping in performing many tasks particularly related to natural language problems. Hence we have explored the LLM in our proposed method. We have used the TinyLLama [9], which is a smaller version of the LLama 2 [10] with 1.1B parameters and runs on smaller GPUs of size 16GB.
Explainable Artificial Intelligence (XAI) is the ability of a system to help interpret and understand machine learning models and explain how the decision-making system works. XAI helps to make the system transparent and accountable, allowing humans to understand why the decisions are made. It helps to address the ’black-box problem of AI systems. The problem with AI systems is that they can produce accurate results in many cases, but how they arrive at those conclusions is often unclear. This lack of transparency can be problematic in many instances in which the predictions by AI systems have a significant impact. XAI is an active area of research, and there are several systems to make AI systems explainable. We use the XAI technique to interpret the model’s performance in this proposed work.
All these suggest that fake news detection is a severe problem in the area of language processing. Many types of research are going on, aiming to automatically detect misinformation or false news. The advent of artificial intelligence has added the advantage of reducing human intervention’s time and effort in classifying false information, thereby preventing the spread.
In this proposed work, We have developed a transformer-based model for long document fake news detection with a window-based mechanism. We have explored the N-gram expulsion technique and used explainable AI methods to interpret the model performance on long document fake news detection. We have also experimented with the Large Language Model(LLM) to see how it can identify fake news in the case of long documents.
Related works
Social networks are flooded with enormous amounts of data, which pave the way for analysis of fake news identification and detection. Social media companies must curb the spread of fake information through their communication channel. We have conventional machine-learning techniques to categorize fake and authentic news. However, the performance of such methods is not that precise because of the incapability to comprehend the data. Deep Learning is becoming crucial to many fake news identification systems because of its computational capacity and ability to understand the data. We have analyzed some recent studies that worked on fake news or misinformation detection and are briefed below. We have mainly divided the works based on binary classification and multiclass classification, dealing with long-text and word overlaps. We have added details about the recent fake news detection dataset, which is widely used. We can see that most of the recent works are based on binary classification.
A title text similarity and emotion-aware fake news detection, incorporating multimodal context information and the author’s sentiment proposed by [11]. BERT [12] and ResNeSt [13] are used to learn the textual and image features and have utilized the public emotion extractor to capture the subjective emotion. They have used binary classification for fake news detection. The work by [14] has developed a Linguistic Feature Based Word Embedding (LFWE), which generated word embeddings over linguistic features. They have focused on 24 key features for detecting Hindi Fake news. The work by [15] has proposed a multimodal fake news detection model that uses progressive fusion networks. The fusion network captures the representational information of each modality at different levels and achieves between the modalities at the same and different levels.
A stacked ensemble multimodal inferencing framework for faster fake news detection was proposed by [16]. They have focused on the faster performance of the model with fewer parameters. They have used an ensemble of BERT and ELECTRA [17] models for text and NasNet Mobile for the image. A multilingual fake news detection in low-resource language by [18], which comprises translated news data from FakeNewsAMT and Celebrity Fake News. The data had two classes Fake and Legit. They have used the BERT model to build their system. A hybrid feature fusion that comprises sixteen features extracted from images, text, and users to identify fake news was proposed by [19]. They have used deep learning models like ERNIE and VGG-19, and we have developed a binary classification model for fake news detection.
The work by [20] used a CNN-LSTM Deep Learning method to identify the relative stance of fake info toward its headings. They have utilized PCA and Chi-Square to reduce the dimensionality of features to predict a news article’s relative stance toward its heading. The F1-Score shows a 20% improvement, and PCA outperforms the Chi-Square and existing SOTA methods. An ensemble method for COVID-19 misinformation identification was presented by [21], which used the Constraint COVID-19 dataset by [22]. They have used a combination of pre-trained models with a heuristic algorithm based on tweet handle, tweet username, and link domain. An experimental investigation on fake COVID-19-related information on Twitter was done by [23]. The primary analysis was on misinformation spread, authors, and content to achieve earlier understandings and classify tweets into four classes: false, partially false, true, and other. Moreover, they discovered that false information propagates faster than partially false ones.
The work by [24] presented a transformer model for fake information classification of a specific domain dataset, including human explanation and metadata for added performance. Multiple BERT-based models with shared weights have been used to control various inputs. An integrated multi-task model for fake information detection was presented by [25]. The primary concern was on news subjects and authors, as any of these can have a higher ratio of fake information. The performance of short fake news was achieved by investigating the impact of subject labels and contextual info at the same time. A theoretical approach to efficiently block fake information’s influence in social media was presented by [26]. The main thought was to restrict the reach of misinformation as much as possible.
An annotation framework for multimodal social-media data was proposed by [27]. They have introduced a semi-automated framework for gathering multimodal annotated data from social media incorporating machines and humans in the data collection method. The same method has been adopted for gathering COVID-19-related misinformation. The work by [28] has proposed a long text classification method on Chinese news using BERT and CNN. They have proposed a dynamic way to extract short text within the long text based on the traditional LEAD algorithm. The short text features and CNN-based features are fused to build their model. A method that extracts the overlapping word for the multiclass hope speech data and analyzes their impact on the detection of hope speech was proposed by [29]. Concerning the data imbalance, they have proposed different data augmentation strategies to improve the model’s performance.
Research questions
This section discusses the research questions we address through this work. We mainly use transformer-based models to classify news articles as authentic or fake. The problem is that those models based on transformers could handle only tokens of sizes up to 512. When we have lengthy documents or news articles, it will be difficult for the model to accommodate the entire news; it truncates the sentences, which can cause the model to train for a limited number of words. Though there are models developed to handle these long documents, they are computationally expensive. Thus we introduce a novel window-based technique to tackling this long document problem. When we consider building a model for fake news classification, there can be some common type of words or phrases that occurs in the articles that contribute to the overall tone of the sentence and make it unauthentic. To handle this issue, we have developed a novel N-gram expulsion approach. We have combined the window-based and N-gram expulsion to develop a model that could classify long news articles without losing information. We have also explored how the large language models can interpret lengthy document news.
Based on the above observations, we have formulated the research questions below. - How to improve the performance of the transformer-based model for fake news detection on long documents?

- Does the expulsion of common N-gram impact the long document fake news detection model using transformers?

- How far does Explainable AI help interpret the long document fake news detection model developed using transformers?

- How large language models (LLMs) help in interpreting long document fake news?

Data Preprocessing
The dataset was mainly retrieved from social media websites and news pages, so initial preprocessing was required before training any model using the data. We have used the clean-text1 python library, which helped to remove URLs and ASCII conversion. Table 1 shows the dataset, which had 1264 news articles consisting of textual data from lengthy documents. Of the 1264 documents, 149 were duplicates, which we removed before training the model. Some of the news articles didn’t have any title columns, either. Finally, for our proposed model training, we have combined the title and text fields from the dataset and fed them to the model for training. As the deep learning model cannot handle text documents as it is, we had to convert them into numbers. We have tokenized the whole document consisting of text and title using the tokenization mechanism corresponding to the transformer-based model. The tokenization was done with the methods available in the hugging face library,2 which will be tokenizing the text document based on the corresponding model.

Methodology
This section presents the baseline BERT model and other variants, the proposed Window-based transformer method, and the N-gram expulsion techniques. The Window-based transformer method is combined with the N-gram expulsion to build a model that classifies the long document fake news articles into four classes. We have illustrated the need for the proposed Window-based transformer method and the algorithm. We have presented the algorithm for the proposed N-gram expulsion technique. The subsequent section explains the Window based approach and how it trains the CLEF fake news dataset using transformer models, followed by the N-gram expulsion technique and LLM-based approach.
Experimental setup
We used the CLEF CheckThat! Lab fake news classification dataset to identify fake news from news articles. The dataset had 1264 values with columns, titles, text, and labels. We have combined the title and text data for training the model. The text column had lengthy news articles, so we had to train the model with reasonable computing power. We conducted the experiments on NVIDIA GPU P100 with 16 GB memory. The batch size of 8 was used for training, validation, and testing.

Generally, deep learning models like transformers are used to train language models for sentiment analysis, fake news detection, or classification tasks. Researchers had used sequential models like RNN and LSTM for NLP with word embeddings to convert textual data to numerical ones before the transformer model was introduced. The main advantage of transformer models like BERT and RoBERTa is that they can capture the meaning of a word based on the context where it occurs because of its distribution semantics and bidirectional learning mechanism. For example, if we consider the word ‘bank’, the model assigns different weights based on the context, like whether it’s a river ‘bank’ or related to banking. Moreover, one more advantage of these models is the subword tokenization method, which helps reduce the number of out-of-vocabulary (OOV) words; for example, a word like cafeteria would be split into ‘cafe’ and ‘teria’. Though these are the advantages of the models, one of the drawbacks is that when we have a lengthy article/document with a count of tokens/words more than 512, the model can’t capture the entire context because of its restriction to the maximum token size of 512. Some models like Longformer [33] and Bigbird [34] have been developed to address this issue, but they are more computationally expensive than the existing ones. This motivates us to develop the proposed Window-based transformer model to effectively handle the long document fake news detection.
As discussed in the Sect. 3.1, we use the fake news dataset from CLEF-2022 CheckThat! Lab. The dataset contains news articles with long text; Fig. 1 depicts the word distribution of training data. From Fig. 1, it is clear that the number of samples with tokens greater than 512 is more than average; hence, if we use the traditional transformer-based models, as it is, it learns only from the first 512 tokens, and the rest will be truncated. Also, we can see that the number of tokens/words ranges up to 7000, particularly for the PFALSE class. While coming to test data from the box plot in Fig. 2, we can see that the distribution is different, and the outlier goes up to 17,500 words for the FALSE class. Most classes have outliers of around 5000 tokens/words in their articles, contributing to around 30% of the dataset. To address this issue of long documents, we have developed a new window-based transformer method for building the multiclass fake news detection model, which could handle the context of the entire news article using the transformer models with a maximum token size of 512.

Algorithm 1 describes the window-based transformer model for training the multiclass fake news classification task. To begin with, we consider a particular article: the title and text combined. We split one document based on words, call it a token, and then make a separate list in which each item has 512 tokens/words, and the corresponding label for that particular document is kept as it is. Here the list is made so that the number of tokens is retained at 512, which we call window size, and the sentences in the document can be overlapped using stride ratio values for the window. Consider an article having 2000 words so that it can be divided into four sentences of size 512 (with the fourth sentence having 464 words), which is a straightforward approach. But in the case of identifying an article as fake news or not, we are not aware of which part of a document can contribute to making the entire article fake. Hence we added a stride ratio value for the window-based mechanism, which helps us focus on different sentences overlapped based on the stride ratio values. We have explored different values for these stride ratios with various models and finalized the best one to further examine with our proposed N-gram expulsion method. An example showing a lengthy document with 2048 words/tokens with a stride ratio of 1 and 0.5 is shown in Fig. 3. The experiments and the result analysis are explained in section 5.
Expulsion of common N-grams
For most NLP-related tasks like classification, text generation, or any other, we use a word embedding model that encodes the textual data into meaningful vector form. The encoded data is fine-tuned with additional layers or other mechanisms to build a final model. We can see in the literature that most of the studies have followed this procedure to develop a model for classification and fake news identification tasks. But in the case of fake news, classification/identification tasks, we generally annotate the data based on the information gathered. Moreover, fake news can have some patterns or some words/phrases that contribute more to the fakeness of the entire article, and this can also be a reason the model gets trained for that data and can cause generalization. Hence if we do a word level analysis using N-gram, we can identify the most common words occurring for the different classes and do an expulsion of those N-grams. Thus the N-gram expulsion method can impact the overall performance of the model.
In this work, we propose an N-gram expulsion method which will be used along with the window-based method discussed in the previous section. For our experiments, we have used unigrams and bigrams; beyond that, the performance can degrade as we might remove the relevant N-grams from the overall data. RoBERTa
RoBERTa [36] (Robustly Optimized BERT pre-training approach) is a transformer-based NLP model which is a modified version of BERT. It mainly changes certain aspects with respect to the BERT model: training size, dynamic masking pattern, replacing next sentence prediction objective, and training sequences.
RoBERTa uses CC-NEWS [37], OpenWebText [38], Stories [39], and BookCorpus [40] data which amounts to 160GB of data for pre-training, whereas BERT was trained on Wikipedia BookCorpus of size 16GB. As previously seen in section 4.4.1 BERT pre-training as MLM is done randomly, whereas RoBERTa avoids the same training masks for each training instance by duplicating training data 10 times, which results in masking each sequence in 10 different ways. Moreover, BERT was trained via a loss called Next Sentence Prediction (NSP), which gives two pieces of documents, whether they belong to the same or different ones. BERT performance degraded when this was removed. RoBERTa showed that NSP loss wasn’t necessary by using Full-sentences and Doc-sentences without NSP loss. RoBERTa is also trained on 2k longer sequences with 125k steps compared to 256 sequences with 1 M steps of BERT.
DistilBERT
DistilBERT [41] (a distilled version of BERT: smaller, faster, cheaper, and lighter) is a modified version of BERT that improves the performance of the BERT model using knowledge distillation during the pre-training phase. The knowledge distillation helped to reduce the size of a BERT model by 40 %, while retaining 97% of its language understanding capabilities. Moreover, the distilled model is 60% faster than the BERT model. The authors have also introduced triple loss, which combines language modeling, distillation, and cosine-distance losses.
DistilRoBERTa
DistilRoBERTa [41] (a distilled version of RoBERTa: smaller, faster, cheaper, and lighter) is a modified version of RoBERTa that improves the performance of the RoBERTa model using knowledge distillation during the pre-training phase. The model is similar to that of DistilBERT, with the difference being the various approaches using RoBERTa are used here, with knowledge distillation applied on top of it. The model can achieve similar evaluation scores on NLP tasks with comparatively less computation cost and time.
TinyLlama
TinyLlama [9] by StatNLP Research Group is a smaller or tiny version of the Llama 2 model developed by Facebook [10], which has 1.1B parameters as compared to the 7B and up to 70B parameter models of Llama 2. The main advantage of TinyLlama is that the compact size of 1.1B parameters allows it to cater to different applications that have restricted resource and memory constraints. As the same architecture is incorporated as Llama 2, TinyLlama gives a similar performance with limited resource and parameters.

Interpretation of proposed experimental methods using explainable AI (XAI)
We use the explainable AI (XAI) to interpret proposed model results using the widely Local Interpretable Model-agnostic Explanation (LIME) [47] method. As the name suggests, LIME employs a local linear model, which tries to fit our developed model predictions in the neighborhood of the feature space. LIME treats any model as a black box; hence, it doesn’t see how complex the internal working of a particular model is, and irrespective of the model, it can explain all the predictions. [See PDF for image]
Fig. 6 Test data word cloud for all classes


As discussed in the previous sections, we have incorporated the window method and N-gram expulsion for classifying fake news. We have applied the XAI method to know how the predictions vary according to the documents. Some random samples from the test data are chosen and used LIME to get the interpretations. As our model was only getting an F1-score of 0.3492, hence it is evident that it will succeed with the predictions only around 35%. LIME interprets the text classification model by incorporating some possible changes in the overall sentence by removing words using Bag of Words (BOW); this sentence is then fed to the model to make predictions and compare them with LIME’s internal predictions. Thus LIME gives the importance of words in a particular sentence which can contribute to different classes based on the black box model we have trained on. Impact of sentence expulsion
This section discusses the impact of sentence expulsion from long document news and how it can affect the model’s performance. As long documents news are considered, the individual sentences can contribute to the overall authenticity of the news. [See PDF for image]
Fig. 9 Example for sentence expulsion, original class: PFALSE predicted class: FALSE


If we take a long news article, two or three lines can change the overall tone of the entire article. Accordingly, as we are dealing with long document news, some sentences in the document can contribute to fakeness. Therefore we further explored the result using XAI by removing some sentences from the whole document. We took the same example as in 7b and applied LIME after removing some random sentences from the article, whose result is given in Fig. 9. Moreover, as we have developed a window-based transformer method that takes the max voting of the predictions of all the sentences, it would be predicted as fake if we had more sentences with a tone of fakeness.
Conclusions and future work
Nowadays, social media is deemed to be the primary source of news. So, spreading accurate and verifiable information via social media is necessary. However, this is not the case, as fake news or information spreads faster than authentic information. In this work, we study the fake news detection problem from two perspectives: N-gram expulsion for managing similar words in fake and authentic news and the window-based transformer method for handling long documents. To this end, we introduced the baseline BERT model along with the variants of BERT (Sect. 5.1) and studied the effect of N-gram expulsion and the window method. To our knowledge, this is the state-of-the-art performance achieved by some modifications in the BERT model. We have used the large language models (LLM) for identifying the classes, and we fine-tuned using the TinyLlama model, which couldn’t give better results compared to the BERT and its variants. Llama 2 is used to summarize the document, and we train those documents with different models and apply N-gram expulsion. We have further evaluated the model with these strategies and found potential drawbacks. Firstly, we cannot increase the number of N-grams expulsion beyond some point as it can negatively affect the model performance. We used a striding approach regarding the window method, which gave us the best results, but it can vary depending on the underlying data. In the future, we will explore the possibility of training data by adding multiple sources and improving performance. We further explore the effect of N-gram expulsion based on the label classes, which are predicted with a low score. We have done N-gram expulsion on words common to both ‘FALSE’ and ‘PFALSE’ classes (in addition to existing ones) and trained the model to see how it affects our results. We explored the possibility of eliminating only the ‘OTHER’ content from ‘FALSE’ and analyzed how the model performs, but the result didn’t change.
