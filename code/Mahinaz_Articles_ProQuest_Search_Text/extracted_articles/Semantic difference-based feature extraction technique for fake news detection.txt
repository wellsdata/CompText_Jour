Introduction
The Internet is crucial in modern society as it provides access to vast information, enables communication and collaboration, and powers many essential services and industries. Social media[1, 2] and easy access to news through the Internet have made information convenient, but some individuals abuse this advantage. Several websites generate false news by altering content that is not accurate. Fake news can be spread by individuals, media organizations, and even governments. Social media platforms have made it easier for false information to spread quickly[3]. Fake news can be spread through any website or platform, including social media, traditional news websites [4], and even websites that are specifically created to spread false information. Fact-checking information and verifying sources are important before accepting and sharing news [5]. One example is widespread false information about the coronavirus, causing fear and panic among the public. Hence, fact-checking methods are imperative to verify the accuracy of news.
The goals of fake news can vary, but it often aims to misinform or deceive the public, spread propaganda or political agendas, manipulate public opinion or disrupt elections. Figure 1 shows two representative examples of fake news collected from internet searches and verified by many agencies.1 Artificial intelligence-based fake news detection is now a very active and dynamic field of study, with many different methods being investigated [6].
These include natural language processing (NLP), sentiment analysis, ML, DL [7], and network analysis [8], which are the tools researchers use to detect fake news. These approaches are being evaluated on various datasets and benchmarks, and significant progress has been made in the accuracy of fake news detection. However, fake news remains a challenging problem, and there is ongoing work to improve the robustness and generalizability of AI-based fake news detection systems.
Many fake news articles are designed to be misleading by omitting crucial context, presenting partial truths on the title of the news, or completely fabricating information. This can make it hard for both algorithms and humans to verify the truth without extensive background research.
Traditional approaches to fake news detection have predominantly focused on analyzing the content of articles or the credibility of their sources. However, these methods often fail to capture the nuanced discrepancies between what is promised in the headline and what is actually delivered in the content. Such semantic disparities can be a strong indicator of misleading or false news, as sensational or deceptive titles are frequently used to attract readership, regardless of the content’s veracity.
Addressing this gap, our work proposes a novel approach that leverages the semantic differences between news article titles and their corresponding content.
Semantic analysis is a technique in NLP that involves analyzing the meaning of words and phrases in a text [9, 10–11]. By analyzing the semantic coherence and consistency of the text, as well as the tone of language employed in both the title and content of the news, semantic analysis can be employed as a means to detect fake news. By utilizing semantic analysis based on distributed vector representation [12] and various similarity computations [13], this work achieves better results. The semantic analysis adds additional elements to the text classification process.
The similarity metric can be used to assess how similar two text data samples are. There are various methods for measuring distances for this, including the Euclidean, Manhattan, Chebyshev, Cosine, and Jaccard distances.
Machine learning and deep learning are powerful tools for automatically detecting fake news, as they can analyze large volumes of text data and identify patterns, contextual information, and features that are indicative of fake news. DL models, particularly deep neural networks, can learn complex representations and patterns from the data, enabling more accurate classification. Analyzing a large volume of text data related to headlines and content to distinguish between fake and real news requires significant effort; thus, we have employed ML and DL to detect fake news automatically [14]. The data are preprocessed by removing unnecessary characters and reducing its dimension with the mutual information (MI) technique. The research utilizes the basic FE technique and ensemble-based distance method to calculate the distance between the ‘title’ and ‘text’(content) of the news before feeding it into the ML model for automatic fake news detection.
The following works have been completed using this method:
- An additional feature extraction (FE) method based on vector distance measure has been proposed to get a more accurate result for fake news detection.

- Ensemble of different vector distance calculators is used to calculate dissimilarity between title and content vector.

- We have tested the model on two previously available datasets and a combination of these datasets.

Previous work
In recent years, fake news detection has attracted a lot of attention, inspiring a variety of research investigations and methodologies. Yuhang Wang et al. [15] suggest SemSeq4FD for early fake news detection using improved text representations. The model learns global and local sentence representations, which are combined to create enhanced sentence representations. It does this using a graph-based neural network with self-attention and a 1D convolutional network. The order of these representations is modeled using an LSTM-based network to produce the final document representation for fake news identification. Mykhailo Granik and Volodymyr Mesyura have developed a straightforward approach for detecting fake news by using an NB classifier. They evaluated the method using a dataset of news posts from Facebook and achieved an accuracy of approximately 74% in categorizing the posts [5].
Junaed Younus Khan et al.[16] examine various pre-trained language models, traditional methods, and deep learning techniques for detecting fake news. The authors find that pre-trained models such as BERT perform the best, especially when working with small datasets and languages with limited electronic content.
Singh et al. [17] suggested a multimodal method combining text and visual analysis to identify fake news automatically. They conduct predictive analysis to find features most strongly linked to fake news, and they determine text and visual characteristics associated with unreliable or reliable news articles. In order to identify fake news, they combine these features using a variety of machine learning methods.
Pawe Ksieniewicz et al. [18] propose a modified preprocessing method called ‘Alphabet Flatting’ for feature extraction from language corpora. The method enables the construction of classifier ensembles with support accumulation, which is capable of competing with state-of-the-art models in time-constrained environments. The method was evaluated through computer experiments and showed potential usefulness in preventing the spread of fake news through automatic systems.
Kaliyar et al. [20] consider both the content of the news article and the presence of echo chambers in social networks. It creates a tensor that combines information from news articles, user profiles, and community interactions to represent the social context. By fusing the news content with this tensor, coupled matrix-tensor factorization is applied to obtain representations of both the news content and social context. The classification task is performed using an ensemble machine learning classifier (XGBoost) and a deep neural network model (DeepFakE). Qin and Zhang [21] explore various fine-tuning strategies for BERT in the context of fake news detection, identifying a combination of parameter initialization, freezing, and training iterations as optimal. It introduces a fine-tuning approach, FGM-FRAT, which uses feature regularization to enhance model generalization and detection accuracy from 61% to 73% on benchmark datasets.
Zhao et al. [22] introduce a model, a dual-channel graph convolutional attention network that enhances fake news detection by integrating heterogeneous graphs to model semantic and dissemination interactions among tweets, users, and words. The model handles semantic information and structural data by applying a graph attention mechanism and dynamic feature fusion, improving performance on real-world datasets.
Khan et al. [23] present the SCA-HDNN, a sentiment and context-aware hybrid deep neural network (DNN) model that incorporates an attention mechanism alongside BERT, BiLSTM, and CNN components in conjunction with linguistic semantic knowledge for advanced sentiment analysis. Utilizing sentiment lexicons, linguistic rules, and POS tagging, the model effectively captures and emphasizes relevant sentiment features within the text, markedly improving classification accuracy on multi-domain datasets.
This article [24] introduces the Gated Fusion Semantic Relation (GFSR) network, designed to understand and interpret the semantic relationships between different modalities. The use of a multi-head cross-attention mechanism between images and adjective noun pair (ANP) embeddings is pivotal for capturing semantic similarities and dissimilarities. This approach allows the network to focus on specific semantic contents that are relevant for sentiment analysis, enhancing the accuracy of sentiment prediction.
Wu et al. [25] address challenges in fake news detection across different domains by introducing a label-irrelevant multi-domain feature alignment (LIMFA) framework. LIMFA includes modules for center-aware feature alignment and likelihood gain-based feature disentanglement, enhancing domain alignment and discrimination simultaneously. Experimental results show that LIMFA improves generalization to target domains while outperforming existing methods in diverse cross-domain scenarios, making it a promising approach for combating fake news dissemination. Proposed methodology
Figure 2 depicts each stage in the process of determining if the news is false or not by looking for similarities between the title and the content of the news. [See PDF for image]
Fig. 2 Steps in the proposed approach


The content of the article and the related text in the title often contain extraneous characters and words that do not contribute meaningfully to the news. Preprocessing is crucial to remove these unnecessary elements. Feature extraction assigns numerical values to words, creating vector representations using methods such as TF-IDF where each word becomes a dimension. However, to compare two texts effectively, dimensionality reduction through feature selection is essential, as well as selecting the most relevant features for better analysis. This step, which implies summarization of the content, is particularly vital for the main news content but less so for shorter titles.
In the next step of the flow chart, we create a layer where the distance between vector representations of the news title and content can be calculated. The layer defines the calculating distance (d) between two vector representations of the news title (T) and content (C). In this system, various methods have been used to calculate distance, and the distance values are concatenated and used to train the classification ML model. The proposed approach calculates the difference between T and C and then gets the final input as N={(d1, θ ),(d2, θ ),..,(dn, θ )} for training the ML classifier. Different distance-calculating techniques have been applied in this system, and the distant values are used to train the classification ML model.
A detailed analysis of the proposed methodology is given in the following sections.
Preprocessing

Text preprocessing is a series of steps to prepare text data for analysis, modeling, or learning. It often involves many steps. In natural language processing (NLP), there are several preprocessing steps to clean and prepare text data for further analysis. Some common preprocessing steps include removing special characters and unnecessary symbols, removing URLs, removing stopwords, tokenization, stemming, and converting emojis into corresponding text. Removing special characters and unnecessary symbols involves removing non-alphanumeric characters, such as punctuation marks, symbols, and whitespaces, using regular expressions. URLs can also be removed using regular expressions. Stopwords are removed using a predefined stopwords library, NLTK, in Python. Tokenization involves dividing the text into smaller pieces, either at the sentence or word level. Stemming involves removing word affixes to normalize text. Emojis, which are commonly used in social media platforms, can be converted into corresponding text for analysis purposes. Feature Extraction (FE) and Feature Selection (FS)
In the case of linguistic theory and NLP, Syntax means the arrangement of words in a particular way. In contrast, semantics is related to the meaning of a sentence or word. Term frequency counts how many times a word occurs on a document, whereas every news article is a document. The vector from the frequency count of words in news content is used to match the vectors of the news title. But then there will be many words that do not contribute any value to the sentence, so we have to cancel out these words, reducing the dimensions of the vector. TF-IDF[27] helps to do so.
The reasoning behind the value is that a term should have a higher TF-IDF weight if it only appears in a few articles or papers; however, if it appears across multiple documents, it is a candidate term for a lower TF-IDF value.

t f i , j = t i , j ∑ k t k , j The inverse document frequency (idf) of word i is defined as: 2

MI is a well-known information theory concept that has been utilized to capture feature relevance and redundancy. The idea is to select the features that have the highest mutual information with the target variable, as these are likely to be the most informative and useful for making predictions. Lowering the number of input features is essential for a variety of reasons, including the fact that it reduces the actual cost of feature selection [29] and preprocessing. There is no need to acquire additional 95 features if the data are to be collected 100 features, and just 5 of them serve our goal[30]. We reduce the feature size of the ‘text’ of the news content to k best features, as there is only k number of features in the ‘title’ set of the news dataset. To perform feature selection using tion, we need to compute the mutual information between each word and the target variable (e.g., the category of the news article). Proposed distance-based feature generation
The degree of resemblance or closeness of data samples is assessed using similarity metrics, which show the degree of connection between them. On the other hand, dissimilarity metrics are used to evaluate how distinct or dissimilar data objects are. Furthermore, these phrases are frequently applied while grouping similar datasets. If two words, x and y, are semantically more different than x and z, in that case, we will find that the distribution of x and y is more different than x and z, where x is the word used in the title of the article, y is used in genuine news content and z in fake news content. The semantic space has dimensions corresponding to possible contexts as gathered from a given corpus. The target word’s semantic difference can be achieved by calculating the difference between two vectors. Initially, there are only text data for both ‘title’ and ‘text’ (the content of the news). Traditional vector representations of words are applied before we can measure the semantic difference between text and title vectors.
The purpose of ensemble methods is to use the strengths of different techniques to achieve better results than any individual technique. Features from different FE techniques are combined together to form the input for the ML model. Cosine distance, Euclidean distance, and Jaccard distance are three distance measuring techniques used in this work to achieve better results. All these three techniques give results from 0 to 1 except Euclidean. Performance measures
This approach can detect fake news with greater accuracy (Acc) by employing supervised classification. To evaluate the system performance, we use precision [39], recall [40], F1-measure, and accuracy as defined below.
Precision is the portion of a fake text assignment that genuinely belongs to the fake class.
The recall is a fraction of fake news classified correctly.
The F1 score is a classification performance metric computed as the harmonic mean of precision and recall. 9

Discussion
Our study suggests that incorporating the difference vector between the title and content of news articles can significantly improve the accuracy of machine learning models for fake news classification. Our proposed model achieved an accuracy of nearly 99%, compared to other basic models trained solely on the content of news articles. This indicates that the difference vector can provide valuable information for distinguishing between real and fake news.
However, our study also has limitations. Our experiments only utilized datasets containing both ‘content’ and ‘title,’ so it is important to note that the results of our model may differ when applied to other datasets. Additionally, our model (using an almost balanced dataset) may not perform well when applied to real-world datasets which is skewed or in different languages. Lastly, our study focused solely on exploring the use of the difference vector between the title and content. Future research could delve into the potential of other features to enhance fake news classification.
Despite these limitations, our findings have important implications for addressing the challenge of fake news. Machine learning models that can effectively identify fake news articles can help prevent the spread of misinformation and protect individuals from the harmful effects of false information. Future research could explore ways to improve the performance of such models by incorporating additional features or using more advanced ML techniques.
Conclusion
Fake news makes it difficult for a user to retrieve essential information from an Internet service, which is especially important when making judgments. This research has addressed the critical challenge of detecting fake news across various digital platforms, a task that is increasingly vital in an era dominated by rapid information dissemination and manipulation. We have demonstrated substantial improvements by developing and applying the semantic similarity-based model. Our experiments across multiple datasets confirm that the method effectively identifies and categorizes fake news content by leveraging semantic difference-based features and machine learning algorithms. The proposed model is able to adapt to the nuances of different distance values from different vector distance calculation methods without significant degradation in performance.
In the future, we will use a transformer-based model to explore deeper semantic analysis and integrate multimodal data that could enrich the detection capabilities using semantic differences of different modalities, considering the complex nature of how information is presented in the digital age.
