Introduction ‘Social media’ are defined as a group of Internet-based applications that build on the ideological and technological foundations of Web 2.0 and that allow the creation and exchange of user-generated content [1]. Current trends in social media explore new and futuristic phenomena in consumers’-sharing of information with all its opportunities and threats [2]. Social media generally refer to internet-based tools that allow individuals and communities to access and to share information, opinions, images, videos, and other content within internet applications [3]. Health-care professionals can use social media platforms to improve professional education and to promote health-care organizations, patient care and education, and public health programs [4–6]. Such social media platforms have been reported to have pitfalls: delivery of poor quality, unreliable, unreferenced, non-professional, and misleading medical information [4, 7]. A recent review by De Martino et al. [8] documented that it is difficult to regulate the source and the quality of medical information, and therefore, false and misleading information can be detrimental for patients and could compromise patients’ confidence in physicians and their mutual relationships.
Internet-based technologies have made it easier for third parties who are independent of commercial firms (manufacturers, packers, and distributors) to disseminate information about drugs and medical devices. The US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) admits that: Information created by the third parties or user-generated content might not always be accurate; positive or negative incorrect representations or implications about a firm’s product created and disseminated by independent third parties who are not under firm’s control influence and that is not produced by, or on behalf of, or prompted by the firm in particular have major implications for regulatory requirements… the internet, social media, and other technological venues contain a vast amount of information including misinformation about medicinal products [9].

‘WhatsApp’ is one of the several social media platforms used for communicating health-related information between mobile devices [10]. WhatsApp is a cross-platform instant messaging tool that allows iPhone, Blackberry, Android, Windows phones, and Nokia smartphone users to exchange text messages, images, video, and audio messages free of charge [11]. This preliminary study analyzed the WhatsApp messages and truthfulness or otherwise misleading potential.
Methods The study was conducted between June 2016 and September 2017 in the Kingdom of Bahrain. All messages related to medication-and health-related products received on WhatsApp social media. The research team collected a convenience sample consisting of all messages received during the study period. From the personal smartphone of the first author, we retrieved all drug-related claims, text messages, or email voice notes in the Arabic language. The first and second bilingual authors (KAA, AKA) carried out the coding, message classification, and drug-related judgment (Table 1). - An objectively true claim is a claim that presented all positive and negative information of about a drug.

- A potentially misleading claim is a claim that omitted important facts about the drug or used poor-quality clinical evidence to support a claim, and exaggerates the clinical importance of this poor-quality evidence.

- A false claim is a claim that is objectively false, as it directly contradicts evidence, or lacks any evidence to support it.


‘Misinformation’ is unintentionally false information, whereas ‘disinformation’ is intentionally false or inaccurate information that is spread deliberately. Preston’s typology was originally for claims about drugs advertised on television [12] and based on the modified definition and classification suggested by Faerber et al. [13]. We made scientific judgments on drugs based on clinical evidence and data derived from United States FDA, European Medicines Agency (EMA), United States Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC), plus recommended international treatment guidelines.
Statistical analysis We used descriptive statistics, such as percentages, to test the difference between proportions.
Results Our research team traced 22 social media claims on drugs (72.7%), dietary supplements/nutrition (22.7%), and potentially toxic bisphenol-A (4.5%) between June 2016 and September 2017 (Table 1). Different individuals and groups on WhatsApp launched most of these claims several times. The most frequently cited claims were for paracetamol and non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs). We suspect that voicemail notes were probably narrated by different persons, with different Arabic accents.
Potentially misleading information was the most frequent type of claims disseminated on WhatsApp (59.1%; 13 out of 22). Specific cases for such claims were: (a) exaggerated adverse effects, apparently without considering the potential predisposing factors [canagliflozin (Invokana ® )]; (b) exaggerated efficacy and clinical importance [dapagliflozin (Farxiga ® )]; (c) overemphasis on the adverse effect of a specific drug, ignoring other drugs with comparable class-specific adverse effect profiles (e.g., prednisolone among corticosteroids; Celebrex ® and ibuprofen among NSAIDs); (d) use of less robust clinical evidence to support the claims (ichthammol, mupirocin); and (e) insufficient evidence and regulatory approvals for supporting the therapeutic claims [ Annona muricata (pure Graviola ® ), Bios life C, chromium picolinate, Himalaya Cystone, and Omega 3-6-9 (Triple omega complex ® ).
Approximately 28% (27.3%; 6 out of 22) of the drug-related claims were false. The majority of these false claims on paracetamol (66.7%) were probably unfair trade practices and likely motivated by competition or deception by manufacturers.
The objectively true claims amounted to 13.6% (3 out of 22) of overall analyzed claims. Sufficient clinical evidence supported these claims and their therapeutic uses have been approved by FDA, EMA, and recommended by International Guidelines of World Health Organization (WHO). The therapeutic indication of daclatasvir and sofosbuvir for treatment of chronic hepatitis C and l-glutamine to reduce the pain in patients with sickle cell disease are examples.
Discussion The content analysis of the messages and commentary on each case summarized (Table 1) suggests three possible scenarios: (a) objectively true claims [daclatasvir (Daklinza ® ), sofosbuvir (Gralisovir ® ), and l-glutamine (Endari ® )]; (b) false claims [Paracetamol preparations, Flutab ® and ibuprofen (Neurofen ® )]; and (c) potentially misleading claims (all other cases).
It is worth noting that potentially misleading claims comprised the majority of the cases. Such misinformation (inaccurate) and disinformation (deceptive), if propagated, can be detrimental to the public and raise several health policy-related issues. Both misinformation and disinformation are forms of information behavior. Information behaviors range from true to deceptive, and include ‘complete’, ‘current’, and ‘informative’ in between. Often disinformation is treated as a form of misinformation [22, 23] and it can be misleading in the context of a situation [24]. The motives for propagating misleading information are speculative and need to be investigated prospectively.
Information literacy is the ability “to identify, locate, evaluate, and effectively use information” [25]. Thus, drug literacy, a form of information literacy, is expected by the public to be realistic. It is in this context the potential use or misuse of the social media should be critically evaluated. The diffusion model of information, misinformation, and disinformation proposed by Karlova et al. [26] incorporates information recipients’ own reasons for believing, plus diffusers’ unknown interests in the particular social, cultural, and historical milieu.
The key overarching benefits of social media for health-related messages have been identified [7]. These include (a) increased interaction with others, (b) more available, shared and tailored information, (c) increased accessibility and widening access to health information, (d) peer/social/emotional support, (e) public health surveillance, and (f) potential to influence health policies. The limitations consist primarily of quality concerns; lack of reliability, confidentiality, and privacy has also been recognized.
The content analysis approach used in this study was previously applied for detecting false and misleading claims in television advertisements of prescription and nonprescription drugs [13]. Potentially misleading claims were prevalent throughout consumer-targeted drug advertisements on television. These findings, taken together with our results, conflict with what proponents say. They argue for the social value of drug advertising and information diffusion through social media. Such exposure of the public has the potential to influence consumer perceptions of drug efficacy, risks, and benefits [27].
Effects of comparative claims in prescription drug direct-to-consumer (DTC) advertising that mislead consumers about the drug efficacy and risk have been recognized; caution is required when comparative claims in DTC advertisements are made [28]. Misrepresentation about the safety of drugs (such as selected NSAIDs) or therapeutic claims that cannot be substantiated by robust clinical evidence (e.g., ichthammol, mupirocin, almost all dietary supplements) in our study illustrate a similar trend. Not uncommonly, comparative claims may also be motivated by unhealthy competition and market forces, best illustrated by the Neurofen ® case (Table 1), that highlights the role of regulatory bodies to curb unfair trade practice [29].
Disinformation can have significant consequences for various stakeholders–individuals, governments, and pharmaceutical companies. When individuals believe deceptive information, it can influence their actions and decisions [26]. In the context of the present study findings, there are major implications for health benefits and health-seeking behavior of individuals emanating from the disseminated drug disinformation. There should be regulatory mechanisms for the surveillance of such ‘misleading information’ on social media in the larger interest of society.
Spam, misleading information, and disinformation can be better detected in social media platforms with public information such as Facebook, Instagram and Twitter, in which various algorithms can be designed for detecting such spurious information [30]. For example, Ahmed and colleagues have used machine learning to analyze fake news [31]. Others such as the Fake News Initiative, which consist of volunteers in academia and industry aim to explore how machine learning, natural language processing, and artificial intelligence can be used to identify fake news and misleading information online [32].
The FDA has provided guidance on how: firms of prescription drugs and medical devices should respond, if they choose to respond, to misinformation related to a firm’s own FDA approved products when that product is created or disseminated by independent third parties on the social media or other technological avenues regardless of whether that misinformation appears on firms own forum or an independent third-party forum [9].

In a more recent draft guidance, FDA clarified for industry how to assure that medical product communications are consistent with the FDA required labeling [33].
Limitations of the study While WhatsApp as an instant messaging application offers more privacy and intimacy in communication compared to other tools [34], WhatsApp has certain limitations in social media studies. Because of its private nature, data collection is limited. Moreover, unless watermarked in an image, it is relatively difficult to trace originator and source of a message. Due to the private nature of chat groups, it is difficult to measure the impact of messages on WhatsApp as compared to other social media platforms. Impact based on the feedback of users (i.e., likes in Facebook, Instagram, or Twitter) or dissemination (i.e., reposts in Facebook/Instagram and re-tweets in Twitter) is not possible in WhatsApp.
Conclusion The majority of the drug-related messages on social media analyzed were ‘potentially misleading’ or ‘false’ claims that lacked credible scientific evidence. The drug misinformation and disinformation propagated on social media can be detrimental to the public’s health and raise several health policy-related issues. Although there is no clarity on user-generated information dissemination on social media platforms, the regulatory authorities should monitor such information in the public interest. There is a need to explore the quality of information on health and medical interventions circulating on social media and its potential impact on health equity.
