1. Introduction The rapid growth of social media has made online news dissemination decentralized, fragmented, and highly interactive. While these characteristics increase the speed of information circulation, they also provide fertile ground for fake news, rumors, and misleading claims. Unlike simple spam or low-quality posts, modern fake news is often written to appear logically coherent. It may retain a real background, replace a key entity, fabricate a causal chain, distort temporal order, exaggerate attributes, or borrow the authority of a credible-looking account. Therefore, fake news detection requires more than coarse text classification: it requires the ability to extract, organize, and reason over fine-grained knowledge elements.
Existing fake news detection methods can be broadly divided into text-based, social-context-based, knowledge-enhanced, and graph-based approaches. Text-based methods learn semantic patterns from article content, while social-context methods exploit user engagements and propagation structures. Knowledge-enhanced approaches introduce external knowledge bases or fact-checking resources, and graph neural networks model relations among news, users, comments, entities, or propagation nodes. These methods have achieved promising results, but they still face three practical limitations. First, generic information extraction systems usually focus on named entities or predefined event types, whereas fake news often depends on relations among entities, events, claims, attributes, and contradictions. Second, supervised extraction models require costly annotations, which are difficult to obtain for newly emerging rumors and cross-domain misleading content. Third, large language models (LLMs) have strong zero-shot and few-shot extraction ability, but direct prompting may produce unstable outputs, incomplete elements, and malformed structures that cannot be reliably consumed by downstream detection models.
Recent LLM-based information extraction studies have explored unified schema-based generation, instruction-guided extraction, multi-turn prompting, demonstration retrieval, and constrained structured output. These methods have substantially improved zero-shot and few-shot extraction, but most of them focus on general entities, relations, and events. They rarely model credibility-sensitive elements such as claims, source attribution, attribute conflicts, temporal inconsistencies, and contradiction candidates in a unified representation. Moreover, the usefulness of their extracted structures for downstream fake news detection has not been systematically examined. HPDO-KEE is therefore positioned as a fake-news-oriented extension of schema-guided LLM extraction rather than as the first general prompt-based extraction framework.
To address these limitations, this paper studies knowledge element extraction as an independent and reusable module for fake news detection. We propose HPDO-KEE (Hierarchical Prompting with Dynamic Optimization for Knowledge Element Extraction), which combines a fake-news-oriented schema, a four-layer hierarchical prompt, dynamic prompt optimization, rule-guided post-processing, and authority-enhanced feature mapping. The core idea is to guide an LLM to extract not only entities and events, but also claims, attribute–value pairs, relation types, potential contradictions, and user authority.
The main contributions are as follows: We define a fake-news-oriented knowledge element schema that integrates content-level elements (entities, events, claims, and attribute–value pairs) with a social-level element (user authority), enabling extraction outputs to support credibility-oriented reasoning rather than generic text mining only.
We propose a four-layer hierarchical prompting framework that explicitly separates task description, core information, structure awareness, and demonstration assistance, improving the LLM’s ability to extract structured knowledge elements without model fine-tuning.
We specify an auditable prompt-configuration pipeline that clearly separates one-time offline template rewriting from input-adaptive demonstration retrieval, schema validation with bounded corrective retries, and deterministic post-processing. The quantities in Equations (5), (7), and (8) are used as retrospective diagnostic metrics for analyzing prompt completeness, output validity, and redundancy reduction rather than as optimization objectives or parameter-update rules.
We evaluate HPDO-KEE on standard English and Chinese information extraction benchmarks and further assess the downstream utility of the extracted structured knowledge on the LIAR fake-news classification task. The evaluation covers extraction accuracy, ablation analysis, output-format compliance, robustness, and inference efficiency, while also establishing a reproducible protocol for authority-feature sensitivity, run-level variance, statistical significance, and error analysis. The current results demonstrate the effectiveness of the extraction framework and the complementary value of structured knowledge for downstream classification, whereas the independent contribution of the authority feature and its statistical robustness require further evaluation.

2. Related Work 2.1. Content-, Context-, and Knowledge-Oriented Fake News Detection Fake news detection has been widely studied from the perspectives of content understanding, social computing, credibility assessment, and evidence-based verification. General surveys have summarized the field as a multi-source reasoning problem in which deceptive content may be reflected in linguistic style, publisher reliability, user engagement, propagation behavior, and factual consistency [1,2]. Benchmark datasets such as LIAR and FakeNewsNet further promote reproducible evaluation by providing labeled claims, news articles, social context, and temporal interaction information [3,4]. These resources have encouraged the development of neural classifiers that move beyond shallow lexical cues and learn semantic or contextual representations from large-scale data.
Existing fake news detection models can be roughly grouped into content-based, multimodal, social-context-based, and knowledge-enhanced approaches. Content-based methods use textual representations to identify sensational wording, stance patterns, topic cues, and semantic inconsistencies. Multimodal methods extend this setting by incorporating visual information; for example, EANN learns event-invariant textual and visual representations to improve generalization to newly emerging events [5]. Social-context methods exploit user responses, propagation traces, or source behavior. CSI integrates article text, user response sequences, and source-user characteristics, while dEFEND jointly models news sentences and user comments through co-attention to provide both detection and interpretability [6,7]. Graph-based social-context methods further represent relationships among news, users, sources, and engagements. FANG constructs a factual news graph and learns social-context representations for fake news detection and related credibility tasks [8]. Evidence-based verification, represented by FEVER, formulates claim verification as evidence retrieval and textual entailment over external documents [9].
Although these methods have achieved strong detection performance, most of them treat fake news detection as an end-to-end classification or verification problem. They usually do not explicitly output a reusable set of fine-grained knowledge elements. As a result, critical manipulation patterns such as entity substitution, temporal disorder, causal distortion, attribute exaggeration, and source-authority mismatch may be hidden inside latent representations. For downstream graph construction and interpretable credibility reasoning, it is necessary to transform raw news into structured elements, including entities, events, claims, attribute–value pairs, relations, contradictions, and user-authority signals. This requirement motivates the knowledge element extraction framework proposed in this paper.
2.2. Information Extraction and Structured Knowledge Modeling Information extraction (IE) aims to convert unstructured text into structured representations such as named entities, relations, events, event arguments, attributes, and facts. Classical neural IE methods formulate named entity recognition as sequence labeling and use models such as BiLSTM-CRF or pre-trained encoders including BERT [10,11]. Event extraction is typically evaluated on resources such as ACE2005 and DuEE, where systems need to identify triggers, event types, arguments, and roles [12,13]. These benchmarks provide important foundations for extracting entities and events from general text, but they are not specifically designed for fake-news-sensitive reasoning.
Recent work has attempted to unify different IE tasks through schema-based or generative formulations. UIE, for instance, introduces a unified text-to-structure generation framework that can model multiple extraction targets with a structural schema instructor [14]. This line of research shows that structured generation can reduce task fragmentation and support entity, relation, event, and sentiment extraction under a common framework. However, fake news detection requires a different extraction emphasis. In misinformation scenarios, the key issue is not only whether an entity or event exists in the text, but also how extracted elements interact with each other. A claim may be suspicious because it attributes an event to the wrong agent, assigns an impossible time, exaggerates a numerical value, or combines a credible-looking source with unsupported content. Therefore, generic IE schemas need to be extended toward credibility-oriented knowledge modeling.
Knowledge-enhanced fake news detection and fact verification methods introduce external knowledge bases, retrieved evidence, or factual triples to improve reliability. Such methods are useful when relevant evidence is available, but they may be limited by knowledge-base coverage, evidence retrieval quality, and the timeliness of newly emerging events. Moreover, external evidence alone does not guarantee that the internal logical structure of a news item has been adequately captured. The proposed HPDO-KEE framework therefore focuses on extracting structured, low-noise, and machine-readable knowledge elements directly from the news item itself, while leaving the extracted elements available for later evidence retrieval, graph construction, or classification.
2.3. Prompting Large Language Models for Low-Resource Extraction Large language models have introduced a new paradigm for low-resource information extraction. Instead of training a task-specific extractor from annotated data, an LLM can follow natural language instructions and demonstrations to perform zero-shot or few-shot extraction [15]. Research on instruction design and reasoning-oriented prompting has shown that task descriptions, examples, and intermediate reasoning patterns can substantially affect model behavior [16,17]. For IE, GPT-NER reformulates named entity recognition as a generation task and uses self-verification to alleviate hallucinated entity predictions, while ChatIE converts zero-shot IE into a multi-turn question-answering process covering entity-relation triple extraction, named entity recognition, and event extraction [18,19]. More recent systems broaden this design space. InstructUIE uses multi-task instruction tuning to unify diverse IE datasets and tasks [20]; ODIE introduces on-demand extraction in which users specify or induce a target table schema [21]; and ADELIE aligns an LLM with heterogeneous closed, open, and on-demand IE data [22]. As summarized in Table 1, these methods differ in their extraction scope, prompting or learning strategies, schema design, LLM usage, multilingual capability, and output constraints.These approaches strengthen general-purpose IE, but they do not jointly center claims, source priors, attribute conflicts, temporal inconsistencies, and contradiction candidates for misinformation analysis.
Despite their flexibility, direct LLM prompts still face several problems in structured extraction. First, the model may output incomplete elements when the instruction does not clearly distinguish entities, events, claims, attributes, and relations. Second, it may generate redundant or inconsistent elements when similar spans appear multiple times. Third, it may produce malformed JSON or extra explanatory text, which prevents automatic parsing. Fourth, general-purpose prompting methods rarely encode fake-news-specific cues such as contradiction, authority, causal compatibility, or temporal consistency. These limitations are particularly problematic when LLM extraction results are used as inputs to downstream graph neural networks or credibility classifiers.
The proposed HPDO-KEE method is positioned at the intersection of fake news detection, structured information extraction, and prompt-based LLM extraction. Unlike end-to-end fake news classifiers, it emphasizes reusable knowledge element extraction. Unlike generic IE models, it defines a credibility-oriented schema that includes claims, attribute–value pairs, contradiction relations, and user authority. Its novelty is therefore the combination and operationalization of these fake-news-sensitive elements with layered prompting, adaptive example retrieval, constrained output, and contradiction-candidate post-processing; it is not presented as the first schema-based or prompt-based IE framework.
3. Fake-News-Oriented Knowledge Element Schema 3.1. Problem Definition Given a news item x = ( u , t , d ) , where u denotes the publisher or account metadata, t denotes the publication time, and d denotes the news text, the goal is to extract a structured knowledge element set: (1) K = { E , V , C , A , R , U } ,
where E is the entity set, V is the event set, C is the claim set, A is the attribute–value pair set, R is the relation set, and U is the user authority element. Each extracted element must contain its textual span, type, normalized fields where applicable, and relation links to other elements.
3.2. Content-Level Knowledge Elements Content-level knowledge elements are the semantic units directly expressed in news text. They provide the basis for identifying temporal conflicts, causal conflicts, attribute inconsistencies, and entity mismatches. Table 2 summarizes the proposed schema.
3.3. Social-Level Knowledge Element: User Authority The source signal is modeled as an uncertain prior rather than as a factual credibility label. Verification or official affiliation does not guarantee correctness: verified accounts may be compromised or impersonated, or may publish content outside their area of competence. The nominal values in Table 3 initialize the feature only and must be calibrated on the development data used by the downstream task.
The calibrated authority feature is (2) w u = clip ( w 0 + α Δ + β Γ − λ H ,   0.1 ,   1.0 ) ,
where w 0 is the initial source-category prior, Δ is a historical reliability update, Γ is a content–source consistency term, and H ∈ [ 0 ,   1 ] is an account-risk term covering hijacking, spoofing, anomalous behavior, and identity uncertainty. The coefficients α , β , and λ must be selected on a development set and reported with a sensitivity analysis. The authority feature is auxiliary and must not override textual or external-evidence signals.
3.4. Relation Types and Contradictions The extracted knowledge elements are connected through six relation types: participation, attribution, description, causality, association, and contradiction. For two elements k i , k j ∈ K , a relation is represented as (3) r i j = ( k i ,   k j ,   τ i j ,   s i j ,   e i j ) ,
where τ i j is the relation type, s i j ∈ [ 0 ,   1 ] is a confidence score, and e i j is the supporting text span or metadata field. A contradiction candidate is represented as (4) c i j = ( k i ,   k j ,   q i j ,   s i j ,   e i j ) ,
where q i j is either a temporal, causal, attribute, or entity-association contradiction. These labels denote internal inconsistency candidates to be checked against context or external evidence; they do not by themselves establish that a claim is factually false.
4. Methodology 4.1. Overview The proposed HPDO-KEE frameworkshows in Figure 1 contains five modules: (1) fake-news-oriented schema definition, (2) hierarchical prompt construction, (3) dynamic prompt optimization, (4) extraction post-processing, and (5) feature enhancement and numerical mapping. The LLM is used through in-context learning and does not require parameter fine-tuning.
4.2. Four-Layer Hierarchical Prompt The prompt is divided into four layers.
Task description layer.This layer defines the extraction task and instructs the LLM to output a strict JSON object. The required keys are entities , events , claims , attr_pairs , and relations . Empty lists are required when no element is detected.
Core information layer. This layer provides the publisher, publication time, and news text. It explicitly asks the model to focus on fake-news-sensitive information such as temporal order, causal links, attribute values, and entity associations.
Structure-awareness layer. This layer requires relation extraction together with element extraction. The prompt serializes the formal relation in Equation (3) using the fields source , target , type , confidence , and evidence . Contradictory relations are emitted as contradiction candidates with an explicit subtype rather than as definitive factual judgments.
Demonstration-assistance layer. This layer provides one or two domain-matched examples. Each example contains a short fake news case and its standard extraction output. The demonstrations are selected from a small prompt library covering politics, finance, society, and technology.
The generic output format is
{
  "entities": [{"text": "…", "type": "…"}],
  "events": [{"trigger": "…", "arguments": {…}, "type": "…"}],
  "claims": [{"speaker": "…", "content": "…"}],
  "attr_pairs": [{"subject": "…", "attribute": "…", "value": "…"}],
  "relations": [{"source": "…", "target": "…", "type": "…",
                 "confidence": 0.0, "evidence": "…"}]
}
Fora contradiction relation, type is set to contradiction and a subtype field records temporal , causal , attribute , or entity-association .
4.3. Dynamic Prompt Optimization The term “dynamic” refers only to input-adaptive demonstration retrieval and automatic validation/retry during inference. Prompt-template rewriting is a one-time offline author-designed step; the LLM does not autonomously rewrite its own instructions for each test item. This distinction prevents Equations (5), (7) and (8) from being misinterpreted as gradient objectives or update rules.
4.3.1. Offline Prompt-Template Rewriting The initial verbose prompt is manually rewritten once before evaluation to foreground the schema, contradiction subtypes, temporal order, causality, attributes, authority uncertainty, and the required JSON keys. The rewritten template is then frozen for all test examples. Let N before and N after denote the numbers of correctly extracted core elements obtained with the original and frozen rewritten templates on the development set, and let N gold denote the number of gold elements. The following quantity is a retrospective completeness diagnostic, not an optimization update: (5) I c = N after − N before N gold .

4.3.2. Domain-Aware Demonstration Selection A fixed example may not match the input news domain. At inference time, the same frozen domain-classification prompt assigns z ∈ { politics , finance , society , technology } . Candidate demonstrations with the same domain label are ranked by cosine similarity: (6) S * = arg max S k ∈ S z cos ( E θ ( x ) , E θ ( S k ) ) ,
where E θ is the fixed embedding encoder also used in Section 4.5 and S z is the domain-filtered demonstration library. The top k = 2 demonstrations are used.
4.3.3. Format Constraint Reinforcement The prompt ends with strict output constraints: the response must be directly parseable JSON , must not contain explanations, Markdown code fences, comments, or additional text, and must preserve the predefined keys. The output is automatically parsed and checked against the schema. If parsing or schema validation fails, a fixed corrective suffix containing the validation error is appended and the model is called again, for at most r = 2 retries. No demonstration or schema is changed after a valid output is obtained.The complete executable procedure, including domain assignment, demonstration retrieval, hierarchical prompt construction, bounded schema-validation retries, and deterministic post-processing, is summarized in Algorithm 1. Adversarial-input safeguard:News text is treated as untrusted data and is delimited from system instructions. Instructions embedded inside the news item are not executed; unexpected keys and free-form text are rejected by schema validation, and anomalous retry or output patterns are logged for review. Behavioral-fingerprinting research on jailbreak detection provides complementary security context for identifying abnormal user–LLM interaction patterns [23]. These safeguards reduce prompt-injection risk but do not constitute a complete security guarantee.
4.4. Post-Processing Even with a structured prompt, LLM outputs may contain redundant elements, missed contradictions, or type errors. We apply three post-processing steps.
4.4.1. Redundancy Elimination Each element is assigned a uniqueness key. For entities, the key is (type, text); for events, it is (trigger, core arguments); for claims, it is (speaker, content); for attribute–value pairs, it is (subject, attribute, value); and for relations, it is (source, target, type). Redundant elements are removed by keeping the first occurrence. The redundancy elimination rate is a post-processing diagnostic rather than a dynamic optimization rule: (8) R d = N removed N before .

4.4.3. Type Correction Type correction uses dictionaries and regular expressions for time, organization, person, location, number, event trigger, and attribute markers. If the predicted type conflicts with high-confidence lexical evidence, it is corrected. Ambiguous cases are flagged for manual review.

5. Experiments 5.1. Datasets We evaluate HPDO-KEE on three standard information extraction datasets.
CoNLL03 is a benchmark for named entity recognition in news text, including person, location, organization, and miscellaneous entity types [24].
ACE2005 contains entity, relation, and event annotations across multiple text genres and is widely used for entity and event extraction [12].
DuEE2.0 is a Chinese event extraction dataset covering real-world scenarios such as society, finance, sports, and emergencies [13].
The dataset split statistics used in this study are summarized in Table 5.
5.2. Baselines and Settings We compare HPDO-KEE with six representative baselines: BERT-CRF, XLNet-BiLSTM-CRF, BART-Gen, FineCSDA, GenMeta-ZS, and GPT-3.5 zero-shot. These baselines cover sequence labeling, generative extraction, few-shot extraction, zero-shot extraction, and direct LLM prompting.
The implementation is based on Python 3.9, PyTorch 2.1, and Transformers 4.35.2. Experiments are conducted on an Intel Core i9-13900K CPU and an NVIDIA RTX 4090 GPU. GPT-3.5 is used as the LLM backbone in few-shot in-context learning mode. The maximum input length is 5120 tokens, and the number of domain-matched demonstrations is set to k = 2 . Each experiment is repeated five times, and the average result is reported.
5.3. Evaluation Metrics Precision ( P ), recall ( R ), and F 1 score are used as the main evaluation metrics: (14) P = T P T P + F P , R = T P T P + F N ,
(15) F 1 = 2 P R P + R .

We also report format compliance rate, extraction consistency, single-sentence inference time, long-text F 1 , and noisy-text F 1 .

5.9. Downstream Fake-News Detection on LIAR To evaluate whether the extracted knowledge elements are useful for the target fake-news detection task, we further conduct downstream experiments on the LIAR dataset. As shown in Table 12, using raw text alone achieves an accuracy of 26.20% and a Macro-F1 score of 25.12%. After incorporating the content-level knowledge elements extracted by HPDO-KEE, including entities, events, claims, attribute–value pairs, relations, and contradiction-related information, the accuracy increases to 31.94%, while the Macro-F1 score improves to 29.53%, corresponding to an absolute Macro-F1 gain of 4.41 percentage points over the raw-text baseline. These results indicate that explicitly modeling structured semantic information provides complementary signals beyond the original textual representation.
When all HPDO-KEE features are incorporated, the downstream performance is further improved to 46.81% accuracy and 44.61% Macro-F1. Compared with the raw-text baseline, this represents an absolute Macro-F1 improvement of 19.49 percentage points. The substantial gain suggests that jointly representing textual content and the complete set of structured knowledge features can provide a richer representation for fake news classification. In particular, the results support the usefulness of the proposed knowledge-element extraction framework as a preprocessing and feature-enhancement module for downstream misinformation detection. Overall, the progressive improvement from raw text, to content knowledge elements, and finally to the complete HPDO-KEE representation demonstrates that the structured knowledge features contribute additional discriminative information for LIAR classification.
6. Discussion The experimental results show that structured prompt design is crucial for LLM-based information extraction. Direct zero-shot prompting already benefits from the general language understanding ability of LLMs, but it does not sufficiently enforce schema completeness, relation awareness, or output validity. By decomposing the instruction into four layers, HPDO-KEE reduces task ambiguity and encourages the model to produce extraction results that are both semantically rich and machine-readable.
The corrected ablation scope indicates that hierarchical prompting, input-adaptive prompt configuration, and post-processing address different extraction failure modes. Feature enhancement is not interpreted as an extraction component because it is computed after extraction. The contradiction module proposes internal inconsistency candidates, which require contextual or external-evidence verification and must not be equated with a final fake-news judgment.
The current evidence establishes information-extraction performance on CoNLL03, ACE2005, and DuEE2.0, and provides preliminary downstream fake-news classification results on LIAR. However, authority-feature sensitivity analysis, run-level variance, and statistical significance testing have not yet been fully reported, and no GNN-based experiment is conducted in the present study. Consequently, conclusions regarding the independent contribution of the authority feature, statistical robustness across repeated runs, and effectiveness in graph-based settings should be interpreted with caution. Inference cost is also a limitation: LLM calls are slower and potentially more expensive than sequence-labeling models, and operational suitability depends on the measured token usage, retry rate, latency, and budget.
7. Conclusions This paper proposed HPDO-KEE, a hierarchical prompting framework with dynamic optimization for knowledge element extraction in fake news detection. The method defines a fake-news-oriented schema covering entities, events, claims, attribute–value pairs, relations, contradictions, and user authority. The framework separates offline template rewriting from input-adaptive demonstration retrieval and bounded format repair, formalizes relations and contradiction candidates with explicit evidence and confidence, and treats authority as an uncertain prior rather than a truth label. Experiments on CoNLL03, ACE2005, and DuEE2.0 demonstrate the effectiveness of the proposed extraction framework, while downstream experiments on LIAR indicate that the extracted structured knowledge elements can provide complementary signals for fake-news classification. However, the independent contribution and sensitivity of the authority feature, run-level variance, and statistical significance require further evaluation. In addition, no graph or GNN-based model is implemented in the present study; therefore, the effectiveness of HPDO-KEE in graph-based settings remains to be investigated. Future work will further examine authority calibration, statistical robustness, inference cost, and heterogeneous graph integration.

Low authority 0.1 Ordinary users, anonymous accounts, or accounts without verification Upgrade only when consistent professional evidence is observed; absence of verification alone is not evidence of falsehood.
Table 4 Rules for proposing internal contradiction candidates.
Contradiction Type Candidate-Generation Rule
Temporal contradiction Propose a candidate when a stated cause occurs after its stated effect, or when an entity is assigned an action before its recorded existence time, subject to time-normalization confidence.
Causal contradiction Propose a candidate when an asserted causal relation has semantic compatibility below a development-set threshold τ c and the evidence spans do not contain an explicit hypothetical or negation cue.
Attribute contradiction Propose a candidate when the same subject and attribute receive mutually incompatible normalized values in comparable contexts and time scopes.
Entity-association contradiction Propose a candidate when the same event role is assigned to mutually exclusive entities, or when an entity is linked to an event role incompatible with its normalized type, identity, or time scope.
