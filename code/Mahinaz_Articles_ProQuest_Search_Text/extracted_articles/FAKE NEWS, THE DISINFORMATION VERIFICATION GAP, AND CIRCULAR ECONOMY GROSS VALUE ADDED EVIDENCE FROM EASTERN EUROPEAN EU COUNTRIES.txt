Abstract
This study investigates whether a wider disinformation verification gap (DVG) is associated with weaker gross value added (GVA) in circular economy sectors. Empirically, the analysis relies on a balanced two-period panel of 11 Eastern European EU countries for 2021 and 2023 and applies a first-difference OLS specification. The results suggest a negative but sample-sensitive association between the DVG and circular economy GVA. However, the DVG coefficient is statistically insignificant in the full sample and becomes more meaningful only when one influential country observation is excluded. The evidence should therefore be interpreted as exploratory rather than conclusive. The analysis also considers skills-based non-verification as an additional channel through which digital fragility may be linked to economic performance, although this channel is not supported by the estimated results. The study contributes in three respects. First, it develops a clear and measurable macro-level indicator of misinformation-related vulnerability. Second, it links fake news and verification behaviour to a concrete economic outcome rather than to attitudes or intentions alone. Third, it connects the literature on digital misinformation, digital resilience, and circular economy performance within a harmonised cross-country framework. Overall, the findings suggest that the digital information environment may represent an underexplored dimension of sustainability-oriented economic performance, but its economic relevance requires further testing with richer data and longer panels.
Keywords: Disinformation verification gap, circular economy gross value added, fake news, digital resilience, Eastern European EU countries, circular economy.

Introduction
Europe's digital and circular transitions are often treated as parallel policy agendas, yet they increasingly converge on the same issue: the quality of online information (Bertoncelj, 2022; European Commission, 2020, 2023). In 2023, only 56% of EU adults aged 16-74 had at least basic digital skills, with markedly lower shares in Romania (28%), Bulgaria (36%), and Poland (44%) (Eurostat, 2023). Verification behaviour is also weak: only 27% of EU adults verified online information from other sources, while several Eastern European countries recorded particularly low levels of verification. At the same time, the EU circular-materialuse rate reached 11.8% in 2023 and 12.2% in 2024, with Romania remaining at 1.3% (Eurostat, 2024a, 2024b, 2025). These figures suggest that Europe faces not two isolated challenges, digital misinformation and circular economy performance, but a shared problem in which weak digital resilience may undermine the economic vitality of circular sectors.
Existing research points to three related but still weakly connected strands. A first strand shows that misleading online content, fake reviews, and verification deficits shape credibility assessment, consumer response, and market interpretation; recent studies show, for example, that new media literacy conditions engagement with fake news, while fake reviews distort informativeness and undermine trust (Miller et al., 2024; Sahut et al., 2024). A second strand demonstrates that scepticism toward web seals, sustainability disclosures, and green claims weakens the effectiveness of credibility signals and can generate adverse behavioural and reputational outcomes in sustainability-oriented markets (Lins et al., 2024; Sajid et al., 2024; Zhou et al., 2023). A third strand suggests, more broadly, that digital capabilities and information infrastructures matter for sustainability and market functioning. Yet, these literatures remain only partially integrated (Barns et al., 2017; Chowdhury, 2013; Grego et al., 2025; Henfridsson & Bygstad, 2013). The misinformation literature remains largely centred on micro-level behaviour, whereas the sustainability literature focuses mainly on disclosure, trust, and green market responses. No prior study directly tests whether a macrolevel gap between exposure to doubtful online content and verification behaviour is associated with circular economy gross value added across countries.
This study addresses that gap by introducing the Disinformation Verification Gap (DVG), defined as the difference between exposure to doubtful online content and the verification of that content as a macro-level indicator of digital information fragility. A wider DVG implies that misinformation pressure exceeds users' capacity or willingness to verify, making the digital environment less reliable and potentially impairing economic performance. The study therefore tests whether a higher DVG is associated with a lower gross value added (GVA) in circular economy sectors across Eastern European EU countries. Two hypotheses follow: H1: a higher DVG is associated with a lower circular economy GVA; H2: a higher share of individuals who do not verify online information because of insufficient skills or knowledge is associated with a lower circular economy GVA.
This study contributes to the literature in three ways. First, it operationalises the diffuse notion of digital misinformation into a measurable macro-level indicator that can be constructed from official survey data. Second, it links digital information fragility to a concrete economic outcome gross value added in circular economy sectors rather than to attitudes or intentions alone. Third, it focuses on Eastern European EU member states, where digital-skill gaps and circular economy performance gaps remain particularly pronounced, thereby informing both misinformation scholarship and the economics of sustainability and information asymmetry. Beyond its academic contribution, the study also has practical relevance. Circular economy sectors are central to the European Green Deal through recycling, repair, reuse, and resource efficiency, yet they still contribute modestly to value added and employment. If misinformation widens the gap between what individuals encounter online and what they are able to verify, digital information fragility may represent an underexplored dimension of circular transition, although its economic relevance must be interpreted cautiously and tested empirically.
Empirically, the analysis relies on a balanced two-period panel of 11 Eastern European EU countries for 2021 and 2023 and applies a first-difference OLS specification. The dependent variable is the change in the logarithm of circular economy GVA, while the key regressors are the change in the DVG and the change in skills-based non-verification. Robust HC3 standard errors and leave-one-country-out checks are used to ensure conservative inference in the small sample.
The remainder of the paper proceeds as follows. It first reviews the literature and identifies the research gap, then presents the data, variables, and econometric strategy, before reporting the empirical results, discussing their implications, and concluding.
1. Literature review
1.1. Digital misinformation, credibility signals, and sustainability-oriented market responses
Recent scholarship increasingly treats digital misinformation not as an isolated communication problem, but as a structural feature of contemporary information environments with tangible economic implications. A useful starting point is Metzger et al. (2021), who challenge the assumption that exposure to misinformation automatically results in belief and subsequent sharing. Drawing on conceptual discussion and large-scale evidence from more than 2.5 million online comments, they show that misinformation is often circulated for reasons other than conviction, including irony, emotional expression, and identity signalling. Their contribution is important because it shifts attention from exposure alone to the way users interpret and respond to doubtful content. In this sense, the economic relevance of misinformation depends not only on the amount of false or doubtful content in circulation, but also on the behavioural processes through which individuals engage with it.
This behavioural dimension is examined more directly by Miller et al. (2024), who use an experimental design to analyse how linguistic fluency, new media literacy, and personality traits shape the engagement with fake news on social media. Their results indicate that higher levels of new media literacy reduce the engagement with low-veracity content, identifying digital literacy as a protective mechanism against misinformation. This finding is directly relevant to the present study because it suggests that the consequences of misinformation are conditioned by users' capacity to assess information critically rather than by exposure alone.
A similar logic appears in the literature on fake reviews. Sahut et al. (2024) provide a broad synthesis of the antecedents and consequences of fake reviews from a marketing perspective. Although their contribution is review-based rather than econometric, it is analytically important because it identifies the main explanatory mechanisms linking misleading digital content to reduced informativeness, lower credibility, distorted product perceptions, and weaker firm-consumer relations. The broader implication is that false or doubtful online content undermines the informational efficiency of markets by weakening the credibility of digital signals.
A more direct connection between misinformation and economic outcomes is offered by Yannopoulou et al. (2024), who examine how disinformation affects sales in the case of a socially responsible brand. Using a case-based interpretive design, they show that disinformation alters message credibility and consumer trust in ways that materially affect market performance. This is an important advance because it moves beyond attitudes and behavioural intentions toward an observable economic outcome. Yet the focus remains essentially at the firm or campaign level. Taken together, this first strand shows that misinformation affects credibility, verification, and market response, but it remains largely confined to micro-level behavioural and firm-level outcomes.
A second strand extends the credibility problem into sustainability-oriented markets, where information quality becomes especially critical because green claims, web seals, and sustainability disclosures rely heavily on trust. Lins et al. (2024) analyse the emergence and consequences of scepticism toward web seals using an online experimental design with 757 participants. Their explanatory variables include perceived seal unreliability and sealauthority incredibility, while the main outcomes are scepticism, manipulative-intent inferences, mistrust, and lower feelings of protection. Their results show that scepticism emerges when credibility signals are weak, and that such scepticism significantly undermines the intended trust-building role of web seals. This contribution is important because it demonstrates that credibility deficits can become economically relevant even in the absence of explicit falsehood; what matters is whether the informational signal is perceived as reliable.
This issue becomes even more salient in sustainability communication. Sajid et al. (2024) investigate the effects of greenwashing on brand avoidance and negative word-of-mouth using a scenario-based survey grounded in the stimulus-organism-response framework. Greenwashing operates as the main stimulus, while brand hate and perceived hypocrisy act as internal mechanisms linking misleading claims to behavioural outcomes. Their findings show that misleading environmental communication significantly increases both brand avoidance and negative word-of-mouth. This evidence is particularly relevant because it suggests that misleading sustainability-related information does not only weaken trust; it also generates concrete reputational and market costs.
A more strategic perspective is developed by Zhou et al. (2023), who analyse consumer scepticism toward blockchain-enabled sustainability disclosure in supply chains through a game-theoretical model. Their results show that scepticism can materially alter firms' incentives to invest in sustainability disclosure, while policy intervention can restore both environmental and economic gains. The importance of this contribution lies in the fact that credibility is incorporated into an explicit economic framework: scepticism is treated not merely as a psychological disposition, but as a determinant of profitability and strategic disclosure behaviour.
Taken together, these studies show that sustainability-oriented markets are highly sensitive to the credibility of information. When trust in seals, claims, or disclosures weakens, both behavioural and economic outcomes are affected. However, this strand still concentrates mainly on consumers, brands, and firm strategy. It does not yet establish whether credibility deficits in the digital information environment are associated with macro-level indicators of sustainability-oriented economic performance.
1.2. Research gap and study contribution
The literature reviewed above leads to a clear but incomplete picture. On the one hand, misinformation research shows that the effects of doubtful content depend on literacy, verification behaviour, and user interpretation, rather than on exposure alone. On the other hand, scepticism research demonstrates that weak credibility signals and misleading sustainability communication erode trust, distort decision-making, and generate reputational and economic losses. A further implication emerging from business-oriented evidence is that when message credibility deteriorates, market performance may deteriorate as well. Yet these strands remain disconnected in one crucial respect. No prior study directly tests whether a macro-level gap between exposure to doubtful online content and verification behaviour is associated with circular economy gross value added across countries. This omission is analytically important because the relevant mechanism is not exposure in isolation. A society may be highly exposed to doubtful online content and still remain relatively resilient if users verify, cross-check, and discount unreliable signals. Conversely, even moderate exposure may become economically consequential when verification is weak and digital skills are insufficient. The relevant explanatory concept is therefore not misinformation exposure alone, but the gap between misinformation pressure and verification capacity.
This aggregation step can be theorised through an information-economics and marketsignalling mechanism. Circular economy sectors are particularly dependent on credible information because many of their value attributes - recyclability, repairability, reuse potential, environmental performance, certification, and sustainability claims - are not fully observable before purchase, contracting, or investment. In such markets, online information operates as a credibility signal that helps consumers, firms, and investors reduce uncertainty. When exposure to doubtful content rises faster than verification behaviour, the reliability of this signal weakens. Therefore, a wider disinformation verification gap may increase informational uncertainty, reinforce scepticism toward sustainability claims, and weaken trust in circular products, services, and business models.
At the sectoral level, this mechanism does not imply that individual verification behaviour directly or mechanically determines circular economy gross value added. Rather, aggregate verification behaviour is interpreted as one dimension of the broader digital information environment in which circular economy markets operate. A country-level imbalance between exposure to doubtful content and verification behaviour may reflect weaker information reliability, higher credibility frictions, and less favourable conditions for market coordination. These frictions can plausibly affect circular economy value creation through lower consumer confidence, weaker acceptance of circular business models, reduced credibility of green claims, and higher transaction or signalling costs for firms engaged in repair, reuse, recycling, and resource-efficiency activities. The expected relationship between the DVG and circular economy GVA is therefore indirect and exploratory, but it is theoretically grounded in the view that information reliability and credibility signals condition the functioning of sustainability-oriented markets.
The present study addresses this gap by introducing the Disinformation Verification Gap (DVG) as a country-level indicator of the imbalance between exposure to doubtful online content and verification behaviour. Rather than treating misinformation only as a communication problem, the study examines whether this imbalance is associated with a concrete economic outcome: gross value added in circular economy sectors. The contribution is threefold. First, it provides a transparent and operational measure of misinformationrelated digital fragility using harmonised Eurostat data. Second, it provides exploratory empirical evidence on a possible association between the digital information environment and circular economy value creation. Third, it connects the literatures on misinformation, credibility signals, and sustainability-oriented economic performance within a cautious macro-level framework. Given the short panel and small sample, the study does not claim causal identification; instead, it offers an initial measurement and testing framework for a relationship that remains theoretically plausible but empirically demanding.
2. Research methodology
2.1. Research design
The empirical analysis is based on a balanced two-period panel covering 11 Eastern European EU Countries: Bulgaria, Croatia, Czechia, Estonia, Hungary, Latvia, Lithuania, Poland, Romania, Slovakia, and Slovenia-for 2021 and 2023. After harmonisation, the final estimation sample contains 22 country-year observations with no missing values. The sample starts in 2021 for methodological reasons. Eurostat's revised digital-skills framework applies from 2021 onward, making earlier observations non-comparable with the newer series. In addition, several ICT-individual indicators are collected on a biennial basis, which makes 2021 and 2023 the cleanest common observation window for the variables used in this study.
The sample is purposive rather than random. These countries were selected because they share a common EU statistical and regulatory framework, which improves comparability, while still displaying meaningful variation in digital skills, verification behaviour, and circular economy performance. This combination of institutional comparability and crosscountry heterogeneity makes the sample appropriate for a bounded exploratory macroeconometric analysis. The restriction to Eastern European EU member states is therefore not intended to support EU-wide generalisation. It defines a bounded regional sample of countries that share EU data standards while also facing relatively pronounced digital-skill and circular economy performance gaps. A broader EU-wide analysis would introduce substantially greater structural heterogeneity between advanced Western and catching-up Eastern economies, which could obscure the exploratory relationship examined here. The findings should therefore be interpreted as region-specific evidence for Eastern European EU countries rather than as conclusions generalisable to the entire European Union.
2.2. Variables, operational definitions, and data sources
The empirical design relies on one dependent variable, two main explanatory variables, one constructed gap indicator, one robustness control, and two auxiliary variables retained for descriptive purposes. The underlying logic is that if circular economy sectors generate measurable economic value, then deterioration in the digital information environment should be reflected not only in trust or verification behaviour, but also in the economic performance of those sectors. The variables included in the empirical analysis are presented in Table no. 1.
All variables used in the empirical analysis are drawn from harmonised European statistical sources. The misinformation-related indicators are taken from Eurostat's ICT-individual database, which provides consistent measures of exposure to doubtful online content, verification behaviour, and skills-based non-verification. The economic outcome variable is drawn from the circular economy database, where gross value added in circular economy sectors is reported in million euro at factor cost. Since several ICT-individual indicators are available on a biennial basis, 2021 and 2023 constitute the most coherent common observation window across all variables.
The country-year panel was harmonised by matching observations across datasets using country and year identifiers. After harmonisation, only country-year observations with complete information for all variables used in the baseline estimation were retained, resulting in a balanced panel with no missing values.
The choice of variables follows directly from the study's theoretical argument. The dependent variable, ln(GVAit), is preferred to purely physical indicators because the purpose of the article is to assess whether digital information fragility is associated with a businessrelevant economic outcome rather than only with material-flow performance. The variables Exposureit and Verifyit are retained because they are the conceptual components of the gap indicator, but they are not entered jointly with DVGit in the baseline model in order to avoid tautological overlap and potential multicollinearity. The variable DVGit is therefore treated as the core explanatory indicator, since it captures more parsimoniously the distance between misinformation pressure and verification capacity.
The second main regressor, LackSkillsit, captures a more specific dimension of digital information fragility. Whereas DVGit reflects an imbalance between exposure and verification, LackSkillsit isolates the inability to verify due to insufficient skills or knowledge. The robustness control, Unemploymentit, is included to account for changes in general macroeconomic conditions. By contrast, GDPPPSit and InternetUseit are retained for descriptive interpretation and possible sensitivity analysis only. Given the limited effective sample size after differencing, these auxiliary variables are not included in the baseline specification in order to preserve parsimony and avoid an unnecessary loss of degrees of freedom.
The misinformation-related variables used in this study originate from individual-level survey responses, but are used only after aggregation at the country-year level. Consequently, the empirical analysis does not infer that the same individuals who are exposed to doubtful online content, or who do not verify it, directly cause changes in circular economy gross value added. The estimated coefficients should therefore be interpreted as country-level associations between aggregate digital information conditions and aggregate circular economy performance. This distinction is important in order to avoid ecological fallacy. The DVG is treated as an indicator of the national digital information environment, not as evidence of individual behavioural causality.
Moreover, the DVG is computed as the arithmetic difference between two aggregate Eurostat survey indicators: exposure to incorrect or doubtful online content and verification of online information using other sources. It should, therefore, not be interpreted as a within-individual measure showing that the same respondents who encountered doubtful content did or did not verify that specific content. Rather, it captures a country-level imbalance between two related but distinct dimensions of the digital information environment: misinformation exposure and verification behaviour. For this reason, the DVG is used as an aggregate proxy for national digital information fragility, not as a direct behavioural measure of individual exposureverification episodes.

The estimator choice is dictated by the data structure. With only two time periods, dynamic panel estimators such as Arellano-Bond are not appropriate because they depend on internal lag instruments and moment conditions that are not informative in a two-period panel (Arellano & Bond, 1991; Arellano & Bover, 1995; Blundell & Bond, 1998). Moreover, when T = 2, the fixed-effects transformation is algebraically equivalent to first differencing, so a two-way fixed-effects model offers no gain over a first-difference specification (Hausman, 1978; Hausman & Taylor, 1981; Mundlak, 1978). Random effects would impose the stronger assumption that unobserved country-specific effects are uncorrelated with the regressors, which is difficult to justify in cross-country data (Hausman & Taylor, 1981; Mundlak, 1978). Pooled OLS in levels would, in turn, ignore time-invariant heterogeneity and risk mixing cross-country variation with the relationship of interest. Hence, the most defensible approach is a first-difference OLS on country-level changes between 2021 and 2023.
This modelling strategy is also conceptually consistent with the purpose of the paper. The study does not aim to identify a long-run structural parameter or to make strong causal claims. Rather, it provides a bounded applied-econometrics assessment of whether countries whose digital information environment deteriorated more strongly also experienced weaker changes in the economic performance of circular economy sectors over the same period. First differencing is therefore the appropriate response because it removes time-invariant country characteristics and focuses directly on within-country change (Chamberlain, 1982; Griliches & Hausman, 1986). Given that the estimation sample contains only 11 differenced observations, inference must remain conservative. The preferred specification therefore relies on HC3 heteroskedasticity-robust standard errors, which are generally more reliable than conventional OLS standard errors in very small samples (Long & Ervin, 2000; MacKinnon & White, 1985). In addition, the estimates are complemented by leave-one-country-out sensitivity checks in order to assess whether the fitted relationship is unduly driven by a single influential observation.
Because the 2021-2023 period coincided with major macroeconomic disruptions in Europe, including the energy price shock following the war in Ukraine, elevated inflation, and the persistence of supply-side pressures, the estimated coefficients should be interpreted with caution. The baseline model includes the change in unemployment as a parsimonious macroeconomic control, but the differenced sample contains only 11 observations, which prevents the inclusion of a broader control set without increasing overfitting risk. Accordingly, the estimates are interpreted as exploratory conditional associations rather than as isolated effects net of all contemporaneous macroeconomic shocks (European Central Bank, 2022).
3. Results and discussion
3.1. Descriptive results
This subsection presents the main descriptive patterns emerging from the balanced twoperiod panel of 11 Eastern European EU countries observed in 2021 and 2023. The analysis focuses on changes in the logarithm of circular economy gross value added, Δln(GV Ai), the Disinformation Verification Gap, ΔDV Gi, and skills-based non-verification, ΔLackSkillsi. Since the final sample contains no missing observations, the descriptive evidence is directly comparable across countries and variables.
As shown in Table no. 2, circular economy GVA declined in most countries between 2021 and 2023. Only Bulgaria, Estonia, and Lithuania recorded non-negative growth, while the largest contractions occurred in Romania, Slovenia, Slovakia, Hungary, and Czechia. The pattern also shows substantial heterogeneity in the DVG-GVA relationship. In seven countries, ΔDV Gi increased, but GVA rose in only three. Croatia stands out as a highleverage case, combining a sharp rise in ΔDV Gi with only a moderate decline in GVA. This heterogeneity supports the need for sensitivity analysis and cautions against interpreting the full-sample relationship as stable across countries.
Descriptive statistics for the variables expressed in first differences are reported in Table no. 3. Table no. 3 indicates substantial cross-country variation in the main variables used in the econometric specification. On average, Δln(GV Ai) = -0.046, which corresponds approximately to a 4.5% contraction in circular economy gross value added. At the same time, the mean value of ΔDV Gi is 0.544, indicating that exposure to doubtful content rose more rapidly than verification. By contrast, the average ΔLackSkillsi is - 0.218, suggesting a modest improvement in skills-based verification capacity. Taken together, these patterns indicate that the digital information environment deteriorated on average, although one dimension of capability-related fragility improved slightly. A further point emerging from Table no. 3 is that unemployment declined on average by 0.44 percentage points while circular economy gross value added contracted. This suggests that the observed weakening of circular-sector performance cannot be interpreted as a simple reflection of deteriorating aggregate labour-market conditions. Rather, the descriptive evidence points to a more specific adjustment pattern affecting circular economy activities during the period under study.
As illustrated in Figure no. 1, the correlation pattern indicates that the full-sample relationship between changes in the DVG and changes in circular economy GVA is weak, despite the expected negative sign. This suggests that the DVG alone cannot explain cross-country variation in circular economy performance and supports the need for cautious interpretation and sensitivity analysis.
As shown in Figure no. 2, the bivariate evidence points to a negative DVG-GVA association, but the relationship remains limited in strength. The dispersion of countries around the fitted line indicates weak explanatory power, while Croatia appears as a high-leverage case that helps explain the sample-sensitive nature of the estimates.
As indicated in Figure no. 3, the evidence provides little support for the expected negative relationship between skills-based non-verification and circular economy GVA. This reinforces the regression results and suggests that skills-based non-verification is a weaker macro-level predictor than the broader DVG indicator.

The baseline first-difference OLS estimates with HC3 robust standard errors are reported in Table no. 4. Model (1), which provides the direct test of H1, shows that the coefficient on ΔDV Gi is negative (- 0.0031), but statistically insignificant and substantively very small, indicating only a weak association between changes in the disinformation verification gap and changes in circular economy gross value added in the full sample. Model (2), which directly tests H2, yields a positive coefficient on ΔLackSkillsi (0.0074), again statistically insignificant, and therefore not supportive of the expected negative relationship. In Model (3), where ΔDV Gi and ΔLackSkillsi are entered jointly, the former remains negative (- 0.0047) and the latter remains positive (0.0135), but neither estimate is statistically distinguishable from zero. Model (4) adds ΔUnemploymenti as a parsimonious macroeconomic control; its coefficient is also small and statistically insignificant (0.0050), and its inclusion does not materially alter the two main coefficients. Across all four specifications, the explanatory power remains low, with R2 values ranging from 0.006 to 0.031, which is consistent with a weak and difficult-to-detect full-sample relationship in a very short balanced panel.
The sensitivity of the baseline DVG specification to single-country influence is reported in table no. 5. This diagnostic is particularly important because the estimation sample contains only 11 differenced observations, meaning that a single country may exert disproportionate leverage on the fitted relationship. The preliminary pattern suggests that the baseline coefficient on ΔDV Gi remains weak across almost all leave-one-country-out estimations, but changes markedly when Croatia is excluded. In that case, the estimated coefficient becomes much more negative, reaching - 0.0537, with a corresponding p-value of 0.004 and an R2 of 0.309. By contrast, when any other country is excluded, the coefficient remains close to zero, ranging approximately between - 0.0039 and 0.0007, and always remains statistically insignificant. This indicates that the estimated full-sample relationship is highly sensitive to a single influential observation.
3.3. Discussion
At the descriptive level, the evidence is broadly aligned with the study's theoretical expectation. Circular economy gross value added contracted in most countries between 2021 and 2023, while the digital information environment deteriorated on average, as exposure to doubtful content increased faster than verification. In addition, the bivariate association between ΔDV Gi and Δln(GV Ai is negative in sign. However, once the analysis moves from descriptive patterns to econometric estimation, this relationship remains weak and statistically fragile in the full sample. The appropriate interpretation is therefore not that the underlying theoretical argument is rejected, but that the available evidence supports it only cautiously and incompletely.
From this perspective, the results provide partial support for H1. Across the baseline specifications, the coefficient on ΔDV Gi remains negative, which is consistent with the expectation that a deterioration in the digital information environment may be associated with weaker circular economy performance. This directional consistency is meaningful because it does not contradict the broader literature on misinformation and credibility erosion. Previous studies show that misleading online content, fake reviews, and weak verification environments reduce trust, distort informativeness, and affect behavioural and market outcomes (Metzger et al., 2021; Miller et al., 2024; Sahut et al., 2024; Yannopoulou et al., 2024). In this sense, the present study examines whether these credibility-related frictions may also be reflected in a macro-level economic outcome. At the same time, the effect remains statistically insignificant in the full sample, which means that the evidence is not strong enough to sustain a robust macro-level inference.
The weak explanatory power of the models should also be interpreted in light of the macroeconomic context of 2021-2023. Circular economy GVA may have been affected by energy price pressures, elevated inflation, input-cost changes, and post-pandemic supply chain disruptions across European economies (European Central Bank, 2022). These factors are not fully captured by the unemployment control and may partly explain why the DVG coefficient remains statistically fragile in the full sample. Therefore, the estimated relationship should be understood as an exploratory association rather than as an isolated effect net of all contemporaneous macroeconomic shocks.
The sensitivity analysis further qualifies the results. Once Croatia is excluded, the estimated coefficient on ΔDV Gi becomes substantially more negative and statistically significant, suggesting that the relationship may exist but is highly sensitive to single-country influence. The Croatia-exclusion result should be read as a sensitivity diagnostic, not as a preferred estimate. Croatia is retained in the full sample because it is a valid observation, but its influence shows that the DVG-GVA relationship is not stable across all countries. The result suggests that the negative association becomes clearer when this high-leverage case is removed, while also confirming that the full-sample evidence remains fragile and should be interpreted with caution. Accordingly, the article does not claim definitive confirmation of H1; instead, it shows that the hypothesis remains theoretically plausible but empirically sample-sensitive.
The evidence for H2 is weaker. The coefficient on ΔLackSkillsi is positive rather than negative, and statistically insignificant in both the separate and joint specifications. Therefore, the results do not support the second hypothesis. This suggests that skills-based non-verification, although conceptually relevant, does not translate directly into weaker circular economy gross value added over the short period considered here. One possible explanation is that this variable captures a narrower behavioural constraint than the broader DVG measure. It reflects one stated reason for non-verification, namely insufficient skills or knowledge, but it does not measure all forms of verification capacity, actual verification quality, or exposure-verification episodes at the individual level. While digital literacy may reduce engagement with doubtful content at the micro level, its aggregate economic consequences may be indirect, slower-moving, and more strongly conditioned by institutional and sectoral factors.
These findings are broadly consistent with earlier research, but they also refine it. The literature on web seals, sustainability disclosures, and green claims demonstrates that weak credibility signals undermine trust and can produce behavioural, reputational, and economic losses (Lins et al., 2024; Sajid et al., 2024; Zhou et al., 2023). The present study does not contradict these theoretical expectations. Rather, it suggests that moving from micro-level and meso-level mechanisms to a macro-level outcome such as circular economy gross value added is empirically demanding. The Croatian case reinforces this interpretation: a sharp increase in the DVG does not necessarily coincide with a proportionately large contraction in circular economy GVA. This indicates that the relationship may be conditioned by additional country-specific factors, such as sectoral structure, institutional resilience, circular economy maturity, investment dynamics, or policy support.
Overall, the results do not provide strong full-sample confirmation of the study's hypotheses, but they remain broadly compatible with the underlying theoretical argument and with earlier research on misinformation, credibility, and market response. The contribution of the study lies less in establishing a definitive macroeconomic effect than in opening a new empirical line of inquiry that connects misinformation vulnerability, verification behaviour, and sustainability-oriented economic performance within a harmonised cross-country framework.
Conclusions
This study examined whether weaknesses in the digital information environment are associated with the economic performance of circular economy sectors in Eastern European EU countries. More specifically, it introduced the Disinformation Verification Gap (DVG), defined as the difference between exposure to doubtful online content and the verification of that content, and linked this measure to changes in gross value added (GVA) in circular economy sectors. Using a balanced two-period panel for 2021 and 2023 and a conservative first-difference specification, the study provided an initial econometric assessment of whether misinformation-related fragility may be associated with weaker circular value creation.
This makes three main contributions. First, it offers a clear and directly measurable indicator of macro-level misinformation vulnerability by operationalising the DVG from harmonised official data. Second, it extends the misinformation literature beyond trust, attitudes, and purchase intentions by connecting the quality of the online information environment to a concrete economic outcome, namely circular economy GVA. Third, it brings together three strands of research that are usually treated separately: digital misinformation, consumer verification and skills, and the economics of circular transition. In doing so, the article suggests that misinformation should be considered not only as a communication or behavioural issue, but also as a possible source of economic friction in sustainability-oriented sectors.
The evidence indicates that exposure to doubtful content increased faster than verification in several countries, while circular economy GVA weakened in much of the sample. The regression estimates are broadly consistent with the expected negative role of the DVG, but they remain statistically fragile in the full sample, reflecting the short time dimension and small number of observations. The study therefore does not claim strong causal identification. Its value lies instead in establishing a coherent measurement framework, demonstrating the feasibility of the research design, and showing that the relationship merits deeper investigation.
The implications of this study should be interpreted cautiously. For researchers, the article opens a new line of inquiry at the intersection of information asymmetry, digital resilience, and circular economy performance. For policymakers, the findings suggest cautiously that verification behaviour, digital skills, and trustworthy sustainability communication may be relevant complementary dimensions of the digital environment surrounding circular economy policy, although stronger evidence is needed before drawing firm policy conclusions. For firms and organisations operating in circular sectors, the results suggest that credibility, transparency, and trustworthy communication may be economically relevant in environments where misinformation is widespread.
At the managerial level, this implies that circular economy firms should treat information credibility as part of their market strategy. Firms operating in repair, reuse, recycling, and resource-efficiency activities should document sustainability claims with verifiable evidence, monitor misleading online narratives and fake reviews, respond rapidly to misinformation affecting their products or services, and communicate certifications, environmental performance, and circular value propositions in a transparent and easily verifiable manner. These practices may help reduce credibility frictions and strengthen trust in circular business models.
The study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings. First, the analysis is based on a short two-period panel and a small purposive sample, which limits statistical power and prevents strong causal identification. Second, because the misinformation-related variables are derived from individual survey items aggregated at the country level, the results should not be interpreted as evidence that individual verification behaviour directly causes sector-level changes in circular economy GVA. This ecologicalinference limitation reinforces the exploratory character of the study. Third, the DVG should be interpreted as a country-level indicator of the broader digital information environment rather than as a direct within-individual measure of exposure and verification behaviour. Fourth, the period analysed coincided with major macroeconomic disruptions, including energy price pressures, inflation, and post-pandemic supply chain adjustments, which may have affected circular economy GVA independently of the digital information environment.
Future research should extend this analysis by building longer panels, incorporating additional indicators such as circular material use and circular employment, and testing alternative measures of digital resilience. A broader time horizon and richer data would make it possible to assess whether the relationship examined here is temporary, structural, or conditional on specific institutional contexts. Even with these limitations, the present study suggests that the digital information environment deserves closer attention in future research on the economics of the circular transition.
