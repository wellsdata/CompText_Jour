Introduction The internet provides access to a wealth of information through social media platforms, which enable communication with other users. Some people create fake content on social media platforms to gain financially, often taking advantage of features, such as likes, shares, and comments [1]. Although not all online content is reliable, many people believe that shared information is true. Individuals create and share fake news consisting of misleading information that simulates real media content. A small piece of shared misinformation will quickly gain attention and published within no time, which reflects the current trend on social media platforms [2]. Fake news originates from various sources and aims to influence readers’ perceptions of people or ideas [3].
Fake news is disseminated in different domains such as political, healthcare, business, and entertainment. This diffusion of information is impacting on society and public trust across social media platforms [4]. There are some instances that have gone through discussions. For example, in 1835, the “The Sun” newspaper article, which is called the “moon hoax”, was the first fake news published that mentioned there were human-like creatures on the moon [5]. In the 2016 U.S. democratic presidential campaign, there was a lot of fake stories shared on social media which gained significant attention and people changed opinions about the election [6].
It is essential to understand how fake news is generated and disseminated across social media platforms by going beyond addressing only its impact for effective detection of fake information. As illustrated in Fig. 1. The life cycle of fake news has four interrelated stages: purpose, creation, platform-selection and propagation. The purpose stage explains the main intention behind creating the particular fake news, such as creating false beliefs, damaging the reputations of businesses, or exploiting situations for personal benefit. In the creation stage, the creator chooses multimodal data for gaining the attraction of user by making them believe in fake content, tampered images and deep-fake videos. Next, the platform-selection stage creators to disseminate the information in real-time, reaching millions of users at a time, especially targeting social media platforms. Finally, in the propagation stage, the fake content is spread widely across social media platforms by using social bots, attractive headlines, or including emotional content [7]. To eliminate the bias of previous research focusing on the effects of fake news, this paper aims to give information on structured pattern of fake news to build and implement more robust real-time multimodal detection systems. Furthermore, it seeks to optimize accuracy of detection through a systematic analysis of how fake news is generated and identified on social platforms. Fig. 1 [Images not available. See PDF.]

Multimodal datasets of social media The prevalent use of social media sites has increased the sharing of diverse content types, such as text, images, and videos. The variety of data formats presents significant challenges in accurately identifying fake information [8]. Fake news often contains textual and visual information and is typically created with the intent of misleading people, even though it is possible to verify that it is false [9]. Fake news creators manipulate text and images to fabricate real information to influence public opinion and stimulate rapid dissemination. Fake news content appears in multiple formats, such as text, audio, and visual media. As the fake news is spread using multimodal content across social media platforms, only relying on unimodal for fake news detection is challenging[10]. There are different feature analysis techniques for particular type of the content that is used for detection. Text analysis using natural language processing techniques, writing style, linguistic features, and temporal analysis of the content. Image analysis is through pixel distortions or altered images. Audio or video analysis has various techniques like frame interpolation, audio-visual consistencies or considering level of brightness [11]. It is very crucial to incorporate all kinds of content analysis since the multimodal content has influence over plain text information across social media platforms. This encourages the exploration of various multimodal datasets that are publicly accessible for social media data for effective detection of fake news [12]. When working on these datasets, it is critical to understand the relationship between multiple modalities while verifying the fake content as it may either be consistent or contradict each other for effective multimodal fake news detection [13].
Existing studies of multimodal content analysis used isolated approaches to analyze text and image features. There is a lack of studies on identifying similarities and differences in multimodal fake content which needs to be addressed for accurate detection of fake news [14]. It is necessary to establish superior capabilities in multimodal analysis techniques and to use appropriate tools for identifying inconsistencies in multimodal content to understand relationships between diverse modalities [15]. To tackle these challenges in the context of multimodal fake news identification, it is essential to move beyond traditional machine learning techniques to deep learning techniques for detecting deepfake content from real content. Traditional machine learning techniques Supervised, semi-supervised, and unsupervised learning are examples of traditional techniques [27]. Recent research techniques have employed supervised classifiers such as decision trees and gradient boosting with annotated datasets, semi-supervised support vector machine (SVM) techniques with both labelled and unlabeled data, and unsupervised techniques such as hierarchical clustering and variational autoencoders to detect fake news. Studies that combined word embedding with linguistic aspects on social media networks improved accuracy by 1.73% [23]. The XGBOOST and random forest (RF) classifiers achieved F1- scores of 0.81, with the highest area under the curve (AUC) scores of 0.86 and 0.85, respectively. The use of RF, along with user behavior modelling on the basis of social media activity and network propagation, resulted in 94% accuracy [28]. Using the XGBOOST and RF algorithms, an analysis of media channels achieved an accuracy of 82.3% [29]. The study explores social media data from twitter for disaster reporting. It utilizes a distributed system to analyze geolocation-based content communities and address misinformation [30]. While adding many classifiers can enhance outcomes, traditional models lack automation for real-time data and exhibit lower accuracy in detecting fake news [31].
Deep learning techniques Deep learning techniques demonstrate strong generalization capabilities by leveraging large-scale datasets, enabling effective performance on unseen data through automated feature extraction and multilingual understanding [32]. For the identification of fake information, deep learning offers sub techniques such as deep residual networks (ResNets), attention mechanisms, long short-term memory (LSTM) model and gated recurrent units (GRU) networks. A pre-trained model combines textual and visual elements and is tested on four datasets [33]. Techniques for cross-lingual and cross-domain learning highlight underutilized models for detecting misinformation. Attention mechanisms in neural transformer networks integrate contextual meanings and content features which are essential for effective model performance [34]. Using Chinese and English datasets, multilingual integration outperformed LSTM by 8.6% [35]. In fake news detection, mixed-domain techniques outperform single-domain techniques [36]. Three neural models trained on specific datasets were employed in fake video detection techniques [37]. Pre-trained word embeddings process text data to detect false information. Recurrent neural networks (RNN) show higher accuracy than logistic regression[38]. BART (Bidirectional and Auto-Regressive Transformer) and RoBERTa (Robustly Optimized BERT Pre-training approach) sentence embeddings identified misinformation with 92.50% accuracy outperforming FakeBERT model’s 70% accuracy[39]. The study uses mixture of transformer models to identify sexism across different languages on social media conversations [40]. Furthermore, highlighting the lack of comprehensive frameworks and reliance on static datasets that lead to overfitting, this review revealed inconsistencies in detection.
Ensemble techniques Research has shown that ensemble techniques that combine multiple modalities and techniques, achieve better accuracy [41]. Multimodal detection techniques including CNN, Bidirectional Encoder Representations from Transformers (BERT), and stacking achieved 99.8%, 99.9%, and 99.8%, respectively [42].The multi-layered network achieved strong accuracy by interpreting words and extracting visual features using attention mechanisms [43]. One of the studies utilized temporal patterns and text features improved the cross-domain performance by 4.43% and enhanced the detection precision [44]. The study proposes a deep neural network combining textual and social context data to assess news accuracy on social media achieved high accuracy using limited training data [45]. However, due to their processing demands, many ensemble techniques are unsuitable for real-time applications, which remains a significant challenge.
Natural language processing techniques Earlier research has combined machine learning and NLP to detect fake news, demonstrating more effective detection techniques than previous methods did, although they were limited to small datasets. For both qualitative and quantitative reasons, NLP techniques examine the language and semantic features of social media messages. Studies have identified linguistic features that distinguish genuine publications from less trustworthy sources [46]. The F1- score for the detection of erroneous information using neural network techniques was 0.82 [47]. In terms of the F1- score, Roberta outperforms other pre-trained models. To highlight the psychological factors that impact news trust, studies have examined misinformation during the U.S. democratic election and the elements that drive trust in newspaper headlines [48]. Using sentence transformers with Bi-LSTM (Bidirectional Long Short-Term Memory) model achieved 0.53 accuracy on English data, while cross-lingual transformers showed lower performance [49]. Transformer-based embeddings excel over other deep learning techniques. The primary factor influencing fake news detection is the type of embedding method used, rather than the model’s complexity[50].
Graph based techniques Graph-based techniques utilize networked processes to identify fake news by mapping the relationships among user profiles, content, and the dissemination of information. These models are effective for real-time applications and perform well in identifying false information. Compared with recurrent neural networks (RNNs), event adversarial networks have enhanced accuracy by 10.6% [51]. Various control techniques are employed to mitigate fake news spread on social media [52]. Although graph-based techniques are computationally expensive, data-driven and knowledge-driven techniques perform effectively on multilingual datasets [53]. The study uses GETAE (Graph Information Enhanced Deep Neural Network Ensemble), which uses node embeddings to track information flow from the source through the network showing greater effectiveness in early fake news detection [54]. The study introduces a deep learning framework to predict the virality of news topics by analyzing dissemination patterns and audience engagement on social media through twitter likes and retweets [55].
This study highlights challenges and proposes future research directions by critically examining employed techniques and their associated limitations, as outlined in Table 2. The key findings are current detection methodologies lack in real-time processing, exhibit poor semantic alignment across modalities and rely on unimodal approaches. Thus, there is a critical need for real-time, multimodal approaches to fake news detection.
Tools With the rapid growth in social media users and the large-scale content they produce, it has become unfeasible to track online posts manually. This highlights the need for automated tools to prevent misinformation spread [65]. Automation helps to identify viral posts making it easier to combat fake news effectively. Detecting misinformation early helps reduce societal impact by providing accurate information, allowing users to make better decisions and be more resistant to misleading content [66].
Tools are essential for detecting, validating, and mitigating false information, and are categorized by data type including knowledge bases and application programming interfaces (APIs) [67]. Knowledge bases contain a huge volume of information for verification of factual claims, whereas APIs provide access to large-scale data across social media platforms to verify the content in real-time[68]. By leveraging APIs, fake news can be identified early as it enables rapid analysis of information shared online across social media. There is a critical need to process different modalities of social network content to enhance the accuracy of multimodal detection of fake news [69]. Text content analysis APIs Text APIs use natural language processing techniques to analyze the hidden meanings of the text, such as misleading language patterns, emotional content, incorrect claims, or false headlines to accurately identify such fake information across social media.
Google API It is essential for extracting valuable insights from text-based data through the application of techniques such as word embedding, syntactic categorization, term frequency-inverse document frequency (TF-IDF) analysis, and entity identification [15]. These NLP techniques convert text patterns into more organized vectors for evaluating content by using machine learning techniques for effective fake news detection.
IBM API This API is used to identify the emotions and opinions of news articles. Based on the emotion applied, the article is divided into positive type, negative type or neutral type. The use of emotional or biased content in certain information indicates that such information is potentially false[69]. By accessing these APIs, sentiment scores can be given to text to accurately identify the fake news detection.
SpaCy API This API is used to identify similarities between text and image modalities to find out if there are any inconsistencies between them, which is the most valuable API for multimodal fake news detection. By detecting relationships such as images with different captions, temporal information change among modalities enhances detection capabilities in verifying the consistency of multimodal content [70].
Image content analysis APIs The visual APIs are used to identify image elements and collect the metadata of posts for the assessment of the visual content in real-time using deep learning techniques.
Microsoft API This API is utilized for the identification of tampered images by considering various features of image such as color distributions, altered images, pixel degradation, and object recognition [13]. Machine-learning techniques are trained for analyzing these image features to accurately identify fake news detection.
TinEye API This API is utilized to identify if any previous images are reused to spread fake information and mislead users about any specific events. It also helps identify relationships between image and text by analyzing the underlying consistencies between the image and text content on social media posts. For instance, by using embedding techniques, the CLIP model analyses the consistency of multimodal content[71].
Amazon API This API recognizes objects within images for analyzing correlations with the text data to verify the image content. For instance, the BLIP-2 model used this API for the identification of objects in images to increase the reliability of multimodal fake news detection.
Video content analysis APIs Video content analysis APIs are utilized to identify the deepfake content used for creating false opinions of people about specific events across social media platforms. Deep learning techniques are mostly preferred for video content due to its complexity.
Video API This API identifies inconsistencies between audio and visual elements of the video using various features such as key frame analysis, object recognition and audio signal interpretations for accurate identification of deepfake content [41].
OpenPose API This API is utilized to detect actions in videos that are match with the activities of the video content, and notice inconsistencies such as human interactions and background occurrences for comprehensive analysis of video for effective multimodal fake news detection [72].
Face API This API is used to verify faces in video content and verify if it contains deepfake or AI-generated content which promotes the authenticity of users by analyzing facial expressions and mitigates the spread of fake information on social media platforms [72].
Performance indicators Accuracy (A) This metric assesses the effectiveness of machine learning techniques. It is defined as the ratio of correct predictions relative to the total number of predictions [73]. It provides accuracy of the model based on the positive and negative samples present in the dataset. However, if the dataset has an imbalance of positive and negative samples, it shows poor performance of the model by predicting majority class samples correctly most of the time and misclassifying the minority class samples. 1

Precision (P) This metric evaluates the capability of the model to identify true positive instances that are actually correct. If the model has lower number of false positives, it indicates the effective performance of the model [74]. 2

Recall (R) This metric measures the number of true positive instances the model has correctly classified, and reduces false negative instances. There is a trade-off between precision and recall as optimizing one metric results in decreased performance of other metrics [31]. Thus, both metrics should be considered for the effective performance of the model. 3

F1- score (F1) This metric measures harmonic average of precision and recall, it assesses performance of the model by considering both false positives and false negatives. This metric is utilized for imbalanced datasets, the model’s performance can be assessed by considering both precision and recall when classifying classes. 4

Area under the curve (AUC) This metric uses thresholds to evaluate the accuracy of the model in classifying the samples. The higher the value of AUC, the better is the effectiveness of the model. For proper assessment, it is advised to use AUC along with other metrics such as precision, recall, and F1-score [75].
Multimodal consistency score (MCS) This metric measures the consistency across diverse modalities. It finds the inconsistencies using the visual manipulation detection rate (VMDR) technique, which promotes effective detection of modified content [20].
For instance, one of the studies proposed a deep learning technique that employs joint embeddings to identify inconsistencies which enhance the performance of multimodal systems[12]. Furthermore, the paper presents a hierarchical bilinear fusion model which uses VMDR for assessing visual and textual coherence [76].
Modality attribution This metric works well on balanced datasets containing multimodal data, enhancing accurate detection by integrating different modalities. If this metric is used on a single modality, it gives biased results, resulting in poor performance of the model [77].
Challenges and future directions for multimodal fake news detection: a comprehensive analysis Challenges identified in multimodal detection Dataset limitations In multimodal fake news detection, many difficulties are faced due to limited and imbalanced datasets. Large, high-quality datasets containing both text and images are not provided. Most datasets are created with either text or visual content, but rarely both together [75]. Real-world news is usually shared with multiple formats, yet such complete examples are not collected properly. When datasets are built, enough labelled examples are not included. Diverse languages and cultural content are also not covered. Most datasets are created using Western or English-based content. As a result, models are trained on biased and incomplete data. The content is often reused from older fake news stories, which makes the models outdated. Annotated datasets are built using crowd workers, but the labels are not always accurate. Misleading image-text pairs are not corrected before training. Because of these gaps, real fake news is not detected effectively. More balanced, up-to-date, and multilingual datasets are still not being released. Without such data, progress in multimodal detection has slowed.
Multimodal fusion challenges Serious problems are observed when multiple data types such as text, image, or video, are combined. Different features are extracted from each modality, but these are not always well aligned. Simple fusion techniques such as feature concatenation are being applied, but important relationships between modalities are being missed. Sometimes, fake news is hidden when an image that does not match the text is used. Such mismatches are not noticed by the models. In many systems, one strong modality, like text, is given more weight, while the image is ignored. As a result, false conclusions are being drawn. Advanced fusion techniques have been proposed, but high resources are required for their training [76]. Dimensionality problems are also faced when early fusion is applied. Late fusion often results in less effective detection. Attention-based methods are being explored, but their benefits are being reduced when noisy data are used. Due to these difficulties, better integration between text and visual content is still not being achieved.
Real-time detection constraints Real-time fake news detection is not achieved efficiently in multimodal systems. When text, images, and videos are processed together, considerable memory and time are consumed. Live posts are created quickly, but detection is delayed. Models trained on old data are not updated fast enough to handle new types of fake content. Some fake news posts are uploaded without full information, such as missing images or broken text. In these cases, detection is badly affected [77]. Mobile devices and weaker networks are not well supported by current systems. On-the-spot detection is not provided because of heavy model requirements. Incremental learning is not performed properly, so changes in fake news styles are not being adapted. Often, when detection is complete, the fake content is already being shared widely. The speed and accuracy are not balanced effectively. Therefore, real-time protection from fake news is not provided as needed.
Multimodal semantic misalignment A major challenge is caused by semantic misalignment between different media types. A caption may be saying something completely different from the image shown, but such issues are not being caught. Visuals are often used with false context, and these tricks are being missed by the models [78]. Irony, sarcasm, and symbolic images are not interpreted correctly. Even when tools such as object detection are used, deeper meanings are not understood. Visual-textual mismatches are overlooked in many cases. Cultural meanings and political messages are also not identified when symbolic images or memes are used. Fake news creators use real images with fake narratives, and such examples are not filtered. The semantic relationships between text and visuals are not well checked by current systems. Because of this, many mismatched fake stories are marked as real. However, better semantic alignment tools are still not designed or deployed widely.
Cross-platform generalization Cross-platform detection is not handled effectively by current models [79]. Content is being shared in different formats on Twitter, Instagram, YouTube, and WhatsApp, but each platform uses its own unique style and structure. Often, models are trained on data from one platform and then tested on another platform, which leads to poor results. For example, short tweets, visual-heavy posts, and private forwarded messages are processed differently. Platform-specific slang and symbols are not understood. Additionally, data from many platforms are not made public due to privacy restrictions. Because of that, training data are limited. Even when access is granted, the data structure and language style are being changed too much between platforms. Unified learning is not achieved across them. Model performance decreases when the same news is shared in different ways. Generalization is not performed correctly, and fake news is not being caught equally across all platforms.
Adversarial threats Multimodal fake news detection models are being misled by adversarial techniques. Fake news is modified in small but smart ways so it cannot be detected. The text is being rewritten, and visuals are slightly changed to avoid the use of detection tools. AI-generated videos and texts are being used more often now, and these are not being spotted easily. Deepfake technology is used to create real-looking fake content [34]. Models are attacked by subtle edits that go unnoticed. Few defenses are trained to handle such inputs. Most systems are trained with clean examples, not adversarial ones. As a result, when new methods are used, models are confused. Cross-modal attacks are being applied, where only one part of the content is changed to affect the overall detection. Fake content is passed through platforms without being flagged. Therefore, current systems are easily bypassed.
Lack of explainability A lack of explainability is noted in most fake news detection models [60]. When a news item is classified as false, the models often fail to provide clear reasons. This causes distrust among users and news organizations. Deep learning models are treated as black boxes, where internal processes are not shared. In multimodal systems, text, images, and videos are combined, making explanations even more difficult. Without clarity, the results are not accepted by people. Sometimes, real news is incorrectly marked as fake, and no explanation is provided. Public trust is not being built, and systems are being rejected. Simple tools such as attention visualization are not being used enough to help explain decisions. Human reviewers are not being supported with enough evidence. Transparency is not maintained, and accountability is not enforced. Because of this, the adoption of detection models is slowing, and their usefulness is being questioned.
Future research directions for multimodal fake news detection in real-time Amid the rise of digital era, the widespread dissemination of fake news has become a significant issue, which influences key domains such as political elections and public health [82]. As misinformation continues to circulate rapidly across online platforms, there is an urgent need for effective strategies to identify and mitigate the dissemination of false information. Detecting fake news involves a complex, multi-faceted approach that incorporates technological tools, human oversight, and interdisciplinary strategies. We explore the multimodal datasets utilized in the current research, while some datasets are self-aggregated, and there is limited research on computational approaches. Traditional machine learning methods rely on pre-trained data, restricting them from processing real-time information because of the changing nature of news and the diversity of social media platforms publishing both accurate and misleading content [83]. Additionally, large, high-quality datasets are lacking, which hinders the development of effective detection models. There is a need for sophisticated deep learning models to handle vast amounts of real-time data generated worldwide. Research has shown that deep learning techniques achieve greater precision than other techniques when trained on comprehensive datasets [74].
Our literature review explored various techniques for fake news detection, including traditional machine learning techniques, deep learning techniques, graph-based techniques, hybrid techniques, and natural language processing techniques. It is essential to consider several factors to develop a more robust and effective fake news detection model [84]. First, real-time knowledge bases should be incorporated, and models should be trained with large volumes of recently updated data. Second, advanced algorithms are needed to efficiently process large datasets, minimizing computational time while maximizing accuracy [63]. Third, applying ensemble techniques enhances the detection of fake information. Ensemble techniques are applied for cross-verification of different methods which achieve high accuracy[83]. Furthermore, psychological insights provide information about the underlying reason for fake information, which helps to reduce bias in training data and ensure effective real-time systems [84]. Network analysis techniques are utilized to understand the virality of fake news across social media networks to identify the spread of fake information. It is also useful for identifying user engagement patterns and tracing the origin of content before it reaches a large audience [85]. Fact-checking websites and knowledge bases are crucial in verifying the authenticity of the content and predicting fake news in real-time [86].
Researchers can integrate different techniques into a single framework that incorporates multiple domains, data types, and social media platforms to build robust multimodal detection systems[87]. This integrated framework can improve predictions in complex tasks such as verification of deepfake content. Furthermore, the growing issue of deep fake content used to spread fake information on video platforms has gained very limited attention in research[88]. The development of real-time fake news detection systems is critical, as existing studies demonstrated on static datasets which failed to adopt the fast-changing nature of social media content [89].
There is a necessity to incorporate multilingual datasets as new comes in different languages for an effective detection system[89]. Also, the cognitive and emotional biases behind the information need to be given importance to understand the influence of propagation and educating the public on non-credible sources can minimize the impact of fake information on society [90]. It is very essential to address all challenges to prevent the dissemination of fake news in real-time.
Conclusion This review explores multimodal datasets, tools, and machine learning techniques, emphasizing the critical need for real-time, multimodal systems capable of processing dynamic data. The study underscores the importance of considering diverse types of content on social media platforms, where fake news is often widely spread. Understanding how fake news is disseminated is crucial for developing techniques that can efficiently identify and automate the detection of fake news. Additionally, the paper outlines performance indicators that can optimize model efficiency and discusses the significant challenges in detection. To address these challenges, our study proposes several future research directions. These include developing deep learning models capable of handling large-scale real-time data, improving cross-modal analysis to capture relationships between textual and visual content for multimodal fake news detection. By addressing these areas, we can develop reliable, efficient, and trustworthy computing systems to mitigate the harmful social impact of fake news in digital world.
