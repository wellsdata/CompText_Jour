What a Load of CRAAP: Evaluating Information in an Era of "Fake News"
by Heather R. Duby
------------
Heather Duby is the Library Director for the Sullivan County Public Library System and a member of the Intellectual Freedom Committee. She can be followed on Twitter (@Kelshei) and may be contacted at slchduby@gmail.com.
------------
It is nearly impossible for anyone to turn on network (or even cable) news or scan through various social media or other websites without having something proclaimed as “fake news.” This phrase is so neatly woven into our media consumption that most of us have become inured to it, but as librarians, we need to be aware of the idea of “fake news,” especially in how it relates to our patrons coming to us for assistance in finding credible sources of information.
Despite the notion that libraries are a dying institution and that people are more likely to get their information from Google than from a librarian, the fact of the matter is that, if you are reading this article, you are aware that we still have a number of patrons that seek our assistance in proving, or disproving, the veracity of the information that they read, whether in a newspaper or on a social media or news site. So, how can we best assist our patrons – especially patrons at a public library who may or may not have had any experience with evaluating sources – with becoming discerning consumers of information without imposing our own biases onto their consumption?
When I was a baby librarian, one of the gold standards of information evaluation was the CRAAP Test wherein an evaluator would rate the information based upon the Currency, the Relevance, the Authority, the Accuracy, and the Purpose of the source at hand. And, while that test is still not a bad way to evaluate information, the speed with which we are inundated with new sources of information makes going through every single thing we read or hear and applying this test to it unwieldy, at best, and many people don’t even bother.
In her article, “Beyond Fake News,” Meredith Farkas discusses the notion that there is no such thing as a truly unbiased source, which is, of course, true. She states that the “solution is not just helping people develop simple, common-sense web evaluation habits. It’s also helping them determine what they are going to trust, since the vast majority of sources lie in the gray area between unimpeachability [sic] and fake news” (Farkas 2018).
Combatting “fake news” is also not as simple as linking to a Snopes article every time a friend posts something on Facebook without doing even cursory fact-checking. That’s something I am called upon to do with, sadly, increasing frequency. But, even as a librarian, I, too, am guilty of posting things without doing my due diligence regarding the accuracy of the article of which I am posting because, like so many other people, time gets away from me.
So, are those of us who desperately want to force feed critical thinking skills to all and sundry idealistic? Some might think so, but I don’t believe that should stop us. Libraries and librarians should be bastions of, and warriors for, intellectual freedom. It is incumbent upon us to help those we serve to get the most accurate information as we possibly can. Unfortunately, one of the edges of the intellectual freedom sword is that we cannot censor what we see as “fake news” and we must, instead, share the sources that we find with the patron and let them come to their own conclusions.
