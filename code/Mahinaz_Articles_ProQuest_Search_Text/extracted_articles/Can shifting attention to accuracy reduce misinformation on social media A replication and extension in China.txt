Introduction
The spread of online misinformation can easily evolve into an “information crisis”, posing serious challenges to government emergency management. During the COVID-19 pandemic, misinformation spread rapidly through social media, and the World Health Organization even issued a warning of an “infodemic” to attract people’s attention (Fleming, 2020). This misinformation comes in many forms, including conspiracy theories about the origin of the pandemic, false treatment information, and false government policy and disposition information (Zeng and Chan, 2021). Exposure to such misinformation can lead people to take misguided actions that are detrimental to national emergency management, such as overreacting (e.g., hoarding commodities) or underreacting (e.g., engaging in risky behavior and inadvertently spreading the virus) (Pennycook, 2020). To effectively combat the spread of online misinformation, we need to understand why people share misinformation online.
Scholars have proposed different theories to explain the behavior of spreading online misinformation. One theory that has attracted particular attention in recent years is the inattention-based theory proposed by Pennycook, Epstein, et al. (2021). The theory challenges the traditional “post-truth” interpretation that people intentionally share misinformation. Conversely, inattention-based theory holds that most people do not want to spread misinformation, and in many cases, people are also able to distinguish between true and false information. However, when disseminating online information, their attention may be shifted to factors other than the accuracy of the information. Experiments from the United States have shown that this theory can explain the sharing of COVID-19 misinformation and that the spread of misinformation can be effectively reduced by designing a variety of accuracy-prompt interventions that focus the public’s attention on accuracy as information is disseminated online (Pennycook, 2020). On a practical level, this accuracy-prompt intervention is particularly attractive because it does not require government regulators or technology companies to decide (e.g., through machine learning or human review) what is true or false; instead, the user’s own ability to identify misinformation is relied upon, making the approach more scalable (e.g., unlike professional fact-checking, which is often slow and covers only a small portion of all content) (Pennycook and Rand, 2022a).
However, it is worth noting that the effectiveness of inattention-based theory and accuracy-prompt interventions has currently been verified mainly in the United States. Given the global nature of online misinformation, it is important to assess the generalizability of this theory and its corresponding measures (Pennycook and Rand, 2022b). Before encouraging decision-makers to implement accuracy-prompt interventions, we also need to assess the effectiveness of various measures in specific contexts. Arechar et al. (2023) conducted an experiment in 16 countries and found that subtly prompting people to think about accuracy was broadly effective at improving information-sharing quality. Their study has advanced the replication research of inattention-based theory. However, they did not further analyze what proportion of misinformation sharing can be explained by inattention-based theory—the explanatory power of the theory influences the implementation prospects of accuracy interventions. Furthermore, to enable cross-country comparisons among 16 nations, they employed identical experimental materials, which consequently led to insufficient consideration of each country’s actual information dissemination context.
Based on the above considerations, this paper selects COVID-19 in China as a research field to replicate and expand on the original study of Pennycook et al. (2020). To this end, this study carried out three experiments. In Study 1, we tested whether there was a disconnect between accuracy judgments and sharing intentions. In Study 2, we tested the effectiveness of an intervention—designed to induce participant accuracy concepts—for improving the quality of the COVID-19 information people share online. In Study 3, we analyzed the proportion of misinformation shared due to inattention.
The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. In the second section, literature review and an introduction to the original study are carried out. Sections 3, 4, and 5 explain the participants, materials, experimental procedures, analytical methods, and results of Studies 1, 2 and 3, respectively. The final section compares the findings of the original study with those of this study and discuss the theoretical and practical implications of the findings.
Literature review and original study
Why do people spread misinformation? There are three theoretical explanations. The first theory is confusion-based theory, which holds that people share misinformation because they mistakenly believe that the information is accurate. Reasons that lead people to believe in misinformation include the use of heuristic cues and a lack of careful reasoning and relevant knowledge. Heuristic behavior is based on intuition, and simple judgments are usually made based on external cues of information. When judging the veracity of information, people are likely to use heuristics or mental shortcuts. Source credibility, feelings of familiarity and social feedback provided by social media platforms all increase beliefs about misinformation (Avram et al., 2020; Pornpitakpan, 2004). Systematic behavior is based on rationality and requires the use of sufficient cognitive resources to evaluate information. Those who lack deliberative reasoning are more likely to believe misinformation (Bago et al., 2020).
The second theory is preference-based theory, which holds that although people are aware that information may not be true, they place their preferences for political identity (or related motivations, such as virtue signaling (Jordan and Rand, 2019)) above the truth and thus intentionally share misinformation on social media. This purposeful sharing could be motivated by advancing one’s political agenda (Van Bavel and Pereira, 2018), sowing confusion (Petersen et al., 2023), or sharing news that would be interesting if it turned out to be true (Mercier et al., 2022). However, research suggests that preference-based theories cannot fully explain the disconnect between accurate judgments and sharing intentions (Pennycook et al., 2021).
The third theory is inattention-based theory, which holds that the sharing of misinformation is often unintentional. People generally want to avoid spreading misinformation, and in many cases, people can also distinguish between true and false information. The reason people share misinformation is that the social media context focuses their attention on factors other than accuracy, such as the desire to attract and please followers/friends (Marwick and Boyd, 2011), identify themselves as group members (Donath and Boyd, 2004), or engage in emotionally or morally uplifting content (Brady et al., 2017). Therefore, when sharing information, they may not have considered the accuracy of the information and thus shared misinformation.
Various approaches have been developed to combat the spread of misinformation. Algorithmic approaches, which are commonly employed by social media companies, utilize machine learning, natural language processing, and network analysis to automate the identification of misinformation, followed by interventions that reduce its exposure frequency to prevent dissemination (Pennycook & Rand, 2021a). A notable advantage of this approach lies in its capacity to process vast volumes of data at scale. However, algorithmic approaches remain constrained by limitations arising from their inherent lack of prior social, cultural, and political knowledge (Shin, 2024).
Inoculation approaches represent promising strategies for combating the spread of misinformation (Musi et al., 2023). Inoculation theory posits that, analogous to how medical vaccines build resistance to specific diseases, informational “vaccines” can achieve a similar effect at the cognitive level. By preemptively exposing users to a refuted or a weakened version of the message beforehand, this approach aims to increase their cognitive resistance to future misinformation (Compton et al., 2021). Inoculation approaches can be categorized into two types: passive inoculation and active inoculation. Passive inoculation directly offers individuals counterarguments that refute misinformation, whereas active inoculation encourages individuals to generate those counterarguments (Shin, 2024). Research has demonstrated that inoculation approaches effectively strengthen individuals’ belief in truth and discerning ability, while reducing misinformation-sharing behaviors (Compton et al., 2021).
However, inoculation approaches fundamentally aim to increase people’s foundational knowledge or skills (Pennycook and Rand, 2021a). However, as posited by the inattention-based theory, the spread of misinformation occurs not only because individuals are confused or lack the ability to recognize misinformation but also because they fail to consider the accuracy of information at all when it is shared. Therefore, accuracy-prompt interventions have emerged as a promising strategies, aiming to encourage people to slow down, reflect on the accuracy of the information they encounter on social media, and thereby mitigate the spread of misinformation.
Pennycook et al. (2021) verified inattention-based theory through a series of experiments in political news. First, they found a disconnect between accuracy judgments and sharing intentions through a survey experiment. When asked to rate the “accuracy of information,” most participants were skilled in distinguishing between true and fake political news, but the accuracy of information had little effect on participants’ intentions to share on social media. Next, the authors tested whether inattention-based theories could explain the disconnect through four investigative experiments and a field experiment on Twitter. The results showed that a subtle shift of attention to accuracy reduced participants’ sharing of misinformation and improved the quality of information sharing. Third, the authors conducted a survey experiment that quantifies the relative contribution of inattention-based theory to sharing intentions. The findings showed that inattention-based theory could explain 51.2% of people’s behavior in sharing misinformation, indicating that inattention played an important role in influencing people to share misinformation.
To verify the generalizability of the inattention-based theory, the authors conducted a series of replication experiments and proved that inattention-based theories as well as accuracy-prompt interventions were replicable in the United States (Pennycook et al., 2020; Pennycook and Rand, 2022a). For example, Pennycook et al. (2020) carried out two experiments and found that the findings from the domain of political misinformation could be generalized to COVID-19 misinformation. Given the global nature of the misinformation challenge, the authors suggest that there is a need to test whether these findings can be generalized to different countries. Our research interest is whether inattention-based theory is replicable in the Chinese context. China is one of the most difficult countries to control misinformation, and the current approach of mainly relying on government and technology companies to combat misinformation has disadvantages such as high cost and poor scalability. Inattention-based theory provides a different governance idea that can reduce the spread of misinformation through simple accurate intervention measures, so as to make this method more scalable. This study uses three survey experiments to answer the following three questions: Is there a disconnect between accuracy judgments and sharing intentions in the context of Chinese social media? Is accuracy-prompt intervention effective at improving the quality of information sharing among the Chinese public? If so, what proportion of misinformation sharing on Chinese social media can be explained by inattention-based theory? Answering these questions can help us understand the replicability and application potential of inattention-based theory in China to propose corresponding countermeasures for China’s misinformation governance.
Study 1
Purpose of the study
The purpose of Study 1 was to test whether there was a disconnect between accuracy judgments and sharing intentions. The key concept in analyzing this problem is truth discernment, defined as the difference in accuracy judgments (or sharing intentions) between true and false information (Pennycook et al., 2020). The original study showed that participants were willing to share misinformation about COVID-19 that they were clearly able to identify as untrue if they were asked directly about accuracy. In other words, when participants were asked whether they would share a piece of information on social media, they were far less likely to have truth discernment than when asked about its accuracy (Pennycook et al., 2020). This phenomenon is called the disconnect between accuracy judgments and sharing intentions (Pennycook, Epstein, et al., 2021). We expect the same results as in the original study; thus, the following hypothesis is proposed:
H1 : There is a disconnect between accuracy judgments and sharing intentions; that is, people’s truth discernment is worse when they share information on social media than when they evaluate accuracy.
This study also tested the relationships between the cognitive reflection test (CRT) and truth discernment. The CRT is a widely used indicator for measuring individual analytical thinking, and individuals who perform well in the CRT tend to engage in analytical and deliberative thinking (Frederick, 2005). A theoretical perspective based on cognitive science suggests that when people rely on intuition instead of analytical thinking, they are more likely to believe in misinformation (Pennycook and Rand, 2021a). Multiple empirical studies, including those from China, have also confirmed that individuals who are more reflective and analytical (compared to those who rely more on intuition) are better able to distinguish between true and false information on social media (He and Yan, 2021; Pennycook et al., 2020; Pennycook and Rand, 2020). In addition, evidence suggests that people who are more reflective also have greater discernment when sharing information on social media (Mosleh et al., 2021; Pennycook and Rand, 2021b). Therefore, the following hypothesis is proposed:
H2 : People with higher CRT scores have greater truth discernment.
Participants
This study was conducted between October 21, 2022 and October 26, 2022. We recruited 360 people from the Credamo platform (https://www.credamo.com, an online survey platform similar to Qualtrics from China) to participate in the experiment. Participants were screened using three criteria. First, considering that WeChat and Weibo are currently the two most commonly used social media platforms in China and our research interest is information sharing, only those who normally use WeChat or Weibo and indicated that they would share information on WeChat or Weibo were allowed to participate in the survey. Second, we added two attention detection questions before and after the “Accuracy Judgments and Sharing Intentions” task; for example, in a question ostensibly about which websites people trust more, we asked participants to choose a specific option to check whether they were reading the question. Finally, we manually screened the sample of those questionnaires with questions, mainly including two types of questions: identical answers and incomplete information. Participants who failed to pass the screening were automatically rejected by the system or manually rejected. Overall, 419 participants initially entered the survey, and 59 participants failed the above screening. Among the participants who did not pass the screening, 1 participant did not use WeChat or Weibo, 16 participants did not pass the attention detection, 13 participants gave identical answers when expressing their sharing intentions or judging the accuracy of all 30 pieces of information, and 29 participants’ questionnaire information was incomplete (missing information due to failure to respond within the specified response time). We ultimately obtained 360 samples, of which 128 (35.6%) were male and 232 (64.4%) were female. The average age of the participants was 30.27 years (SD = 8.07), the age range was 17–58 years, and the vast majority (306 or 85%) of the participants were 21 to 40 years old. Most participants (262 or 72.8%) held undergraduate degrees. A total of 165 (45.8%) were not affiliated with any political party, 98 (27.2%) were members of the Communist Party of China and 95 (26.4%) were members of the Communist Youth League of China. Materials
We showed participants 15 pieces of false information and 15 pieces of true information (available at https://osf.io/7md5n/), which were presented to each participant in random order. We gathered the information needed for this study following the practical guide to performing behavioral research on misinformation given by Pennycook, Binnendyk, et al. (2021). For false information, the guide recommends using false information that is already widely spread on social media because it contains features that help it spread on social media. Fact-checking organizations are suitable channels for obtaining such information because they keep a record of false information spread on the internet. The false information we used in this study came from a fact-checking platform (https://vp.fact.qq.com) operated by Tencent. For true information, the guide recommends choosing the same information from mainstream news outlets as false information. The true information for this study comes from People’s Daily (https://www.people.com.cn), China’s most influential government news website. The types of true information include preventive measures, treatment measures, epidemic status, and government policies. The information is presented in the form of a Weibo post that includes a headline, a lead, and an image. Admittedly, the information presented in this form is not a complete story in the traditional sense, but research shows that this information is what social media users pay the most attention to when reading the information. In addition, it provides enough information for readers to understand the story (Boczkowski et al., 2018; Meijer & Groot, 2014). Figure 1 shows the display format of a piece of false information, and Fig. 2 shows the translated English version. Our data and materials are available on the Open Science Framework (https://osf.io/7md5n/).

Procedure
We asked the participants to complete a questionnaire on the Credamo platform for approximately 10 min. Through the rules set by the platform, we limited one IP address and one user to completing only one survey. At the beginning of the survey, we explained the purpose of the survey to the participants and obtained informed consent. In the “Accuracy Judgments and Sharing Intentions” task, we first explained to the participants, “In this study, you will see a series of 30 social media messages about COVID-19, and you will be asked to answer the corresponding questions according to the actual situation. It may take time for the image to load”. Subsequently, each participant was randomly assigned to one of two conditions. In the “accuracy judgments” condition, participants were required to judge the accuracy of all 30 pieces of information. In the “sharing intentions” condition, participants were required to express their willingness to share 30 pieces of information. Following the design of the original study, we also balanced the order of options among participants (yes/no vs. no/yes; will/will not vs. will not/will). To prevent participants from searching the web for relevant information before answering the questions, we limited the response time to 60 s for each question. If participants did not answer within 60 s, the answer to the corresponding question was missing, and we manually excluded such samples from the data screening. In addition, this experimental study presented information in an image format, which technically further restricts participants from searching for information online by copy-pasting.
After completing the “Accuracy judgments and Sharing intentions” task, we conducted a 3-item CRT (Frederick, 2005) for the participants. The participants then completed several demographic questions, including gender, age, education, and political status. Because this experimental study involved false information, at the end of the investigation, we explained to the participants that half of the information in the study was false information and the other half was true information. We showed participants true information, and false information was not represented to avoid the risk of exposure effects (Pennycook et al., 2018).
Measurement
The following variables were defined and measured according to the original study of Pennycook et al. (2020):
Accuracy rating
Participants were asked to rate each item of information: “As far as you know, is the above information correct?” (1 =  yes , 0 =  no ).
Sharing intention
Participants were asked to express their sharing intention: “If you saw the above information on WeChat or Weibo, would you share it with others?” (1 =  will , 0 =  will not ).
The independent variable is truth discernment , measured as average accuracy ratings of true information minus average accuracy ratings of false information (measured as average sharing intentions of true information minus average sharing of false information in the sharing condition). For example, an individual who judges that 9 out of 15 true pieces of information and 12 out of 15 false pieces of information are true has a discernment level of −0.2 (i.e., 0.6–0.8).
CRT
The three items of the original Frederick (2005) CRT were used in this study. All CRT items are designed to elicit intuitive but incorrect responses. For example, consider the following problem: On a loaf of bread, there is a patch of mold. Every day, the patch doubles in size. If it takes 40 days for the patch to cover the entire loaf of bread, how many days would it take for the patch to cover half of the loaf of bread? For many people, the intuitive reaction is 20 days, but that is not right. Each item was coded as 1 (correct) or 0 (incorrect). The Cronbach’s alpha was 0.8. A higher score indicates more reflective thinking.

Sociodemographic variables
As in the original study of Pennycook et al. (2020), we included the following sociodemographic variables: gender (0 = female, 1 = male), age (continuous variable), education (0 = less than undergraduate degree, 1 = undergraduate degree or higher), and political status (coded as 1 if the respondent was a member of the Chinese Communist Party and 0 otherwise).
Analysis
First, we conducted a regression analysis to test Hypothesis 1. Given that multiple ratings of the same information and multiple ratings from the same participant are nonindependent, we used linear regression with robust standard errors clustered on participants and information. Because the experimental information contains two types of information, true information and false information, we added a dummy variable, information veracity, to represent the information type. As in the original research of Pennycook et al. (2020), information veracity (centered) was encoded as −0.5 for false and 0.5 for true. The condition was encoded as −0.5 for accuracy judgments and 0.5 for sharing intentions. The analyses used linear probabilistic models, with the coefficient of information veracity indicating the overall level of discernment and the interaction between condition and information veracity indicating the extent to which discernment differed between conditions (i.e., accuracy judgments and sharing intentions).
Second, we examined the relationships between CRT and truth discernment to test Hypothesis 2.
Results
H1: There is a disconnect between accuracy judgments and sharing intentions. First, we compared the public’s truth discernment under the conditions of “accuracy judgments” and “sharing intentions”, that is, the difference in the public’s response to true information and false information. As in the original study, we also found a significant interaction between information veracity and condition (β = −0.231, F (1,10791) = 21.58, p  < 0.001), suggesting that truth discernment was greater in the “accuracy judgments” condition than in the “sharing intentions” condition (as shown in Fig. 3). In other words, information veracity had a greater effect on accuracy judgments (Cohen’s d  = 1.006, F (1,10791) = 21.58, p  < 0.001) than sharing intentions (Cohen’s d  = 0.448, F (1,10791) = 23.06, p  < 0.001). Specifically, for false information, 68.1% more people were willing to share the information than judged it as true. For example, for the following information: “Frequent nucleic acid testing caused a sharp increase in laryngeal cancer and nasopharyngeal cancer”, only 3.89% of people judged this information to be true, but 38.89% of people were willing to share this information. Therefore, the experiments showed a disconnect between accuracy judgments and sharing intentions and that confusion-based theory cannot fully explain the spread of misinformation on social media, which supports Hypothesis 1.

Preference-based theory offers an explanation for the disconnect between accuracy judgments and sharing intentions. This theory suggests that people prefer other factors over accuracy when sharing information, so people spread false information on purpose. To test whether this theory can fully explain the disconnect, following the approach of Pennycook, Epstein, et al. (2021), at the end of Study 1, we asked participants whether it is important to share only true information on social media. Nearly 97% of people answered that it is important or very important (see Fig. S1 in the Supplementary Information). This suggests that, in terms of attitudes, although people agree that veracity is important for information sharing, in reality, they still spread identifiable false information. This contradiction in attitudes and behaviors is inconsistent with preference-based theories. Therefore, preference-based theories cannot fully explain the disconnect between accuracy judgments and sharing intentions. Inattention-based theory compensates for the fact that preference-based theory cannot fully explain the disconnect between accuracy judgments and sharing intentions. It argues that people’s preferences for information veracity conflict with actual sharing behavior because the social media environment shifts people’s attention away from information veracity to other factors. Therefore, the spread of misinformation by people may be unintentional.
H2: People with higher CRT scores have greater truth discernment. The study revealed that there was no significant relationship between CRT and truth discernment under either the conditions of “accuracy judgments” ( p  = 0.189) or “sharing intentions” ( p  = 0.884). Specifically, CRT had a significant negative correlation with the belief in false information but not with belief in true information. CRT was not significantly associated with the willingness to share false or true information. Therefore, Hypothesis 2 is not supported.
Study 2
Purpose of the study
Study 1 revealed a disconnect between accuracy judgments and sharing intentions, and some participants seemingly did not consider accuracy when sharing social media information. According to inattention-based theory, interventions that induce people’s concept of accuracy can improve truth discernment when sharing information. The reason why these interventions are promising is that they can increase the role of accuracy in information sharing by making people pay attention to accuracy and thus improving the discernment of information sharing. The purpose of Study 2 was to determine whether accuracy-prompt interventions are effective in the Chinese context by testing the following hypothesis:
H3: Interventions that nudge people to think about accuracy can significantly improve truth discernment when people share information on social media.
In Study 2, we tested an intervention which were designed to induce participants to consider accuracy before deciding to share information. The intervention from the original study subtly induced people to think about accuracy by asking participants to rate the accuracy of a piece of information unrelated to COVID-19 before deciding what information to share. The intervention has proven to be effective in the United States (Pennycook et al., 2020). Our data and materials are available on the Open Science Framework (https://osf.io/7md5n/).
Participants
This experiment was conducted from October 26, 2022, to October 29, 2022. We recruited 600 people from the Credamo platform to participate. We prohibited participants in Study 1 from participating in Study 2 through the filtering function of the Credamo platform. Participants were also screened through the three criteria in Study 1. Overall, 700 participants entered the experiment, and 101 failed the screening. Among the participants who did not pass the screening, 1 participant did not use WeChat or Weibo, 1 participant used Weibo or WeChat but would not share any information, 48 participants did not pass the attention detection, 25 participants gave identical answers when expressing their sharing intentions or judging the accuracy of all 30 pieces of information, and 26 participants’ questionnaire information was incomplete (missing information due to failure to respond within the specified response time). Ultimately, we obtained 599 eligible samples, of which 247 (41.2%) were male and 352 (58.8%) were female. The average age of the participants was 30.55 years (SD = 8.49), the age range was 18–67 years, and the vast majority (504 or 84.1%) of the participants were aged 21–40 years. Most participants (446 or 74.5%) held undergraduate degrees. A total of 316 (52.7%) were not affiliated with any political party, 167 (27.9%) were members of the Communist Youth League of China, and 113 (18.9%) were members of the Communist Party of China. The demographic characteristics of the sample are presented in Table 1.
Materials and procedure
The true and false information used in the “sharing intentions” task in this experiment was the same as that used in Study 1. The CRT, demographic characteristics, and attention tests included in Study 1 were also included in this experiment. Unlike in Study 1, prior to the “sharing intentions” task, we added an intervention to prime the concept of accuracy. Each participant was randomly assigned to one of two conditions. In the control condition, participants were only asked to complete the “sharing intentions” task. In the treatment condition, participants were asked to judge the accuracy of a piece of information unrelated to COVID-19 before entering the “sharing intentions” task. In this experiment, four pieces of information unrelated to COVID-19 were prepared (available at https://osf.io/7md5n/), and each participant was randomly assigned to one of them. Before answering the questions in the intervention, we explained to the participants, “First, we want to pretest a question for a future study, and you only need to truly answer this question. Then, we will continue to perform the main investigation task.” Ostensibly, the intervention was a pretest of another study that was not explicitly tied to the main task. The goal was to avoid convincing participants that the questions were designed to allow them to consider accuracy when making sharing decisions, thereby reducing the impact of social desirability bias on the experiment.
In the “sharing intentions” task, participants were presented with 15 pieces of false information and 15 pieces of true information, as in Study 1. Figure 4 summarizes the study flowchart.

Measurement
Sharing intention
As in the original study (Pennycook et al., 2020), to increase the sensitivity of the measurements, participants were asked to express their sharing intention: “If you saw the above information on WeChat or Weibo, how likely would you be to share it with others?” (ranging from 1 ( highly unlikely ) to 6 ( highly likely )). Sharing intentions were rescaled such that 1 on the 6-point Likert scale was 0 and 6 on the 6-point Likert scale was 1.
The measurements of truth discernment, CRT and sociodemographic variables were the same as those in Study 1.
Analysis
We tested whether the truth discernment in the treatment group was different from that in the control group by the information interaction between condition and veracity. Condition was encoded as 0 for the control group and 1 for the treatment group. This was followed up by tests for simple effects of condition for each of the two types of information. All analyses were conducted at the rating level using linear regression with robust standard errors clustered on participants and information.
Results
The study revealed that the treatment significantly improved truth discernment when participants were asked about sharing intentions (β = 0.036, F (1,17955) = 4.58, p  = 0.032) (Fig. 5). Therefore, Hypothesis 3 is supported. Specifically, participants were less likely to share false information than the control group after evaluating the accuracy of information unrelated to COVID-19 (β = −0.032, F (1,17955) = 4.17, p  = 0.041), but they were equally likely to share true information with the control group (β = 0.004, F (1,17955) = 0.08, p  = 0.777). This findings shows that the improvement of treatment on truth discernment is mainly due to the decrease in the willingness to share false information rather than the improvement in the willingness to share true information. Quantitatively, the truth discernment of the treatment was 1.2 times that of the control group.

Is the treatment effect of intervention on sharing intentions due to the intervention that prompts participants to consider accuracy more when deciding whether to share information? If this mechanism is at work, then the more inaccurate a piece of information seemed to be to participants, the more its sharing was decreased by the treatment. In contrast, the more accurate a piece of information seemed to be to participants, the more its sharing was increased by the treatment. In short, because it enhances participants’ attention to accuracy, in the treatment, changes in participants’ sharing intention should have a significant relationship with the accuracy of the information. To verify this mechanism, first, we calculated the impact of treatment on each piece of information’s sharing intention (that is, the average sharing intention in the experimental condition minus the average sharing intention in the control condition). We then analyzed how this impact was related to the accuracy of the information. Because the participants in Study 2 did not rate the accuracy of the information, we used an average score for the accuracy of the information in Study 1. As shown in Fig. 6, there was a significant positive relationship between the impact of treatment and the perceived accuracy of information ( r  = 0.785, p  < 0.001). This suggests that the less accurate the information was perceived by participants, the less it was shared due to the accuracy-prompt intervention. In other words, the accuracy-prompt intervention reduces the disconnect between information judgments and sharing intentions by increasing public attention to information accuracy.

Study 3
Purpose of the study
Study 2 showed that the quality of information sharing could be improved by the accuracy-prompt intervention, which confirmed that part of the sharing of misinformation is attributable to inattention. In other words, inattention-based theory can account for a certain proportion of COVID-19 misinformation sharing. The purpose of Study 3 is to quantify this proportion, which will reveal whether inattention-based theory plays a marginal or central role in misinformation sharing. The nature of the role (marginal vs. central) fundamentally determines the implementation prospects of accuracy interventions. Therefore, we pose the following research question:
RQ1 : What proportion of misinformation is shared due to inattention?
To answer this question, we employed a full-attention treatment that directly forced participants to evaluate the accuracy of each piece of information before deciding whether to share it. This approach enabled us to determine the maximum effect achievable by focusing attention on accuracy within this specific context. Additionally, by utilizing the accuracy judgment obtained during the full-attention treatment, we could assess the proportion of shared misinformation believed to be accurate versus inaccurate by the sharer. Together, these analyses allowed us to infer the fraction of the sharing of misinformation is attributable to inattention (forgetting to consider the accuracy of the information; as per the inattention-based theory), confusion (incorrectly believing the information is accurate; as per the confusion-based theory), and purposeful sharing of misinformation (as per the preference-based theory).
Notably, in the original study on COVID-19 misinformation (Pennycook et al., 2020), the proportion of misinformation that was shared due to inattention was not analyzed; however, in another study on political misinformation (Pennycook, Epstein, et al., 2021), the author analyzed this issue. Therefore, the explanatory power of inattention-based theory for the sharing of COVID-19 misinformation is unclear. This study expands the original research on COVID-19 misinformation by analyzing the proportion of misinformation that is shared due to inattention.
Participants
This experiment was conducted from October 30, 2022 to October 31, 2022. We recruited 360 people from the Credamo platform to participate. We prohibited participants of Studies 1 and 2 from participating in Study 3 through the filtering function of the Credamo platform. Participants were also screened through three criteria in Study 1. Overall, 422 participants entered the experiment, and 62 failed the screening. Among the participants who did not pass the screening, 19 participants did not pass attention detection, 15 participants gave identical answers when expressing their sharing intentions or judging the accuracy of all 30 pieces of information, and 28 participants’ questionnaire information was incomplete (missing information due to failure to respond within the specified response time). Ultimately, we obtained 360 eligible samples, of which 141 (39.2%) were male and 219 (60.6%) were female. The average age of the participants was 30.46 years (SD = 8.09), the age range was 18–58 years, and the vast majority (309 or 85.8%) of the participants were aged 21–40 years. Most participants (252 or 70%) held undergraduate degrees. A total of 168 (46.6%) were not affiliated with any political party, 100 (27.8%) were members of the Communist Youth League of China, and 86 (23.9%) were members of the Communist Party of China. The demographic characteristics of the sample are presented in Table 1.
Materials and procedure
We presented participants with the same 30 headlines used in Study 1. The experimental design was identical to that of Study 1 except for the “Accuracy Judgments and Sharing Intentions” task. In the “Accuracy Judgments and Sharing Intentions” task, each participant was randomly assigned to one of two conditions: the control condition or the full-attention treatment condition. Participants in both conditions were required to express their willingness to share 30 pieces of information. Crucially, in the full-attention treatment condition, before being required to express sharing intention, participants were required to judge information accuracy.
Measurement
The following variables were defined and measured according to the original study of Pennycook et al. (2020):
Accuracy rating . Participants were asked to rate each item of information: “As far as you know, is the above information correct?” (1 =  yes , 0 =  no ).
Sharing intention . Participants were asked to express their sharing intention: “If you saw the above information on WeChat or Weibo, would you share it with others?” (1 =  will , 0 =  will not ).
Analysis
Through sharing intentions under these two conditions and accuracy judgments under full-attention treatment, we can quantify the relative contribution of inattention-based theory, confusion-based theory, and preference-based theory to the sharing of misinformation on social media. Because participants in the full-attention treatment were forced to consider the accuracy of information before deciding whether to share the information, inattention to accuracy was completely eliminated in this condition. Thus, the difference in the sharing of misinformation between the control and the full-attention treatment was due to inattention. For example, 50% of the misinformation was shared in the control condition, 30% of the misinformation was shared in the treatment condition, then (50–30)/50 = 40% of the misinformation shared in the control condition was attributed to inattention. Accuracy judgment can be used to determine how much of the sharing of misinformation in the full-attention treatment was attributable to confusion (indicated by the fraction of shared misinformation that participants rated as accurate) versus purposeful (indicated by the fraction of shared misinformation that participants rated as inaccurate) sharing. Following the above example, if 25% of the misinformation was shared and rated as accurate in the treatment condition, then 25/50 = 50% of the misinformation shared in the control could be attributed to confusion. If 5% of the misinformation was shared and rated as inaccurate in the treatment condition, then 5/50 = 10% of the misinformation shared in the control could be attributed to purposeful sharing. Results
As illustrated in Fig. 7, the analysis revealed that among the sharing of misinformation, inattention-based theory explained 37.3%, confusion-based theory explained 51.6%, and preference-based theory explained 11.1%. Therefore, in addition to the sharing of misinformation because individuals cannot judge the veracity of information, the proportion of unintentional sharing is greater than that of purposeful sharing in misinformation sharing. This suggests that inattention-based theories have a greater level of explanatory power than preference-based theories and play an important role in the sharing of misinformation in our experiment.
Discussion and conclusions
The inattention-based theory proposed by Pennycook et al. (2021) explains why people share misinformation on social media, and accuracy-prompt intervention based on this theory has proven to be an effective countermeasure to the spread of misinformation (Pennycook et al., 2020; Pennycook, Epstein, et al., 2021). To test the generalizability of this theory and intervention in other cultural contexts, this paper replicates the original Pennycook et al. (2020) study using social media information about COVID-19 in China. Our findings are consistent with those reported by Arechar et al. (2023) in their study of misinformation about COVID-19 in China. In Study 1, we found a disconnect between participants’ accuracy judgments and sharing intentions. Specifically, when asked about whether they would share information, they discern far less about what is true and false than when they are asked about the information’s accuracy. In Study 2, we found that the accuracy intervention in the original study, which prompted people to think about accuracy by asking participants to evaluate the accuracy of information unrelated to COVID-19 before the “sharing intentions” task, significantly improved participants’ discernment when they were asked about sharing intentions. Therefore, we can conclude that we successfully replicated the findings of Pennycook et al. (2021).
In addition to replicating the original research of Pennycook et al. (2021) on COVID-19 misinformation (Pennycook et al., 2020), we have also expanded our research by referring to Pennycook et al.‘s research on political misinformation (Pennycook, Epstein, et al., 2021). Specifically, we analyze how much inattention-based theory has explanatory power for misinformation sharing in the Chinese context. This study showed that inattention-based theory could explain 37.3% of COVID-19 misinformation sharing, while the study by Pennycook, Epstein, et al. (2021) on political misinformation found that inattention-based theory could explain more than 50% of misinformation sharing. This finding indicates that the proportion of misinformation sharing caused by inattention might be greater in the United States than in China. Moreover, this study also revealed that accuracy-prompt interventions improved sharing discernment less in China than in the United States. Specifically, in China, the intervention improved sharing discernment by 1.2 times compared with 2.8 times in the United States. This indicates that the accuracy-prompt intervention has a greater effect on improving the sharing discernment in the United States than in China. We believe that there are two reasons for this difference. On the one hand, compared with China, the United States has a longer history of using the internet and social media, which also brings a higher level of digital literacy (Mardikyan et al., 2015), which leads to the fact that the truth discrimination of news in the United States is stronger than that in China. On the other hand, as we have found, the proportion of misinformation sharing caused by inattention was greater in the United States than in China. Therefore, by bringing people’s attention back to information accuracy through accuracy-prompt interventions, the effect of improving people’s information sharing discernment in the United States will be greater than that in China.
The research in this paper has important theoretical and practical implications. Theoretically, we confirm the inattention-based theory in China, a cultural context very different from the United States, which strengthens the external validity of the original survey experiment. Second, the establishment of inattention-based theory in the Chinese context provides a new theoretical perspective for studying the spread of misinformation on social media in China. Unlike the view of intentional misinformation sharing, inattention theory reveals that a large proportion of the public’s misinformation sharing is unintentional behavior. Future research should focus on such unintentional sharing and related interventions.
Practically, the inattention-based theory provides a new way for China to address the spread of misinformation on social media; that is, the unintentional sharing of misinformation can be greatly reduced by deploying effective accuracy-prompt interventions. Traditional methods of fighting misinformation, such as deleting posts, blocking accounts, and flagging misinformation, all require government or technology companies to decide what is true and what is not. For example, professional fact-checking often requires many resources to investigate whether a piece of information is false or misleading, and it is impossible to keep up with the volume of content posted on social media every day. As a result, much misinformation is never fact-checked. In contrast, accuracy-prompt interventions leverage the user’s own ability to identify misinformation and, as a result, are more scalable. Social media companies or other stakeholders, such as governments or nonprofit organizations, can reduce the spread of misinformation by shifting attention to accuracy through a variety of accuracy-prompt interventions. For example, social media platforms can periodically ask users to rate the accuracy of randomly selected information, in a subtle way reminding the public to consider accuracy when making subsequent sharing decisions. Importantly, although accuracy-prompt interventions hold potential promise, this does not mean that other approaches to combating misinformation are no longer necessary. Therefore, we should construct an integrated system incorporating diverse approaches—such as algorithmic approaches, inoculation approaches, and accuracy-prompt interventions—to more effectively combat misinformation through human judgment and AI (Shin, 2024).
There were several study limitations and topics requiring further study. First, although the sampling in this study was conducted through Credamo, which is currently an important online research platform in China, we cannot guarantee that the sampling will be truly nationally representative; therefore, generalizing the conclusions to social media users throughout China requires caution. One promising study is to conduct field experiments on social media platforms such as Weibo and WeChat to test the validity of the conclusions. Second, this study examined the effectiveness of accuracy-prompt interventions but did not investigate potential moderating factors such as digital media literacy. Even though accuracy-prompt interventions focus individuals’ attention on accuracy, their effectiveness may diminish if individuals lack sufficient media literacy to judge the veracity of information. Thus, integrating digital media literacy initiatives could increase the effectiveness of accuracy-prompt interventions. Future research can explore how accuracy-prompt interventions interact with digital literacy interventions. Third, this study has successfully demonstrated the short-term impact of accuracy-prompt interventions, but their long-term effects have not been evaluated. Repeated exposure to such interventions may strengthen users’ cognitive resistance to misinformation (Shin, 2024). Repeating accuracy prompts over time could lead to habituation in users’ information-processing strategies, making them more likely to instinctively scrutinize content for accuracy. Future research should explore the long-term effects of accuracy-prompt interventions. Fourth, because the experiment was conducted in a “laboratory” environment, it remains to be tested whether our measured share intention represents real information sharing. Although studies in the United States have shown that the two are positively correlated, in the Chinese context, this relationship needs to be further validated. Fifth, this paper examines the spread of misinformation about COVID-19; future research should test the replicability of the conclusions in other fields, such as politics, health and technology. Finally, the form of information studied in this paper is Chinese microblog information, future research should explore whether the findings can be generalized to other forms of misinformation. Especially for AI-generated misinformation, such as deepfake videos, how effective the accuracy-prompt interventions are remain to be further explored.
