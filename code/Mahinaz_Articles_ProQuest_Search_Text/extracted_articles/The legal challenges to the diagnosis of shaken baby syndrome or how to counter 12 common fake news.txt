Introduction The shaken-baby syndrome (SBS), associating subdural hematomas (SDH), encephalopathy, and retinal hemorrhages (RH), was discovered by Caffey in 1972 [1]; since then, SBS has been widely recognized as a common cause of mortality and heavy morbidity in infants.
Despite the wide consensus, the diagnosis of SBS is at present aggressively challenged in courts, and some medical authors even contest its very existence [2, 3–4]. Few topics are as controversial, and the medical literature is swamped in letters, responses, and editorials from opinionated experts [5, 6, 7–8], despite valiant attempts at consensus [9]. Scientific debate, knowing no absolute truth, and subject to diverging opinions, leaves open many loopholes for controversy, which is amplified and deformed in mass media [8]. As a result, dissenting opinions become more and more vocal even in the scientific community.
In a previous paper, we reviewed some of the scientific evidence gathered from the literature and our clinical experience over the last two decades [10]. In the face of rampant challenges to the diagnosis of SBS in legal courts and in the media, we found it important to review the most common of these challenges and discuss each of these in the light of the available scientific evidence.
Material and methods We listed the most common challenges raised against the diagnosis of SBS and reviewed the most significant controversial arguments published in the medical literature, and the scientific evidence available.
Results Fake news 1: the bleeding is not caused by shaking Based on pathological investigations, some authors consider that intracranial bleeding is caused by hypoxia and resuscitation [11, 12], a common feature in newborns and fetuses [5, 11]. However, in dystocic birth, it is difficult to ascribe the lesions either to asphyxia or mechanical trauma. Some authors have shown that hypoxia is associated with infantile SDH [13]; however, association with bleeding does not equate with causality. It has also been hypothesized that elevated thoracic pressure due to cardio-pulmonary resuscitation can induce venous hyperpressure and cause intracranial bleeding [14]. However, autopsy reports did not confirm this hypothesis [15]. Subdural bleeding has also been reported in some cases of sudden infant death syndrome (SIDS) [11], although other authors have found no evidence of bleeding at autopsy in SIDS [16]; this issue is complicated by the fact that some cases of SIDS are sometimes suspected of being undiagnosed SBS.
Another critic arises from the frequency with which evidence of impact is found in severe cases of SBS, in particular at autopsy, the shaken-impact syndrome described by AC Duhaime [17]. Some authors conclude that shaking alone is rarely if ever fatal [7].
The facts Even if the precise mechanism of bleeding is debatable, the correlation of SBS, with or without impact, with pericerebral hemorrhage is undoubted. Violent and repeated shaking is the main mechanism reported by perpetrators [18, 19]. Several causes of intracranial bleeding may coexist in SBS, directly through tearing of the corticodural veins (Fig. 1), or indirectly through ischemia. Autopsy of confessed cases of SBS shows torn corticodural veins, well correlated with imaging [20]. This mechanism is consistent with animal experiments [21] as well as computer models [22, 23]. Fake news 2: bleeding is caused by minimal shaking like play This fake news is reported not in medical literature but rather in the popular press [8]. The aim is to downplay the perpetrator’s responsibility from abuse to negligence. Baby tossing is often performed as a play, and also as a religious ritual in western India. When tossed in the air, the child keeps his head in line with his torso, and no excessive head movement occurs (Fig. 2). Repeated oscillations with amplification of movement are necessary and sufficient to attain energy levels producing traumatic lesions [23]. Fig. 2 [Images not available. See PDF.]
Child tossed in the air during play (with permission). Note that the baby’s head is well aligned with the torso, and that no excessive motion occurs

The facts The description of the true mechanism of abusive shaking comes from the perpetrators’ confessions: they describe forceful, repeated shaking [18], having gone crazy (“pété les plombs”), and shaken the child like a rag doll (“secoué comme un prunier”). Violent shaking occurs at the paroxysm of anger and has nothing to do with play.
Fake news 3: the lesions are caused by unwitnessed accidental trauma Although small infants cannot fall from high, catastrophic deterioration can occur after a fall from their height, a bed, or a couch [24], mostly in relation with an extradural hematoma (EDH). Children of pre-walking age are regularly diagnosed with a skull fracture, after a bump is discovered incidentally, without associated lesions, and with no consequences.
The facts A mild accidental impact to an infant’s head may cause a fracture [25] and result in EDH, but not in SDH or brain edema, unless associated with clotting anomalies or vascular complications. Accidents causing severe intradural lesions are major, not trivial falls [26], associated with prominent signs of impact (Fig. 3A, B) [19]. The pattern of intracranial bleeding in SBS is clearly different from accidental trauma [27]. RH are rarely found in accidental trauma, generally of high velocity (Fig. 3C); moreover, accidental RH are never as severe as in SBS [28, 29]. Fig. 3 [Images not available. See PDF.]

B Traffic accident with projection in a 5-month female, CT scanner at 12 h, showing hyperdense left frontal contusion, hypodense subdural collection, clots coating the sagittal sinus, and subcutaneous swelling. C Two-month male, baby cart hit by scooter, fundoscopy on day 2 shows pearl-shaped hemorrhages. Accidents causing SDH or RH infants are high velocity traumas with prominent impact signs, unlike SBS

Fake news 4: we cannot prove that there was a trauma Almost all cases of SBS occur in private places without independent witnesses or recording. External signs of trauma are missing in the majority of SBS, although they are found in a number of severe cases of abuse, especially at autopsy, as mentioned above [7, 17]. Certainly, an impact enhances the energy of the trauma, but shaking alone is regularly the only mechanism described during confessions from perpetrators [8, 19].
The facts An impact is unnecessary to cause lesions and qualify as trauma and abuse. Experimental shaking causes meningeal bleeding in lambs [21] and computer models indicate that repetition and amplification accumulate an energy comparable with impact [23]. In confessed shaking, impact is reported in only 24% [18]. The findings in SBS are stereotyped, with a very specific constellation of lesions, not found in accidental trauma. These lesions are by themselves evidence of the trauma. In the presence of SDH, RH, and brain lesion, the absence of signs of impact is in fact a positive feature indicating SBS [19].
Fake news 5: traumatic lesions date from birth trauma Difficult labor is common and often results in intracranial bleeding. With systematic MRI, traumatic intracranial bleeding was visible in 9/111 apparently normal newborns [30]. Dural and subdural bleeding as well as anoxic ischemia are common in perinatal death [11], and RH are also common after birth [31].
The facts Birth mechanical trauma is often associated with extensive lesions of the soft tissue and skull, and subdural bleeding typically located in the vicinity of the tentorium (Fig. 4) [32]. Lesions in birth trauma and SBS thus show distinct patterns. Furthermore, imaging studies with MRI have shown that bleeding caused by birth trauma disappears in less than 4 weeks [30]. Neonatal RH are much less severe than in SBS [31], and mild RH may disappear in just a few days or weeks [10]. Fig. 4 [Images not available. See PDF.]

Fake news 6: bleeding is caused by structural lesions Infants very rarely present with intracranial bleeding from structural lesions, such as aneurysms, arteriovenous malformations, thrombophlebitis, or arachnoid cysts (Fig. 5). In addition, aneurysmal bleeding can also be associated with RH. However, vascular lesions cause intracerebral and subarachnoid hemorrhage, rather than subdural bleeding, and are disclosed on imaging. Arachnoid cysts are often complicated with subdural CSF collections containing very little or no blood and are often disclosed because of skull-base scalloping visible on CT scanner. The location of arachnoid cysts producing subdural collections is the sylvian fissure or brain convexity; these cysts are rarely congenital but generally develop later in life [33]. The facts To the trained clinician and radiologist, the clinical presentation and imaging in bleeding of structural origin are notably different from SBS, and imaging generally shows positive features indicating the cause of hemorrhage.
Fake news 7: extensive investigations are needed to eliminate all non-traumatic causes of bleeding Infantile SDH can result from many systemic causes, including clotting [34] or metabolic disorders, the most well known being glutaric aciduria type 1 [9], and Menkes’ disease [35], or from medical treatments like aspirin. Osteogenesis imperfecta can exceptionally be revealed by a SDH [36] but is diagnosed on account of ocular and skeletal anomalies and family history. A panel of systemic conditions associated with SDH in infants is presented in Fig. 6. Certainly, other causes of SDH are waiting to be discovered. Obviously, it cannot be expected from the pediatrician or neurosurgeon on call to rule out all the potential causes of SDH, many of which are known only from hyperspecialists, or even totally unknown. What is expected is a reliable medical diagnosis beyond a reasonable doubt. Fig. 6 [Images not available. See PDF.]

The facts The standard biology in emergency eliminates the largest part of non-traumatic causes of bleeding, and the rest is generally known previously from the medical context. Furthermore, by the time the case is brought to justice, the absence of inborn condition is confirmed by the absence of any new manifestation. The diagnosis of SBS is in most cases straightforward and unambiguous, made by a trained practitioner with the help of standardized radiology and biology.
Fake news 8: incomplete SBS triad does not indicate abusive trauma Some authors have contended that evidence was lacking to correlate the different lesions of the triad to SBS [2, 4], and that the triad was often incomplete, undermining the diagnosis. Historically, SBS was defined as the presence of SDH, cerebral edema, and RH [1]; it was later shown that edema is in fact ischemic rather than traumatic [37, 38], and that, hopefully, it was absent in many cases of SBS [19].
Recent evidence also shows that victims often suffer repeated shaking before being diagnosed [18], attesting that, in many instances of shaking, the child remains asymptomatic or paucisymptomatic. Imaging can be falsely negative: fresh bleeding can be missed on early MRI as well as on late CT scanner. Fundoscopy can become falsely negative as well, because flame-like and dot-like hemorrhages can resorb in just a few days (Fig. 7). Fig. 7 [Images not available. See PDF.]
Seven-month male, confessed shaking with subdural hematoma. A Fundoscopy on day 1 after the trauma, showing extensive pearl-shape and flame-shaped RH. B Control fundoscopy on day 6, showing marked regression of the RH. Further control showed total normalization (courtesy from dr DeFoort-Dhellemmets)

The facts The triad described by Caffey was a historical landmark; however, each of its element requires thorough analysis individually. Incomplete triad does not mean absence of shaking, and even more absence of abuse. Furthermore, beaten children frequently have normal fundi and no or minimal intracranial lesions.
Fake news 9: all SBS are severe, and if the child is fine, it cannot be SBS SBS causes high morbidity and mortality in infants, with a mortality rate of 21% reported by Kesler [39]. However, mortality and morbidity are inversely correlated with the exhaustiveness of diagnosis. In our recent experience, mortality was “just” 8.5% (unpublished data). Follow-up studies by rehabilitation specialists report severe disability in 24%, and moderate disability in 34% after 2 years [40]. However, these figures reflect a selection bias (patients requiring rehabilitation), as well as a high rate of patients lost to follow-up [41]. With a systematic approach, including the less severe cases, overall morbidity is less than previously reported [41]. As mentioned above, shaking does not always cause SDH [42], and children have often been repeatedly shaken, with little visible effect, until they present with clinically overt lesions [43].
The facts The opinion is that all SBS are severe results of selection biases and circular reasoning. This prejudice can lead to miss the mildest cases (Fig. 8A). Many cases of SBS are not clinically severe but must not be missed because of a high risk of fatal recurrence [44]. Fig. 8 [Images not available. See PDF.]

Fake news 10: if there are no clots, this is not a hematoma Acute SDH contain clots and liquid blood, while chronic SDH contain fluid and membranes derived from degraded clots and scar tissue. Although in adults, only these two entities are acknowledged, things are different in infants (Fig. 9). Infantile SDH is a specific entity, containing little blood and mostly cerebrospinal fluid (CSF), the proportion of which increases with time (Fig. 10). The term “hematoma” can thus be misleading regarding traumatic pericerebral collections in infants. This led several authors to prefer terms like hydroma, or hygroma [2, 42, 45, 46], and amalgamate these loosely defined entities with expanded arachnoid spaces (arachnoidomegaly). Young infants around three months regularly develop arachnoidomegaly, which regresses before 2 years, suggesting an age-specific hydrodynamic imbalance [10]. Arachnoidomegaly is often associated with infantile SDH [45, 47] and has been suspected of facilitating tearing of the stretched bridging veins. However, this view was contradicted by computer modeling, which suggests on the contrary that it has a buffering effect [22]. Fig. 9 [Images not available. See PDF.]

The facts Infantile SDH build up CSF and are an age-specific entity, and trauma is by far its main cause [10]. The fact that arachnoidomegaly and infantile SDH occur in the same age group does not imply that the former causes the latter but explains why infantile trauma results in this particular subdural collection.
Fake news 11: the caretakers’ denial is sincere Clinicians and defenders can become intoxicated by the denials of parents suffering the agony of having their child in dire condition, and at the same time being grilled for their possible responsibility. The mental mechanisms of self-denial are well-known to psychiatrists. A perpetrator, after a violent burst, and faced with its terrible consequences, can experience a dissociation mechanism similar to witnesses of catastrophes, dissociation being understood as “a break between the memory, the perception, the consciousness and the identity…when faced with unbearable feelings” [48]. Sincere denial easily elicits compassion from the medical staff as well as defenders [49], a natural response which is enhanced by professional training. Some authors have documented with functional imaging the sincerity of denial in a case of convicted child abuse and concluded that the sincerity of denial is not a criterion for innocence [50].
The facts Establishing innocence or culpability is not in the medical domain. As well as a judge cannot challenge a medical diagnosis without the advice of a medical expert, a medical expert is not competent to declare miscarriage of justice. Caregivers should know the limits of their competence, rein in their natural feelings, and remain neutral.
Fake news 12: confessions of shaking are invalid Several authors contest the validity of confessions because they consider these to be extorted or used as a token in plea-bargain [2, 42, 46, 51]. Some even accuse the prosecution of duress, deceit, and subterfuge in order to obtain confessions [7]. However, among a growing number of publications reporting confessed child abuse, the confessions are stereotyped [18, 19], generally related to the baby’s unbearable cries.
The facts Plea-bargain is not a legal practice in all countries. It does not exist in France, where confessions are obtained during close cross-interrogation, under no physical pressure. In democracies, discussing the fairness of the judicial inquiry is the unassailable privilege of the nation, its elected representatives, and public opinion impersonated in the media; it concerns caregivers in their quality of citizens, not as health professionals.
Discussion Two systems of thought following parallel paths are compared in Table 1. The apparent weakness of the “mainstream” group lies in the methodic doubt, as advocated by René Descartes. We think that its superiority over “dissenters” is that it endeavors to be grounded on facts; general De Gaulle used to quip: “facts are stubborn things.” However, facts in SBS are complex and require thorough analysis. Pathophysiology and nosology Some confusion arises from different conceptions in pathophysiology and nosology and is reflected in the terminology. Several authors use terms like hydroma, or hygroma [2, 42, 45]; however, these terms have no widely admitted definition: “hygroma” originally designates “a cystic tumor of lymphatic origin” (the Merriam-Webster dictionary). We think the use of such vague and ambiguous terms should be avoided.
As mentioned above (fake news 10), we think it is important to make a distinction between acute, chronic, and infantile SDH (Fig. 9). The infantile SDH, a hallmark of SBS (but also found in accidental trauma), is mostly composed of CSF, which accumulates over a few days [10]. It happens generally at an age between 2 and 9 months and shows a strong male bias. The same epidemiology is also found in post-meningitis subdural collections [51] and sylvian arachnoid cysts [33], suggesting precarious hydrodynamics in male infants.
The diagnosis of SBS With systematic and rigorous evaluation, the diagnosis of SBS is generally unambiguous. As mentioned above, a number of differential diagnoses must be eliminated before using such loaded words as child abuse: accidental trauma (fake news 3), obstetrical trauma (fake news 5), non-traumatic bleeding of structural or systemic causes (fake news 6 and 7). The time-tested screening protocols applied for the diagnosis of SBS eliminate virtually all differential diagnoses beyond a reasonable doubt. In addition, the time interval between clinical presentation and judgment allows any potential inborn disease to manifest itself.
Science and justice Some authors consider that a verdict of acquittal proves that the diagnosis was wrong [2], which is another example of circular reasoning (Fig. 8B). However, the role of a judge is to defend justice, not to establish scientific truth; in consequence, a verdict cannot be taken as scientific fact. Reciprocally, a medical diagnosis of SBS cannot be reversed by a lawyer, unless assisted by another medical expert. By challenging the medical diagnosis, a defender becomes the accuser, on which falls the burden of proof. Rare but loudly publicized cases of miscarriage of justice caused by medical error have hit the headlines; this emphasizes the need for objectivity, restraint, and thoroughness in medical expertise but does not undermine the scientific bases of SBS. Reciprocally, the competence of experts who deny SBS has been questioned [52], with regard to insufficient pediatric background and practice.
Conclusion Despite diverging opinions, the validity and autonomy of the concept of SBS are firmly corroborated with stereotyped descriptions by perpetrators, in good correlation with experimental studies and computer models, and its diagnosis is established beyond a reasonable doubt. Medical experts must master the knowledge of the different traumatic and non-traumatic cerebral lesions in infants, and their age-specific physiopathology. Pediatric neurosurgeons should involve more, and be a driving force in this controversial domain.
