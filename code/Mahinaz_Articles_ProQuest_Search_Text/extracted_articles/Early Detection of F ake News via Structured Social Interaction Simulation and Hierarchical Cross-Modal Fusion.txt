1. Introduction The proliferation of multi-modal fake news on social media threatens public discourse, yet its detection remains challenging. Current approaches are bifurcated and insufficient. Mainstream multi-modal models, even with attention mechanisms, often use single-layer interactions that miss hierarchical discriminative patterns [1,2]. Conversely, social context-based methods depend on complete real-time user data, which is scarce in early propagation stages, causing a cold start problem. Although recent efforts use LLMs to generate synthetic social signals, they produce unstructured output lacking contextual depth. This leaves a gap between sophisticated modeling and practical early detection. To bridge said gap, this paper proposes a unified framework that integrates a hierarchical cross-modal attention mechanism with a structured LLM-powered social interaction simulator.
Current research in multimodal fake news detection encompasses multiple competing paradigms, characterized by significant architectural divergence in how multi-modal information is integrated. While early fusion techniques [3] provide a straightforward baseline through feature concatenation, they ultimately fail to model higher-order interactions, inevitably introducing feature conflicts and imprecise cross-modal alignment. Consequently, subsequent methodologies have employed model-level fusion [4] and attention mechanisms [5] to dynamically weight modality importance through learnable interactions. Although these methods demonstrate improved performance over early fusion, their representational capacity remains fundamentally constrained by single-layer interaction paradigms, inherently failing to capture the subtle discriminative patterns of sophisticated disinformation [6]. Moreover, the efficacy of existing social context-dependent models is fundamentally dependent on the availability of comprehensive, well-observed social metadata, including user comments, engagement patterns, and propagation networks [7,8], to establish robust feature representations. During the initial phases of information dissemination, user interaction data typically exhibits severe sparsity, thereby plunging these models into a persistent cold-start bottleneck. This limitation not only impedes early detection capabilities but also constrains the deployability of existing solutions in real-world scenarios where disinformation evolves rapidly.
In recent years, hierarchical attention mechanisms have driven progress in multi-modal fusion. Models like HAN [9] and CMHA [10] show that structured, multi-level interaction pathways can improve cross-modal alignment. However, these approaches typically ignore social context simulation and fail to tackle the data sparsity problem during early news propagation. Meanwhile, other efforts have aimed to alleviate the cold start issue through synthetic data generation. Although some studies leverage large language models (LLMs) to produce artificial social signals [11], these methods are still limited by their dependence on static demographic profiles and their tendency to generate unstructured text that lacks contextual specificity and semantic coherence [12]. As a result, a critical gap remains in the literature: the lack of a unified framework that effectively integrates hierarchical cross-modal modeling with context-aware social simulation.
To address these challenges, this paper proposes the hierarchical cross-modal fusion framework with structured LLM-simulated social interaction (HCF-LSIM). The framework incorporates a progressive cross-modal attention mechanism that aligns semantic representations across multiple levels. Additionally, it includes an LLM-powered social interaction simulator, which generates structured triplets to compensate for missing real-time social context and provides interpretable reasoning cues. By integrating context-aware social signal generation with hierarchical cross-modal modeling, HCF-LSIM effectively mitigates the cold start problem and enhances detection robustness, thereby improving early fake news identification in practical settings.
The main contributions of this paper are summarized as follows: A novel hierarchical cross-modal fusion framework is proposed that progressively aligns textual, thematic, visual, and simulated social interaction features through a three-stage strategy, overcoming the limitations of conventional single-layer fusion methods.
An LLM-based social interaction module is designed that generates structured triplets from adapted user profiles. This module addresses the cold start problem by supplying context-rich simulated signals, advancing beyond prior methods reliant on static or unstructured data.
Experiments on public datasets showed that our approach achieved the best performance on Weibo (93.5% accuracy) and competitive second-best performance on X (formerly Twitter, 87.2% accuracy) across different languages and social media environments.

The remainder of this paper is structured as follows. Section 2 reviews the related work. Section 3 details our methodology. Section 4 presents the experimental results and discusses the findings and implications. Section 5 addresses the ethical considerations regarding LLM simulation, and Section 6 concludes the paper and outlines future research directions.
2. Problem Definition and Related Works In this section, the formal introductions of early fake news detection are given first, and then the related research is introduced after.
2.1. Problem Definition Formally, the task of early fake news detection in social media can be defined as a time-sensitive, multimodal classification problem structured as follows.
Let us say we are given the following: A news item C , composed of textual content T and visual content V , posted at time t 0 ;
A corresponding, partially observed social context S ( t e ) which includes user engagement data (e.g., comments or reposts) and user profile information, where the context is typically sparse for t 0 → t e (the early propagation stage);
A set of historical data D from the platform for model training.

Note that this encompasses the first minutes to a few hours after a post’s release ( t 0 ). The exact duration is platform- and context-dependent but is fundamentally defined by a short observation window ( t e − t 0 ), where t e is the time of detection.
The goal is to make a veracity prediction at t as close to t 0 as possible, namely one which is to learn a prediction function F that maps the multi-modal content and the available social context at an early time t e (where t e > t 0 and the observation window is short) to a veracity label y ∈{ Fake , Real }: (1) y = F ( T , V , S ( t e ) : D )

The function F must be optimized to achieve three interconnected objectives: (1) to hierarchically fuse multi-modal features by aligning and integrating representations from textual ( T ) and visual ( V ) content across multiple semantic levels, thereby capturing nuanced, discriminative patterns of deception beyond shallow single-layer fusion; (2) to handle social-context sparsity by maintaining robustness when the observed social signals S ( t e ) are incomplete or scarce, thus mitigating the persistent cold start problem in early-stage detection; and (3) to ensure generalization across diverse environments by sustaining high classification accuracy over different social media platforms (e.g., Weibo or Twitter) and varied linguistic contexts.
Thus, the core challenge is to design F such that it achieves high accuracy and robustness despite the dual constraints of complex multi-modal disinformation cues and severely limited early stage social signals.
2.2. Related Works Research on fake news detection has evolved along three primary dimensions. Early work focused on textual and thematic analysis using linguistic and stylistic features, later advanced by deep learning for detecting semantic anomalies. A parallel strand utilizes social metadata to assess credibility, though these models struggle with the cold start problem due to sparse early stage data. Recently, multi-modal fusion has become dominant to address mixed text-image content, employing attention mechanisms for cross-modal alignment. However, most fusion methods remain shallow and neglect social context. Consequently, a key gap remains: the lack of a unified framework that integrates hierarchical cross-modal modeling with context-aware social simulation to jointly address complex multi-modal deception and early stage data scarcity.
2.2.1. Text- and Topic-Based Detection Methods Textual analysis forms the methodological foundation of fake news detection. Early research concentrated on identifying deceptive patterns through linguistic cues, with semantics, rhetorical structures, and stylometric features systematically studied as discriminative indicators. For example, Rubin and Lukoianova [2] quantified truthfulness by mapping articles into a multidimensional rhetorical space, while Przybyla [13] established the statistical significance of stylometric features. The advent of deep learning fundamentally reshaped the field; representation learning techniques, particularly word embeddings [1] and transformer-based architectures [14], significantly improved the detection of subtle semantic anomalies and contextual discrepancies [4,5]. Building on this, contemporary work integrates symbolic reasoning with distributed representations. Liu et al. [15], for instance, developed a logic-enhanced neural model combining first-order logic with neural embeddings for cross-domain generalization, whereas Peng et al. [16] introduced a context-aware architecture that hierarchically integrates local and global semantic patterns.
Complementing textual analysis, topic modeling provides thematic perspectives to capture latent discourse structures. While traditional methods like latent Dirichlet allocation (LDA) established the baseline [6,7], recent advances focus on deeply fusing thematic semantics with detection objectives. Bazmi et al. [7] innovated beyond conventional topic models by constructing a multi-view co-attention network to dynamically model topic-specific cognitive biases across users and sources. Similarly, Zhu et al. [3] proposed a memory-guided multi-view framework for joint optimization across semantic, sentiment, and stylistic dimensions. Other validated approaches include weighted topic modeling, community-aware document clustering, and context-aware topic modeling [17]. These methods find application in diverse domains such as social media analysis, information propagation prediction, and sentiment analysis.
2.2.2. Social Metadata for Fake News Detection Social metadata provides auxiliary signals for news credibility assessment, which mainly encompass three categories: user profiles, behavioral patterns, and propagation networks. User profiles include personal descriptions, verification status, demographic attributes, and historical credibility metrics [18,19]. Behavioral metrics capture engagement (e.g., likes, reposts, and comments) and activity patterns [20,21]. Propagation metrics involve retweet network topology, diffusion paths, and spread scale [22,23]. Empirical studies show that fake news tends to propagate with greater depth and speed than real news [14,24,25], often amplified by influential or highly active users [26,27].
This distinctive propagation pattern highlights the need for early detection and has motivated various social context-based models. For instance, researchers have employed hypergraph neural networks to model global and local diffusion [5], integrated repost counts with content features [4], used graph networks to learn user behavior embeddings [6,28], and designed tree-structured transformers to track stance evolution in propagation paths [27]. Others have framed claims and evidence as graphs to enable deep reasoning [29]. However, these methods rely heavily on the availability of complete, high-quality social metadata, a requirement often unmet during early dissemination when interaction data is sparse, leading to a persistent cold start problem.
To address this, recent efforts have turned to large language models (LLMs) to generate synthetic social signals [11]. However, existing approaches remain limited by their dependence on static demographic profiles and their tendency to produce unstructured text, which lacks the contextual specificity and semantic organization needed for robust early detection.
2.2.3. Multi-Modal Fake News Detection The proliferation of multimedia content on social platforms has positioned multimodal fake news detection as a critical research frontier, centered on the challenge of achieving semantically coherent fusion across different modalities. Early approaches predominantly relied on feature-level concatenation. For example, Khattar et al. [30] introduced a multimodal variational autoencoder that learns a joint representation by co-optimizing a bimodal autoencoder with a classifier, while Singhal et al. [31] enhanced feature extraction in their SpotFake+ model via transfer learning.
The introduction of attention mechanisms marked a significant shift in fusion strategies. Zhou et al. [32,33] developed a similarity-aware framework that aligns textual and visual signals in a joint embedding space. Qian et al. [34] advanced hierarchical attention mechanisms to capture both intra-modality saliency and cross-modality correspondence using BERT and ResNet features. Further innovations include Wu et al.’s [35] stacked co-attention architecture, which integrates textual semantics with spatial and frequency-domain visual features, and Chen et al.’s [36] cross-modal ambiguity learning, which dynamically adjusts fusion weights based on inter-modal consistency.
Despite these advancements, most current models still operate through shallow cross-modal interactions. Moreover, existing frameworks lack the capacity to unify textual, thematic, visual, and social contexts, which limits their ability to capture the nuanced semantic relationships characteristic of fake news.
2.2.4. Social Intelligence and Crowd Wisdom The concept of the “Wisdom of Crowds” has been widely recognized as a powerful mechanism for credibility assessment and truth discovery. In the domain of social sensing, foundational works [37] demonstrated that aggregating observations from a large crowd of unvetted social sensors can yield highly reliable truth estimations. Building upon this, recent literature on fake news detection has increasingly leveraged social intelligence. For instance, Allen et al. [38] empirically proved that crowdsourced accuracy judgments from groups of laypeople can match the performance of professional fact-checkers. Furthermore, Wei et al. [39] proposed frameworks that combine machine intelligence with scalable crowd judgments extracted from user responses, while Yang et al. [40] utilized crowd intelligence-based semantic features combined with large language models to enhance rumor classification.
Despite the demonstrated efficacy of crowd wisdom, these methodologies are fundamentally predicated on the availability of an actively participating crowd. In the critical early stages of fake news propagation (i.e., the extreme cold start scenario), authentic crowd feedback and social interactions are severely sparse or entirely absent, rendering these crowd-dependent models ineffective. To bridge this gap, our work introduces a novel paradigm: when authentic crowd intelligence is unavailable, we leverage large language models as a computational sociology engine to simulate a diverse virtual crowd. By generating structured social interactions across different cognitive archetypes, our LLM-simulated module serves as a natural and innovative extension of crowd wisdom, providing the necessary social signals to overcome the cold-start bottleneck in early fake news detection.
3. Proposed HCF-LSIM Framework In this paper, the hierarchical cross-modal fusion framework with LLM-simulated social interaction (HCF-LSIM) is proposed, a novel architecture designed to address the cold start problem in early fake news detection, where conventional social signal-based methods often fail. A key departure from prior hierarchical models, such as HMCAN [34], which primarily operates within a single modality, is our cross-modal fusion pathway. This design enables the coherent transfer and refinement of semantics across increasing levels of abstraction. Furthermore, unlike previous LLM-based approaches that rely on static user templates [11], our framework introduces a dynamic user profiling mechanism. By generating adaptive user profiles and corresponding structured triplets, HCF-LSIM overcomes the limitations of inflexible profiles and unstructured text synthesis, thereby effectively mitigating the cold-start bottleneck.
As represented in Figure 1, the framework is built upon four synergistic components: the multimodal feature extractor, the LLM-based social interaction generator, the Hierarchical Cross-Modal Attention Fusion module, and the fake news detector. It is important to emphasize that while the LSIM module is positioned early in the flowchart to reflect the chronological order of synthetic data generation, it is by no means a mere preprocessing step. Architecturally, the extracted social interaction feature (F S ) serves as a foundational, parallel modality that holds equal architectural weight to the source content features. The model implements a three-stage progressive fusion strategy. The process initiates with semantic alignment between textual and latent topic representations, establishing a foundational semantic layer. This is followed by the integration of discriminative visual evidence through cross-modal attention, which enriches the initial semantic understanding. The final stage incorporates structured social interaction generated by an LLM to simulate the social media user group commentary typically absent in early dissemination phases.
3.1. Multi-Modal Feature Extraction Building upon the formal task definition, the feature extraction module is designed to process the heterogeneous raw inputs from the four defined modalities: the textual content T , latent topics Θ , visual evidence V , and LLM-simulated social context S ^ . For each modality M ∈{ T , Θ , V , S ^ }, a specialized encoder f M ( · ) is employed to transform the raw input into a dense representation E M = f M ( M ) . This encoding procedure aims to preserve the distinct semantic characteristics inherent to each modality while producing feature vectors that are structurally conducive to the subsequent hierarchical cross-modal alignment and fusion process.
3.1.1. Text Encoder We employed the pretrained BERT model [41] as our text encoder due to its self-attention mechanism’s proven capacity to capture bidirectional contextual dependencies across long texts, which have proven effective across different linguistic contexts [13], enabling a unified architecture across our multilingual datasets. Moreover, BERT-base balances representational power with computational efficiency, facilitating extensive ablation studies and reproducibility. Prior research has validated BERT’s effectiveness in fake news detection [13], particularly in identifying implicit linguistic cues such as evasive expressions and emotional manipulation [42].

It is computed as the average of topic distribution vectors derived from textual content using latent Dirichlet allocation (LDA). Specifically, each textual element t i is processed by LDA to obtain a topic vector w i ∈ R k , which encodes its distribution over k latent topics. Then, F Θ is the mean of these vectors across all textual elements. This aggregated feature captures the overall thematic structure of the text, serving as a compact representation for integration with other modalities (text, visual, and social context) in downstream tasks.
At the word level, LDA infers a latent topic assignment for each token in the input sequence T , producing a word–topic vector w i ∈ R k per token. This fine-grained representation highlights terms indicative of each latent topic. At the document level, the model aggregates word-level distributions to construct a document–topic vector d ∈ R k , which captures the global thematic composition of the news item and supports semantic understanding beyond lexical patterns. The hyperparameter k , which determines the dimensionality of the topic space, is optimized via perplexity minimization over the training corpus.

3.1.4. LLM-Simulated Social Interaction Generator To address social signal sparsity during early-stage propagation, we introduce an LLM-driven social interaction simulation module that generates structured social signals to compensate for missing real-world engagement data. The module follows a two-stage simulation pipeline designed to mirror real-world social perception.
In the first stage, the LLM functions as a computational sociology engine grounded in Shults’ [45] framework of multi-agent artificial intelligence modeling, which conceptualizes AI-driven simulations as social digital twins capable of integrating multiple social theories within a unified causal architecture. The LLM generates a set of K user roles { R 1 , R 2 , … , R K } conditioned on the news text T . Each synthesized role R i is described by a profile tuple ( D i , C i ) , where D i encapsulates demographic and behavioral descriptors and C i captures cognitive traits and value orientations.
In the second stage, based on Shults’ [45] principle of generativity, namely the capacity to causally generate macro-level social phenomena from micro-level agent interactions and meso-level social dynamics, the LLM produces a structured feedback triplet 〈 s i , r i , c i 〉 for each role R i . Here, the stance s i , drawn from five predefined categories ( Support , Oppose , Question , Neutral , and Satirical ), reflects the role’s attitude toward the news; the rationale r i explains the reasoning behind the stance; and the comment c i simulates natural-language discourse expressing the stance in a communicative context: (7) s = Softmax ( [ f r e q ( s i ) ] ) , s i ∈ S
(8) r = MeanPool ( BERT ( Concat ( r 1 , … , r K ) ) ) ∈ R d h
(9) c = MeanPool ( BERT ( Concat ( c 1 , … , c K ) ) ) ∈ R d h
(10) F S = [ s ; r ; c ] ∈ R d s

In Equation (7), f r e q ( s i ) denotes the frequency of the stance category s i (e.g., Support or Oppose ) among the K generated roles. These frequencies form a raw count vector, which is normalized via softmax to obtain the stance distribution vector s . Conceptually, s represents the simulated collective stance of the generated social group toward the news content.
In Equation (10), F S represents the extracted feature vector for the LLM-simulated social context modality S ^ . It is a composite vector constructed by integrating three distinct but complementary components that model different aspects of the social environment.
The first component s is a probability distribution derived via a softmax function over the frequencies of generated stance categories, which represents the relative prevalence or weight within the constructed scenario. The second and third components r and c are dense semantic embeddings obtained by processing the generated rationales and comments, respectively, through a BERT model, followed by mean pooling. Each results in a vector in  R d h .
These capture the nuanced linguistic and relational semantics of the involved social actors and situational settings. By concatenating or otherwise combining these three elements into a tuple 〈 s , r , c 〉 , the final feature vector F S ∈ R d s forms a comprehensive, structured representation of the simulated social landscape. This representation is designed to encapsulate the statistical, role-based, and contextual dimensions of social dynamics, enabling its fusion with features from textual, topical, and visual modalities for the target task.
The stance distribution across all roles is normalized to form a probabilistic social reception vector P s o c , as defined in Equation (7). 3.3. Fake News Detector The fake news detector employs a multilayer neural classifier designed for robust prediction. The fused feature representation F ( 3 ) is first transformed through two fully connected layers, which perform nonlinear mapping and dimensionality reduction. This is followed by a ReLU activation layer to introduce nonlinear decision boundaries and enhance representational capacity and a dropout layer for regularization, stochastically deactivating neurons during training to mitigate overfitting. Finally, a softmax layer normalizes the outputs into a probability distribution over the target classes. This pipeline yields a prediction vector y ^ =[ y ^ 0 , y ^ 1 ], where y ^ 0 and y ^ 1 denote the predicted probabilities for real news and fake news, respectively. 4. Experiments and Analysis 4.1. Experimental Set-Up 4.1.1. Datasets To evaluate the performance of the proposed model, we conducted experiments on two public benchmark datasets, the Chinese Weibo dataset [4] and the English Twitter dataset [46] collected from X (formerly Twitter). To maintain consistency with the prior literature and facilitate fair comparison with existing methods, we retained the original dataset name “Twitter” throughout this paper. All subsequent mentions of this dataset name refer to the same benchmark collection. Each data sample comprises a textual claim and, when available, an associated image. The detailed statistics of the datasets are summarized in Table 1.
4.1.2. Implementation Details For fair comparison, we employed a standard 7:1:2 random stratified splitting strategy (train:validation:test) consistent with mainstream baseline studies. To strictly control variables and ensure that any performance gains are attributable to our proposed hierarchical fusion and LSIM modules rather than the parameter advantages of newer LLMs, we employed BERT as the backbone text encoder, using bert-base-chinese for the Weibo dataset and bert-base-cased for the English dataset. This setting aligns with state-of-the-art baselines such as HMCAN [34] and MCAN [35]. To rigorously operationalize our definition of the early stage as the extreme cold-start phase ( t ≈ 0 ), we completely masked all authentic historical interaction data during the experiments. The LLM simulator was driven solely by the source post content to generate virtual feedback. This setting is significantly stricter than traditional time window cutoff methods (e.g., extracting the first two hours of propagation data), aiming to evaluate the model’s predictive capability in an absolute zero interaction state. Regarding the LLM-simulated social interaction module, the GPT-4o-mini simulator was configured with a generation count of K = 3 , a temperature of 0.7 to balance deterministic logic and diversity, and a top p value of 1.0.
After standard text preprocessing, including URL removal and tokenization (e.g., using Jieba for the Chinese dataset), text sequences were padded or truncated to fixed lengths of 160 for Weibo and 25 for Twitter. Visual features were extracted from the penultimate linear layer of a pretrained vision transformer (ViT), resulting in a feature dimension of 1000. All parameters of the pretrained encoders were frozen during our experiments to ensure a fair comparison. The model was trained for 50 epochs using the Adam optimizer with a batch size of 64. A learning rate of 0.0001 was used for both datasets. The ReLU function served as the activation function for all hidden layers. The number of topics for LDA was set to 35 for Weibo and 25 for Twitter. Model performance was assessed using standard metrics, including accuracy, precision, recall, and F1 score.
4.1.3. Baseline Models We compared our HCF-LSIM framework against eight state-of-the-art multimodal fake news detection models, which can be categorized into three groups: early fusion models (att-RNN, EANN, and MVAE), cross-modal attention models (MCAN, HMCAN, and CAFE) and social context and LLM-enhanced models (CSFND and FND-LLM): att-RNN [4]: A model that fuses text, visual, and social context features using an attention mechanism;
EANN [47]: Incorporates an event discriminator to remove event-specific features from concatenated text and image representations;
MVAE [30]: Employs a variational autoencoder to learn joint representations of text and image features, combined with a binary classifier;
MCAN [35]: Integrates spatial and frequency-domain features of images with textual features via cross-modal co-attention blocks;
HMCAN [34]: A hierarchical multimodal attention network based on self-attention for fusing text and visual features;
CAFE [36]: Detects fake news by adaptively aggregating text and image features and their cross-modal correlations;
CSFND [29]: Introduces social context semantic representations, fusing local and global semantic features, and employs a context testing strategy for decision making;
FND-LLM [12]: A multimodal framework that combines deep learning models with LLMs to leverage their complementary strengths.

4.2. Main Results and Analysis The comparative experimental results of fake news detection on the Weibo and Twitter datasets are summarized in Table 2. From the experimental results, we can draw the following observations.
On the Weibo dataset, HCF-LSIM consistently achieved superior performance across all evaluation metrics. Specifically, it outperformed the strongest baseline FND-LLM by 2.3% in terms of accuracy. Particularly noteworthy is the model’s exceptional performance in recall for fake news, reaching 94.7% and significantly exceeding all comparative methods. This indicates that the hierarchical attention mechanism effectively mitigates false negative errors by reducing the misclassification of fake news as genuine content. On the Twitter dataset, HCF-LSIM maintained highly competitive performance, ranking second overall. While it slightly trailed HMCAN, it still significantly surpassed other models. The improvement stems from two key mechanisms. One is the hierarchical cross-modal attention, which progressively aligns fine-grained textual claims with visual regions, detecting subtle local mismatches that flat attention models miss. This explains the high recall for fake news (94.7% on Weibo). Another is the LLM-simulated social interaction (LSIM), which generates structured 〈 stance ,   rationale ,   comment 〉 triplets, creating a simulated reasoning environment that exposes semantic inconsistencies even when visual data is compromised. On Twitter, HCF-LSIM outperformed the content-only baselines (e.g., MCAN and CAFE) by more than 6% in terms of accuracy, confirming LSIM’s role beyond simple text augmentation.
A notable observation is the performance gap between the Weibo (93.5%) and Twitter (87.2%) datasets. This gap primarily stemmed from severe visual modality sparsity within the Twitter dataset. As detailed in Table 1, while the Weibo dataset maintains 100% visual coverage (13,252 images for 13,252 texts), the Twitter dataset has suffered significantly from the expiration of external image URLs. Consequently, its effective visual coverage is only approximately 2.5% (485 valid images for 19,200 texts). The absence of complementary visual cues, which are essential for cross-verifying textual claims, led to the observed decrease in detection accuracy compared with the modality-rich Weibo dataset. Nevertheless, the fact that HCF-LSIM ranked first on Weibo and second on Twitter underscores its robustness and generalization capability across different languages and platforms.
The performance advantage of HCF-LSIM was particularly evident when compared across different architectural paradigms. The framework substantially outperformed the early fusion models, demonstrating the superiority of hierarchical attention mechanisms over simple feature concatenation. When compared with cross-modal attention models, HCF-LSIM showed significant improvements through its capability to model and align high-order semantic information. Against the latest social context and LLM-enhanced models, HCF-LSIM achieved accuracy improvements of 2.3% and 4.0% over FND-LLM and CSFND, respectively, on Weibo and 3.9% over CSFND on Twitter.
Furthermore, we observed a strategic trade-off in the results. On Weibo, HCF-LSIM achieved a precision of 92.5% and a recall of 94.7% for fake news. Compared with the strong baseline FND-LLM, our model improved the recall by 3.6% while also improving the precision by 1.0%, demonstrating that HCF-LSIM holds an advantage in reducing false negative errors.
4.3. Ablation Studies To evaluate the contribution of each component in our HCF-LSIM framework and rigorously isolate module contributions, we conducted a comprehensive three-dimensional ablation study on both datasets. As shown in Table 3 and Table 4, the experimental variants were categorized into three distinct evaluation groups: architectural integrity (Group I), LSIM internal mechanisms (Group II), and data modality contributions (Group III).
Group I: Architectural Contribution. We first investigated the necessity of our hierarchical attention design. Comparing HCF-LSIM against a flat concatenation variant ( w / o Hierarchy) and a self-attention variant ( w / o Cross-Attn) revealed a significant performance drop (e.g., accuracy dropped to 89.0% on Weibo for the flat variant). This demonstrates that simple early fusion inevitably introduces cross-modal semantic conflicts. Furthermore, removing the first-level topic-fusion attention ( w / o CVAtt_layer_1 on Weibo; w / o Topic-Fusion Layer (L1) on Twitter) reduced the accuracy to 91.3% on Weibo and 85.8% on Twitter, indicating that semantic alignment between lexical and thematic representations is crucial. Ablating the second-level visual-fusion attention ( w / o CVAtt_layer_2 on Weibo; w / o Visual-Fusion Layer (L2) on Twitter) caused the accuracy to drop to 91.6% on Weibo and 84.8% on Twitter, with consistent performance degradation across all precision, recall, and F1 score metrics.
Group II: LSIM Internal Contribution. To prove that our LLM simulator provides structured cognitive value rather than merely generating redundant semantic noise, we ablated its internal components. Disrupting the structured schema ( w / o Structure, outputting unstructured raw text instead of JSON) reduced the accuracy to 91.0% on Weibo. Removing the causal reasoning step ( w / o Rationale) or stance distribution ( w / o Stance) also led to noticeable degradation. This confirms that forcing the LLM to output a structured chain of thought (Rationale) significantly enhanced the logical discriminability of the generated social signals.
Group III: Data Modality Contribution. We then examined the impact of each foundation modality. Ablating textual features ( w / o Text on Weibo; w / o Text Feature on Twitter) caused the most dramatic performance degradation, with the accuracy dropping from 93.5% to 69.5% on Weibo and from 87.2% to 60.3% on Twitter, underscoring text as the most critical modality. Removing visual features ( w / o Vision on Weibo; w / o Visual Feature on Twitter) resulted in the second-largest performance decrease, reducing the accuracy to 88.2% on Weibo and 83.5% on Twitter, confirming visual evidence as a crucial complementary signal. The absence of thematic semantics ( w / o Topic on Weibo; w / o Topic Feature on Twitter) led to accuracy reductions to 91.5% on Weibo and 86.1% on Twitter. Crucially, eliminating the LLM-simulated social interaction ( w / o LSIM on Weibo; w / o LSIM Module on Twitter) caused the accuracy to decline to 90.2% on Weibo and 85.2% on Twitter, validating the importance of simulated social signals in enhancing detection robustness in cold start scenarios.
The ablation studies reveal that the textual content serves as the foundational modality for fake news detection, while visual evidence provides critical complementary information. The topic modeling and social interaction modules offer substantial performance enhancements by capturing semantic nuances and social reception patterns. Most significantly, the hierarchical attention mechanism and the structured LLM generation prove essential for modeling complex cross-modal interactions. The consistent performance trends across both datasets further validate the robustness and generalizability of HCF-LSIM.
4.4. Feature Distribution Visualization To further evaluate the representation capability of our proposed approach for fake news detection, we employed t-SNE [48] to visualize the feature distributions of test samples from the Weibo dataset, as shown in Figure 2. The t-SNE visualizations clearly demonstrate the effectiveness of hierarchical attention fusion layers in enhancing inter-class separability.
The complete HCF-LSIM model (Figure 2a) exhibited well-separated clusters for fake and real information, indicating its strong capability in capturing discriminative semantic patterns. In contrast, removing the cross-modal attention layer between text and topic representations (Figure 2b) resulted in substantial feature overlap, significantly compromising the model’s ability to distinguish between the two classes. A similar degradation in separation quality between real and fake information occurred when ablating the cross-modal attention layer that integrates text–topic features with visual evidence (Figure 2c), validating the critical role of hierarchical cross-modal attention in effectively combining multimodal cues. Furthermore, comparing HCF-LSIM with the variant excluding the social interaction generator (Figure 2d) revealed that incorporating LLM-simulated social metadata substantially enhanced the model’s capacity to identify latent features of fake news, resulting in more compact and separable class clusters. These visualization results are consistent with the experimental findings presented in Table 3 and Table 4, providing additional evidence for the importance of both the hierarchical cross-modal attention mechanism and the social interaction generator in identifying subtle distinctions characteristic of fake news.
4.5. Case Study Analysis To gain insights into the operational mechanisms of HCF-LSIM, we conducted case studies on representative samples from the test set, with the results presented in Table 5. The first case examined a real news piece about the Garissa attack, combining emotional textual content with corresponding factual imagery. Our complete HCF-LSIM model correctly identified this content as real, demonstrating robust multimodal verification capability. The ablation variants revealed that removing textual encoding ( w / o Text), visual features ( w / o Vision), LSIM, or cross-modal attention layers ( w / o CVAtt_layer_1, w / o CVAtt_layer_2) caused false positive detections. In contrast, removing topic modeling ( w / o Topic) still preserved the correct prediction, suggesting that topic information was helpful but not decisive for this case.
The second case involved a supernatural claim about a samurai ghost. As shown in Table 5, the complete HCF-LSIM model predicted this difficult sample as real, indicating a remaining failure case under visually ambiguous supernatural content. The ablation results showed mixed behavior; removing visual features, textual features, LSIM, or the second cross-modal attention layer led to the correct fake prediction, whereas removing topic modeling or the first cross-modal attention layer still predicted the story to be real. This pattern suggests that detecting this type of fake news remains sensitive to the interaction among thematic cues, visual evidence, and simulated social signals.
5. Ethical Considerations Given the integration of large language models for simulating user profiles and social interactions, it is crucial to address potential ethical concerns regarding user privacy and demographic biases. First, regarding data privacy, our proposed HCF-LSIM framework operates entirely on synthetic social signals. The model does not scrape, collect, or store any real-world user metadata, personally identifiable information, or authentic historical engagement traces. The user profiles generated by the LSIM module serve merely as virtual cognitive archetypes inferred directly from the multimodal news content, fundamentally eliminating the risk of privacy infringement.
To mitigate the inherent demographic biases and stereotyping often exhibited by LLMs, we implemented a strict algorithmic constraint within our prompt design. Rather than allowing the LLM to freely generate homogenous profiles that might perpetuate a single-perspective echo chamber effect, our system explicitly forces the generation of diverse and opposing stances. This prompt-level intervention ensures a balanced, multi-perspective simulation of crowd wisdom, effectively preventing the model from anchoring onto specific demographic stereotypes or skewed ideological representations.
6. Conclusions In the proposed HCF-LSIM, the hierarchical cross-modal attention mechanism introduces a principled approach to modeling multi-granular semantic interactions, enabling progressive alignment from local features to global semantics. This architecture effectively addresses the limitation of flat attention paradigms in capturing complex cross-modal relationships. Simultaneously, the LLM-driven social interaction simulator represents a paradigm shift in handling cold-start scenarios, generating theoretically grounded social signals that maintain sociological plausibility while providing interpretable reasoning cues. The integrated framework demonstrates how structured social simulation can complement content-based analysis to create more robust detection systems. Through systematic experimentation, we have demonstrated that our approach achieved the best performance on Weibo and competitive performance on Twitter while providing valuable insights into multimodal fake news characteristics.
Future work will focus on addressing information incompleteness and enhancing model generalization across several dimensions. Crucially, to mitigate the impact of modality sparsity, we plan to leverage generative AI for visual context reconstruction and integrate external knowledge graphs to compensate for the absence of explicit visual evidence in sparse datasets. Furthermore, the realism and diversity of our social interaction simulator could be refined by incorporating social dynamics models or adversarial training. Finally, extending the framework to encompass video and audio modalities represents a vital direction for achieving comprehensive multimodal disinformation detection.
