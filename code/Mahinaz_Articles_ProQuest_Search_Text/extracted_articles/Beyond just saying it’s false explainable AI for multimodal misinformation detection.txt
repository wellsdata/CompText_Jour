Introduction
According to “Pew Research Center”1, over 80% of U.S. adults get news from online social media including several news websites (e.g., BBC 2, National Public Radio 3). However, many such news sites (e.g., Natural News 4, Humans Are Free 5) deliberately publish manipulated contents to harm the reputation of a famous person or to spread “infodemic”6 etc. Additionally, such unreliable news articles gain more popularity when social media users put their opinions, sentiments, etc. on these news stories. The spread of such false news has many negative repercussions, thus identifying fabricated information has become a crucial research challenge in the modern world. For example, a news analysis on BuzzFeed reported that top misleading election news stories on Facebook showed more engagement than the election contents from top reliable news websites during the last three months of the US presidential campaign [1].
To deal with the aforesaid issues, a variety of different deep neural network (DNN)-based misinformation identification techniques have been proposed in recent times. Existing DNN-based misinformation detection approaches utilize propagation structure [2, 3], Multi-Task Learning [4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9, 10–11], and trendy DNN models such as RNN [12], GCN [13, 14], Co-Attention [15], LLMs [14, 16, 17, 18, 19–20, 20], Variational Autoencoder [5], etc. to achieve state-of-the-art (SOTA) performance. More recently, researchers have also started leveraging visual information such as images that are attached to news articles or tweets for veracity identification, giving rise to the multimodal misinformation detection area. One line of work [21] in this direction focuses on capturing correlations between modalities while the other [22] attempts to retain the unique properties of each modality during feature fusion. Additionally, some works [23] extract high-order complementary information from the multi-modal context for detecting misinformation. However, these models’ performance is compromised due to their incapability to identify components that contribute significantly to a misinformation sample. This also limits their performance compared to other existing modals that can harness the explainable components of a misinformation.
Existing misinformation detection models lack transparency, often failing to provide sufficient explanation why a claim is identified as fake/unreliable. To combat this, researchers have developed explainable algorithms for misinformation detection [24, 25–26]. dEFEND [24] is the one such technique that provides explanations with the help of sentence-comment co-attention component. However, dEFEND requires long documents for news articles and it also relies on textual content of user comments [25]. To mitigate these problems, researchers propose another explainable method GCAN [25] which works in a more realistic scenario on the online social network. One major limitation of both dEFEND and GCAN is that they can not be applied into multimodal setting [26]. Moreover, existing multimodal misinformation detection approaches [21, 27, 28] overlook the information consistency issue between textual and visual contents. To address these challenges, researchers further develop a multimodal explainable misinformation detection framework, DGExplain [26] that captures the complex association between different modalities and leverages user comments to identify and explain misinformation.
Despite all, these explainable models can achieve a moderate performance gain and this is caused by their vague classification of explainable components. By explainable components, we mean, those minimum and the most significant (MS) elements in a misinformation sample whose removal would render the sample as non-fake. Hence, mere ranking of the elements of sample would not provide any information about the fakeness of misinformation sample since, one can not bring this information into use. As a rank of 2 in a sample of 2 elements and in a sample of 10 elements might have different importance in context of these misinformation samples. However, if we know that exactly x elements of first sample and y elements of second sample are the reasons of fakeness of the sample, we can have a much stronger classification model as well as an informative outcome to the end users who is interested in visualizing and presenting explainable and non-explainable components of the sample. A good application of such model can be found in social media/journalism where any misinformation sample need to be segregated into fake and non-fake components automatically. Nevertheless, the assumed model will also rank the elements in terms of significance for both explainable (MS) and non-explainable (least significant or LS) components. However, here the elements might have repeated ranks and the number of explainable components would vary from sample to sample. Such a model is assumed to perform much better in terms of classification along with giving a clear picture of explainablity of the sample components. To conclude, the existing explainable misinformation detection systems [24, 25–26, 29] observe a limited performance since they mostly rely on the ranking system (using attention weights) to generate the fixed-size explanations such as top k sentences or user comments. However, in a realistic scenario, multiple elements (sentences or visual grids) might have equal importance/rank. We notice similar observations for other samples too. Related work
To gain a comprehensive understanding of multimodal explainable misinformation detection, we should first examine the role of explainable artificial intelligence (XAI) in the modern era. Next, an in-depth review of the literature on the use of multimodality in misinformation detection is discussed. Finally, the integration of XAI into the misinformation detection task outlines the additional methods that can be used to explain black box models.
Explainable AI (XAI)
To build trust in the black box (machine learning (ML) or deep learning (DL) algorithms) models, researchers have recently developed Explainable AI [30] that provides understandable explanations for its predictions. XAI has gained popularity across several domains where the potential repercussions are significant. (a) Healthcare: Due to the high-risk factor in clinical applications, doctors and practitioners must understand how the black box model arrives at its conclusion. As a result, a plethora of XAI solutions [31, 32–33] was proposed in the medical domain. (b) Natural Language Processing (NLP): XAI can also become helpful in providing the justifications for various NLP applications including sentiment analysis [34, 35], text classification [36, 37], language translation, and so on. (c) Recommendation Systems: To present better transparency and to increase user trust, a variety of explainable recommendation techniques was developed [38, 39–40] in recent times. (d) DeepFakes Detection: With the recent advancement of DNN models and AI techniques, a lot of manipulated multimedia contents (images, videos, etc.) has become ubiquitous in online social media. Hence, numerous explainable deepfake detection solutions [41, 42] were introduced to improve the trust of users. While XAI techniques have proven essential in various domains, including healthcare, NLP, recommendation systems, etc., their application in other challenging domains such as fake news detection, particularly in multimodal settings is still in its early stages. Therefore, the following section will study the key research that currently exists in the area of Multimodal Misinformation Detection.
Multimodal misinformation detection
One of the primary multimodal misinformation detection solutions was proposed by Jin et al. [43]. The authors in [43] first extracted features from three different modalities: textual content, social context, and visual content of a post, and further efficiently aggregated these three modalities to capture effective representation for a tweet instance. However, existing multimodal misinformation detection systems primarily focus on capturing event-specific features. Therefore it has become a unique challenge to detect misleading content on unseen events. To overcome this issue, authors in [27] proposed an Event Adversarial Neural Networks (EANN). The main challenge of using EANN was the dependency on additional sub-task such as event discriminator. Hence, another study introduced SpotFake [28] that employed BERT language model and pre-trained VGG-19 to extract textual as well as visual features respectively. Nevertheless, all these above-mentioned studies failed to capture the correlations between the modalities. Hence, to learn the correlations across the modalities, MVAE [21] jointly trained a Multimodal Variational Autoencoder and a Fake News Detector component. Recently, a study [23], focused on capturing high-order complementary information from the multi-modal context; thus assisting in misinformation detection. Another work [22] utilized two modules (CARN and MCN) to retain the unique properties of individual modalities while fusing multiple modalities. More recently, a Knowledge-guided Dual-consistency multimodal misinformation detection model was proposed to exploit inconsistencies in social media rumors [44]. Drawing inspiration from human reasoning, another study introduced HCCIN [45], a unique framework that analyzes consistency and inconsistency in multi-modal information for fake news detection. Another line of work [46] introduced a solution called CMMTN to jointly learn inter-modality and intra-modality relationship between text and images. Additionally, CMMTN employed masking technique to reduce the noise in the multimodal data. To capture the inter-modality relationships in misinformation detection task, a novel contrastive learning-based TTEC model [47] was developed. In short, TTEC combined four approaches: (a) data amplification, (b) multimodal information encoding, (c) feature fusion and (d) joint learning. Another recent study [48] proposed a robust domain and cross-modal method called RDCM to tackle the difference between training data and real-world data (domain shift). Different from the existing multimodal studies, Wu et al. [49] investigated human’s various reading habits to generate a new system called MRHFR. Authors in [50] presented CAF-ODNN, a unified framework that employs Complementary Attention Fusion and an Optimized Deep Neural Network. This technique efficiently captures crucial cross-modal relationships; thus improving multimodal fake news identification task. Another framework FMC [51] was introduced to effectively detect fake news by integrating multi-granularity feature fusion with contrastive learning, leveraging pre-trained models for textual and visual feature extraction, cross-modal alignment, and self-attention mechanisms. However, these models can not solve the black box challenges in misinformation detection. Therefore, there is an urgent requirement to develop explainable systems for detecting misleading information.
Explainable misinformation detection
To increase the trustworthiness of misinformation detection techniques, many efforts have been made to develop explainable solutions for identifying misinformation [24, 25–26, 29, 52, 53]. For example, Shu et al. introduced an explainable fake news detection system by capturing top -k justifications [24]. Yang et al. employed three separate networks ( MIMIC , ATTN , and PERT ) to provide explanations from different perspectives of news statement [52]. Kou et al. proposed a graph-based explainable fauxtography detection model to capture existing connections among different components of a post [29]. To detect fake news in a more realistic scenario on online social media, Lu et al. developed an explainable graph-aware dual co-attention mechanism [25]. To combat COVID-19 infodemic, Ayoub et al. employed DistilBERT for accurate misinformation detection and SHAP for transparent model explanations [54]. Recently, DGExplain models cross-modal interactions between news text and news images, utilizing user comments to spot and explain misinformation [26].
However, current explainable misinformation detection models struggle to separate the MS information from the LS counterpart in multimodal data, hindering progress in misinformation detection. In this study, we present a new multimodal explainable framework, mEXACT that improves misinformation detection by learning variable-size MS buckets.
Research objective
While promising, current explainable AI methods for detecting misinformation, both single modal and multimodal, face limitations. These approaches use ranking systems to generate explanations of a fixed size elements (text and images). Additionally, these solutions fail to differentiate between elements crucial for understanding why something is misinformation (explainable) and those that are not (non-explainable). Hence, our goal is to build an unique multimodal explainable misinformation detection technique that seggregates explainable elements from non-explainable with the help of variable-length bucket of MS information; thus enhancing the misinformation detection task. In addition, we aim to demonstrate that explanations for unreliable samples are most effective when they focus on the key pieces of information from text and images that significantly impact the model’s prediction.

This component is composed of two output terminals with parameters sharing. The second terminal works in an adversarial manner; this terminal is trained on inverted ground truth labels in the case of unreliable samples. To isolate fake content within unreliable samples, we aim at finding the most significant information contributing to their unreliability. Hence, removing this critical MS information should reverse the sample’s label. That is the reason the output terminals are of inverted labels during the fake samples. Thus it is necessary to provide an adversarial setup for the output terminals during the training for such samples. Datasets
We conduct experiments on two recently published and publicly available multimodal COVID-19 datasets: ReCOVery  [57], and MMCoVaR  [58]. As shown in Table 1, both datasets initially use two fact checking websites in order to annotate each news media site with one of the possible labels: (i) “Reliable”, and (ii) “Unreliable”. Next, ReCOVery uses three COVID-19-related (SARS-CoV-2, COVID-19, and Coronavirus) keywords to crawl the required news articles. Different from ReCOVery, MMCoVaR employs 9 COVID-19 vaccine-related keywords (“vaccine”, “vaccinated”, “COVID-19 Pfizer”, “COVID-19 Moderna”, “COVID-19 Janssen”, “Moderna vaccines”, “Janssen vaccines”, “Johnson & Johnson’s vaccines”, “Biontech vaccine”) for article crawling. For the news article dataset, authors provide various information such as “News ID”, “URL Link”, “Image”, “Body Text”, “Label” etc. for both the repositories where “Body Text” and “Image” provide textual and visual modality information for each news. Finally, to collect the social media data for each of the gathered news articles, Twitter Premium Search API7, and Twitter developer API8 are used by ReCOVery and MMCoVaR respectively. It is noteworthy that we use the same number of news articles and tweets as suggested in [26]. To make a fair comparison with DGExplain, we use 80% of the data for training purposes and the remaining 20% for model testing. We preprocessed both datasets based on methods outlined in relevant literature  [57, 58].

SOTA methods
Our proposed approach is compared to a variety of SOTA techniques, encompassing rumor detection (HSA [61]), multimodal fake news detection (BTIC [62], SAFE [63], EANN [27], SpotFake [28], MVAE [21]), fauxtography (ExFaux [29]) identification, explainable multi-view fake review detection (EMVD [64]) and explainable misinformation detection models (dEFEND [24], DGExplain [26]). This comprehensive evaluation ensures a robust assessment of our method’s effectiveness.
HSA  [61]: A hierarchical bidirectional LSTM-based rumor detection model that employs attention mechanism to combine social information.
ExFaux  [29]: ExFaux is the first weakly supervised end-to-end DNN-based explainable fauxtography detection system that identifies which component (text, image, or their association) of the post is false.
dEFEND  [24]: A popular explainable fake news detection solution that provides justifications using a sentence-comment co-attention network.
BTIC  [62]: A contrastive learning based unreliable news detection framework that leverages both textual as well as visual information.
SAFE  [63]: A multimodal fake news detection solution that captures similarities between textual content and visual information.
EANN  [27]: To capture shared features for newly emerging rumors, authors propose an Event Adversarial Neural Networks (EANN) that has three components: multimodal feature extractor, fake news detector, and event discriminator.
SpotFake  [28]: To detect fake news, SpotFake uses BERT language model and pre-trained VGG-19 to extract textual as well as visual features respectively.
MVAE  [21]: MVAE jointly trains Variational Autoencoder and Fake News Detector components to identify whether a multimodal post is fake or not.
DGExplain  [26]: To capture the complex association between different modalities, DGExplain utilizes duo-generative cross-modal feature generation mechanism.
EMVD  [64]: A soft voting based E nsemble M ulti- V iew D eep learning approach that is used for fake review detection task. In our study, we compare our proposed model with two variants of EMVD. (a) EMVD 1: In this ensemble technique, news text, user comments and news image are fed through Bidirectional-Long Short-Term Memory (Bi-LSTM), Deep Neural Network (DNN) and Convolutional Neural Network (CNN) respectively.(b) EMVD 2: This variant applies DNN, Bi-LSTM and CNN to news text, user comments and news image respectively. Comparison with SOTA
We evaluate mEXACT on two multimodal COVID-19 news datasets and compare these results with several baselines. As shown in Table 2, our proposed approach outperforms the best-performing baseline with a good margin, obtaining (6.8–8.7)%, and (4.9–5.4)% improvements in terms of (Accuracy-F1 Score) across ReCOVery and MMCoVaR respectively. Two influential factors enhance the model performance: (1) Applying the acute thresholding mechanism to each of the encoded representations of ,, and help our proposed model learn the variable-size bucket of key MS information in identifying misinformation. (2) Employing the second output terminal in an adversarial manner (in case of “unreliable” labels) forces our framework to efficiently leverage learned MS information in obtaining such promising results. Different from our approach, existing explainable misinformation detection systems solely depend on the ranking methods; thus capturing redundant information or truncating important information. This constraint of the ranking system hinders the existing SOTA from exploring another dimension of explainability. On the contrary, our approach identifies a strict lower bound of significant information, the key components of a false claim. Note that the reported reconstruction loss of our autoencoder model on ReCOVery and MMCoVaR datasets are 0.0033, and 0.0043 respectively, demonstrating the efficacy of this image reconstruction technique. We have also conducted a comparative analysis (as mentioned in Table 2) of our proposed model against SOTA techniques based on FLOPs, and the results indicate that mEXACT requires the fewest operations, indicating it achieves the highest execution efficiency compared to other baseline methods. Explainability
This section provides proper justifications that describe how effectively our model captures explainable multimodal features. To validate our claim, we perform the following tasks. (1) Distribution of the MS sentences in : Existing explainable systems overlook the fact that multiple components might carry similar significance and hence ranking them lose the vital information. To illustrate this fact, we plot histograms of MS sentences in a news text in terms of TF-IDF, Embedding, Sentiment, and encoded representation (generated by our proposed model). As mentioned in Fig. 2, and Figure 6, each of the histograms follows a Gaussian distribution intuitively; thus justifying our claim that there can be more than one significant as well as of equally significant information contributing to the fakeness of a sample. Computation of TF-IDF, Word-Embedding, and Sentiment Analysis are described as follows. TF-IDF: For a single MS sentence, we compute the TF-IDF score of each word in that sentence relative to all the MS sentences in . We further take the summation of all the TF-IDF scores corresponding to each word, thus obtaining a single score for each MS sentence. We repeat this process for the remaining MS sentences and finally generate the histogram of all the MS sentences in . Word-Embedding: We use GloVe word embedding [59] to extract meaningful vector representation of each word in a single MS sentence in . We then take the mean of all the obtained vectors in a MS sentence; thus obtaining a single MS sentence representation. Sentiment Analysis: We use VADER [65], a rule-based method that extracts polarity scores for each given MS sentence. Next, we choose the compound score as the final MS sentence representation. (2) Identifying the MS information in unreliable samples: Existing explainable misinformation detection approaches can not provide a boundary between MS and LS components. Since only these components can alter the prediction results of the sample, capturing such information has become an urgent need in the world of Explainable AI. Moreover, an end user can easily get a clear vision of explainability by manually inspecting the MS and LS components of an image (or text). As shown in Figure 7, we illustrate a few correctly predicted news images, and their MS and LS counterparts. A similar observation is also reported for news text (see Table 3). Just to reiterate, it is intuitive that the amount of MS component cannot be the same for every sample (image or text), this is proved in this Fig. 7 and Table 3. However, no other recent models are able to identify this crucial information which can alter the model prediction and hence affect the end users observations. (3) Usage of Shapley values: To ensure that our model explanations (MS) are consistent, reliable, and trustworthy, we take the help of SHAP ( SH apley A dditive ex P lanations) [66], an interpretability tool based on Shapley values. Shapley values describe how each feature has contributed to the model prediction (Figs. 8, 9 and 10). As shown in Fig. 5, we observe that the Shapley value is always positive for each unreliable MS bucket of test sample, indicating that each MS bucket significantly contributes to the misinformation detection process. (4) Reducing the size of MS bucket: To understand the importance of identified MS bucket in each sample, we delete different amount of entries in this bucket and then conduct experiments. Results demonstrate that reducing the involvement of MS features directly hampers the model performance, as shown in Table 4. [See PDF for image]

Ablation study
As shown in Table 5, we perform three types of ablation experiments. (1) Ours w/o : Ignoring the TFE component heavily affects the model performance on both datasets; indicating that news text carries the most crucial information for detecting misinformation. (2) Ours w/o : This variant fails to meet the optimal performance because user opinions have an influence on the veracity of the false news articles. (3) Ours w/o : Visual information plays a crucial role in identifying misinformation for various reasons such as spotting manipulations, capturing inconsistencies, etc. Therefore, ignoring such important visual cues can lead to a degradation in classification performance.
Limitations
The proposed model can be seen an efficient explainable fake news detection model based on standard metrics, however, being a deep learning model, its architectural origin limits its degree of explainability. Like any other explainable deep learning models, the current model has dependency over external techniques for justification of its working when the dataset changes. Our current experimentation has adopted the dataset of similar domain, hence the exact performance effectiveness of the proposed model for dataset from different domain would require another set of experimental trails. Additionally, the empirically chosen threshold could be automated, but that finding is subject to architectural modification and the observation of new experimental results.
Discussion and implications
Current explainable misinformation detection techniques mostly rely on top k elements (sentences or visual grids) to generate explanations. However, in reality, many different elements of the content might be equally important for spotting misinformation; hence trusting on the fixed-size explanations hinders the end users to properly comprehend the understanding of explanability. In addition, these studies struggle to achieve high performance gain due to their vague classification of explainable components. Our proposed approach is different. It aims to identify a lower bound of MS information whose removal can toggle the model prediction. Coming to the practical implications of our work, we can state that our proposed solution can be beneficial for social media platforms, journalists, and news organizations. It allows these platforms to more effectively highlight misleading content by segregating explainable and non-explanaibale components. To check the validity of our proposed model, we conduct experiments on two multimodal benchmark datasets. Results report that mEXACT achieves significant performance gain over the best performing baseline. Ultimately, the proposed model is capable of identifying the most significant elements of samples that explain the fakeness of the sample in text as well as visually.
Conclusion
Existing models often employ fixed-size explanations, leading to information loss and limited performance. Additionally, the provided results are less user-friendly and ambiguous for the end users. We propose mEXACT , a novel approach that leverages misinformation thresholding to identify variable-size buckets of check-worthy misinformation, enabling the distinction between contributing and non-contributing components for improved explainability. Results show that mEXACT constantly outperforms SOTA approaches across two COVID-19 datasets in terms of accuracy. Our future work involves automating the threshold of the proposed model and deploying this model for explainable deepfake detection, highlighting inconsistencies in the video such as unnatural expressions or lip movements that do not match the audio. Furthermore, we aim to test how well our model performs on diverse datasets from various research areas.
