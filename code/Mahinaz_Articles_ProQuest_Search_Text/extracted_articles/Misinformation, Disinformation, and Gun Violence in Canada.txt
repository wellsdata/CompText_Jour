Disinformation: The dissemination of deliberately false information, es[x when supplied by a government or its agent to a foreign power or to the media, with the intention of influencing the policies or opinions of those who receive it; false information so supplied, (oed)
Misinformation: Wrong or misleading information. (oed)
IN MARCH OF 2022, the Government of Canada published a news release describing its efforts to wage a war against the "growing spread of harmful misinformation and disinformation" (Canadian Heritage 2022). The government focused its efforts on curbing the circulation of both, with the Minister of Canadian Heritage committing $2.5 million to "fund initiatives" aimed specifically at countering propaganda that spreads virtually. The press release emphasized the ways in which the Liberal government's Digital Citizen Initiative (dci), first launched in 2019, has been designed to build "citizen resilience" and increase "civic literacy." The document argues that " [tjhese projects are reaching Canadians on a national and local scale, on and offline, in both official languages, and in Indigenous communities." Most notably, the Minister, Pablo Rodríguez, is quoted in the press release as insisting that "[tjhere is an urgency to act. As Canadians, we can't tolerate disinformation and propaganda. Democracy didn't happen by accident. We need to fight for it." These kinds of blanket assertions about Canadian democracy and inclusivity are particularly troubling to me, having just completed a manuscript called Canada Through American Eyes: Literature and Canadian Exceptionalism.
In my book, forthcoming from Palgrave in 2023,1 explore what I am calling "the limits of Canadian exceptionalism." I examine American voices that have challenged the desire of Canadians to see themselves as better than their American neighbours by looking at recent U.S. and Canadian authored novels and films. The monograph includes chapters that explore such topics as: 1) the myth of the Underground Railroad and the propensity to presume that Canada did not have slavery; 2) the belief that Canada treated ethnic and racial others fairly throughout the country's history as a colony and subsequently a nation in its own right; and 3) the idea that Canada has always been a site of escape and salvation for individuals facing persecution south of the Canada-U.S. border.
The final chapter of the book probes the ways in which the existence of gun violence, particularly directed toward Indigenous populations, and the history of racism that underpins this phenomenon have continued to be ignored by Canadians and bolstered by comparisons to the U.S. that frame Canada as a kinder and gentler nation. Chapter 7 offers a reading of American Michael Moore's 2003 documentary film, Bowling for Columbine, Canadian creators Irene Sankoff and David Hein's 2013 musical, Come From Away, and Cree filmmaker Tasha Hubbard's 2019 film, Nîpawistamâsowin: We Will Stand Up. The chapter specifically examines the differing representations of gun violence on both sides of the Canada-U.S. border.
While the chapter covers a range of issues and arguments, for the purposes of this roundtable I want to focus on a crucial moment in Hubbard's 2019 documentary. Hubbard's text is the final one I explore in the chapter, in order to demonstrate the ways in which Canadian narratives of exceptionality continue to circulate without probing the ongoing reality of gun violence faced by Indigenous and Black populations in Canada, all too often inflicted by white gun owners.
Michael Moore's documentary explicitly depicts the absence of gun violence in Canada as compared to the U.S. by filming Moore's own efforts in Toronto to knock on the doors of, and walk into the houses of, Canadians unannounced. Not only is Moore greeted with kindness, but he does not encounter the gun-wielding homeowners whom he depicts as dominating the American landscape, most notably through his interviews with Michigan militia men who argue that protecting one's property is "an American tradition" and that " [i]f you are not armed, you are not responsible."
The hit Broadway musical Come From Away provides a similarly romanticized view of Canada through the portrayal of a Black American named Bob who finds himself stranded in the days after 9/11 when his flight unexpectedly lands in Gander, Newfoundland. Derm Flynn, the mayor of the small town of Appleton who billets Bob asks him to borrow barbeques from local residents so that the community can feed all of the passengers. This seemingly innocent request serves to demonstrate the differences between Newfoundlanders-and by extension Canadians-and their Americans counterparts. Bob explains to Derm that he fears being accused of stealing and getting shot if he tries to borrow a bbq. But when he goes to people's yards and asks to take their bbqs, the reaction is the opposite of what he expected. The lesson Bob and the audience members learn from Come From Away is that Newfoundlanders are incredibly hospitable and presumably not racist. Instead, Bob is offered "a cup of tea in every single backyard" (Sankoff no). The bbq owners help him move their bbqs to the local community centre, an encounter which stands in stark contrast to his life in America. The narrative of Come From Away reinforces this dichotomy by describing Bob's feelings when his flight finally leaves Gander and he finds himself flying over New York City, an experience that, given the smoke rising from the ashes of the 9/11 attacks, immediately invokes a renewed fear of racial discrimination in the U.S against anyone who is racially other.
We Will Stand Up, however, fundamentally rewrites the presumption that Canadians are free from the deeply rooted racism of their southern neighbours. Instead, Hubbard's film examines the execution style killing of Gölten Boushie, a young man and member of the Red Pheasant Cree Nation, by white Saskatchewan farmer Gerald Stanley. Stanley uses a shotgun to kill Gölten at close range. He justifies the brutality of the event by claiming that Boushie posed a threat because he was ostensibly trespassing on Stanley's farmland. The documentary tracks the trial of Stanley and his subsequent acquittal by an all-white jury. It then follows the Boushie family's sustained efforts to attain "Justice for Colten" (Hubbard 2019) through meetings with Canadian federal government officials in Ottawa and their presentation of a public plea for the United Nations Permanent Forum on Indigenous Issues in New York City to launch a study of racism against Indigenous peoples within "the judicial and legal systems in Canada" that will produce actionable recommendations (Hubbard). The movie concludes with Hubbard's son learning how to use a pellet gun carefully and responsibly, providing an ironic rejoinder to the irresponsible and deadly use of force by Gerald Stanley against Boushie.
Central to the documentary are the words of Debbie Baptiste, Colten's mother, and Jade Tootoosis, Colten's cousin, who repeatedly describe the hate speech they encounter online, particularly through a Facebook group created by white farmers in Saskatchewan who defended Stanley's actions in the weeks and months following Colten's murder. In the movie, Jade recounts examples of the horrific nature of the racism propagated online against Gölten and Indigenous peoples in the immediate aftermath of his death. Similarly, Debbie Baptiste points to the vitriol perpetuated online, stating that " [t]his racism needs to stop" (Hubbard 2019). She specifically asks Conservative Members of Parliament to remove the "Farmers with Firearms" Facebook page, a site that Baptiste contends perpetuates racism against Indigenous peoples. She also notes that there is no Indigenous equivalent to the "Farmers with Firearms," yet the Boushie family lawyer points out that they are the ones receiving "hate mail" (Hubbard 2019).
The "Farmers with Firearms" site becomes a flashpoint for misinformation and disinformation with farmers creating the Facebook page in response to Boushie's death. As Ryan McIntyre, who co-founded the page, explains, the arming of farmers to protect their property is essential at a time when rcmp are understaffed and unable to respond in a timely fashion to thefts or property damage ("Why"). McIntyre insists that "[rjacist comments from Facebook users" are removed if they appear on the site and notes that the percentage of people posting such comments is extremely low (he estimates twenty out of five thousand members). Moreover, McIntyre insists that "I do know there is racism out there and I get that. [...] If somebody doesn't agree with your point of view, you're automatically labelled a racist and that's not fair" ("Why"). He also notes that "[tjhere's theft for every ethnic group" ("Why"). What is important to remember here is that these farmers and their property were once lived on by Indigenous peoples who were removed without recognition or compensation. This foundational theft of homeland is entirely erased from Canadian historical records and narratives of nation. Instead, Indigenous people like Boushie are perceived as intruders who threaten the survival of colonial narratives that justify and support an ongoing occupation of Turtle Island.
Unsurprisingly, "Farmers with Firearms" seems to have disappeared from view altogether on Facebook. The Boushie family's request for accountability given the pervasively racist attitudes that flow unchecked on the internet provide a powerful example of the ways in which both misinformation and disinformation continue to perpetuate the dominance of colonial perspectives that justify state sponsored violence. As Hubbard points out in 2019, even currently serving rcmp officers contributed to this discourse, seemingly without recrimination; the comments included that statement that Boushie "got what he deserved" (Sawatzky).
One might speculate that the federal government's interest in countering misinformation and disinformation is aimed at precisely this kind of racial hatred. But the news release from March 2022 suggests that the covid "pandemic" and "the Russian invasion of Ukraine" which "now threatens democracies around the world" take precedence (Canadian Heritage 2022). So, where does that leave Indigenous communities that have faced hundreds of years of discrimination, racism, and colonialism- and whose democratic rights to territory continue to be ignored under a system of governance and citizenship that fails to acknowledge the reality that Indigenous lands were not ceded or surrendered to the Canadian government?
As Katie Doke Sawatzky explains in a 2021 article, "Sharing Treaty Land," the Treaty Land Sharing Network has emerged as a potential tool for countering misinformation and disinformation that circulates by bringing together "settler farmers, ranchers, and other landholders willing to share their land with Indigenous land users" (Sawatzky). Sparked by the killing of Boushie and the acquittal of Stanley, along with the efforts of farmers to lobby for rights to defend their property with firearms and the Saskatchewan government's commitment to actively sell off Crown land without consultation with First Nations (a million acres between 2008 and 2020), this network has aimed to reverse racial tension and colonial behaviour by advocating for a "safe and inclusive society" that educates settler members about "Indigenous relationships to land," including historical land marks (such as the stones used to create a buffalo run or erect a tepee), the recognition of Indigenous hunting rights, and the sharing Indigenous knowledge with farmers and ranchers (Sawatzky). Launched July 2021, the network now has a robust website (https://treatylandsharingnetwork.ca) and signs that identify over thirty-six locations and more than hundred acres of (in Treaty 4 and Treaty 6 territory) that private landholders have designated as accessible to Indigenous peoples "to hunt, gather and practise ceremony" (Tait).
While a network like this is a step in the right direction, the reality is that misinformation and disinformation about Canadian racism and specifically the historical and contemporary colonization of Indigenous peoples remains shrouded in the perpetuation of a narrative of Canadian superiority and exceptionality, particularly in relation to its southern neighbour, the United States. To own up to Canada's past and present requires acknowledging how stories of Canadian virtuosity and inclusivity fail to account for the forcible removal and genocide of Indigenous peoples in Canada. Bowling for Columbine and Come From Away sustain and reinforce a mythic version of Canada that needs to be fundamentally challenged. Canadians have long been responsible for writing and circulating their own forms of self-serving propaganda. We Will Stand Up provides a much-needed exploration of how misinformation and disinformation are part and parcel of Canada's colonial legacy and contemporary national narratives. Only by listening more carefully can we start to attend to these hidden histories and rewrite "false information" (OED) to accurately reflect Canada's complex past, present, and future.
