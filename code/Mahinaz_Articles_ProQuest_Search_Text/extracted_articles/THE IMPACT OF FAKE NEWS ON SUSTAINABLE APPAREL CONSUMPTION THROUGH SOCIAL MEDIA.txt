Abstract
In contemporary society, consumers are increasingly confronted with a fundamental paradox: the tension between the aspiration for environmentally responsible fashion consumption and the economic pressure to prioritise cost-efficiency and fast-fashion accessibility. Consumer preferences and subsequent purchasing behaviours are increasingly shaped by the proliferation of digital content on social media platforms. The prevalence of fake news within these networks significantly distorts consumer perception, ultimately steering consumption decisions toward choices that may contradict stated ethical values.
This research investigates how consumers purchase sustainable clothing in response to fake news spread through social networks, as well as their future behavioural intention to adopt such apparel. To address this, the authors employed an argumentative deduction framework based on established academic literature. This conceptual model maps the behavioural antecedents influencing consumer choices in the sustainable fashion sector when exposed to fake news. The quantitative research was conducted using the survey method. The research instrument employed was an online questionnaire disseminated via social media platforms. A convenience sampling technique was adopted, the objective being to maximise the number of respondents. Data collected from 539 individuals were subsequently modelled using Structural Equation Modelling (SEM) via SmartPLS.
The research reveals that trust in social media platforms renders consumers more vulnerable to fake news when seeking to purchase sustainable apparel, as they may enter an online flow state that impairs their critical thinking. Brand managers must position their products as 'anchors of truth' by ensuring full transparency and providing authentic certifications, rather than relying solely on attractive messaging; this approach mitigates the potential negative impact of online misinformation regarding the benefits of sustainable clothing.
Keywords: sustainable clothing, social media, fake news, consumer behaviour, digital disinformation/greenwashing.
JEL classification: D12, D83, D91, L67, M30.
Introduction
In contemporary society, the apparel industry represents a dynamic and increasingly prominent domain complex global ecosystem that has evolved significantly through the influence of modern technologies and social media. It has transitioned from traditional garment manufacturing into an expansive sociocultural mechanism that enables individuals to express their personal identities (Chen, 2025). Clothing transcends its role as a symbol of pragmatic and functional utility, serving as a defining element within the sphere of visual language through which individuals assert their personality, creativity, emotions, and mood. Furthermore, attire facilitates individual affiliation with specific social groups, subcultures, or communities that share common values (Soboleva & Sánchez, 2026).
In the era of digitalisation and accelerated fast-fashion consumption, this form of identity expression through fashion is no longer confined solely to aesthetics and social belonging. Societal values, and indeed, personal values shared by communities, increasingly encompass environmental stewardship and production ethics. This paradigm shift imposes a specific level of responsibility upon all manufacturers, distributors, and retailers of apparel within their organisational processes (Hong et al., 2024).
Consequently, mankind is witnessing a paradigm shift in which brand success is no longer measured solely by its ability to facilitate identity expression, but also by the ecological footprint left during production, distribution, and retail processes. Accordingly, a sustainable approach to the entire value chain, from manufacturing to the final sale of apparel, is imperative (Abbate et al., 2023). The integration of sustainability principles within the fashion industry is no longer merely a sine qua non for operational resilience; it has become a core element of brand identity and a fundamental pillar of corporate governance, evolving into the defining global business strategy of the digital century (Cappuro et al., 2023). The transition toward sustainable fashion and apparel, hence, represents a necessary strategic response to both regulatory pressures and the depletion of natural resources.
In promoting sustainable fashion, influencers shape their social media content by employing arguments related to nature conservation, sustainable resource consumption, efficient waste management, the adoption of a balanced, green, or vegan lifestyle, and mobilisation within environmental activism movements, among others (Knupfer et al., 2023). However, the content posted by influencers on social media, as well as the news regarding apparel, is not always trustworthy; rather, it can be subject to disinformation or the manipulation of truth (Obadă & Dabija, 2022a). Fake news and/or disinformation regarding sustainable apparel can range from claims that organic cotton or wool is 'more harmful' than polyester due to water and raw material consumption (Gonzalez et al., 2023), to viral videos falsely asserting that no clothing items are actually recycled and that textile collection containers with old clothes go straight to landfill (Andini et al., 2024).
While the existing literature has explored the impact of fake news across various domains, such as fast fashion (Bonilla-Quijada et al., 2024) and eco-friendly apparel (Obadă & Dabija, 2022a), as well as the influence of green disinformation as a form of misleading sustainability communication (Oppong-Tawiah & Webster, 2023), research remains limited. Specifically, there is still a lack of detailed investigation into how fake news regarding sustainable clothing propagated via social media affects consumer behaviour and future purchase intentions. An analysis of the literature highlights significant gaps in understanding consumer behaviour toward greenwashing in the fashion industry, as well as the necessary benchmarks for developing strategic mechanisms to combat disinformation (Li et al., 2025); instead, existing studies tend to focus on the influence of generational or intercultural behaviour (Mathew & Spinelli, 2025) in relation to social responsibility (Faludi, 2025).
Consequently, the aim of this research is to examine consumer behaviour concerning the purchase of sustainable apparel under the impact of fake news circulated via social media, as well as the future behavioural intention to purchase such clothing (see Figure 1). Empirical survey-based research was conducted in 2026 among Romanian social media users, with the resulting data subsequently analysed using Structural Equation Modelling (SEM) in SmartPLS. Flow Theory, which is frequently employed in research focusing on the influence of content shared via social networks (Obadă & Dabija, 2022a; Fârte et al., 2025), is here extended.
The remainder of this paper is organised as follows. First, a comprehensive literature review contextualises Flow Theory within the sustainable fashion domain, while critically examining the dual roles of social media communication and the proliferation of fake news. Building upon this theoretical foundation, hypotheses are derived and the conceptual framework is formalised. The subsequent section details the research methodology, specifying the research design, and the two-stage analytical approach involving Confirmatory Factor Analysis (CFA) for the measurement model and Structural Equation Modelling (SEM) for the structural model. Finally, the empirical results discussion, and their broader significance are presented. The paper concludes by synthesising the theoretical contributions and managerial implications, followed by an acknowledgement of the study's limitations and suggestions for future research trajectories.
1. Literature review
1.1. Conceptual framework
Digital communication relies on Flow Theory to explain how individuals lose track of time during their interactions on social media platforms. Users experience an online 'flow' state through parasocial interactions, which enables them to build simulated emotional bonds with digital influencers. Individuals who reach high levels of concentration lose their ability to evaluate information critically, as they begin to share content without verifying its accuracy (Obadă & Dabija, 2022a). Recent studies demonstrate how flow states facilitate the spread of fake news, as individuals prioritise the continuous enjoyment of their online experience over the verification of information sources (Obadă & Dabija, 2022b). Social media platforms utilise automated systems to manage the flow of information, which necessitates that individuals employ their own psychological capacity to process and interpret that information effectively. Users who enter a flow state unknowingly become agents for fake news, leading to a significant erosion of trust in the enterprises affected by this type of information dissemination (Farte et al., 2025).
Digital communication and social media platforms serve as significant catalysts in the identity formation process, thereby influencing consumer behaviour (Vázquez-Atochero & Romero-Sanz, 2025). Social media platforms, such as Instagram, Facebook, and TikTok, operate as environments that facilitate the construction of values, the reinforcement of social norms, and the shaping and influencing of user behaviours (Vladimirova et al., 2024). Influencers play a significant role, acting as agents who promote various norms, ideas, trends, values, or behaviours; many of these individuals are particularly active in the promotion of apparel (Cabeza-Ramírez et al., 2023).
1.2. Hypothesis Development and Research Model Formulation
The fashion industry is known for the significant negative pressures it exerts on natural ecosystems (Abbate et al., 2023). Nevertheless, a compelling research area remains the exploration of how the advantages associated with social media communication processes can facilitate the development and/or consolidation of enduring consumer relationships, thereby stimulating sustainable purchasing behaviour (Li et al., 2025). Nowadays, consumers are becoming increasingly aware of the deceptive practices surrounding brands promoted via social networks, many advocating for the adoption of authentic, sustainable practices to resolve the cognitive dissonance generated by exposure to disinformation (Herciu et al., 2023; Andika et al., 2025). Therefore, fashion brands must provide transparent and credible information, capable of meeting the increasingly high and complex expectations of informed consumers (Li et al., 2025). As the effects of climate change intensify, the issue of sustainability is significant for both companies and consumers alike (Choi & Ahn, 2023). The sustainable approach is increasingly relevant in contemporary society and, by extension, in relation to online consumer behaviour and social media interactions; furthermore, studies indicate that individuals are more prone to awareness of the 'green footprint' left by their purchasing decisions (Vladimirova et al., 2024). Thus, the contemporary consumer who is active on social media and exhibits a 'green consciousness' alongside a favourable attitude toward nature preservation generates, in turn, an increased demand for brands that promote green values and adopt or implement sustainable business practices (Busalim et al., 2022).
However, users who engage intensively with social media are more vulnerable to fake news due to their superficial processing of information. Furthermore, intensive social media use favours heuristic information processing (Li et al., 2025), primarily driven by information fatigue or 'online fatigue' (Apuke et al., 2024; Stepaniuk et al., 2024). In the context of sustainable fashion, this mechanism diminishes the user's critical thinking competence, leading them to rely on superficial visual cues, which can increase vulnerability to fake news or 'greenwashing' campaigns (Andika et al., 2025). This susceptibility is further heightened by the prevalence of fast and intuitive thinking during social media navigation, as opposed to deliberative and analytical thinking (Pennycook & Rand, 2021). Thus, it can be considered that:
H1: Social media use positively influences exposure to fake news regarding sustainable fashion brands.
Frequent user exposure to sustainable clothing content and/or posts across various social platforms creates a sense of familiarity, which can lead to increased trust in the available information, ultimately evolving into an accepted social norm (Gao et al., 2026). Young individuals place significantly greater trust in social media content created and disseminated by influencers compared to official advertising campaigns launched by corporations (Li et al., 2025), thereby increasing their risk of exposure to fake news. Consequently, it is postulated that:
H2: Social media use exerts a positive influence on the level of trust consumers place in information regarding sustainable clothing disseminated through these platforms.
For Gen Zers, social networks contribute to the formation of favourable attitudes toward environmental protection, while simultaneously supporting the purchase of sustainable clothing. Furthermore, social media platforms facilitate the search for such products, stimulating the development of a 'green consciousness' oriented toward sustainability and nature preservation (Vladimirova et al., 2024), as well as socially responsible behaviour (Dinu et al., 2024). These platforms exert a catalytic role in the transition toward sustainable consumption models and enable the consolidation of sustainability knowledge among users (Schiaroli et al., 2024). Furthermore, social media posts favour aspirational purchases, stimulating community engagement and the viral transmission of educational content regarding sustainability principles (Horrich et al., 2025). Therefore, it is assumed that:
H3: Social media use has a positive influence on the purchase of sustainable clothes.
Consuming content shared on social media platforms (Instagram, TikTok, YouTube, etc.) through official corporate advertising campaigns, as well as electronic word-of-mouth (eWOM), positively influences the purchase intention of sustainable apparel, user online engagement, and repurchase intention (Gao et al., 2026). These platforms frequently host campaigns that promote pro-sustainability values, attitudes, and behaviours, contributing to consumer information and education; furthermore, influencers, online communities, and user-generated content stimulate purchase intention for sustainable clothing (Ramandini et al., 2025). Social media content that delivers promises of quality, comfort, and style, promoted in alignment with new manufacturing technologies and sustainability principles, contributes to supporting sustainable consumption. This, in turn, fosters acceptance among consumers who share these values and whose purchasing decisions are driven by the extent to which their preferred clothing helps project a favourable attitude toward environmental protection (Schiaroli et al., 2024). Consequently, it can be argued that:
H4: Social media use positively influences future purchases of sustainable clothes.
Users' exposure to content and the information flow disseminated through social media increases the likelihood that, at some point, they will perceive it as credible, without further verifying its authenticity; moreover, exposure to contradictory information regarding a specific context or phenomenon heightens the risk of accepting fake news (Li et al., 2025). A high level of trust in online information and content facilitates both the inclination and the risk of redistributing fake news, as well as the tacit acceptance of disinformation (Sun & Xie, 2024). Given the heightened receptivity of users toward online content, there is an increased probability of exposure to ecological disinformation ('greenwashing'). This compels the fashion industry to adopt transparent and authentic communication strategies to mitigate consumer cognitive dissonance induced by fake news in this field (Li et al., 2025). Consequently, leveraging influencers as primary communication vectors becomes essential for conveying authentic and accurate messages regarding the fashion industry, as well as the ethical and sustainable principles of garment production (Pelau et al., 2024; Andika et al., 2025). Thus, it is assumed that:
H5: Trust in social media on shared content has a positive influence on exposure to fake news about sustainable clothing.
The use of concrete communication messages generates a higher purchase intention compared to abstract messaging. This effect is mediated by the perceived authenticity of the message and the extent to which the product benefits from a favourable, sustainable image (Kapoor et al., 2023). The lack of transparent, clear, and verifiable information regarding a product's sustainable attributes constitutes a major barrier to purchasing decision-making (Jestratijevic et al., 2020). Social cynicism negatively influences brand trust, whereas conspicuous consumption motives are positively correlated with the purchase of sustainable clothing, as these allow consumers to project a pro-social image and express their commitment to the environment (Apaolaza et al., 2023; Policarpo et al., 2023). Electronic word-of-mouth (eWOM) supported by influencers contributes to enhancing user trust in information and content disseminated through social networks, serving as an effective vehicle for online campaigns focused on adopting sustainable consumption (Ngo et al., 2024). Consequently, eWOM practices increase the credibility and emotional appeal of online content, providing informative utility, and contributing to an extended model of advertising value. This model prioritises digital recommendations over traditional corporate strategies for credible communication (Tiwari et al., 2025). Therefore, it is presumed that:
H6: Trust in social networks towards the information shared on sustainable clothing has a positive influence on purchasing behaviour.
Both brand-generated content (BGC) and useror influencer-generated content positively determine purchase intention. While official organisational communication typically has a more pronounced effect due to professional quality and emotional appeal (Gao et al., 2026), the personalised messaging of influencers is more readily accepted as it appears more credible and objective, often fostering user identification (Mărincean et al., 2025). Communication through influencers fosters a superior positive perception due to authenticity, direct interaction, and a simple, seemingly direct, and objective style of communication (Tran & Uehara, 2023). In purchasing decisions, users rely heavily on the attractiveness, credibility, and expertise, as well as the robust competencies of influencers (George et al., 2025). Thus, it is hypothesised that:
H7: Reliance on social media for sustainable clothing content has a positive influence on future purchasing.
Literature suggests that consumers' level of expertise and environmental (green) literacy conditions are the way companies adopt strategic solutions to optimise brand image and sustainable product sales. In this regard, a pivotal role is played by the effective management of perceptions concerning online disinformation (Rehman et al., 2025). Therefore, the identification of 'greenwashing' tactics and the recognition of the genuine benefits of green products stimulate sustainable consumption; this process is facilitated by reducing consumer confusion, which, in turn, empowers them to more effectively select brands with a verifiable environmental impact (Apostolopoulos et al., 2025). The decision to purchase sustainable products is grounded in both personal motivations and social pressures (Dabija & Băbuț, 2013); however, the impact of these factors is moderated by the suspicion of 'greenwashing,' which can significantly weaken the link between a consumer's positive intentions and their actual purchasing (Do et al., 2025). Thus, it is assumed that:
H8: Exposure to fake news about sustainable clothing on social media has a positive influence on purchasing behaviour.
Clothing purchase decision-making integrates social ethics with consumer expectations regarding product quality, with customers being willing to pay a premium for sustainable items (Valencia-Arias et al., 2025), particularly when a product demonstrates green authenticity. Essentially, success within the sustainable fashion market depends on the extent to which a company's green vision resonates with the buyer's personal principles (Cascavilla et al., 2025). An individual's core values (whether egocentric or pragmatic) shape a novel sustainable vision, which triggers personal responsibility and the desire to purchase ethical clothing that preserves resources and protects the natural environment (Hong et al., 2024). Although social pressure and trust in recycling systems directly stimulate purchasing decisions, the latter functions as an independent driver, without altering the strength of other psychological factors within the decision-making process; furthermore, sustainable consumption values accentuate ethical concerns throughout the acquisition process (Upadhyay & Kamble, 2025). Consequently, it is presumed that:
H9: Purchasing behaviour concerning sustainable clothing has a positive influence on future purchases.
Based on these arguments derived from existing literature, the authors propose an investigation of the relationships illustrated in Figure 1. This model examines the impact of information consumption on social media platforms on consumer behaviour, specifically investigating how fake news exposure (FNE) and social media usage (SMU) influence trust in online information shared via social media (TOI). These factors, in turn, determine sustainable apparel purchasing decisions (SAP) and future purchasing intention (FPSA) towards sustainable apparel under the impact of fake news.
2. Research methodology
2.1. Research design
The fashion industry increasingly leverages social media platforms to intensify the promotion of apparel in general, and sustainable fashion in particular. Contemporary consumers are becoming more acutely and rigorously aware of the critical perspective regarding how companies operationalise sustainability principles (De Lenne & Vandenbosch, 2017). Social networks serve as primary information sources that shape consumer attitudes and influence purchasing decisions (Apaolaza et al., 2023). While these platforms grant users access to authentic content, they may simultaneously disseminate false information and content concerning sustainable apparel, contributing to the propagation of disinformation and fake news through the increasingly prevalent technique of 'greenwashing' (Andika et al., 2025).
By accessing social media content, users are transposed into a state of 'flow,' leading to diminished attention regarding the depth of the information accessed (Obadă & Dabija, 2022a). Consequently, they can easily perceive fake news as being authentic, which, over time, exerts adverse effects on their behaviour, as well as on the entire decision-making process concerning the acquisition of sustainable apparel (Widayati et al., 2025). Once such fake news on sustainable apparel is propagated, many consumers share it via social networks without verifying the accuracy of the information (Fârte et al., 2025). Within this context, the present research aims to determine consumer behaviour regarding the purchase of sustainable apparel and the impact of fake news disseminated through social media, as well as the future behavioural intention to acquire such items (see Figure 1).
Data collection was executed via an online survey targeting a purposive cohort of social media young users who actively engage with news content and frequently purchase sustainable apparel. A convenience sampling technique was utilised to maximise the response rate and ensure a robust sample size for subsequent analysis. Prior to participation, respondents provided informed consent following a briefing on the study's ethical protocols, which guaranteed total anonymity and the confidential treatment of data. To minimise cognitive bias and ensure contextual clarity, the survey preamble included a brief overview of the adverse effects of misinformation, emphasising the necessity of verifying news veracity on digital platforms. The resulting dataset was analysed using Partial Least Squares Structural Equation Modelling (PLS-SEM) via SmartPLS, facilitating a rigorous assessment of both the measurement and structural components of the proposed model.
A total of 539 individuals participated in the research, comprising 228 men (42.3%), 305 women (56.6%), and 6 respondents (1.1%) who preferred not to indicate their gender. The vast majority, 405 respondents (75.1%), used social media daily, while 27 (5.0%) rarely used it, and 107 (19.9%) used it moderately. Regarding educational attainment, 102 individuals (18.9%) had completed high school, 24 (4.5%) had received secondary vocational education, 342 (63.5%) held university degrees, and 71 (13.2%) had postgraduate qualifications.
2.2. Evaluation of the measurement model
The research model presented in Figure 1 was estimated using the Partial Least Squares Structural Equation Modelling (PLS-SEM) method, employing SmartPLS 4 software (Ringle et al., 2024). This variance-based approach is particularly well-suited for assessing complex dependency relationships among latent constructs. All constructs were specified as reflective and subjected to a rigorous two-stage validation process (Hair et al., 2010). First, the measurement model was evaluated in terms of reliability and convergent validity (Table 2). The reflective constructs within the conceptual model were examined through reliability analyses, internal consistency, factor loadings, Average Variance Extracted (AVE), and composite reliability (Table 2). As indicated, all factor loadings exceeded the recommended 0.70 threshold, confirming that the indicators effectively captured their respective latent phenomena (Hair et al., 2010). Internal consistency was established through Cronbach's alpha (α) and Composite Reliability (CR), both of which surpassed the conventional 0.70 benchmark (Hair et al., 2010; Henseler & Sarstedt, 2013). Specifically, the lowest recorded α was 0.740 for 'Social Media Usage' (SMU), confirming robust construct reliability. Furthermore, the Average Variance Extracted (AVE) for all constructs exceeded the 0.50 requirement (Chin, 1998), further validating convergent validity. Finally, discriminant validity was confirmed by satisfying both the Fornell-Larcker criterion and the more stringent Heterotrait-Monotrait (HTMT) ratio (Table 3), ensuring that each construct was empirically distinct within the structural framework (Hair et al., 2010).
The assessment of discriminant validity was subsequently performed to ensure the empirical distinctiveness of the latent constructs, utilising both the Fornell-Larcker criterion and the Heterotrait-Monotrait (HTMT) ratio (Table 4). Following the Fornell-Larcker protocol, the authors confirmed that the square root of the Average Variance Extracted (AVE) for each construct exceeded its highest correlation with any other latent variable, thereby satisfying the requirement for diagonal dominance. Furthermore, the HTMT ratio was employed as a more rigorous diagnostic for identifying a lack of discriminant validity (Henseler et al., 2014). All HTMT values remained consistently below the conservative 0.90 threshold, providing robust evidence that the constructs were conceptually unique. These results collectively validated the structural integrity of the model, confirming that each latent variable captured a distinct facet of the investigated phenomenon without excessive overlap.
The authors assessed item collinearity by calculating the Variance Inflation Factors (VIF) for all indicators. All VIF values remained below the recommended 3.3 threshold (Sarstedt et al., 2017), with the highest recorded value of 3,079<3,3 for the 'FNE2' item, thereby confirming the absence of multicollinearity within the measurement model. Subsequently, the bootstrapping procedure was applied to test the hypotheses and examine the relationships among the latent variables. Ultimately, nine hypotheses reflecting significant and positive influences were supported by the empirical testing.
3. Results and discussion
The collinearity analysis among constructs revealed no multicollinearity concerns, as the highest Variance Inflation Factor (VIF) value recorded was 1.335 (Sustainable Apparel Purchase (SAP)→ Future Purchase of Sustainable Apparel (FPSA), remaining well below the threshold of 3.3. The goodness-of-fit of the saturated model was likewise acceptable, with the Standardised Root Mean Square Residual (SRMR) yielding a value of 0.058 < 0.08 for the estimated model and 0.058 < 0.08 for the calculated model, thus satisfying the required threshold. Furthermore, sustainable clothing purchases, social media use, and trust in information shared online via social media collectively accounted for 50.0% of the variance in future sustainable clothing purchases (R2 = 0.500), indicating a high predictive power of the structural model (Figure 2).
H1 assumed that the use of social media usage (SMU) has a positive influence on fake news exposure (FNE) on sustainable clothing (Table 4). Results (β=0.233; T-value=5.317; p<0.001) confirmed the existence of a positive, strong and significant influence, which allows the hypothesis to be confirmed. Previous literature (Dong, 2024) has analysed this relationship, demonstrating that social media is used by organisations to strengthen the symbolic power of own brands or products, allowing them to influence public perception of sustainable fashion through transparent management of information and visual content. The flow of news transmitted through social networks plays a mediating role in the distribution of fake news regarding organic products (Obadă & Dabija, 2022a), with social media exposure patterns influencing the credibility given to fake news (Wu et al., 2025).
H2 investigated the influence of social media usage (SMU) on the generation of trust in online information (TOI). The results confirmed this influence (β=0.322; T-value=7.592; p < 0.001), certifying the existence of a positive, intense, and highly significant relationship. Existing literature (Huslan & Hassim, 2024) has demonstrated that these platforms not only inform consumers but also build trust in sustainability-related information. When frequently interacting with a specific type of content on social media, users become accustomed to it and more readily believe the information provided online without further verifying its authenticity or veracity. In practice, the credibility of the viewed content increases as users becomes habituated to accessing it (Pelau et al., 2023).
H3 investigated the influence of social media usage (SMU) on sustainable apparel purchase (SAP). The results showing a lower but positive and significant intensity (β=0.161; T-value=3.802; p<0.002), allowed for confirmation of the hypothesis. Existing literature (Jacobson & Harrison, 2022; Sahu et al., 2025) has demonstrated that the purchasing process for sustainable clothing is directly facilitated by content created and shared on social media by influencers. Once validated in the online environment, this content can shift the consumer's ethical motivation, thereby facilitating their acquisitions.
H4 analysed the relationship between social media usage (SMU) and future purchase of sustainable apparel (FPSA). The results indicated a positive, albeit low-intensity, yet significant relationship (β=0.097; T-value=2.796; p < 0.017). The hypothesis was accepted, consistent with previous research findings (Pakpahan et al., 2025), which have shown that social media exerts a substantial impact on the choices of 'green' apparel consumers. Naturally, social media platforms favour both content supporting green trends and 'greenwashing' information, which is occasionally promoted (Obadă et al., 2024).
H5 assumed the existence of a positive influence of trust in online information (TOI) on fake news exposure (FNE) regarding sustainable apparel. The results confirmed a positive, highly significant, and medium-intensity relationship (β = 0.156; T-value = 3.321; p < 0.001), thus supporting the hypothesis. This finding is congruent with the literature (Obadă & Dabija, 2022a), which notes that users who trust social media platforms tend to reduce their vigilance regarding the viewed content, thereby increasing their exposure to false information about the accessed products.
H6 was based on the premise that trust in online information (TOI) shared via social media positively influences the purchasing behaviour of sustainable apparel. The results confirmed this situation (β=0.381; T-value=8.567; p < 0.001), showing a strong and significant influence; thus, the hypothesis is accepted. Existing literature (Wu & Long, 2024) has found that when users trust social media and find useful information, their cognition regarding the viewed content shifts rapidly, often favourably influencing their purchasing decisions. Furthermore, social media users' trust in environmental issues functions as an internal mechanism that helps them perceive ecological products as authentic, especially when they are intensely promoted in digital environments (Pelau et al., 2023).
H7 investigated the effect of trust in social media information (TOI) on future purchase of sustainable apparel (FPSA). The results revealed a medium-intensity but strongly positive influence (β=0.137; T-value=3.615; p < 0.001), allowing for the hypothesis to be accepted. Existing literature (Amath & Kajendra, 2025) suggests that social media users make online purchases because of their trust in digital platforms, highlighting an increased predisposition toward acquiring clothing produced according to ethical and sustainable principles.
H8 analysed the influence of fake news exposure (FNE) on social media regarding sustainable apparel on sustainable apparel consumption (SAC). The results revealed a weak and significant influence (β=0.141; T-value=3.377; p<0.001.), leading to the acceptance of the hypothesis. The positive influence of exposure to fake news on sustainable clothing purchasing behaviour on social media could be explained through information processing and credibility transfer mechanisms. Prior literature (Visentin et al., 2019) demonstrates that the objective truthfulness of media content does not exert a direct effect on purchase intention; instead, fake news operates through a chain of mediated effects, were perceived credibility shapes brand trust and, ultimately, brand attitudes. Applied to the context of sustainable fashion, this mechanism suggests that exposing consumers to favourable claims about sustainability, even if factually questionable, can generate positive associations with those products, thereby reinforcing the motivation to buy. Furthermore, the rapid dissemination of viral content on social media hinders fact checking. This allows misinformation to reach consumers before any verification can occur, paradoxically functioning as a persuasive stimulus that amplifies awareness of sustainable consumption and, consequently, drives the purchasing behaviour of eco-friendly products (Li et al., 2025)
H9 analysed the influence of sustainable apparel purchase (SAP) on future purchase of sustainable apparel (FPSA). The results revealed a highly significant and positive influence (β=0.592; T-value=18.300; p<0.001), thus supporting the hypothesis. In effect, previous purchases of sustainable products serve as a predictor of future acquisitions. Furthermore, by purchasing sustainable clothing, consumer-users develop a self-image that makes them feel actively involved in environmental protection. Consequently, the purchase of sustainable apparel is no longer merely a transaction; it becomes a long-term lifestyle choice, where individuals consume goods in a manner that contributes to nature preservation and environmental protection (Khan et al., 2025).
In contemporary society, consumers navigate a complex informational landscape where the need for self-actualisation through consumption frequently conflicts with the necessity of adopting sustainable and ethical behaviour. Perceived ethical consumption (Olson, 2022), such as purchasing second-hand clothing can paradoxically generate an intensification of overall consumption by providing psychological validation for additional purchases, or by mitigating the feelings of guilt associated with such behaviour (Mizrachi & Sharon, 2025).
To counteract the phenomenon of social media disinformation (Raman et al., 2025), alongside the active and rapid combating of fake news (Obada & Dabija, 2022b; Fârte et al., 2025), existing literature (Arribas et al., 2025) emphasises the necessity of developing digital literacy skills and cultivating critical thinking among social media platform users. Furthermore, efforts to mitigate the negative impact of consuming, generating, and/or distributing fake news include awareness campaigns on the importance of digital and media literacy, source verification, and fostering collaborations between experts in programming, journalism, psychology, sociology, and/or cybersecurity (Burcă-Voicu et al., 2024; Pennycook & Rand, 2021). Frequent social media usage increases the risk of overexposure to fake news, as well as the risk of content redistribution by users without the activation of critical thinking skills (Andika et al., 2025).
Information aimed at spreading disinformation via social media regarding the advantages of sustainable choices represents a significant barrier to the promotion of sustainable apparel. Specialised literature indicates that false or oversimplified information, whether through intentional or unintentional omission concerning composition or recycling methods, distorts consumer perception and erodes trust in choices promoted as natural (Kumar et al., 2021). Online content tends to exhibit a positive pro-sustainability bias, often amplified by platform algorithmic mechanisms, which facilitates the transmission of distorted information and digital 'greenwashing' (Li et al., 2025). Effectively counteracting this phenomenon requires educational interventions focused on the correct association of promoted values with their accurate perception and digital literacy, alongside interdisciplinary collaborations among experts in fields such as computer science, journalism, psychology, and cybersecurity (Arribas et al., 2025). A relevant integration of practices and approaches in sustainable marketing communication must include transparent communication processes, ensuring that consumers are well-equipped to make informed decisions regarding sustainable apparel purchases.
Conclusions
From a theoretical perspective, this paper contributes to the expansion of research regarding the application of Flow Theory within the context of proliferating fake news and the digitalisation of apparel sustainability. The research establishes the 'state of flow' not merely as a precursor to brand loyalty (Obadă & Dabija, 2022a), but as a dual psychological mechanism with contradictory effects on consumer discernment. On the one hand, the attitudinal-persuasive and experiential-immersive dimensions optimise the internalisation of ethical values by reducing message resistance and enhancing perceived utility. On the other hand, deep digital immersion induces a 'cognitive opportunity cost': the flow state facilitates heuristic processing, thereby diminishing the rigor of source verification and increasing vulnerability to disinformation. Within this framework, the research proposes a reassessment of the interaction between the visual authority created by influencers and the acquisition of cognitive resources, arguing that the transition from knowledge to purchase intention in sustainable fashion is mediated by the fragile balance between the psychological reward of flow and the risk of overconsumption. Thus, the theoretical contribution lies in identifying the preconditions of flow that can orient actual behaviour toward product longevity, moving beyond mere pro-environmental attitudes, which are often redundant among price-sensitive segments.
From a managerial perspective, this research demonstrates that trust in social platforms acts as a catalyst both for exposure to fake news and for the decision to purchase sustainable apparel. Managers of companies retailing such products must recognise the paradoxical vulnerability of the loyal consumer. By frequently accessing social media, these users reduce their critical and rational thinking; consequently, upon entering a 'state of flow,' they are more exposed to fake news and exhibit an increased tendency to trust it. Therefore, managers must identify the levers and vectors through which consumer/users can consistently verify the veracity of the content viewed in the online environment.
Furthermore, managers could implement 'radical transparency' by employing traceability technologies for social media content, effectively transforming official profiles into 'truth anchors' that pre-empt the phenomenon of greenwashing. Instead of communicating solely aspirational messages, sustainable apparel brands should provide raw data and third-party certifications aimed at bolstering trust, thereby neutralising the disruptive potential of fake news before it escalates into perceived universal truth. Simultaneously, marketers are called to collaborate with content creators (influencers) who can replicate the 'familiar' aesthetic and tone of social networks while maintaining ethically rigorous content. The strategic objective is to utilise experiential design to keep the user in an informative 'state of flow' that facilitates purchasing, while allowing for moments of reflection that enable the verification of product authenticity. This approach would counteract users' predisposition to believe fake news simply due to its frequency in their information feed. Lastly, managerial strategies should treat sustainable clothing customers as full partners in environmental conservation. Loyalty programs should shift their focus from mere price discounts toward validating the customer's self-image and specific contribution to the company's sustainability strategy. As sustainable consumption evolves into a lifestyle, acquisition and retention strategies should emphasise storytelling, transforming every purchase into a confirmation of the user's sociocultural values.
Despite the methodological rigor and statistical significance of the results, the present research entails several limitations that must be considered. Although structural equation modelling (SEM) techniques indicated significant influence relationships for most hypotheses, they did not establish definitive temporal causality. For instance, while the data suggested that current purchasing predicts future intentions, longitudinal research would be necessary to capture the actual evolution of consumer behaviour and to eliminate seasonal or market-specific variations in the fashion industry. Data collection relied on self-reporting instruments, which introduced the risk of social desirability bias. In the context of sustainability and environmental protection, respondents tended to overestimate their ethical behaviours and underestimate their vulnerability to fake news. Another limitation is that the research focused on social media as a unitary ecosystem, without granularly differentiating between platforms (e.g., Instagram vs. TikTok vs. Facebook). Given that each platform relies on different information diffusion algorithms, the impact of exposure to fake news may vary according to the technical specifics of each network. Furthermore, the sample used, although representative of a specific socio-demographic area, might not reflect global cultural diversity, thereby limiting the generalisability of the findings to markets with social norms and values different from those analysed.
Undoubtedly, the findings of the present research open new horizons for investigation, offering researchers the opportunity to explore the underlying mechanisms of disinformation within the context of sustainable 'green' apparel consumption. A primary future research direction should involve the use of experimental methods to test consumer resilience to 'greenwashing.' Such efforts could identify the extent to which media literacy interventions targeted at sustainable clothing consumers contribute to diminishing trust in social platforms and/or the acceptance of fake news following exposure to continuous information flows. Furthermore, the application of neuro-marketing techniques could provide objective data on how the 'state of flow' inhibits the critical processing of information regarding sustainable apparel. Future studies should also conduct detailed analyses of potential moderating constructs, such as digital literacy levels, generational cohorts (Gen Z vs. Millennials), or the type of device used (mobile vs. desktop) during the actual purchasing of sustainable clothing. Examining how these factors attenuate or enhance the relationship between fake news perception and sustainable apparel consumption would add significant depth to the current theoretical framework. Additionally, a compelling perspective would be to analyse the impact of filtering mechanisms designed to reduce exposure to disinformation. Future research could examine whether users already oriented toward sustainability become more vulnerable to 'green' fake news due to recommendation algorithms that deliver content confirming their own sociocultural and personal values.
