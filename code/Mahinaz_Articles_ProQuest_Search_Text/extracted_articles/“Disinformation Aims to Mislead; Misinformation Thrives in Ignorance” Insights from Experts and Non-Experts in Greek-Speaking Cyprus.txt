1. Introduction Misinformation and disinformation are among today’s most severe global risks (World Economic Forum 2024). Their proliferation contributes to diminishing public trust and media credibility, grows skepticism, weakens democratic institutions, and ultimately has significant social consequences. The spread of false information has been amplified by online platforms. Although the latter have made information access more democratic, they have become the main facilitators of spreading false information (Lazer et al. 2018). This phenomenon underscores the urgent need for targeted actions aiming at improving critical thinking and media literacy, as well as strengthening fact-checking efforts.
This paper aims to investigate the challenges of misinformation and disinformation in the context of the internet society. Moreover, it examines effective strategies to improve media literacy among adults. These challenges are especially pronounced in Greece and Cyprus. As it will be examined below, Cyprus and Greece, for instance, deal with a distinct set of challenges characterized by low digital literacy rates (European Commission 2024) and a fragmented media environment due to geopolitical tensions and economic constraints (Papasolomou et al. 2016; Sitistas 2020).
By analyzing diverse perspectives through qualitative methods, this study aims to contribute to the development of targeted educational programs and policy recommendations to combat false information and promote critical thinking. Data were collected through focus groups and expert interviews, ensuring a comprehensive exploration of diverse perspectives. The focus groups capture the broader societal experiences of misinformation and disinformation, while expert interviews provide in-depth insights from stakeholders, including government officials, educators, and media professionals. Unless explicitly stated, for the purposes of this work, “disinformation” and “misinformation” will collectively be referred to under the umbrella term “false information”, encompassing both of the above as well as other types of inaccurate information circulating the internet.
More specifically, the current study poses the following research questions:
- What are the key distinctions between misinformation and disinformation as perceived by non-experts and experts?

- How do individuals assess the credibility of different media platforms and information sources?

- What strategies can be employed to enhance critical thinking and media literacy among adults?

- How do social networks contribute to both the dissemination and mitigation of misinformation and disinformation?

- What role do tailored educational programs play in addressing the challenges of misinformation and disinformation?



This article consists of six sections and proceeds as follows: Section 2 reviews the existing literature on misinformation and disinformation, emphasizing their societal impacts. Section 3 provides a detailed description of the qualitative methodology that was used, which utilized thematic analysis of data collected from focus groups and expert interviews. Section 4 presents key findings, organized thematically in a six-phase framework. Section 5 and Section 6 discuss the findings, highlighting the specific false information challenges faced by Cyprus. These parts address the research questions and propose strategies needed to combat the identified issues. Finally, the conclusion synthesizes the research findings.
2. Literature Review The proliferation of false information has emerged as a global challenge, particularly within the context of social media platforms. The World Economic Forum (2024) has identified false information as the most severe short-term global risk and the fifth most severe long-term risk (p. 11). This phenomenon is not new; instead, it has been prevalent since the invasion of the printing press. However, the rise of the internet society has amplified the power of false information (Soll 2016). A growing body of research highlights a significant increase in the distribution of misleading information across online platforms in the last decade (Shu et al. 2017; Enders et al. 2023). The internet has enabled the spread of fake news by providing new means; therefore, the consequences have become increasingly severe (Burkhardt 2017).
The existing literature employs various terms classified under the umbrella of content containing false information, including misinformation, disinformation, malinformation, and fake news (Caled and Silva 2022). The distinctions among disinformation and misinformation hinge primarily on the underlying intent. Misinformation may be inadvertent or unintentional, while disinformation is meant to deceive (Guess and Lyons 2020). Misinformation refers to false or misleading information that is shared without harmful intent (Chen et al. 2023). In contrast, disinformation refers to deliberately false or misleading information created with the intent to deceive or manipulate audiences. Malinformation is genuine information shared to produce harm (Wardle and Derakhshan 2017). According to Wardle and Derakhshan (2017), the term “information disorder” encompasses all three notions: misinformation, disinformation, and malinformation. Furthermore, the term fake news refers to fabricated or manipulated information presented as legitimate journalism. Thus, fake news is a species of disinformation (Gelfert 2018, p. 103; Caled and Silva 2022). In essence, fake news is understood as a manifestation of disinformation (Gelfert 2018, p. 103). Such information can be disseminated for different purposes, aiming at “managing public opinion, controlling the social situation, forming a specified impression, or justifying someone’s policy and action” (Lamprou et al. 2021, p. 419). Fake news can take many forms (Molina et al. 2019), including clickbait, hoax, rumor, satire, propaganda, framing, conspiracy theories, and others, although researchers and fact-checkers disagree as to what should be included under the term (Aïmeur et al. 2023).
The term “fake news” has become a focal point of scholarly debate due to its complex implications and varied interpretations. One major issue is the ambiguity and overgeneralization of the term. Fake news lacks a precise definition, often encompassing a broad spectrum of false information and even legitimate news labeled as false. This vagueness can hinder effective communication and research (Tandoc 2021). Additionally, political weaponization of the term has contributed to its controversy. Politicians and public figures frequently use “fake news” to discredit unfavorable media coverage, regardless of its accuracy. This tactic undermines trust in traditional media and blurs the line between factual reporting and falsehoods (Farkas and Schou 2024). Lately, both scholars and fact-checkers advocate for more precise terminology when discussing false information, favoring terms like misinformation and disinformation, which provide greater clarity (Wardle and Derakhshan 2017).
The prevalence of misinformation and disinformation is exacerbated by how people consume information on the internet. Social media function as digital spaces where people actively seek and consume a vast array of information. They not only connect people but have also become an integral part of people’s everyday activities, including news consumption (Gilsenan 2019). Most users do not rely on traditional news websites as the main source of online news; instead, they prefer social media, search platforms, or aggregators (Newman et al. 2024, p. 11). Thus, traditional news websites are not the primary sources of information today. This can be explained by several factors. First, traditional news media are more timely and costly and provide fewer opportunities for commentary and discussion than news consumption through social media (Shu et al. 2017). Additionally, growing skepticism toward traditional media—driven by concerns over political bias—has further fueled this trend. Soontjens and Van Erkel (2022) found that many individuals perceive them as partisan, often favoring opposing political views while being unfair to their own. This perception has led some users to seek alternatives that represent their perspectives. Social media platforms address this need by tailoring content to align with users’ specific interests and preferences, a concept that will be further discussed below.
Social media platforms have managed to transform information consumption, fostering a degree of democratization in the access to information and news. The rapid sharing of content enables individuals to engage with information that was previously not accessible to them. Consequently, the scope of information available to the general public has been broadened, enabling users to act as “citizen journalists” by creating and sharing content (Mavridis 2018). Social media delivers news and allows users to interact with it by sharing and spreading information. Citizen journalists post information, photos, and news on social media that reach a mass audience. As a result, online platforms are becoming the main sources of news (Tandoc et al. 2018). This shift changes how journalism is conceptualized, where media organizations are being bypassed. Online platforms’ lack of editorial oversight causes many unverified and authenticated information to spread and mislead users (Lamprou et al. 2021).
Onook Oh et al. (2013) emphasize that social media have a “dual role as a potential tool for social reporting and a collective rumour mill” (p. 408). This democratization of information presents a double-edged sword; it enhances public engagement while raising critical questions about information accuracy, as it enables any individual to create and share unverified content. Thus, social media have a significant role in disseminating information, both good and bad (Muhammed and Mathew 2022).
Research indicates that social media are the primary facilitators of the spread of false information. The ease of sharing and the viral nature of social media enable the quick and easy circulation and dissemination of false information (Lazer et al. 2018, p. 1095). Social media platforms have been structured so that they are facilitators of spreading false information. The algorithms that govern content visibility are designed to enhance user engagement by curating content that aligns with users’ existing preferences (Pavlidis 2024; Muhammed and Mathew 2022). As a result, these algorithms reinforce pre-existing beliefs by exposing users to like-minded viewpoints while limiting access to alternative narratives as users seek confirmation of their perspectives (Sunstein 2017). This phenomenon, referred to as the “echo chamber”, can lead individuals to believe in false information, making it challenging, if not impossible, to correct these misconceptions (Sunstein 2017, p. 19). The echo chamber effect can polarize users’ opinions and facilitate the consumption and belief of false information as these homogenous communities consume and reinforce the same opinions and views that are believed as more credible since they promote their existing narratives (Shu et al. 2017). The phenomenon of the echo chambers can be explained through the employment of cognitive biases, such as the confirmation bias or the availability heuristic (Messing and Westwood 2012; Anastasiades et al. 2021; Piksa et al. 2024; Modgil et al. 2024).
Furthermore, journalists, for their part, interact with the audience on social media and use the platforms to break new stories (Tandoc and Vos 2016). Media organizations have prioritized the development of their online presence by developing websites and engaging through social media platforms (Forja-Pena et al. 2024; Rajapaksha et al. 2018; Ramírez 2021). The internet society has enabled media organizations to reach a wider audience through technological advances and algorithmic adaptations, while posing new challenges in maintaining credibility (Mavridis 2018). Notably, the demand for immediate coverage of news stories exacerbates content verification, leading to incidents where mainstream media incorporate viral false information that first appears on social media (Caled and Silva 2022).
The vast volume of easily accessible online material has significantly contributed to the spread of false information (Bertani et al. 2024). This problem is compounded by the difficulty of reviewing and correcting false information, which spreads rapidly due to new social technologies. Vosoughi et al.’s (2018) study found that “falsehood reaches a depth of 19 nearly 10 times faster than the truth reached a depth of 10” (p. 3). The combination of the above-mentioned vulnerabilities, along with the lack of similar effective tools for fact-checking, has transformed the internet into a vehicle for the diffusion of false information (Caled and Silva 2022).
Moreover, the rise of easy-to-use interfaces for large-scale artificial intelligence (AI) models has led to a surge in false information. AI tools that enable voice cloning and the creation of counterfeits pose serious challenges to trust and authenticity in online interactions (World Economic Forum 2024, p. 18). The rise of AI tools has made the detection of false information an even more challenging problem, adding further uncertainty. The issue is especially noticeable in the United States, where people are concerned about the potential for AI technologies to obscure the identification of false information in sensitive areas like politics (Newman et al. 2024).
Public trust has been impacted by online false information. People are increasingly dismissing digital content as possibly fake. According to research by the Reuters Institute, 59 per cent of respondents expressed concerns about distinguishing between trustworthy and untrustworthy content on online platforms (Newman et al. 2024). This loss of trust and growing skepticism have significant societal implications (Wardle and Derakhshan 2017). Apart from the loss of trust, the spread of false information can lead to uninformed citizens who are less accepting of democratic principles and have lower rates of political participation (Broda and Strömbäck 2024).
With these challenges in mind, social media organizations have made considerable efforts to mitigate the spread of false information. Nevertheless, existing measures are not in a position to detect all types of false information circulating the internet (DiCicco and Agarwal 2020; Olan et al. 2024). Implementing fact-checking tools is considered significant in reducing the spread of false information, mitigating its adverse effects, and building trust in society (Olan et al. 2024; Martel and Rand 2024). The European Commission continues to focus on advancing critical thinking, high-quality journalism, and media literacy. It proposes comprehensive actions that will include improving the traceability of information and involving different stakeholders such as public authorities, online platforms, and media groups. Crucially, for societies to build their resilience to false information, it is important to strengthen digital media literacy, particularly in young people (European Commission 2018). In line with these efforts, in 2022, the European Commission revised the Code of Practice on Disinformation as part of a broader regulatory framework, in combination with the legislation on Transparency and Targeting of Political Advertising and the Digital Services Act (European Commission 2022).
Digital literacy focuses on the technical and practical skills required to engage with digital tools, whereas media literacy is a skill to use and evaluate media content. Wuyckens et al. (2022) note that digital, media, and information literacy co-exist. Park et al. (2020) further assert that information literacy and media literacy are regarded as similar to digital literacy. This article takes into consideration the overlapping issues between low digital and media literacy skills in Greek-speaking societies as contributing factors to the spread of false information and the challenges individuals face in critically engaging with online content.
Given the paramount importance of these concerns today, further research on how false information affects the national context is crucial. While existing studies have addressed their broader impacts within the internet society, there is a gap in the literature explicitly focusing on Greek-speaking societies, with their particularities in the south-eastern-most part of Europe. This gap is particularly significant because of the countries’ digital and media literacy challenges, as the Digital Decade Country Report 2024 and the Media Literacy Index 2023 indicate. According to the first, 49.5 per cent of the population in Cyprus and 52.4 per cent in Greece possess at least basic digital skills, both notably below the EU average of 55.6 per cent (European Commission 2024). According to the second, Cyprus and Greece rank 28th and 29th out of 41 European countries on media literacy skills, well below the European average (Lessenski 2023).
The present study aims to fill this gap, through the collection of data from Cyprus, where such studies are non-existent, and through the application of a qualitative study in order to better understand these phenomena with input from experts and non-experts. Notably, the low media literacy indicators for Cyprus and Greece have created a pressing need for exploring their media landscape in the context of false information (Lessenski 2023). This qualitative approach has allowed us to study experts’ and non-experts’ perceptions, opinions, and needs, which need to be addressed and respected in an information-rich environment where people seek to successfully navigate. Although these issues are not new in the literature, the current study aims to bring them together and investigate them in depth to gain a clearer and more thorough understanding of information usage in the internet world, especially in the Greek-speaking society of Cyprus. The findings highlight the particularities of the Greek-speaking information landscape, presenting new insights that can inform tailored educational programs and policy interventions.
3. Methodology This study investigates the prevalence, characteristics, and impact of false information in the media within the internet society. To achieve this, a qualitative research design was employed, utilizing thematic analysis of data collected from focus groups and expert interviews. The aim was to identify patterns and themes regarding media literacy, information evaluation, and critical thinking. This study was conducted as part of the Erasmus+ project “CheckMate—Empowering Europeans Towards a Media-Savvy Citizenry”, a KA2 cooperation partnership in adult education, and both interviews and focus group discussions were carried out over January and February 2024.
3.1. Participants Participants were purposively selected to represent diverse perspectives on media literacy and information dissemination. This sampling strategy ensured the inclusion of individuals with direct experience in media-related challenges and those impacted by false information.
The sampling included the following:
- Focus Group: Five participants, representing the general public, with a specific emphasis on adult educators and students. This group aimed to capture the broader societal experiences of false information. The focus group included participants across different age groups (18–65) from 19 to 46 years of age. The sample consisted of three women (two academics and one university student) and two men (two university students), ensuring gender equality and age diversity.

- Expert Interviews: Five semi-structured interviews were conducted with stakeholders including government officials, adult education providers, and media literacy educators. Each expert provided insights tailored to their professional domain, contributing qualitative data to complement the focus group findings.



Specifically, the experts were as follows:
- Member of the Cyprus Radiotelevision Authority: A female government official responsible for regulating broadcast content. She provided insights into legal and ethical considerations for combating false information, emphasizing the role of regulation in fostering media accuracy.

- Elected President of a Local Village Community: A male regional public authority with grassroots experience. He discussed the impact of false information on local communities and emphasized the need for accessible media literacy initiatives.

- University Professor and Adult Educator Specializing in Law: She brought an academic perspective, addressing how disinformation parallels propaganda and how misinformation spreads within informal networks.

- University Professor Specializing in Media Studies: She highlighted the evolving nature of trust in traditional versus online media, noting biases and systemic issues within media ecosystems.

- Experienced Fact-Checker and Media Literacy Educator: A professional with over a decade of experience in fact-checking and organizing media literacy seminars, who is also an elected member of the International Fact-Checking Network (IFCN). He provided pragmatic insights into misinformation detection of false information and the role of critical thinking in combating false narratives.



3.2. Design This study adopted a qualitative approach to explore various perceptions and experiences, employing a combination of focus groups and individual expert interviews. The dual approach allowed for a comprehensive exploration of participants’ perceptions and experiences while addressing the roles of experts in tackling false information. This design was selected to ensure both depth and breadth in data collection.
Thematic analysis, as outlined by Braun and Clarke (2006), was used to identify and analyze patterns within the data. This approach allowed for a detailed analysis of participant insights, making it suitable for examining the multifaceted nature of false information in media consumption.
3.3. Tools The focus group and interviews were guided by structured question templates created by the Checkmate consortium and shared with all the partners so that data could be obtained from various countries on the same issues. For the purposes of the current analysis, however, only data from Cyprus were used. The following questions were addressed:
- Definitions and distinctions between disinformation and misinformation.

- Trust in media and social networks.

- The role of critical thinking in evaluating news.

- Preferences for information quality versus quantity.

- Suggestions for media literacy education programs.

3.4. Procedure The focus group discussion was conducted on-site at Neapolis University Pafos and lasted approximately 60 min. A moderator guided the discussion while an assistant recorded observations and ensured logistical smoothness. Expert interviews were held online via Microsoft Teams and were 45–75 min long. Discussions were recorded and later transcribed for analysis.
For both focus group discussion and expert interviews, participants were informed of the study’s purpose, procedures, and their rights to withdraw at any time before the discussion/interviews began. They were also informed that data were securely stored and used exclusively for research purposes. Informed consent was obtained before data collection, ensuring participants’ anonymity and confidentiality.
3.5. Data Processing and Analysis The data were processed using thematic analysis, following Braun and Clarke’s (Braun and Clarke 2006; Byrne 2022) six-phase framework. This framework includes the following phases:
- Familiarization, with transcripts being repeatedly reviewed to identify recurring ideas and patterns.

- Generation of Initial Codes, with data segments being coded using both deductive (predefined themes) and inductive (emerging themes) approaches. For instance, codes such as “media trust”, “digital literacy”, and “critical thinking” were applied.

- Searching for Themes, with codes being grouped into overarching themes, such as “Educational Needs” and “Social Networks”.

- Reviewing of Themes, with the refinement of step 3 to ensure coherence and relevance, eliminating overlaps and ambiguities.

- Definition and Naming of Themes, with each theme being clearly defined and assigned descriptive titles to encapsulate its essence.

- Production of the Report, with themes being synthesized into a narrative, with illustrative quotes used to support the findings.



The results of each individual step are presented in the next section.
4. Results The application of the six-phase framework in the data analysis resulted in the following steps and their corresponding outcomes:
- Phase 1. Familiarization



The first step involved immersing in the data through repeated readings of the transcripts. This helped develop an in-depth understanding of participants’ perspectives. Key initial observations included the frequent confusion between disinformation and misinformation, particularly due to terminological overlaps in Greek, the repeated emphasis on the need for practical skills in media literacy, as well as varied levels of trust in traditional and digital media, depending on familiarity and transparency.
Initial impressions were documented, and notable quotes were highlighted. For example, a Focus Group Participant claimed that “In disinformation, all information is false or a lie; in misinformation, the information is incomplete”., while as part of the Experts Interviews, the Fact-Checker emphasized that “Misinformation often spreads unintentionally, but the damage can be just as severe as deliberate disinformation”.

- Phase 2. Generating Initial Codes


Data were systematically coded by identifying key phrases and recurring ideas. Examples of codes are shown in Table 2. Additionally, each code was supported by data excerpts. For example, the quote “Disinformation aims to mislead; misinformation thrives in ignorance” from a Focus Group Participant was used in Intentionality, while Media Trust included quotes such as “I trust certain platforms because their biases are known; I can weigh them” from an Expert Adult Educator/Academic.

- Phase 3. Searching for Themes


Consequently, initial codes were grouped into broader, overarching themes. This involved identifying patterns and connections among codes. For example, an Intentionality theme emerged, with sub-themes such as Disinformation vs. Misinformation and Propagation. Related codes included Propaganda and Unintentional Errors. Another example is the Media Trust theme, with sub-themes such as Bias in Media and Independent Media. Related codes included Confidence and Familiarity with Platforms.
These themes were chosen based on their frequency and relevance to the research questions. Intentionality emerged as a major theme due to it being the defining difference in the distinction between disinformation and misinformation. On the other hand, Media Trust reflected the participants’ diverse opinions on the reliability of different media formats.

- Phase 4. Reviewing Themes


Themes were refined to ensure their cohesion and distinctiveness. In other words, it was made sure that the themes did not include overlapping issues, and that the sub-themes were intrinsically related to the main themes. This resulted in a comprehensive representation of the dataset. For example, the sub-themes Bias in Media and Media Ownership were consolidated under Media Trust to avoid redundancy. Also, quotes were re-examined to confirm their alignment with the themes. For example, “I prefer independent media because their biases are more predictable” was categorized under Media Trust due to its relevance to perceptions of bias.

- Phase 5. Defining and Naming Themes


Each theme was clearly defined and given a descriptive name:
- Educational Needs: this theme emphasized on the importance of media literacy, training, and digital tools.

- Media Trust: this theme focused on perceptions of reliability in traditional and digital media.

- Intentionality: this theme gave particular emphasis on the understanding of intentionality in the spreading of false information and its societal impact.

- Social Networks: this theme discussed the opportunities and risks in using platforms such as Twitter and Facebook.

- Critical Thinking: this theme investigated the role of biases and objectivity in evaluating information.



As always, each theme was supported by illustrative quotes. For example, Social Networks included comments by experts such as “Twitter is better for following real-time updates from experts”. Critical Thinking, on the other hand, included comments such as “Truth depends on perspectives and cannot always be absolute” (Focus Group Participant).
The finalized themes are presented below, along with a description of the important points made by participants, a division into sub-themes, and providing quotes from participants, both focus group and experts.
The first theme, Educational Needs, highlights the importance of media literacy education in enhancing critical thinking and digital literacy and the critical evaluation of information and sources. Participants emphasized the necessity of workshops designed to teach practical skills such as verifying sources and detecting false information. One participant noted, “Workshops need to reflect real-life challenges, like detecting clickbait”. Tailored programs for specific audiences, including parents, educators, and professionals, were also seen as essential. Experts emphasized that “Parents need media literacy skills to guide their children effectively”. Gamification and interactive activities emerged as effective methods for engaging learners, especially adults, with one expert observing that, “Gamification keeps people engaged while teaching critical skills”.
The second theme, Media Trust, explores participants’ varying levels of trust in traditional and online media, shaped by perceptions of bias and transparency. Many participants, both experts and members of the public, expressed skepticism about traditional media due to their perceived alignment with political or financial interests. One remarked, “Media trust depends on transparency regarding ownership and sponsorship”. Familiarity with certain media outlets was found to increase trust, even when biases were acknowledged. As one participant shared, “I trust platforms I’ve followed for years, even knowing their biases”. To mitigate distrust, participants commonly cross-verified information across multiple sources. “I read multiple sources to ensure I get all perspectives”, explained a focus group member.
The third theme, Intentionality, underscores the distinctions between disinformation and misinformation and their respective impacts. Disinformation is perceived as more dangerous due to its deliberate agenda of achieving various types of gain, such as political, financial, or military. Disinformation was described by one participant as having “… an agenda, making it more dangerous than misinformation”. On the other hand, misinformation’s widespread and often unintentional sharing was viewed as equally problematic. “Misinformation spreads because people share without thinking critically”, noted a focus group member. In Greek, there is an extra layer of complication and confusion, as the terminology for these two forms of false information overlaps. Both are called “παραπληροφόρηση”, which directly translates to “false information” with the added distinction of “intentional” (“σκόπιμη”) if needed. As one participant explains, “The Greek term for misinformation blurs distinctions, adding to the confusion”.
The fourth theme, Social Networks, examines their dual role as valuable information sources and platforms for the circulation of false information. Platforms like Twitter were praised for providing real-time updates, while others, such as Facebook, were criticized for clutter and unreliability. “Twitter provides curated expertise if you know who to follow”, noted an expert. However, it was pointed out by an expert that algorithm-driven visibility often amplifies misinformation, creating echo chambers (“Social media algorithms amplify misinformation, creating echo chambers”). The need to teach users how to navigate social media responsibly was also emphasized, with experts stating that “We need to teach people how to verify claims on social media”.
The final theme, Critical Thinking, emphasizes it as a vital skill for evaluating news and addressing biases. Participants acknowledged the challenges of achieving objectivity but stressed the importance of striving for critical evaluation. “We all bring biases to the table, but it’s important to strive for objectivity”, emphasized a focus group member. Truth was described as relative and shaped by perspectives, with one participant noting that “Truth depends on perspectives and cannot always be absolute”. Education was viewed as essential for fostering critical thinking, as highlighted by an expert who stated that “Critical thinking isn’t about perfection but improving how we evaluate information”.

5. Discussion This study sought to explore the complex challenges posed by false information in the media landscape, focusing on how individuals, both experts and non-experts, perceive these phenomena. Participants assessed the credibility of media sources and described their use of social networks. The findings also present strategies for enhancing media literacy and the role of tailored educational programs. Through thematic analysis, this study answered its guiding research questions by synthesizing perspectives from various people regarding their opinions, perspectives, and needs.
The thematic analysis conducted revealed five overarching themes that kept circulating among participants: Educational Needs, Media Trust, Intentionality, Critical Thinking, and Social Networks. Each of these main themes was subsequently divided into further sub-themes which highlighted specific types of issues within each overarching theme.
The research questions guiding this study were addressed through the thematic findings, providing a thorough and holistic understanding of the challenges posed by false information, as well as the strategies needed to tackle them. The first research question concerned the distinction between misinformation and disinformation as perceived by experts and non-experts. This research question was answered via the third theme, Intentionality. The second research question focused on the credibility of different media platforms and information sources, which was answered by the second theme, Media Trust. The third research question concerned the strategies to enhance critical thinking and media literacy among adults and was answered by the first theme, Educational Needs, and the fifth theme, Critical Thinking. The fourth research question concerned the ways in which social networks contribute to both the dissemination and mitigation of false information, answered mainly by the fourth theme, Social Networks, as well as issues in the second theme, Media Trust. Finally, the fifth research question concerned the role of tailored educational programs in addressing the challenges of the digital world. This was addressed by the first theme, Educational Needs. Each theme is discussed in detail below.
The first theme concerns Educational Needs. The findings highlight the critical role of media literacy education in equipping individuals with the skills necessary to navigate the complex digital information landscape. Participants emphasized the importance of workshops focused on practical skills, such as verifying sources, detecting misinformation, and analyzing media content. This aligns with the previous literature on the issue, which has shown the need for media literacy programs to address the growing prevalence of false information online (Bulger and Davison 2018; Wardle and Singerman 2021). Research supports that teaching individuals how to critically evaluate information is a cornerstone of fostering resilience against false narratives (Guess and Lyons 2020) and also that the perception of one’s media literacy skills positively correlates with their ability to discriminate real from false information (Chan et al. 2024). Additionally, within the existing study, fostering an understanding of how false information spreads and its societal impact was seen as essential for building awareness and proactive responses.
Tailored educational approaches for specific audiences emerged as a key recommendation by experts. Participants noted the importance of designing programs for parents, educators, and professionals to address their unique challenges and roles. Recent studies corroborate this finding, suggesting that audience-specific strategies enhance engagement and efficacy in media literacy interventions (Rasi et al. 2019, 2021). Parents require tools to guide children effectively in navigating online content and recognizing manipulative or harmful material, while educators need structured resources to integrate media literacy seamlessly into their teaching practices (Hobbs and Coiro 2019). Professionals, particularly in fields like journalism and policymaking, can benefit from advanced training focused on combating disinformation campaigns and ensuring ethical communication practices.
The integration of gamification and interactive learning methods was also highlighted by experts as a potential strategy to increase adult engagement in such media literacy programs and trainings. Gamification has been shown to enhance motivation, promote active participation, and improve learning outcomes, particularly in adult educational settings, as supported by Sailer’s seminal work on the issue (Sailer et al. 2017, 2021; Sailer and Homner 2020). Participants suggested incorporating real-life scenarios, such as analyzing viral instances of false information, into training modules to create immersive and practical learning experiences. This approach agrees with the literature in educational methods, which emphasizes the positive outcomes of experiential learning models on the basis of their effects on student motivation and through a constructivist approach lens (Kong 2021). These models directly connect theoretical knowledge to practical application, which is essential in a digital education setting as information users need to be able to apply any skills they acquire in real world settings to identify true from false information (Arvidsson and Kuhn 2021).
Moreover, participants stressed the importance of continuous and systematic educational programs rather than one-time trainings or workshops. Sustained learning opportunities, especially those supported by the state, including online courses and community-driven initiatives, were identified as critical for addressing the evolving nature of misinformation. This perspective aligns with recent findings emphasizing the need for adaptive and lifelong media literacy education (Smirnova et al. 2021), as well as the role of civic literacy (Lilja et al. 2024) and news literacy (Chan et al. 2024) in the distinction between real and false information.
The second theme that emerged from the data, Media Trust, revealed significant insights into participants’ perceptions of both traditional and online media. Participants expressed skepticism toward traditional media, citing concerns about political and financial biases that often influence content. This skepticism aligns with recent literature that highlights declining trust in traditional media due to perceived lack of transparency and accountability (Newman et al. 2021; Strömbäck et al. 2020), although these are still rated as more trustworthy than either partisan media outlets or fake news sources (Pennycook and Rand 2019b). Ideological biases in information credibility have been shown across the political spectrum, with people at the extremes attributing falsehoods to the opposite extreme. Left-wing proponents, in particular, attribute such falsehoods to intentional efforts to manipulate the public in a larger extent than right-wing people do (Hameleers and Brosius 2021). These effects, however, are not global: research shows that conservatives in the USA are stronger believers of news from mainstream media, while South Korean political orientation does not affect the consumption of news from mainstream media (Lee et al. 2024). Explanations of such approaches based on ideology are based on a lack of reasoning, rather than motivated reasoning, calling for media literacy training to become more urgent (Pennycook and Rand 2019b).
Familiarity emerged as a key factor in shaping media trust. All participants indicated one way or another that they were more likely to trust platforms and outlets they had followed for an extended period, even if or because they were aware of their inherent biases. This finding resonates with studies emphasizing the role of familiarity and long-term exposure in fostering trust, as audiences tend to develop a sense of predictability regarding the biases of familiar sources (Park et al. 2024). This was further extended to research showing that domestic media is trusted more than foreign media in the reporting of international disputes also in non-Western communities (Sui et al. 2024).
Another critical strategy that emerged for navigating media distrust was cross-verification. Participants reported actively cross-checking information across multiple sources to gain a balanced understanding. This approach aligns with recent recommendations advocating for media literacy programs to teach cross-verification techniques as a means of combating false information and fostering critical media engagement (Gilmour 2024), as well as source verification (Ecker et al. 2024), with media literacy programs increasingly shown to produce mitigation effects in false information detection (Sui et al. 2024).
While traditional media faced scrutiny, online platforms were viewed with a mix of appreciation and caution. Social media, in particular, was recognized for its ability to provide real-time updates but criticized for its susceptibility to false information and echo chambers. These findings underscore the complexity of media trust in the digital age, where audiences must navigate an information landscape characterized by both unprecedented access and heightened risks of manipulation. Such mistrust has been found to exist among users of platforms such as Facebook in various countries (UK, USA, Canada, and France), with high correlations between perceived misinformation and concerns about misinformation (Boulianne and Hoffmann 2024). However, in authoritative contexts, such as Russia, social platforms are often viewed as more reliable than mainstream media, with concepts such as informational learned helplessness arising within information users (Nisbet and Kamenchuk 2021).
The third theme which emerged from the thematic analysis, Intentionality, emphasized on the distinction between disinformation and misinformation in understanding participants’ perceptions of false information. Disinformation, characterized by its intentionality and agenda-driven nature, was widely regarded as more harmful than misinformation. This finding aligns with the recent literature that differentiates different types of false information (Molina et al. 2019) and underscores the systemic impact of disinformation campaigns, particularly in political and social contexts (Bennett and Livingston 2018).
Misinformation, on the other hand, was described as unintentional and often stemming from ignorance or miscommunication. Despite its lack of intent, participants noted that misinformation could still cause significant harm due to its rapid and widespread dissemination, particularly on social media platforms. This observation aligns with studies that highlight the role of cognitive biases and heuristics in the spread of misinformation (Ecker et al. 2022; Pennycook and Rand 2019a; Anastasiades et al. 2021).
Participants also discussed the challenges posed by cultural and especially linguistic limitations in distinguishing between disinformation and misinformation. For instance, the Greek terminology for these concepts (essentially having a common word for both) often conflates their meanings, adding to the difficulty of addressing them effectively. This highlights the need for localized and context-specific approaches in combating false information, as emphasized in recent cross-cultural studies on false information, although the same studies highlight the difficulty and resistance in changing opinions even when based on false beliefs (Ecker et al. 2022, 2023; Tay et al. 2023).
The fourth theme which emerged from the data, Social Networks, emphasized the dual role of social networks as both valuable information sources and platforms for false information. Participants acknowledged the utility of social media platforms like Twitter for accessing real-time updates and following credible experts. However, this utility was often tempered by concerns about the prevalence of misinformation, algorithm-driven visibility, and the formation of echo chambers. Algorithmic amplification of false information emerged as a key concern, with participants highlighting how platform algorithms prioritize engagement over accuracy, and emphasizing the need for more ethical algorithm designs. Recent studies support these observations, pointing to the role of algorithms in perpetuating filter bubbles and polarizing public discourse (Bakshy et al. 2015; Kitchens et al. 2020).
Participants also stressed the importance of digital literacy in navigating social networks responsibly. Teaching users how to critically evaluate content, recognize manipulative tactics and verify claims was seen as vital, reflecting the broader consensus on the importance of empowering users to become discerning consumers of digital content. This aligns with findings from recent media literacy initiatives that emphasize critical thinking and source evaluation as core components of digital literacy education (Ecker et al. 2024; Kahne and Bowyer 2017).
While participants valued the immediacy and accessibility of social networks, they also called for greater accountability from platform providers. Enhancing content moderation and promoting fact-checking collaborations were identified as potential solutions. These recommendations echo calls in the literature for a multi-stakeholder approach to combating misinformation on social media, involving platforms, policymakers, educators, and users (Lazer et al. 2018; Wardle and Derakhshan 2017).
The final theme which emerged, Critical Thinking, focused on its crucial role in tackling false information. Participants recognized critical thinking as an essential skill in the evaluation of information, discerning credible sources and understanding the broader context of news and media content. Participants also emphasized the importance of fostering awareness of cognitive biases and their influence on information processing. Cognitive biases, such as the confirmation bias or the availability heuristic, often lead individuals to favor information that aligns with their pre-existing beliefs, thereby reinforcing the spreading of false information. Recent studies support these observations, highlighting the pervasive impact of cognitive biases on the consumption and dissemination of false information (Díaz and Cova 2022; Lewandowsky 2019; Roozenbeek et al. 2022a, 2022b).
Truth was another concept frequently discussed in relation to critical thinking. Participants acknowledged the complexity of defining and pursuing truth in a media-saturated environment, where competing narratives often blur the lines between fact and opinion. This aligns with philosophical discussions on the relativism of truth and the need for individuals to navigate multiple perspectives critically (Harambam and Aupers 2015; Lewandowsky 2019).
Educational programs aimed at fostering critical thinking were identified as vital tools for addressing false information. Participants highlighted the importance of integrating critical thinking skills into media literacy curricula to empower individuals. Recent educational frameworks have supported this approach, emphasizing the role of critical inquiry in building resilience against misinformation (Ariawan and Malang 2020; Gilmour 2024). Moreover, participants advocated for practical and experiential learning opportunities to develop critical thinking skills. Using real-world examples, such as analyzing controversial news stories or debunking viral false information, can provide learners with hands-on experience in applying critical thinking to media analysis. This approach aligns with pedagogical research that underscores the effectiveness of experiential learning in fostering deeper understanding and skill retention (Kong 2021).
5.1. Setting the Scene: The Greek-Speaking Information Landscape The current study focuses on the analysis of the information landscape in Cyprus, a Greek-speaking country. This study is unique in its nature as it is the first effort to approach and understand the challenges, needs, and perceptions of information users in this part of the world. Greece and Cyprus, two countries at the south-eastern-most corner of Europe, present a unique set of challenges shaped by cultural, linguistic, and geopolitical factors. The information landscape in both countries appears to share similar characteristics concerning education and policies. Additionally, it seems that experts and non-experts have experiences similar to other countries as reported in the literature, but at the same time, they present with some unique features and combinations that need to be taken into account when designing policies and making recommendations.
In Greece, misinformation exploits political polarization, economic instability, and public distrust in institutions. This distrust is mirrored in surveys like the Digital Economy and Society Index (DESI), where Greece consistently ranks low in digital literacy, leaving its population vulnerable to online falsehoods (European Commission 2024). Common narratives include anti-vaccine rhetoric, conspiracy theories tied to global events, and politically charged disinformation aimed at discrediting opponents. Cyprus, on the other hand, contends with a fragmented media ecosystem influenced by the ongoing division between the Republic of Cyprus and the Turkish-controlled north. This division not only affects the media’s role, but also amplifies false information through narratives that exploit intercommunal tensions. Social media is a dominant source of information, yet the population’s digital literacy remains insufficient to critically engage with content (Christophorou and Karides 2022; Dutta and Lanvin 2024; European Commission 2024; Giomelakis et al. 2024).
Mainstream media have faced significant challenges in both countries in the last decade. In Greece, their quality and influence has deteriorated, especially since 2010 and the beginning of the economic crisis (Sitistas 2020). In Cyprus, the media were also significantly affected by the economic crisis, particularly during the 2012–2013 financial turmoil (Papasolomou et al. 2016). Many printed media and some TV stations went bankrupt, while others (including digital media) had to adapt to the new economic conditions by laying off experienced journalists and hiring inexperienced ones in their place, who were tasked to produce as many articles as possible, either by translating foreign tabloids or by quickly copying content from others. Financial constraints increased the reliance of some media outlets on powerful corporate or political entities for funding. This raised concerns about editorial independence and increased the risk of biased reporting.
Media literacy has not been a central focus in the formal education system, leading to limited critical thinking about media consumption among the general population. Media literacy is not yet a standalone subject in either Greek or Cypriot schools. Although various approaches, including media, digital, film, and news literacy, have been employed to enhance students’ critical thinking and digital skills, these efforts are few and far between (Saklaki and Gardikiotis 2024; Theodosiadou et al. 2021). On top of those, in both Greece and Cyprus, the lack of specialized training for educators poses difficulties in effectively teaching media literacy. In Cyprus, while initiatives exist, there is a need for a more systematic and comprehensive approach to embed media literacy across all educational levels. Furthermore, Cyprus’ small media ecosystem, with close ties to political and economic entities, creates challenges in fostering an independent, media-literate society (Petranová et al. 2017).
Both countries share similar misinformation narratives, due to sharing a common cultural and religious background. Greece and Cyprus have been targeted by significant disinformation campaigns, particularly related to political issues, migration, armed conflicts (e.g., Ukraine), EU-related policies, and public health (e.g., COVID-19) (Christophorou and Karides 2022). Greece and especially Cyprus are vulnerable to Russian disinformation campaigns due to their historical and cultural ties with Russia, shared Orthodox Christian heritage, and longstanding perceptions of Russia as a protector of Orthodox nations. Geopolitical factors, such as tensions with Turkey and the region’s importance in energy politics, make them strategic targets for influence campaigns. Additionally, economic crises in both countries have fueled skepticism toward the EU, creating fertile ground for Russian narratives that criticize Western institutions. A significant Russian-speaking diaspora and economic ties, especially in Cyprus, further amplify Moscow’s reach. Weak media literacy and the proliferation of misinformation on social media exacerbate these vulnerabilities, allowing Russian disinformation to exploit existing divisions, erode trust in democratic institutions, and weaken EU and NATO unity. Additionally, the Greek–Turkish issue in Cyprus is a longstanding conflict rooted in the island’s history, ethnic divisions, and political disputes and it adds more complexity to accessing, evaluating, and tackling misinformation.
Efforts to combat false information in both countries are varied. In Greece, initiatives include alignment with EU directives like the Code of Practice on Disinformation and collaborations with platforms such as Facebook and Google. Fact-checking organizations like Ellinika Hoaxes, AFP Fact Check Greece, and Greece Fact Check play an active role, but their impact is constrained by public skepticism and accusations of bias. Similarly, Cyprus has recently established the Centre against Misinformation Fact Check Cyprus (FCC), under the Social Computing Research Center of the Cyprus University of Technology, FCC is a signatory of the International Fact-Checking Network’s (IFCN) Code of Principles and is an active member of the European Digital Media Observatory (EDMO), as well as of MedDMO, regular contributor of fact-checks. However, these efforts are hindered by the lack of a robust national strategy and low trust in media institutions.
Educational and policy interventions remain critical for addressing these challenges. Media literacy programs tailored to local needs can empower citizens to critically evaluate information and reduce their susceptibility to false information (Theodosiadou et al. 2021). Moreover, fostering collaboration between Greek and Cypriot fact-checking organizations could enhance the regional fight against false information. Integrating media literacy into formal education systems and leveraging EU funding for digital skills development are also promising pathways for improvement. The recent literature emphasizes the importance of such interventions in creating informed, resilient societies (Bennett and Livingston 2018; Wardle and Derakhshan 2017).
Additionally, in Cyprus, community media has repeatedly been suggested to play a decisive role in bringing the two communities together (Carpentier and Doudaki 2013). A bicommunal fact-checking effort could play a pivotal role in countering divisive narratives, building trust between Greek and Turkish Cypriots, and promoting media literacy. By addressing false information that exploits ethnic tensions and fostering collaboration, such an initiative could provide a neutral platform for tackling contentious issues with evidence and transparency. Joint efforts would also strengthen resistance to external disinformation campaigns while encouraging dialogue and critical thinking. Challenges like trust, funding, and political sensitivities can be mitigated through transparency, inclusive materials in Greek, Turkish, and English, and partnerships with local and international organizations.
5.2. Limitations and Future Directions While this study provides valuable insights into the challenges posed by misinformation and disinformation in Greece and Cyprus, several limitations must be acknowledged. First, the sample size was small and this was a qualitative study, which limits the generalizability of the findings to broader populations. This study primarily relied on qualitative data, which, while rich in detail, does not allow for statistical generalization. Additionally, this study focused on self-reported perceptions and experiences, which are inherently subjective and may be influenced by personal biases. Another limitation is the linguistic and cultural specificity of the study; misinformation and disinformation manifest differently across regions, and findings may not be directly transferable to non-Greek-speaking contexts. Finally, while efforts were made to ensure a balanced representation of participants, certain demographic groups may have been underrepresented, potentially limiting the scope of perspectives captured in the study.
Building upon the insights gained from this study, several key areas for future research and intervention emerge. First, collecting data from a larger sample size and employing mixed-method approaches—including quantitative surveys—could enhance the representativeness and generalizability of findings. This would allow for a broader understanding of public perceptions of misinformation and disinformation across different demographics. Additionally, comparative studies between Greece, Cyprus, and other European nations could highlight region-specific vulnerabilities and best practices for combating false information. Given the geopolitical complexities in the area, research that examines the interplay between false information, political ideologies, and historical narratives could be particularly insightful. Finally, greater collaboration between researchers, policymakers, and technology companies is needed to develop holistic strategies for combating misinformation. Future studies should explore ways to bridge the gap between research and policy implementation, ensuring that findings translate into actionable measures that enhance public resilience against false information.
6. Conclusions In conclusion, the research questions were comprehensively addressed through thematic analysis, offering insights into the distinctions between misinformation and disinformation, strategies for assessing credibility, and the dual role of social networks. This study also highlighted the pivotal role of tailored educational programs and critical thinking in fostering media literacy and combating false information. These findings seem to reflect the particularities of the Greek-speaking information landscape, with its unique challenges as part of the EU and also at the outskirts of it. The findings have significant practical implications, particularly for educators, policymakers, and fact-checking organizations. Strengthening digital literacy programs, integrating media literacy into formal education, and fostering collaboration between institutions can help equip individuals with the skills to critically engage with media content. Additionally, policymakers should prioritize transparent communication strategies and regulatory measures to address algorithmic amplification of false information. From an academic perspective, this study contributes to ongoing discussions in media literacy, information trust and digital communication. These discussions can significantly contribute to a more thorough understanding of the ways individuals perceive false information and to the broader discourse on empowering individuals and communities to effectively navigate the complexities of the digital information landscape.
