Introduction
Eyewitness memory has been a topic of intensive study within psychology (Semmler and Brewer 2010; Steblay 2015), with increasing recognition that memory for criminal events may be malleable (see Frenda et al. 2011) and altered according to emotion-based variables (Yuille and Tollestrup 2014). In particular, extensive research since the 1970s has focused on the extent to which misleading information can distort subsequent recall (see Loftus 2005). Empirical evidence suggests that certain factors (e.g., deficits in emotional processing or the emotionality of a scene) may increase an individual’s susceptibility to misinformation and result in memory impairments (Zhu et al. 2010). Given that eyewitness testimony is heavily relied upon in the judicial system and often assumed to be accurate reproductions of experience (e.g., Forgas et al. 2005; Yarmey 2010), the present study was designed to examine the extent to which scene emotionality (i.e., positive or negative valence) and emotional deficits present in psychopathy influence the misinformation effect.
The Misinformation Effect and Emotion
Exposure to misinformation is a reliable method of distorting memory for a stimulus or event (Loftus 2005). By definition, the misinformation effect is when exposure to inaccurate information, following a witnessed event, decreases overall memory accuracy for that event (e.g., Campbell et al. 2007). The most common form of presentation of misinformation is through the use of leading questions, which generally result in false recollections of suggested details that were not present in the original scene/event (e.g., Ibabe and Sporer 2004; Porter et al. 2010). Recent studies have turned their attention to examining misinformation in the context of scenes/events of differing emotional valence (e.g., Porter et al. 2010; Van Damme and Smets 2014). While it is generally understood that emotional stimuli tend to be recalled more accurately then neutral stimuli (e.g., Christianson 1992), memory for emotional events also may be subject to distortion under various conditions (i.e., exposure to misinformation; Porter et al. 2003). Extant research has demonstrated that central details of emotional images tend to be well retained in memory, whereas peripheral details are often overlooked and recall may be more malleable (e.g., Christianson et al. 1996; Scheer 2002). Based on Easterbrook’s cue-utilization hypothesis (1959), one explanation for these findings is the idea that attentional narrowing may occur when individuals are exposed to arousing emotional stimuli (e.g., Kensinger et al. 2006; Safer et al. 1998). As a result, participants focus on the “emotional” (and subsequently central) parts of a scene enhancing memory for such details, and pay much less attention to “non-emotional” (hence peripheral) parts of a scene resulting in impaired memory for those details (also see Van Damme and Smets 2014, for a discussion of this perspective). Various studies have supported attentional narrowing, reporting that both attention and subsequent memories are focused primarily on central, arousing details compared to neutral details (e.g., Arnell et al. 2007; Safer et al. 1998). In addition, there appears to be a particular emotional memory bias associated with negative stimuli independent of arousal levels (e.g., Waring and Kensinger 2009). These (and other) studies have demonstrated that individuals often are more susceptible to misleading information regarding peripheral details of an emotional scene/event relative to central details. That said, other scholars have argued that how we define the centrality of an event can alter our findings concerning the misinformation effect (see Mahé et al. 2015), and that some studies find comparable levels of incorporation of misinformation across both central and peripheral details (e.g., Luna and Migueles 2009).
As a result, there is a lack of consensus in the current literature as to whether emotional stimuli (including emotion type) will enhance, or hinder, the misinformation effect (i.e., do positive or negative scenes result in more memory malleability?). In a recent study, Porter et al. (2010) found that negative scenes were more susceptible to misinformation effects and altered recall. Similarly, Van Damme and Smets (2014) reported that negative scenes were susceptible to incorporation of misinformation for both central and peripheral details when arousal was high. That said, other studies have found that positive and negative scenes are well-remembered and less susceptible to change or misleading suggestions over time relative to neutral events (Brown and Schaefer 2010). These conflicting results may arise from methodological issues (i.e., measuring frequency relative to accuracy, how misinformation is calculated, varying levels of arousal associated with stimuli; Berntsen 2002; Van Damme and Smets 2014), failure to differentiate between central and peripheral details of events/scenes (e.g., Brown and Schaefer 2010; Porter et al. 2010), and a lack of consideration of how individual differences influence susceptibility to misinformation (e.g., Zhu et al. 2010). Our aim in this study was to address scene emotionality foremost, as well as personality factors that influence emotional processing. Specifically, we focused on psychopathic traits as reflecting the epitome of emotional dysfunction.
Psychopathic Traits, Emotion, and Memory
One of the hallmark features of the psychopathic personality is deviant processing of emotional information (e.g., Hoff et al. 2009; Müller et al. 2003). For example, multiple studies have found that psychopaths display deficits in processing of emotional versus non-emotional information (i.e., words and images) when compared to non-psychopaths (e.g., Levenston et al. 2000; Lorenz and Newman 2002; Sutton et al. 2002; Williamson et al. 1991). In some studies, the behavioural or physiological responses to emotional stimuli for psychopaths are associated with impairment (e.g., Prado et al. 2015) or enhancement dependent upon attentional resources (e.g., Anderson et al. in press; Baskin-Sommers et al. 2013). These findings suggest that affective information may not have the same emotional significance for individuals high in psychopathic traits relative to non-psychopathic individuals (Williamson et al. 1991). That said, previous research also has utilized different types of emotional stimuli (including type of stimuli and how evocative they may be) and tasks, which limit the extent to which we are able to draw clear conclusions concerning the relationship between emotion processing and psychopathy. For example, Wilson et al. (2008) reported better recognition memory in a group of males high in psychopathic traits for negative facial stimuli relative to positive stimuli. Glass and Newman (2009) found that psychopaths recalled more emotional relative to neutral words, but were impaired at recalling the context (peripheral information) of emotional words. Collectively, the literature suggests that psychopathic traits influence emotion processing in some way (whether impairment or enhancement depending on the stimuli and the task), which corresponds to variations in memory for emotional stimuli across psychopath and non-psychopath groups.
According to the attentional narrowing view previously discussed (e.g., Arnell et al. 2007), individuals high in psychopathic traits should experience less processing interference when confronted with emotional stimuli relative to normal individuals (Blair and Mitchell 2009). Christianson et al. (1996) directly tested this idea and reported that emotion impaired memory in non-psychopaths, but had little effect on psychopaths. Specifically, prisoners were categorized as either psychopathic or not (based on PCL-R scores), exposed to slides of emotional or non-emotional images for a very brief duration, and later tested on their recall of central and peripheral details. All participants recalled central and peripheral details of neutral scenes in a similar manner. However, when shown an emotional scene, non-psychopaths recalled central details more accurately than peripheral details, whereas psychopaths recalled central and peripheral details equally well (Christianson et al. 1996). These findings indicate that the attentional narrowing effect that occurs in non-psychopathic individuals when exposed to emotional images may not occur in persons high in psychopathic traits. Due to their emotional dysfunction, this group may not be distracted by emotional content displayed in an image (see Sadeh and Verona 2008), allowing their attention to focus on both central and peripheral details in emotional scenes. As a result, their recall accuracy for peripheral details was not impaired by emotion (e.g., Christianson et al. 1996), although these authors did not examine different valences of emotional images.
While it appears that psychopathic traits influence memory, extant research has not addressed the misinformation effect in this context. Zhu and colleagues (2010) reported that individuals with characteristics reflective of psychopathy (although not directly assessed) were more susceptible to false recall due to the presence of misinformation. Previous studies suggest that if persons high in psychopathic traits demonstrate an emotional memory bias and impaired peripheral recollection (see Glass and Newman 2009; Zhu et al. 2010), then they may be susceptible to misinformation similar to (or more so than) persons low in these traits. If, however, psychopathic traits are related to equivalent or unimpaired recall of central and peripheral details (see Christianson et al. 1996), individuals possessing psychopathic traits should be less influenced by the presentation of misleading information (as they would be equally attending to both central and peripheral information in a scene).
The Current Study
The emotional nature of stimuli, psychopathic traits, and misinformation are important factors to evaluate with respect to recall of eyewitness events. In particular, eyewitnesses may have varying levels of psychopathic traits, which could influence their attention to, and processing of, an emotional eyewitness event. Having a better understanding of how psychopathic traits influence recall and susceptibility to misinformation could help police evaluate the accuracy and reliability of eyewitness memory (e.g., Yuille and Tollestrup 2014). Using a basic misinformation paradigm, we sought to determine the individual and collective influence of these variables, and investigate how psychopathic personality traits may moderate memory processes for emotional events. Based on comparable extant research (e.g., Christianson et al. 1996; Porter et al. 2003, 2010; Van Damme and Smets 2014), we predicted the following: (1) emotion would enhance memory for central details and result in poorer memory for peripheral details (overall), and this effect should be especially evidenced for the negative emotional scene; (2) participants should be more susceptible to misinformation in the emotional relative to neutral conditions; (3) individuals high in psychopathic traits would show little difference in their recall of central and peripheral details for emotional scenes; and (4) individuals high in psychopathic traits would be less likely to recall misleading information relative to the low psychopathic trait group in all images (positive, negative, or neutral). We employed a 3 (emotional valence: negative, neutral, positive) X 2 (psychopathic traits: high, low) X 2 (misinformation condition: misleading, nonleading) mixed multivariate design to test these hypotheses.
Method
Participants
Undergraduate students ( N = 334) participated in this online study to receive course credit. The sample consisted of 29.3% ( n = 98) males and 70.7% ( n = 236) females, with a mean age of 20.7 years ( SD = 4.78; range = 17-55). All participants provided informed consent, and the study was conducted in accordance with ethical guidelines.
Materials
Demographic Questionnaire
Participants were asked to complete a 10-item measure containing questions about basic demographic information (e.g., age, gender, ethnicity, year of study), as well as YES/NO questions pertaining to their personal history and memory abilities.
Image Stimuli
Three images of varying emotional valence (positive, negative, neutral) were selected from the International Affective Picture System (IAPS; Lang et al. 1999, 2008). The IAPS (Lang et al. 1993, 1999; Lang and Ohman 1988) is a standardized database of over 900 emotional images, and stimuli for this study were selected on the basis of comparable arousal levels ( M = 5.40, SD = .52) for the emotional images,1 but different valence (emotionality) ratings (positive, neutral, and negative). Each image is associated with normative ratings across age and cultural groups (Lang et al. 2008). The positive image depicted a group of teenagers laughing and having fun on a beach (the beach party image 8461). The neutral image contained several people picking strawberries (the strawberry patch image 2515). Finally, the negative image displayed several onlookers staring at a man lying on the ground surrounded by blood (the bleeding man image 9433). Central and peripheral details for each image where determined by through a pilot study where 50 participants (not involved in the current study) were shown each of the photos (in randomized order) and asked to circle on the images what area(s) they considered the most emotional or what attracted their attention the most. Details selected for each image were classified as central (i.e., within the areas of focus) or peripheral (i.e., outside of the circled areas) based on 90% or greater agreement across all participants, and then utilized for memory accuracy scoring (see below). Similar image stimuli have been used in a variety of studies on memory and misinformation (e.g., Christianson et al. 1996; Porter et al. 2003, 2010; Van Damme and Smets 2014).
Psychopathic Personality Inventory – Revised (PPI-R)
The PPI-R (Lilienfeld and Widows 2005) is a 154-item self-report measure of personality traits associated with psychopathy. The items assess traits across 8 clinical dimensions and 3 validity scales,2 and are rated on a 4-point Likert scale from 1 to 4. Participants were grouped according to high (T-score > 65; M = 70.64, SD = 4.59; n = 178) and low (T-score < 64; M = 59.14, SD = 4.14; n = 156) levels of psychopathic traits according to recommended scoring guidelines (Lilienfeld and Widows 2005).3 These groupings differed significantly from each other, t (332) = 23.89, p < .001. The PPI-R is a promising self-report measure of psychopathic traits, and has good convergent, discriminant, and external validity (e.g., Uzieblo et al. 2010). Further, the PPI-R has been used in much research to gain insight into psychopathic traits in varying populations (e.g., Benning et al. 2005; Miller and Rosenfeld 2004; Ray and Jones 2012; Wilson et al. 2008).
Question sets
The question sets used were based on previous research (Porter et al. 2010; Porter et al. 2003), and designed to test accuracy for details presented in each of the three images. Depending on their condition, the participants either received 10 misleading, or non-leading, questions for each image following the first filler task. The misleading questions range from changing small details in the image (e.g., a hat) to changing a major detail (e.g., a car). In the misleading questionnaire, there were five misleading questions (including one concerning a dramatic misleading detail), and five non-leading questions. Question sets were composed of four central detail questions, five peripheral detail questions (one of which is the major misleading detail), and one general open-ended question. The non-misleading questions were similar in both format and content, without the presence of misinformation. The second question set contained 15 open-ended questions per image that assessed memory accuracy and level of distortion. Regardless of misinformation condition, the same open-ended question sets were given to each participant. For the negative (bleeding man) image, we used identical questions to Porter et al. (2010), and devised a template based on the content of the questions used in this previous study. The template was then utilized to create question sets for the neutral (strawberry patch) and positive (beach party) scenes in relation to the types of details (central/peripheral), size of details (small/medium/large; including a major misleading detail), question format (open-ended/forced-choice), and question type (misleading/nonleading).
Coding Procedure
Two individuals blind to the participants’ condition coded the open-ended questions for memory accuracy and incorporation of misinformation.4 Responses to each question were coded as either accurate (1 point) or inaccurate (0 points), and values for questions were summed to generate total, as well as central and peripheral, accuracy scores. Answers were considered inaccurate if participants provided misinformation or a response different from the correct answer. We also coded responses according to whether suggested misinformation was incorporated (1 point) or not (0 points) in order to address the difference between inaccuracies arising from forgetting versus distortion. These were then summed to determine the frequency of incorporation of misinformation for each participant across the 5 questions containing misleading details, and a percentage was generated (i.e., the misinformation rate).
Procedure
This study was conducted online and utilized the psychology undergraduate research pool to obtain participants. Participants logged into the online research system, and after providing their informed consent, they completed the brief demographic questionnaire, and were randomly assigned into a condition within the 3 (emotional valence; within-subjects) X 2 (misleading question type; between- subjects) mixed factorial design. Depending on the condition they were assigned, participants were exposed (via computer) to three picture stimuli for 45 seconds each (positive, neutral and negative; presentation counterbalanced), with no spacing in between the images. Participants were instructed that images would be presented back to back, and to look at the image for the entire time it was presented. They were not directly informed there would be a memory recall test regarding these images. Following initial exposure, participants completed the PPI-R as the first filler task for a duration of approximately 20 minutes. Participants then completed the first question set (10 questions) that contained either misleading (including one dramatic false detail) or non-leading information. The second filler task involved completing a series of unrelated “personality” questionnaires for approximately 20 minutes. Finally, participants were given the second question set, which contained 15 open-ended questions per image. All participants were then queried about where they focused on the image and whether they were aware the questions asked were misleading (manipulation check), and then debriefed as to the full nature of the experiment. Responses to the second question set were later coded to assess the accuracy of the participants’ memories for the picture details (overall, central, peripheral), as well as whether any misinformation was incorporated into memory and the rate at which this misinformation was incorporated.
Results
Emotional Valence & Misinformation
In order to determine the effect of emotional valence (positive, negative, neutral) and misinformation condition (misled or not misled) on our dependent variables of interest (i.e., overall accuracy, accuracy for central and peripheral details, and rate of misinformation inclusion), a multivariate analysis of variance (MANOVA) was conducted.5 The analysis yielded a main effect of valence, Wilks’ lambda (λ) = .38, F (8,319) = 65.06, p < .001, η 2 = .62. Univariate follow-up analyses indicated that overall accuracy ( F (2,656) = 28.75, p < .001, η 2 = .08), as well as accuracy for central ( F (2,656) = 75.54, p < .001, η 2 = .19) and peripheral ( F (2,656) = 33.45, p < .001, η 2 = .09) details varied according to emotion (see Table 1 for means and standard deviations). Specifically, overall accuracy was higher for the positive ( M = 60.00%; SD = 19.80) and negative ( M = 59.86%; SD = 17.67) images relative to the neutral image ( M = 53.69%; SD = 18.02). However, negative images were associated with the best recall of peripheral details and the poorest recall of central details relative to both positive and neutral images. On the other hand, positive images were associated with higher recall of central details and lower recall of peripheral details (see Figure 1). Comparison of emotional (bleeding man + beach party) versus non-emotional (strawberry patch) recall rates indicated that accuracy was higher for emotional ( M = 59.93%; SD = 16.69) relative to non-emotional images ( M = 53.69%; SD = 18.02; λ = .85, F (1,328) = 56.97, p < .001, η 2 = .15). In order to further evaluate incorporation of misinformation, we conducted a mixed 3 (emotion) X 2 (misinformation condition) ANOVA using the rate of incorporating the major misleading detail as comparable with previous studies (i.e., Porter et al. 2010). This analysis revealed a main effect of emotion, F (2,329) = 21.09, p < .001, η 2 = .11, and misinformation, F (1,330) = 137.00, p < .001, η 2 = .29, but no significant emotion X misinformation interaction ( p > .05). Specifically, participants in the misled condition ( M = .47, SE = .02) falsely recalled the major misleading detail more often than those in the nonled condition ( M = .09, SE = .02). Further, the negative scene ( M = .36, SE = .02) was associated with the highest rate of false recall of the major misleading detail, and the positive scene had the lowest rate of false recall ( M = .19, SE = .02). Chi-squared analysis indicated that when participants were misled, the major misinformation detail was incorporated more into negative scenes (57.2%) compared to neutral (46.1%) and positive (35.9%) scenes, χ 2 (1,166) = 13.23, p < .001.
Psychopathic Traits, Emotion, and Misinformation
Mean levels of psychopathic traits varied across high ( M = 397.94, SD = 16.34) and low ( M = 360.11, SD = 13.98) groups, F (1,332) = 509.45, p < .001. There were approximately equal percentages of males (~30%; overall M = 379.10, SD = 24.9) and females (~70%; overall M = 380.75, SD = 24.07) across high and low trait groupings, and these did not significantly differ in gender composition or score (χ 2 = .18, p > .05). A series of repeated measures ANOVAs were conducted on all dependent variables (total, central, peripheral accuracy and misinformation rate) using misinformation condition and psychopathy as between-subjects variables and emotional valence as the within-subjects factor. This analysis revealed that psychopathic traits were unrelated to total, central and peripheral accuracy (all p s > .05). Participants either low or high in psychopathic traits showed little difference between recall of central and peripheral details. However, there was a trend towards significance for the misinformation rate ( F (1,324) = 3.24, p = .07, η 2 = .01). Specifically, those high in psychopathic traits ( M = 29.27%, SE = 1.37) tended to incorporate more misinformation into their memory for images (independent of valence) relative to individuals low in psychopathic traits ( M = 25.69%, SE = 1.45). Bivariate correlations between overall total plus factor scores on the PPI-R and our variables of interest (accuracy and misinformation rates for each image) failed to yield any significant associations.
Discussion
The purpose of the present study was to examine the impact of emotional valence, levels of psychopathic traits, and the presentation of misinformation on recall of eyewitness events. It was predicted that emotional valence of the images would impact both accuracy and misinformation. Overall, we found both emotional images were associated with more accurate recall, relative to the neutral image. However, contrary to previous research, negative images were not associated with enhanced recall of central relative to peripheral details (see Berntsen 2002; Christianson et al. 1996). In fact, accuracy for central details was highest in positive image, and recall of peripheral details was most accurate in negative image (for similar findings, see Paz-Alonso and Goodman 2008). One interpretation of our finding was that participants were avoiding the emotional central details of the negative image due to its content (i.e., dead body, blood, dogs licking up blood). Previous research has shown that strong negative emotions can be evoked upon exposure to graphic or gruesome images (e.g., Douglas et al. 1997), and that mental processing may be biased in a manner that minimizes negative aspects of memory (e.g., Hoffman 1986; Isen et al. 1978). In our study, it is possible that biased processing occurred in the negative emotional scene due to its’ graphic/gory nature, such that participants paid more attention to peripheral (non-gruesome) details. Although we used the same stimuli as in Porter et al. (2010), the graphic nature of the image may have influenced memory in the aforementioned ways making our results inconsistent with similar studies. Future research should parse out the influence of gore within negatively emotional scenes to investigate whether differential recall of details is evident. In line with this explanation, participants seemed to focus less on the highly emotional elements of the negative scene (i.e., the dead body and the pools of blood), but more on those in the positive scene (i.e., smiling/laughing faces). Previous research has supported the idea that emotional intensity affects memory more so than emotional valence, and that high intensity leads to better memory for emotion-eliciting details (e.g., Talarico et al. 2004; Walker et al. 1997). Although we did not assess the perceived emotional intensity of the images for participants, we did ask our participants where they focused in the scenes.6 Their responses reflected avoidance of the bleeding man (e.g., “I found the blood disturbing so I didn’t look at it) and focus on the happy beachgoers (e.g., “I was drawn to their smiling faces”).
Future studies should assess the relation between emotional intensity and valence according to participants’ perceptions (not just normative intensity data provided with image sets), and ensure that stimuli are evoking the intended emotions in participants. In fact, some scholars suggest that the experience of emotion in response to stimuli is much more complex than simple categorizations of “positive” or “negative” (Barrett 2006). For example, it is possible that for some participants, the positive beach image may have evoked more negative sentiments (such as jealousy or sadness) depending on the individual’s life circumstances. As such, individual interpretations and even physiological experiences of emotion may be important to evaluate in additional studies. A further limitation was that although valence (positive/negative/neutral) differed across images, so did the image details. It is possible that certain types of details are more or less memorable (as well as contextual factors altering recall; see Mahé et al. 2015; Van Damme and Smets 2014). Stimuli that are identical other than varying in the core emotional manipulations would be ideal for this line of research, although difficult to generate. Participants in this study were shown images of emotional events, which differ greatly from real-life experiences (Takarangi et al. 2006). Evidence from studies of autobiographical memory indicates more frequent recall of central details, although the accuracy of such details is often unknown (e.g., Berntsen 2002). Finally, our undergraduate and primarily female sample reflects “psychology students” but may not be generalizable to the widespread community. That said, research has indicated that at least 20-40% of students experience some type of victimization while in school (Agnich et al. 2014), thus students as eyewitnesses is possible. While our results have important implications, they should be replicated in community samples of witnesses to different emotional events.
In relation to misinformation, we predicted that individuals would be more susceptible to misinformation in the emotional relative to neutral conditions. Our findings did not support this hypothesis: emotional and non-emotional images were associated with similar rates of misinformation incorporation (around 40%). Although contradictory to previous studies (e.g., Porter et al. 2010), differential coding procedures help to explain this discrepancy. All levels of misinformation (both major and minor) were coded for and included in our analyses to create one misinformation score, while Porter et al. (2010) only coded for accuracy, and whether the major misinformation detail was present. Our results bring up an important point about memory assessment in these contexts: impaired accuracy should not be considered synonymous with incorporation of misinformation (also see Van Damme and Smets 2014). Accuracy can be impaired by omission or commission of detail, but that doesn’t mean that misinformation suggested to participants was incorporated. We believe this distinction is important in the interpretation of results across misinformation studies. In fact, when the major misinformation detail was analyzed separately, our findings were consistent with those of Porter et al. (2010), in which participants were more likely to incorporate the major misleading detail in their memory for the negative emotional scene. This suggests that there is something distinctive about the major misinformation detail, and a retrospective view produced a potential explanation for this finding. All of the misleading questions contained details that were plausible; however, in the major misleading question, the details tended to be overall more schematic , particularly in the negative image. For example, one of the minor misleading questions in the negative image asked “Was the hat in front of the man on the ground a ball cap or a toque?”, while the major misleading question in the negative image asked “Was the car on the far right blue or black?” Greater incorporation of the suggested car fits with our mental schema that a bloody dead body on a road (depicted in the scene) was likely hit by a vehicle. Crockett (1988) explains how emotion and schemas can be connected to an individual’s interpretation, and subsequent memory, of an event. “When a particular emotion is activated . . . schemas and other cognitive materials that are tagged with that emotion will be primed for both the identification of mood-congruent stimulus material and for the recall of congruent material from memory. In other words, affect influences the schemas people apply to interpret events” (p. 46-47).

That said, our results demonstrated an overall misinformation effect. Interestingly, the presence of misleading information diminished accuracy for peripheral details in positive images and central details in negative images (consistent with attentional reports indicated above). These results lend further support to our gore-avoidance interpretation. Similar to our findings, Paz-Alonso and Goodman (2008) found that participants were more susceptible to misinformation in central relative to peripheral details of a negative event (i.e., murder), and that malleability in memory was increased due to a lack of attention. Nevertheless, the majority of misleading questions in this and other research (e.g., Porter et al. 2010) tend to focus more on peripheral aspects of the scene, possibly leading to outcomes that result from question bias rather than true misinformation-emotion effects (Van Damme and Smets 2014).
The second major purpose of the present study was to examine the influence of psychopathic traits on accuracy and misinformation. We predicted that persons high in psychopathic traits would not recall central and peripheral details differently in emotional images, and that they would incorporate less misinformation due to a lack of emotion-focused attentional narrowing (i.e., with undifferentiated viewing of a scene, they should be encoding all details equally well or equally poorly). Our results did not support these predictions: psychopathic traits were unrelated to recall accuracy across all our dependent measures. These findings suggest that psychopathic traits may not impair memory for emotional stimuli. While this contradicts existing research (e.g., Christianson et al. 1996), it is possible that psychopathic traits are more relevant to attentional factors (see Glass and Newman 2009). Recent studies have argued that impairments on emotional tasks likely result from attentional deficits and not from amygdala-mediated emotional dysregulation (Newman et al. 2010). In support of this, we found that individuals high in psychopathic traits incorporated more misinformation into memory, independent of emotional valence. It is possible that psychopathy influenced overall levels of attention, such that psychopaths were focused less on particular details in the scene (as evidenced by the lack of differences between central and peripheral recall), making them more likely to falsely recall misinformation (e.g, Tousignant et al. 1986). According to the response modulation hypothesis, the emotional processing deficit in psychopathy reflects limitations in the distribution of attention, such that processing of emotional stimuli remains intact but attentional responses to emotional information are impaired (Glass and Newman 2009). Even though all participants were instructed to look at the images for the entire time presented, we were unable to control the extent to which they actually focused their attention on the images given the online testing venue. Further, Baskin-Sommers et al. (2011, 2013) suggested that psychopaths experience an attentional bottleneck when confronted with emotional stimuli, such that initial processing of emotion cues vary according to whether they are considered goal relevant (or central to their focus) versus goal irrelevant (or peripheral to their goal). Blair et al. (1995) also argued that psychopathic traits moderate how individuals attribute emotion. Based on these ideas, the definition of what details are considered “central” or “peripheral” in an emotional image may differ for psychopaths relative to non-psychopaths. Thus in relation to our study, those high in psychopathic traits may define central according to the instructional goals of the task (i.e., please view each image for the duration of the time it appears on screen) and everything else as peripheral. As a result, the entire image may have been processed more in a holistic rather than detail-oriented fashion (e.g., see Hoppenbrouwers et al. 2015). In addition, other characteristics of the psychopathic personality (i.e., lack of remorse, and need for stimulation/proneness to boredom; Hare 1996, 2003) might be associated with indifference for effortful evaluation of the scenes. The low-arousal theory of psychopathy suggests that psychopaths are in a constant state of stimulation/sensation seeking (Vien and Beech 2006). In our study, it is possible that this need for stimulation led to distractibility, decreasing their attention to the images, which subsequently increased their susceptibility to misinformation. Recent studies also are beginning to focus on how the difference facets of psychopathy may be related to processing different types of emotional stimuli, and suggest that negative reactivity and life context may be relevant factors to consider (e.g., Yoon and Knight 2015). Finally, the constraints of our sample in relation to self-reported psychopathic traits and gender (predominantly female) limit the extent to which these findings can be extended to populations of criminal psychopaths (Neumann and Hare 2008). Future studies should address motivational, cognitive, contextual, and attentional components of memory testing for emotional stimuli in individuals with high levels of psychopathic traits or clinical levels of psychopathy.
This research is one of the few studies to investigate how emotional valence and psychopathic traits affect memory accuracy and susceptibility to misinformation, and demonstrated further support for the misinformation effect. However, our findings regarding the recall accuracy of central and peripheral details in negative, neutral and positive images were contrary to previous research (Christianson et al. 1996); therefore, further studies are warranted. Finally, psychopathic traits were not related to recall accuracy; however, a trend towards significance demonstrated that incorporation of misinformation may be more likely to occur in those high in psychopathic traits, possibly due to attentional deficits. Further research needs to accommodate for lack of attention and motivation from psychopathic persons and use different methods to examine how emotional processing deficits affect memory and susceptibility to misinformation. These findings have implications for how both witnesses and perpetrators may recall criminal events, and how misinformation and accuracy should be considered separate constructs within the criminal justice system.
