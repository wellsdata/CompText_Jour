Starting in October 2019, a series of protests by farmers rocked the Netherlands. The farmers were protesting government proposals to reduce nitrate emissions from the livestock sector. This became known as ‘the nitrogen crisis’.1 In 2022, Eva Vlaardingerbroek, a Dutch political commentator, made the following statement to Tucker Carlson on Fox News, giving her take on the farmer protests: What this is about is the Dutch government stealing our farmers’ land and they’re doing this under the guise of the made-up nitrogen crisis […] The government is not doing this because of a nitrogen crisis. They’re doing this because they want these farmers’ land and they want it to house new immigrants.

This quotation misrepresents the nature of the crisis, which was born out of the legal responsibility of the Dutch state to protect vulnerable nature areas in the Netherlands. The speaker denies that there is a problem with excess nitrates in the Netherlands, instead using a conspiracy theory about a land grab linked to immigration, to explain government actions. Throughout the crisis, these and other, less conspiratorial, arguments have been put forward, often by agricultural organisations and farming industry representatives, in an attempt to prevent government action on harmful nitrate emissions from the livestock industry. The goal of this article is to answer the following question: To what extent has science denial ‘polluted’ the debate around the nitrogen crisis?
In a previous exploratory study, we found similarities between arguments used by those seeking to prevent action on the nitrogen crisis in social media posts and those used to deny the nature and seriousness of climate change over the last decades (Hill & Weulen Kranenbarg, 2024). Climate change deniers argue that climate change is not caused by humans, argue that the harms associated with it are exaggerated, and spread myths about problems with proposed solutions (Brisman, 2012; Brisman & South, 2015; Lewandowsky et al., 2020; Wyatt & Brisman, 2017). The climate change denial arguments used and spread by the fossil fuel industry have, in turn, been compared to those used by the tobacco industry against regulations by governments to protect populations from the harmful effects of smoking in the previous century (Oreskes & Conway, 2010). In both these cases, there was and is irrefutable evidence from the scientific community linking the activities of powerful industries, the fossil fuel industry and tobacco industry respectively, to the problem. The denialist arguments became widespread and have been identified in many forms of media, from television (Painter et al., 2023) and newspapers (Ruiu, 2021) to social media (Falkenberg et al., 2022).
Boykoff and Farrell (2020) highlight how climate change denial movements infiltrated the mainstream media narrative on climate change in the USA, influencing public understanding of the issue. In more recent years, however, research by Painter and colleagues (2023) shows that denialist arguments in coverage of the 2021 IPCC2 reports were far less prevalent. Furthermore, they are now concentrated in the ‘right-wing’ television channels and appear to a much lesser extent in mainstream channels, indicating polarisation of the issue, where consumers of these media are heavily exposed to these arguments, but others not.
Turning to the nitrogen crisis in the Netherlands, here too, we point to scientific evidence of the negative impacts of excess nitrate emissions from livestock farming, on non-farming habitats and ecosystems (Erisman & de Vries, 2021). To protect these ecosystems, the Dutch state is obliged by law to decrease nitrate emissions. There remains, however, a high likelihood that proposed solutions to the nitrogen crisis would affect the economic interests of the industrial livestock industry, including many individual livestock farms. Jacquet (2022) argues that when there is an industry for which scientific knowledge threatens its continued existence through regulatory action, the preconditions for science denial are present. Previously we have found evidence of science denial regarding the nitrogen crisis in social media posts (Hill & Weulen Kranenbarg, 2024). The extent to which these arguments are found in mainstream media,3 and thus reach a wider audience, is unclear. This article will therefore first answer the following sub-question:
- Q1: Is there evidence of science denial in mainstream media reporting of the nitrogen crisis in the Netherlands?



Jacquet (2022) points out that denial can take different forms. Research on climate change denial indicates that over the years, the types of arguments used have shifted. Applying Cohen’s categorisations of denial (2001), we have seen a shift from literal denial, ‘climate change is not happening’, to interpretive denial, ‘climate change is not bad’, and implicatory denial, ‘climate change is not caused by humans’ (Wyatt & Brisman, 2017). In addition, Supran and Oreskes (2021) highlight a shift to arguments that the solutions to climate change are not economically viable. This shift has occurred as the science on climate change has become more well-known and societal pressure on fossil fuel companies to take action has increased (Megura & Gunderson, 2022). Regarding the nitrogen crisis, the following sub-question is therefore of interest:
- Q2: Which arguments used to deny the nature, extent, and harm of the nitrogen crisis are found in the mainstream media, and do we see a shift in the arguments used over time?



Together the answers to these sub-questions will inform the answer to our main research question as to whether the societal debate around the nitrogen crisis has been polluted by science denial.
This article draws on a green criminological framework, interested in the activities of powerful actors within society that contribute to social and environmental harm. As Lewandowsky and colleagues (2018) highlight, science is built on scepticism and debate. However, this scepticism that is inherent in the scientific process is qualitatively different to climate change scepticism that happens outside of science, dismissing findings that do not suit a particular ideology (Brisman, 2012). In the Netherlands, tackling the nitrogen crisis calls for a well-informed political and societal debate on how best to meet our legal and moral obligations to protect our natural environment, as well as, more broadly, what our priorities as a country should be going forward. Science denial can disrupt this debate by undermining trust in scientific processes and findings (Lewandowsky et al., 2018), resulting in science being viewed as a ‘dismissible endeavour’ (Brisman, 2012). The effect of undermining science is a delay in action to tackle the causes of environmental harm. Green criminologists take such environmental harm as their subject of study. Researchers examine how macro socio-economic systems exploit, enable, or tolerate this harm, with the perpetrators often acting within the law (South, 2014). In answering our main research question, we take a harms-based perspective, considering the aim of denial around the nitrogen crisis.
Science Denial
Disinformation and other commonly used terms, such as ‘fake news’, whilst rather vague concepts that depend on the user, the context, and their purpose, all refer to falsity of communication. Miró-Llinares and Aguerri (2023) distinguish between false messages and false messengers and between false content and false reactions to the content. Science denial—false messages containing false content—is a subset of disinformation that seeks to refute widely accepted scientific findings (Sinatra & Hofer, 2021). Walters (2018) refers to a ‘doctrine of ignorance’, whereby ignorance about science is not seen as an absence of knowledge. He argues that it has, rather, become a feature of societal discourse promogulated by powerful actors. This refusal to accept scientific findings, science denial, proliferates in, amongst others, the fields of medical science, such as vaccine refusal or vaccine hesitancy, where the harmful effects can be directly life threatening (Lavorgna & Myles, 2022; Prot & Anderson, 2019), and climate science, where the harmful effects are more indirect and long-term, but no less extreme (Cook, 2020). Science denial does not always include direct denial of scientific findings. For example, the phrase ‘just asking questions’ has become a familiar trope (Urban Dictionary, 2010) to describe an oft used tactic by those essentially seeking to foment doubt around accepted realities. Denial is thus a phenomenon that combines both explicit denials and the spreading of doubt (Jacquet, 2022). Because of this, Jacquet points out the importance of looking at the arguments in context , to understand whether we are dealing with science denial, as individual claims in themselves will not always contain clear denials.
Scepticism and continuing to question methods and knowledge are an essential part of the scientific process (Erisman & de Vries, 2021; Lewandowsky et al., 2018). Public discussion about how to address societal challenges and what our priorities are as a society is also an essential part of the democratic process. Whilst scepticism and debate can boost understanding of problems and the means to tackle them, denial of science aims for the opposite effect (Lewandowsky et al., 2018)—namely polluting the information environment in which we operate. Whilst scientific scepticism is a search for further answers or evidence, scepticism as employed by climate change deniers is a means to dismiss science (Brisman, 2012). As Prot and Anderson (2019) highlight, science denial does not just fill an information gap that could be equally filled with scientifically sound information. Science denial is successful because it taps into underlying individual psychological and social psychological processes. Greater understanding of how these processes are manipulated through disinformation and denial, which arguments are used (modus operandi) and by whom (the offenders), can benefit from a green criminological approach.
Such an approach takes harm as its subject of interest, rather than criminal behaviour, and thus is the logical choice when examining environmental problems, which often take place within legal business contexts (South, 2014). In addition, whilst science denial has the potential for harmful effects on society, it is not, and arguably should not (Miró-Llinares & Aguerri, 2023), be criminalized. As highlighted by Kuo and Marwick (2021), though, disinformation is related to power. Therefore, a critical approach, which considers whose interests a certain framing or narrative serves, is crucial. We see this clearly with climate change denial and the fossil fuel industry, Walters’ ‘powerful actors’ (2018). Similarly, critical criminology’s concern with confronting the established order and pointing out inequalities in society (Sollund, 2015) is useful in the context of the nitrogen crisis and, specifically, science denial around this issue. In the nitrogen crisis, the government (local and EU), the agroindustry, which is significant in the Netherlands in terms of revenue and lobbying power (Lelieveldt, 2019), and livestock farmers, of which there are many and which vary from small family farms to large-scale corporations (van der Peet et al., 2018), all play a role and have a political or economic interest in the nitrogen crisis. Furthermore, whilst the nitrogen crisis is a very ‘Dutch’ problem, it should be seen in the wider context of environmental harm resulting from certain agricultural practices, including, but not limited to, intensive livestock farming. As the negative effects of livestock farming on the climate and biodiversity crises becomes increasingly recognised, pushback from the industry is expected to increase (see for example Changing Markets, 2023a and Morris & Jacquet, 2024). White (2020) identifies both the agricultural industry and contrarianism as areas of importance for climate change criminologists to examine. Understanding what this looks like and how this ‘lawful but awful’ (Passas, 2005) practice operates is of interest to green criminologists.
Climate Change Denial: Identifying Arguments
There is much literature on the arguments used to deny the science of climate change, for example, literature on the overarching frames and narratives used to convince us not to believe the science. This set of arguments have been referred to as the ‘playbook of denial’, in which several levels can be distinguished: 1) it is not happening, 2) it is not our fault, 3) it is not that bad, 4) the solutions are too expensive, 5) it is too late (Jacquet, 2022). Another study confirms the first four of these arguments, but replaces the fifth with ‘climate science/scientists are unreliable’ (Coan et al., 2021), for example, by questioning the scientific process and instruments, such as peer-review. Brown (2017) describes how actors focus on the details of climate change science that are less certain and label any conclusion that is not ‘fully proven’ as ‘bad science’. EIKE, the German think tank, tries to discredit scientists by watering down their message, and other European think tanks question the legitimacy of the IPCC (Hornsey & Lewandowsky, 2022). Lewandowsky and colleagues (2018) point out that these attacks on science are fragmented and that when the arguments used are taken together, they appear to contradict themselves. An example of such a logical fallacy is, on the one hand, stating that predictions about future climates cannot be made, whilst, on the other hand, arguing that we are about to enter a new ice age. Another example is maintaining that China should take the lead in cutting emissions, whilst also arguing that climate change is unstoppable.
Wyatt and Brisman (2017) emphasise the many different forms that climate change denial takes, recalling Cohen’s (2001) literal denial, interpretive denial, and implicatory denial. They also discuss another environmental harm, namely bio-piracy, one that is far less visible and for which, consequently, literal denial plays a more prominent role. Wyatt and Brisman link Cohen’s forms of denial to Matza and Sykes’s neutralisation techniques (1957). Where Cohen’s denial categorisations examine what is being denied, Matza and Sykes’ neutralisation techniques highlight how the denial occurs (Wyatt & Brisman, 2017). The technique ‘denial of responsibility’ can include both literal denial, that humans do not cause climate change, and implicatory denial, that the fossil fuel industry did not intend to contribute to climate change. Another form of literal denial can also be found in the technique ‘denial of injury’, when arguing that natural disasters are normal and not associated with climate change. The technique ‘denial of the victim’ shows a form of implicatory denial, by ignoring the fact that the impacts of climate change will be uneven and the ability to adapt is not equal between nations or within nations. McKie (2019), in contrast, finds that ‘condemn the condemners’ and ‘appeals to higher loyalties’ are the most frequently used neutralisation techniques. She also identifies a technique she refers to as ‘justification by comparison’, sometimes called the ‘but China’ defence, in which it is said that there is no point in doing anything to tackle climate change when other countries are currently much bigger polluters. An example of the ‘condemn the condemners’ technique is critique about political hypocrisy of COP participants,4 such as their use of private jets (Falkenberg et al., 2022). An example of the ‘appeals to higher loyalties’ technique is that economic progress would be severely limited by climate change policies, where the capitalist, free market, economy is the higher loyalty (Busch & Judick, 2021). This neutralisation has been used to oppose renewable energy policies and to spread fear of overreach by governments (Jacques & Knox, 2016).
Over the years, the use of arguments aimed at delaying action to curb fossil fuel use has increased. These emphasise economic welfare and how the proceeds arising from fossil fuel use are needed to finance the transition to clean fuels (Daub et al., 2021), or as Megura and Gunderson (2022) call it ‘necessitarianism’. These arguments appeal to the necessity of the fossil fuel industry to maintain the economy and thus our way of life, similar to the neutralisation technique ‘appeals to higher loyalties’, with ‘the economy’ being the higher loyalty. By adding a ‘green gloss’ to polluting products—for instance using the term ‘clean…natural gas’ (Supran & Oreskes, 2021, p. 700)—the fossil fuel industry tries to hide the polluting nature of their activities (Hornsey & Lewandowsky, 2022). Plec and Pettenger (2012) label this ‘greenwashing’. The focus on technological solutions, such as carbon capture, has also been highlighted as a tactic used to delay phasing out the use of fossil fuels (DeLay, 2024). Highlighting the potential of geoengineering to mitigate the effects of climate change has become a tool of climate deniers to hold-up action on reducing fossil fuel use (Petersen et al., 2019).5
In summary, the producers and disseminators of climate change denial use different arguments to cast doubt on the nature, extent, and solutions to climate change. Climate change denial has become a many-headed monster with different actors involved across the globe. There is a wide range of arguments, but all with the aim, and the result, of preventing or delaying action on the problem. As Wyatt and Brisman state ‘doubt = inaction = victory’ (2017, p.336).
Science Denial in the Mainstream Media
Much work on climate change denial has focussed on social media and other online forms of communication (Marwick & Lewis, 2017). Dunlap and Brulle (2020) point out the role of the blogosphere from the 2000s onwards and then the increasing importance of social media. This focus on social media is understandable given certain characteristics, which allow for a contagion effect, whereby disinformation spreads via online social connections similarly to how a virus spreads (Treen et al., 2020). Our previous work on disinformation around the nitrogen crisis (Hill & Weulen Kranenbarg, 2024), examined social media posts from well-known nitrogen crisis doubters, finding recurrent arguments familiar from climate change denial, as well as some novel arguments particular to this issue. Kuo and Marwick (2021), however, point out that legacy media has not been immune to disinformation and this is a problem that persists today (Boykoff & Farrell, 2020; Painter et al., 2023). We see with climate change denial that contrarian scientists are awarded proportionally more space in the digital and print media than expert climate scientists are afforded (Petersen et al., 2019). Brisman (2012) puts this down to the media’s ‘balancing norm’. For many years, climate change deniers were able to utilise this drive to present two-sides of the argument, to gain a disproportionate amount of media attention—disproportionate in relation to the scientific consensus on climate change. Even as this scientific consensus became increasingly clear, studies on climate scepticism in British newspapers find that scepticism persisted (Ruiu, 2021), with Painter and Gavin (2016) finding that sceptical arguments were most often found in editorials and opinion pieces.
Two features of the mainstream media emphasise the importance of examining the presence of science denial in a wider media landscape that includes mainstream media. First, the phenomenon of ‘trading up the chain’ (Krafft & Donovan, 2020) is where a small, local, or niche story jumps to a national news story. Marwick and Lewis (2017) describe how this occurs with Donald Trump, where he refers to a conspiracy or false claim in a social media post or rally, which then gets reported on by mainstream media. They also outline how mainstream media, through its increasing dependency on social media for content, dissemination, and, ultimately, revenue, is vulnerable to disinformation by internet subcultures, such as those that deny climate change. Nimmo (2020) proposes a so-called ‘breakout scale’ to measure the impact of disinformation in society, by looking at the extent to which it has an impact outside of the online context. In this comparative model, data on ‘influence operations’ can be used to determine their impact.6 In categories one to three of the model, the lowest level of impact, information remains in the online space. Category four (of a maximum of six) refers to the crossing of information from social media to mainstream media. According to the model, once the mainstream media has reported on disinformation originating online, its impact increases.
Second, mainstream media have the potential to burst the online filter bubble. Research has shown that, in the Netherlands, the majority of the general public turns to the mainstream media for their news and that these sources have high levels of trust by the public (Moeller & Helberger, 2018). As Brisman and South (2015) pointed out, people have little direct experience of climate change (at least back in 2015) and so they likely rely on the mainstream media to understand the issue. The same can be said for the nitrogen crisis. The ecological effects of excess nitrates would for the majority of the public be invisible. This suggests that the mainstream media retain the potential to shape public perception of issues. On the one hand, this can result in a shared information landscape and, potentially, a robustness against polarisation. On the other hand, as emphasised by Brisman (2012) in his work on climate change contrarianism, the mainstream media run the risk of being compelled to present both sides of the debate, potentially distorting the science and contributing to a delay in action.
For both these reasons, the potential for trading up the chain and the trust of the Dutch public in mainstream media, we choose to examine the extent to which denial of the harms associated with nitrate excesses from the livestock industry is present in mainstream media, the types of arguments found in this form of media, and whether their use has developed over the course of the nitrogen crisis.
Environmental Harm Caused by Excess Nitrates in the Netherlands
Since the 1980s, successive Dutch governments have tried—and failed—to limit the environmental damage caused by the large number of livestock animals in the country, by introducing and adopting policies, laws,and regulations. Whilst in the last two decades, the number of livestock animals has remained largely stable, the numbers had increased considerably in the 1970s through the 1990s (CBS, 2021), significantly impacting ecosystems in the country. The latest in a long line of policies introduced to prevent excess harm to the environment was the Programme Approach to Nitrogen (PAN), introduced in 2015. PAN was a policy instrument designed to allow nitrate-emitting businesses to continue or expand their activities, based on promises of future reductions in nitrates. These future reductions would supposedly be sufficient to protect Natura 2000 sites—EU designated areas of vulnerable species and habitats (EEA, 2023). In 2019, however, the Dutch Council of State (CoS) declared this programme contravened the European Habitats Directive. As a result, provisional permits for nitrate-emitting activities given out under the PAN policy were suspended. Mainly livestock farms, but also permits to build roads, housing, and industrial estates, were affected. The CoS statement thus marked the start of what has become known as the ‘nitrogen crisis’ in the Netherlands and signalled to politicians the urgent need for policies to limit nitrate emissions from the livestock industry in order to meet legal obligations.
Since then much societal debate has ensued, along with protests by farmers, and proposed political solutions, overshadowed only partly by the COVID-19 pandemic in 2020 and 2021. Elections held during this period were, at times, dominated by the nitrogen crisis and the larger environmental crisis at its heart. In the midst of this, and in order to emphasize the need for action and underline the scientific basis of the problem, in 2022, 36 scientists wrote a letter to Trouw , a Dutch newspaper, calling on politicians to take action to protect the Netherlands’ fragile nature (Trouw, 2022). According to these scientists, there is no doubt that nitrates are having a very damaging effect on Natura 2000 areas, but also on our environment, more broadly. Excess nitrates are exacerbating the effects of prolonged droughts that are becoming more frequent due to climate change, as well as affecting biodiversity levels. The science is, therefore, clear on the damaging effects of excess nitrates, and the numbers indicate that the livestock industry is responsible for the majority of these excesses (Erisman & de Vries, 2021). The most logical and only means to tackle excess emissions that is supported by the science, is to reduce livestock numbers (Backes, 2023). Innovations, such as adjusting feed, capturing emissions in the stable, separating urine from faeces in the stables, or watering down slurry, to name but a few, whilst having the potential to reduce emissions somewhat, are as yet not proven scientifically to have a large enough effect (Verhaeghe, 2023; Vink et al., 2021). The nitrogen crisis can be seen as an environmental issue, which, due to poor political decision making over many years, has resulted in judicial crisis. The Netherlands has a responsibility to reduce its nitrate emissions, not just because the (ecological) science indicates it is necessary, but also because this is enshrined in the law. The extent to which this is recognised or denied in the mainstream media, is the focus on this study.
Method
Data Selection
To capture whether the nature of the debate has changed over time we took a longitudinal approach. We focused our data collection around key events from May 2019 up to September 2024. In total, we identified and included 11 key events. The first event was the ruling from the Dutch CoS, on 29th May 2019. Other events include large scale farmer protests, a regional election, publication of government commissioned reports, the introduction of new policies, announcements about changes to policy directions, and policy negotiations. See Table 1 for a list of all events. For each key event, data collection spanned the seven days prior to, the day itself, and the seven days following the event. The choice of 11 key events is somewhat arbitrary. Whilst it is possible to identify more events in the nitrogen crisis, we judged that with these 11 events we had covered enough variety in type of event, as well as enough spread over the five year period, to capture the breadth of societal debate and any changes in the reporting of the crisis. The data used for this article come from 710 newspaper reports covering the nitrogen crisis that refer specifically to farmers and/or the agricultural industry in the Netherlands. Six national newspapers with the highest circulation were used to obtain data ( Algemeen Dagblad; de Volkskrant; Trouw, De Telegraaf; NRC; Reformatorisch Dagblad ). Circulation numbers range from 103,000 ( Reformatorisch Dagblad ) to 802,000 ( Algemeen Dagblad ) (Nationaal Media Onderzoek, 2023). The newspapers have different political leanings (e.g., De Telegraaf is viewed as more right leaning, de Volkskrant as more progressive), although this is less pronounced than is generally found for newsprint in countries such as the UK and USA. They also target different sections of society. For example, the Reformatorisch Dagblad has a strong Christian sentiment, NRC and Trouw are traditionally seen as newspapers for people with a university education, and the Algemeen Dagblad has a prominent focus on regional reporting.
Using the Nexis Uni database, potentially relevant articles were identified using the broad search term ‘nitrogen’ (Dutch: stikstof ). These were then screened using several selection criteria. This selection was done on the basis of the headline or quick scan of the content of the article in cases where the headline was ambiguous. First, the CoS ruling affected not just the livestock industry, but also building and transport sectors. Therefore, articles that did not predominantly focus on the livestock industry in the context of the nitrogen crisis were excluded from the final selection. Second, we did not include articles that discuss the effects of excess nitrates on ecosystems, but do not refer to the role of the agricultural industry. Third, we did not include articles that focused on the farmer protests, but did not refer to the nitrogen crisis at all. Lastly, we excluded letters from readers from the final selection. During this selection process, articles were categorised as news articles (short < 750 words or long > 750 words), editorials or opinion pieces, interviews or other. The final dataset included 710 articles. The number of the articles per period can be seen in Table 1.
Analysis
Categorising Articles
We categorised all articles included in the final selection according to the overarching position taken in the reporting. We used five categories: strongly anti-science, mostly anti-science, neutral/balanced, mostly pro-science, and strongly pro-science. The qualifications ‘anti’ and ‘pro’ refer to the extent to which a platform is given for positions against (anti) or for (pro) taking scientifically substantiated action to reduce environmentally harmful levels of nitrate emissions from the livestock industry. Depending on the extent of the position represented or the presence of the opposite position, we chose the qualifications ‘strongly’ or ‘mostly’. For example, if the majority of the article contains arguments against action to reduce emissions from the livestock industry, but at some point the reason underlying the need to do this is mentioned, then we classified the article as ‘mostly-anti science’. If no mention of the reasons for action are reported, we classified the article as ‘strongly anti-science’. ‘Strongly pro-science’ articles focus entirely on the need for action and/or provide a strong dismissal of the arguments against action, based on the science. ‘Mostly pro-science’ articles do not contain any anti-science sentiment, but are less focused on defending the scientific underpinnings of the crisis. We chose the classification ‘neutral/balanced’ when no position is taken and neither arguments for nor against action to tackle the problem of excess nitrates from the livestock industry appear. Often, these articles simply describe an event. For example, if an article reports on a debate that took place on the second chamber of the Dutch government, where the positions of all political parties regarding the livestock industry and their role in the nitrogen crisis are reported on (both for and against action), but no commentary on those positions is provided, this would be categorised as ‘neutral/balanced’.
Once the selection and categorisation scheme had been developed and tested by the first author, the first and second author both examined a selection of 10 articles independently. Fulfilment of the selection criteria was checked first, followed by categorisation of the articles. We were in agreement that five of the chosen articles were not relevant. Of the remaining five articles three were categorised in the same way by us both. The remaining two differed between ‘mostly pro’ and ‘neutral/balanced’, and ‘mostly anti’ and ‘strongly anti’. Following discussion of the differences, we developed clearer definitions of the categorisation criteria until we reached consensus on the appropriate classification in these cases. These criteria informed the rest of the categorisation process. In the discussion we consider the limitations and challenges of this approach.
Coding Arguments
Articles categorised as ‘strongly anti-science’ ( N  = 58) were then uploaded to Taguette, an online, open-source, qualitative, data analysis tool, to analyse the science denial arguments used. We included only ‘strongly anti-science’ articles given the intensity of this task and because we judged these to include the full-range of denialist arguments. We used the codebook developed by Hill and Weulen Kranenbarg (2024) as a basis. We had originally developed these codes from thematic arguments retrieved from the literature of climate change denial and from novel themes that arose from the data collected from nitrogen crisis disinformation actors. We used the same approach here, whereby new codes were added as the need arose. We carried out several iterations of data analysis to refine the coding. We generated overarching themes to determine how different codes could be meaningfully combined to identify underlying denialist techniques. See Appendix 1 for the complete codebook. Once we had analysed the data in this way, we were able to plot descriptive statistics on the development of themes over the duration of the nitrogen crisis to date.
Results
Article Categorisation
The Algemeen Dagblad published the highest number of relevant articles ( n  = 222), followed by Trouw ( n  = 124), Reformatorisch Dagblad ( n  = 108), NRC ( n  = 97), De Telegraaf ( n  = 81) and de Volkskrant ( n  = 78). We categorised the majority of articles as neutral/balanced ( n  = 259), followed by mostly anti-science ( n  = 178), mostly pro-science ( n  = 133), strongly pro-science ( n  = 82), and finally a minority as strongly anti-science ( n  = 58). Science Denial Arguments
A large number of different arguments were used to deny the science underlying the nitrogen crisis and the consequent need to reduce nitrate emissions from the livestock industry. In total, 43 different types of argument were identified in a total of 614 statements. The arguments were categorised into five themes: confuse ( n  = 150), emotion ( n  = 215), policy ( n  = 123), responsibility ( n  = 294), and science ( n  = 180).7 (See Appendix 1 for which argument types fall under which theme.) The theme ‘confuse’ contains arguments that focus on spreading doubt about the nature, extent, or basis of the problem, for example, by incorrectly interpreting the consequences of excess nitrates on natural ecosystems. The theme ‘emotion’ refers to arguments that rely on triggering emotions in the reader regarding potential action to tackle the problem, for example, by focussing on positive aspects of farming, such as the desire for farmers to provide for their families. The theme ‘policy’ is used for arguments that clearly focus on attacking proposed or enacted policies to tackle the nitrogen crisis, for example, highlighting that reducing livestock numbers would financially affect farmers. The theme ‘responsibility’ is used for arguments that deny the (continued) responsibility of the livestock industry or point to the responsibility of others for the nitrogen crisis, for example, by highlighting nitrate emissions from other industries. The theme ‘science’ is used for arguments that focus on aspects of the science, be it the process, the results, or the need for more research, for example, by questioning the use of modelling to examine the distribution and effects of nitrate emissions.
In Fig. 4, we can see how the different themes are used throughout the time-frame examined in the study. Whilst all themes are found in all periods, there is a difference in the proportion of each over time. We see in October 2019, a period when many farmer protests took place, that attacking aspects of the science at the root of the crisis was a dominant theme. In March 2023, around the Dutch regional elections, this theme emerged again as slightly more dominant than the others. The ‘responsibility’ theme, containing arguments on who is or is not responsible for excess nitrates harming the environment, was dominant in the period around the very first farmer protest at the beginning of October 2019. Arguments focussing on policies to tackle the crisis are found in a fairly modest proportion of all statements throughout the time-frame. We see the greatest proportion of these statements in June 2020, when an important government commissioned report on solutions to the crisis was published, and in June 2023, when the farmers’ union stepped out of talks to resolve the crisis. Except for October 2019, arguments which potentially confuse readers about the nature, extent, or harm of the nitrogen crisis, are fairly consistently found throughout the time-frame, peaking in the final period September 2024, when a letter was leaked to the press about scrapping the National Rural Area Programme policy. Arguments that attempt to draw on the emotions of the reader also represent a fairly consistent proportion throughout the time-frame. Overall, the figure indicates the presence of the full range of arguments used throughout the time-frame of the study. [See PDF for image]

Dominant Narratives
Having established that the full range of arguments is present across the research period, we now focus on the most dominant narratives found in the articles which were classified as ‘strongly anti-science’. The most frequently used argument portrayed the farmers as victims ( n  = 85). This sense of the farmers as victims, and therefore not responsible for the crisis, comes across very strongly in these articles. Often direct quotations from farmers or their representatives are used, such as, ‘we don't deserve to be pushed into a corner’ and ‘entire businesses are being wiped off the map, entire families are being sacrificed’ . Untruths are also used to emphasise the farmer as victim, for example, suggestions that the average farmer earns under the minimum wage.8 Recurring topics are that farmers’ futures are being removed and that their right to exist is under attack. The farmers are portrayed as repeatedly being the victim of politicians and policies and not just in the context of the nitrogen crisis, ‘ [f]armers have been the ones ‘getting the short end of the stick’ for the past twenty years ’. Whilst at times, the past is referred to with a sense of nostalgia, such as ‘ [h]e started [farming] on this spot in 1976. With one stable. There was no nitrogen problem then ’, more often, the idea of always being the victim is used.
The victim role is combined with an attitude that farmers will resist policy proposals to reduce nitrate emissions with which they do not agree. The code ‘fight’ was applied 42 times, with terms such as ‘ battle ’, ‘ destruction ’, and ‘ bleed ’ used repeatedly. These violent terms are often combined in statements labelled with the code ‘enemy’ (i.e., those who want to or try to take action to tackle the crisis), ‘ all decisions of the left-wing environmental mafia, canal district types 9 who think that chocolate milk comes from a cow with brown patches ’. This reference to enemies can contain aspects of the neutralisation technique ‘condemn the condemners’, such as in this quotation, which blames ‘enemies’ for the problem and also manages to deny the harmful effects of nitrates on the environment, ‘ [The] Society for Nature Protection and The State Forestry Department have neglected maintenance, which causes the heathland to become overgrown and some parts of the forest to look bad ’.
Often farmers’ families are mentioned as being victimised, drawing on two other types of arguments. First, the reader’s emotions are triggered, such as sympathy or indignation, and, second, positive aspects of farming industry, which are unrelated to the nitrogen crisis are mentioned. Farmers are consistently portrayed as hard-working, honest people, who are doing their best to provide food for the population. This role as food producers is another dominant narrative that emerges. The neutralisation technique, ‘appeals to higher loyalties’, is relevant, in this case the higher loyalties being feeding the country or the world at the expense of the environment. The Netherlands’ role as a global player in the agricultural industry, is often brought up, for example, ‘ the demand for food is increasing: food that we produce safely and efficiently in the Netherlands ’. Such statements, whilst not necessarily false, ignore the environmentally damaging nature of livestock farming with the aim of delaying action. Other higher loyalties emphasised are the rural economy as a whole, and the importance of the farmers to that economy, as well as the higher loyalty individual farmers have to their business model, where the financial impossibility of reducing livestock numbers on individual farms is argued.
Dominant narratives within the two themes of science and responsibility also emerge. First, that the science on which the nitrogen crisis rests suffers from several limitations. The use of models rather than measurements arises, in an attempt to discredit the scientific process. It is, however, common practice for scientists and policy-makers to use models to make predictions about future situations (which cannot be measured yet) or because it is simply unfeasible to measure everything all the time (de Gruijter et al., 2019). Conclusions that the harm caused by livestock farming is based on assumptions, rather than hard facts, is another position taken. The following quotation also hints at ulterior motives for taking action, ‘ based on ignorance, misuse of science, and shady backroom deals ’. That farmers have already acted to reduce their emissions through innovative techniques is repeatedly brought up as well. Innovation as a way out of the crisis is another repeat narrative. Crucially, the narrative is that by investing in these innovations, we would remove the need to reduce livestock numbers.
Another of the dominant narratives that touches on several themes is that in Germany, there is no nitrogen crisis, because they have a different policy approach. But also, that Germany and other bordering countries are to blame for the crisis, because nitrates blow over the border and are deposited in the Netherlands. Other parties that are consistently blamed for the problem, because they emit nitrates but are not being asked to reduce their emissions, are industrial sectors10 and consumers. Consumers are responsible for the crisis because they insist on flying and driving everywhere and because they will not pay more for their food or eat certain types of food, such as chicken on the bone. ‘ For example, citizens can leave their cars at home more often or eat less meat. But we can't just push everything onto farmers, so that the rest of the country can continue as it is. ’

In this quotation we see an example of piecemeal argumentation. The recognition that consumers could eat less meat as a means to tackling the problem would logically lead to fewer livestock animals.
Finally, the nitrogen crisis is often conflated with other crises, such as the COVID-19 pandemic, the migrant ‘crisis’, and the climate crisis. In this narrative, the government limits our freedoms under the guise of tackling these ‘so-called’ crises. Action to tackle the nitrogen crisis is portrayed as the thin end of the wedge. Derogatory references to experts also form a part of this narrative, with denialist ‘experts’, who argue against action that would affect the livestock industry, held up as speakers of truth to power. ‘ There are many scientists who think very differently about this, but they are not given a voice, just like with corona. ’
Discussion
In this article, we set out to answer the question: To what extent has science denial ‘polluted’ the debate around the nitrogen crisis? To guide us to an answer, we posed two sub-questions. 1) Is there evidence of science denial in mainstream media reporting of the nitrogen crisis in the Netherlands? 2) Which arguments used to deny the nature, extent, and harm of the nitrogen crisis are found in the mainstream media, and do we see a shift in the arguments used over time? By collecting data from mainstream media, namely articles published in Dutch national newspapers, we aimed to determine whether science denial related to the Dutch nitrogen crisis found on social media (Hill & Weulen Kranenbarg, 2024), is also present in the wider societal debate. If present, we wanted to explore the types of denialist arguments used. Any change in arguments over time might provide some indication of whether and how science denial has developed a polluted information environment.
In answer to the first sub-question, we found that arguments that deny the scientific basis of the nitrogen crisis are found throughout the period examined, albeit in a minority of articles examined and with some newspapers overrepresented in the presence of science denial. Three newspapers stood out in both the presence of denial and the lack of robust pro-science reporting, namely De Telegraaf , Reformatorische Dagblad and Algemeen Dagblad . The remaining three newspapers, Trouw , de Volkskrant , and NRC, showed the opposite pattern—a stronger representation for scientifically substantiated reporting, with a small minority of articles falling into the anti-science categorisation. This split indicates a certain level of polarisation of the debate, perhaps to be expected given the political polarisation in the Netherlands (Silva, 2018) and given our previous research findings that (far-)right and populist politicians were involved heavily in science denial around the nitrogen crisis (Hill & Weulen Kranenbarg, 2024). The overall appearance of robustness against science denial in the Dutch mainstream media, therefore, needs to be qualified. Depending on the newspaper read, readers will be exposed to more (or less) denialist narratives, or a more (or less) polluted debate. The media’s ‘balancing norm’ (Brisman, 2012) is to a certain extent present in all newspapers examined. Given the relatively novel nature of the societal debate on the nitrogen crisis, this is to be expected and reflects the approach taken by mainstream media in response to the climate change in the past.
Turning to the second sub-question, different themes emerged. One theme concerned arguments that clearly deny the science at the root of the crisis, either questioning scientific processes, the veracity of the results, or calling for more scientific research to be done. As discussed in the introduction, however, science denial does not just involve direct denial of scientific findings or processes. Under the theme ‘responsibility’, arguments are found that either attempt to deny the responsibility of livestock farming in causing the problem or push the blame for the problem onto other countries or industries, or onto consumers. Denial of responsibility is also a neutralisation technique identified by Matza and Sykes (1957) and one that Wyatt and Brisman (2017) highlight as being used in climate change denial. One clear example of this is that the fossil fuel industry attempts to justify its continued operation by placing responsibility on individual consumers: they are simply fulfilling a societal need (Megura & Gunderson, 2022), a need that nitrogen crisis deniers argue they are also fulfilling.
One of the codes in the responsibility theme is ‘enemy’, which indicates a hostile attitude found in ‘anti-science’ articles towards a group or individual who argues for action to tackle the nitrogen crisis. Whilst the extent to which ‘nitrogen’ scientists are demonised has not reached the same level as seen for climate scientists, the language used does recall this. Brisman and South (2015) compare to this demonisation to Cohen’s folk devils (2003) and suggest that the media portrays climate scientists as a source of moral panic, as a threat to our way of life. The relevance of Cohen’s folk devils and moral panic to the nitrogen crisis appears a stretch on the basis of our data, but would be worth investigating in future research.
Another key feature of climate change denial is the intentional sowing of enough doubt in order to delay action (Jacquet, 2022). We see many arguments in our data that similarly foment uncertainty about the effect of proposed solutions to the problem, present solutions that are not supported by the science, question the extent of the harm done by livestock farming, or misconstrue the nature of the problem. As seen for climate change denial, policies proposed to tackle the crisis are also the focus of attacks. For climate change denial we see this focus on the polices taking increasing prominence in recent years (Coan et al., 2021). In contrast, for the nitrogen crisis, this focus on policy is present in our data from the start of the crisis in 2019. Whilst it is not a dominant theme, its presence likely reflects the nature of the crisis, whereby action by the government is urgently required by the legal system. Together, narratives in the ‘science’ and ‘responsibility’ themes have the effect of delaying action, a goal that is at times explicitly stated (‘ I would like a time-out ’).
The final theme, which draws on the emotions of the reader, appears more particular to the nitrogen crisis. This theme also reflects the duplicitous role of the industry at the heart of the crisis. The victimhood of the farmers is designed to appeal to the general public and elicit their sympathy. There are several reasons why this can be seen as a form of denial. First, there is not a single group of ‘farmers’. For example, nearly half of farming businesses in the Netherlands are arable farms or horticultural businesses (Agrimatie, 2023) and will therefore be affected minimally, if at all, by proposed policies. The constant referral to the ‘farmers’, without the qualifying term ‘livestock farmers’, ignores this, as well as ignoring the farming voices that are arguing for change (e.g., Caring Farmers, 2022). In addition, livestock farmers in the Netherlands are collectively large nitrate emitters. The intensification of the industry over the last decades has been driven by a decrease in the number of livestock farms and little to no decrease in the number of livestock, resulting in larger livestock farms (Hill, 2024). Portrayal of the group as victims ignores any agency livestock businesses exercised in this intensification process and any financial advantages they gained in the process.
Finally, there are other interested parties in the farming sector who barely get mentioned in media coverage of the proposed policies, namely, multinational, agricultural corporations. Several of these operate from within the Netherlands, selling livestock feed and processing meat and dairy products. These parties would undoubtedly face financial consequences from a reduction in livestock numbers in the Netherlands. We encountered, however, only 12 articles that referred to agribusiness as being potentially financially disadvantaged by the proposed policies. The ‘farmer’, who works long hours to provide food for the population and security for his family, fulfils the role of ideal victim (Christie, 1986). Faceless, multinational corporations—agribusinesses—are unlikely to elicit the same level of sympathy were they included in the narrative. Similarly, in the denialist arguments around climate change, the fossil fuel industry does not get portrayed as a ‘victim’, given the lack of sympathy such a narrative would likely elicit. The role of agribusiness in the farmer protests of the last years is somewhat contested (NRC, 2024). What is clear is that they do not appear in the denial narrative and so we have to critically examine why, but also research what possible role they play in pushing a narrative around the nitrogen crisis.
Returning to our main research question, given our findings that science denial is found in the mainstream media, we can conclude that it has polluted the societal debate in the Netherlands around the nitrogen crisis. Whether this is a case of ‘trading up the chain’ (Krafft & Donovan, 2020), where online disinformation makes its way into the mainstream media, is difficult to determine on the basis of these analyses. In addition, as Nimmo (2020) emphasises, measuring the impact of disinformation on social media in wider society is challenging. We do see at the very start of the crisis, in May 2019, that no articles presenting a one-sided narrative strongly denying the science of the problem were found, however. By September 2019, these articles were present. This finding potentially supports ‘trading up the chain’, where denialist arguments jumped from social media to mainstream media in the months between May 2019 and September 2019. In order to definitively make this conclusion, however, more fine-grained research is needed on the timeline of denialist arguments on social media.
What does stand out, though, is that from September 2019 onwards, the full range of denialist arguments identified in previous research (Hill & Weulen Kranenbarg, 2024) are found in mainstream media. We see no shift in focus from arguments in one theme to another. What does this consistency suggest? First, the arguments appeared to be ready to go. There is no change of focus over the years, as seen for climate change denial where, for example, first a focus on the science itself was gradually replaced by more focus on the policies. Other work on science denial within the agricultural industry indicates that the industry has been anticipating action to curb environmental harm associated with their activities for a longer period (Changing Markets, 2023a; Almiron, 2020). The overlap between climate change and nitrogen crisis denial arguments suggests that the agricultural industry learnt from the fossil fuel industry, as the fossil fuel industry previously learnt from the tobacco industry (Jacquet, 2022). Second, our results show that the presence of science denial does not dissipate and the narratives used are consistent across the five-year period. This indicates a hardened debate, as well as one that is polarised, as opposing parties do not appear to shift their position over the years regardless of scientific evidence or societal changes.
In 2006, the Food and Agricultural Organization of the United Nations (FAO) published a report pointing out the link between livestock farming and greenhouse gas emissions. Whilst in this paper, we focus on nitrates, at its heart a judicial problem particular to the Netherlands, the environmental damage of large scale livestock farming has widely recognised by scientists for a long time. It is striking that more than a decade passed between the FAO report and the IPCC first mentioning the potential for more plant-based diets to help tackle climate change (IPCC, 2019). Attention to the role of financially powerful industries in preventing or delaying action to tackle environmental harm, as well as examining the tactics they use to do so, is a key role for green criminologists. As White (2020) points out powerful corporations own the majority of the planet’s resources, such as the land required to feed livestock, and these actors are likely to resist threats to their position. As we have seen, one form of resistance is spreading doubt about the harm of their activities. If we can conclude that we have a polluted debate, as we do in this paper, then it is necessary to undertake a critical exploration and examination of who benefits from this polluted debate, what structures they use to do so, and potential interventions to depollute the debate.
Limitations and Future Research
In this study, we examined a peculiarly Dutch problem, but one that has potential implications for other work on science denial, whether focussing on the agricultural sector or other areas. We found that an important aspect of being able to categorise arguments and recognise aspects of denial is the knowledge base of the person carrying out the coding. When discussing a set of articles independently examined by both authors, we found that a high degree of literacy on the issue was needed to perform this task, which is in line with previous work on climate change denial (Coan et al., 2021). The first author has acquired the knowledge base required to carry out this task. A limitation of the study, however, is that we were not able to use a meaningful interrater reliability check of the coding process. Critical discussions between the first and second author, as well as other colleagues, were used to check for bias, but this limitation means that objectivity of the analyses cannot be guaranteed completely. Similarly, gaps in the first author’s knowledge could have led to missing or misconstruing denialist arguments.
Future research, following a thoroughly developed taxonomy by human researchers, could investigate the potential for large language models to carry out this task at scale. As well as potentially producing more reliable results, this would allow for a larger dataset to be coded. Extending the analysis beyond the nitrogen crisis in the Netherlands—to other places where the agricultural industry is resisting action to tackle its environmentally harmful activities—would be an area for such future research. First, given the significant greenhouse gas emissions related of livestock farming and, second, given the impact of intensive farming practices on other planetary boundaries, such as biodiversity, this work would be broad and consequential. We have demonstrated that taking a green criminological approach is informative and applying this to other areas has the potential to expose further the role of powerful actors in framing debates and shaping reactions to environmental harms within the agricultural industry and elsewhere.
Another area for future research for which a green criminological approach would be informative, would be to bring in to view the producers of nitrogen crisis denial, as has been done for climate change denial (Treen et al., 2020). In this article, we have demonstrated how denialist arguments are found in mainstream media, but where these arguments originate has not yet been examined. Much research has found evidence for the role of the fossil fuel industry in pushing back against action to tackle climate change (e.g., Supran & Oreskes, 2021). Recent work has also started to highlight the role of agribusiness in disinformation (Medeiros et al., 2024) and greenwashing (Changing Markets, 2023b). The Netherlands is an important player in global agriculture (Dutch Agribusiness Worldwide, n.d.) and likely fertile ground for such research.
