1. Introduction Climate change is a serious threat to global sustainability, marked by rising extreme weather events and long-term shifts in drought, flooding, wildfires, and rainfall patterns [1,2]. As a highly vulnerable country, Nigeria faces climate stressors that weaken agricultural systems, reduce yields, and deepen food insecurity [3,4,5,6]. Strengthening the agricultural sector is therefore a strategic priority for Nigeria, given its central role in economic growth and rural livelihoods [7,8]. Before 1960, agriculture provided food, jobs, and raw materials, but the oil boom of the mid-1970s shifted national priorities and contributed to a long-term decline in agricultural productivity [8,9]. Development plans between 1962 and 1985 lacked consistency, and the 1987 Structural Adjustment Program sought to diversify the economy by expanding private-sector participation. Subsequent initiatives, including the Agricultural Transformation Agenda (2011–2015) and the Agricultural Promotion Policy (2016–2020), focused on improving food security and boosting agricultural exports [10].
Small-scale farmers make up 80% of Nigeria’s farming population and produce about 90% of the national output [11]. Extension systems provide the knowledge, technologies, and institutional support needed to raise productivity and improve rural livelihoods [12]. However, the withdrawal of donor funding weakened the Agricultural Development Programme model, leading to staff losses and an overstretched agent-to-farmer ratio [13]. Many farmers still face structural constraints, including limited access to finance, inadequate information, poor input supply, and weak supply chain linkages. Climate variability worsens these challenges by increasing weather uncertainty and crop failures [14,15,16]. As variability grows, the reliability of information reaching smallholder farmers becomes more important for regional food security, yet communication and interpretation gaps persist [17].
Studies link low adaptation rates to farmers’ limited access to information [18,19] but less attention has been paid to the negative effects of inaccurate or misleading information on climate adaptation decisions. To address this gap, this study examines how agri-food misinformation and key information intermediaries influence farmers’ climate-change adaptation decisions amid climate and socio-economic challenges.
Beyond information access, the quality of agricultural information has direct consequences for land management decisions. When smallholder farmers receive agri-food misinformation, the resulting choices such as excessive fertilizer application, abandonment of crop rotation, or improper irrigation timing translate into measurable land degradation outcomes including soil acidification, loss of organic matter, and erosion [15,20]. In Nigeria, where declining soil fertility is already identified as a key driver of yield loss [14], the circulation of agri-food misinformation within farmer networks adds a further dimension to land-use vulnerability. Land-use decisions made under such conditions can accelerate the shift from resilient, diversified systems toward monocropping regimes that expose soils to greater climatic stress [21,22]. Understanding how misinformation shapes these decisions is not only a communication challenge but also a land-governance concern, with direct implications for the long-term sustainability of smallholder agriculture in sub-Saharan Africa.
This study addresses three research questions: I. . What are the primary sources of agri-food misinformation to farmers?
II. . What roles do extension agents and peer networks play in shaping the flow of agri-food misinformation?
III. . How do climate challenges, socio-economic factors, and information quality interact to influence farmers’ climate-change adaptation practices?

The remainder of the paper is structured as follows. Section 2 reviews the relevant literature. Section 3 presents the study’s findings, focusing on the primary sources of climate change adaptation information and the key actors and patterns involved in the dissemination of agri-food misinformation. Section 4 discusses these findings by examining interactions among agri-food actors, and the final section concludes with implications, limitations, and recommendations for policy and practice.
2. Conceptual Framework The spread of misinformation is shaped by the features of communication platforms and user behaviour [23]. Traditional media, such as radio, rely on editorial controls, while social media enables rapid information exchange with minimal oversight [24,25]. These platforms connect users around common interests but also accelerate the dissemination of misleading claims and conspiracy theories [26,27]. Social media thus serves as both an information resource and a source of disorder [28,29]. In contrast, agricultural cooperatives remain important offline platforms for sharing production knowledge, coordinating labour, and pooling resources to enhance efficiency [30].
A study by Chowdhury [31] present a framework for analyzing misinformation in agri-food systems on digital platforms. The framework in Figure 1 focuses on six elements: types of information disorder (misinformation, disinformation and malinformation), sources, diffusion methods, detection strategies, stakeholder impacts, and countermeasures. Information disorder occurs when messages are created or shared that imitate the original content and are transmitted with or without the intention to harm [32]. It can be categorized into three distinct types based on the presence of the intent to cause harm. Misinformation refers to misleading information that is circulated without the intent to deceive [25]. Disinformation involves content that is false and deliberately created to harm a person, social group, organization, or country. Conversely, malinformation is information that is based on reality but is deliberately used to inflict harm [32].
Misinformation leverages trust in familiar sources and amplifies climate skepticism [33]. Agriculture is also vulnerable to climatic extremes that lower productivity and worsen welfare [34]. Uncertainty during these periods increases susceptibility to misleading narratives [35]. Misinformation targets contested agricultural topics to trigger emotional and skeptical responses, often causing sensational claims about genetically modified crops or animal welfare to gain traction on digital platforms [31]. Ruokolainen [36] expands the definition of misinformation to include outdated, inconsistent, incomplete, or intimidating content. In public health and political contexts, misinformation such as false claims during the COVID-19 pandemic and fabricated election content has shaped public perceptions and responses [37,38]. In agriculture, similar messages negatively influence responses to climate change, fertilizer use, and pest management [39]. At the input supply stage, deviations from recommended guidelines have led to excessive chemical use, raising both production and health risks [40].
Individuals are central in the spread of misinformation, with influential actors exerting disproportionate influence over information flows within social networks [41,42]. In Nigeria’s agri-food sector, farmers and extension agents are key actors in information exchange, serving as primary sources of agricultural knowledge and climate-related guidance [17,28,29,43]. Extension agents disseminate technical and climate information, while farmers, through peer learning with farmer groups, facilitate information sharing and improve access to best practices.
Misinformation weakens the evidence base needed for effective climate policy and public cooperation [44,45]. Continued circulation of inaccurate information further erodes trust in scientific evidence and institutions, discouraging long-term adaptation strategies [45]. The impact of misinformation fundamentally obstructs the operational effectiveness of public systems, leading to a breakdown in public trust and institutional compliance [46].
Research highlights inoculation approaches to curb misinformation, especially regarding climate change [47]. Such strategies focus on cognitive awareness and the timely provision of corrective information to counter misinformation [33,48,49]. Debunking provides fact-based explanations to correct false claims, while prebunking builds resistance by exposing individuals to anticipated misinformation tactics in advance [44,50]. Evidence from interventions such as the Go Viral games, which leveraged inoculating messages, shows the effectiveness of building resilience over extended periods [50].
Climate Adaptation Practices
Responses to climate change in agriculture fall into two categories. Mitigation reduces greenhouse gas emissions. Adaptation focuses on practices that improve productivity, resilience, and environmental sustainability [1]. Small-scale farmers prioritize adaptive land and crop management practices to stabilize production under variable conditions [4]. Crop rotation involves growing different crops sequentially on the same land to manage soil fertility, reduce pest and disease pressure, and sustain productivity over time [20,51]. A planned rotation of crops improves soil quality by exploring differences in nutrient demands and root structures, while disrupting pest and disease cycles [52]. Crop rotation diversity is a key feature of resilient farming systems [53]. For small-scale farmers, especially in regions facing climate variability and environmental stress, increasing rotation diversity offers a practical strategy to more sustainable production [7,20]. In Nigeria, legume-based crop rotation improves soil fertility, reduces fertilizer dependence, and stabilizes yields for resource-limited farmers [7,20].
Cover crops are non-commercial crops grown between or alongside main crops to protect the soil, reduce erosion, and add organic matter through improved soil structure [53,54]. Nigerian farmers use cover crops as part of mixed adaptation strategies, combining multiple techniques to improve efficiency and build resilience [7,20,55]. The benefits vary with management practices, climatic conditions, and soil characteristics, with legume cover crops proving particularly effective in low-input systems under warm, humid conditions [15,53]. While the use of cover crops has been associated with higher farm productivity, greater interaction with extension services remains important for improving access to the knowledge and technical support needed to sustain and expand their adoption [7]. Complementary climate-smart practices often adopted alongside cover cropping include planting cover trees, adjusting planting dates, and using improved crop varieties [55].
Water management plays a central role in farmers’ responses to climate variability. Irrigation is the artificial application of water to farmland to supplement rainfall and ensure adequate water for crop growth [20]. As rainfall patterns become more erratic, irrigation becomes an important land management practice. A reliable water supply, independent of rainfall variability, stabilizes production and addresses water deficits during drought [15,55,56]. Irrigation is capital-intensive but offers a high-return investment, showing its potential to enhance farm performance [20]. Recent improvements have been supported by government schemes, political commitment, and farmer training, although infrastructure challenges, such as poor road networks, could limit wider adoption in some Nigerian regions [55].
Declining soil fertility in Nigeria due to erosion, altered soil properties, and salinization makes fertilizer application a key strategy to address reduced crop yields [15]. Nitrogen fertilizers supply essential nutrients for plant growth, but excessive use can limit the benefits of legume-based nitrogen fixation and accelerate residue breakdown in non-legume systems [53]. Applying organic and inorganic nutrients at appropriate rates, timing, and placement improves soil fertility and crop yields while reducing nutrient losses [20]. Farmers in Nigeria use both organic and inorganic fertilizers, adopting diverse methods to manage soil infertility and maintain productivity [7,20]. Management of inorganic fertilizers supports productivity and food security while reducing synthetic fertilizer use and greenhouse gas emissions [53]. Fertilizer performance improves when combined with practices such as adjusted planting schedules, which further enhance crop productivity [55].
Farmers apply agricultural chemicals in response to climate change impacts, especially rising pest and disease pressures [15]. The effectiveness of these chemicals varies across farming contexts, suggesting that their usage does not produce desired pest management outcomes [55]. Among small-scale farmers, pesticides are often used as a substitute for labour, making Nigeria one of the largest pesticide importers in Africa [57]. Chemical inputs provide short-term control, but excessive use induces human health and environmental risks [57,58]. Chemical use is complemented with Integrated Pest Management (IPM), an environmental management approach that combines multiple pest control techniques to reduce reliance on pesticides [55]. Despite its benefits, IPM adoption is limited by factors such as affordability, perceived profitability, farm size, and limited access to information and training [55]. Improved plant varieties are genetically enhanced seeds bred for drought tolerance, pest and disease resistance, and higher yields [55]. These varieties address climate-driven changes that reduce crop performance [15]. By reducing the impact of heat, drought, salinity, and disease, improved varieties help farmers sustain productivity, even as growing seasons shorten and yields decline in Nigeria [15]. However, adoption among small-scale farmers is uneven [20]. Possibly due to the fact that improved varieties are less accessible and more expensive than local seeds [7]. Despite these barriers, improved varieties remain important for crop performance among resource-constrained farmers [19]. Access to agricultural extension services supports adoption by increasing awareness and capacity to use these varieties [55].
In practice, these approaches are commonly implemented together [7]. For example, minimum or conservation tillage, organic manure, improved seed varieties, and agroforestry are often integrated in Nigeria [7,20]. Combining sustainable practices generally delivers greater productivity and resilience than single interventions [59].
3. Materials and Methods 3.1. Research Design and Study Area This study employed a cross-sectional mixed-methods design. Nigeria’s agricultural landscape includes a mix of large-scale land investors, smallholder farmers, and commercial farming enterprises [60]. Large-scale investments typically involve holdings over 200 hectares for commercial production, while smallholders cultivate 1–2 hectares using family labour and diverse crops [60]. There are approximately 40.2 million agricultural holdings in Nigeria, with about 13.4% located in the South West and 764,680 in Lagos State [61]. Individual agricultural holdings account for about 94% (approximately 38 million) of household sector farms. These unregistered, owner- or manager-operated units handle key operational and resource decisions and typically engage in diverse activities, including crop, livestock, poultry, fishery, and forestry production [61]. Agricultural holdings are predominantly male-headed (86.75%), with female-headed units accounting for 13.25%, and a higher concentration in the South-East (29.57%). Educational attainment among holding owners ranges from no formal education (11.23 million) to secondary (13.74 million) and post-secondary levels (6.89 million) [61].
Available evidence shows that a significant proportion of farm income is reinvested in agriculture, ranging from 45% in the Southeast to 30% in the North-East. Spending on food and nutrition varies from 40% in the North-Central to 3% in the South-West, while education consistently receives the smallest share at 8–20% of total farm income [62].
Lagos State, located in southwestern Nigeria between latitudes 6–7° N and longitudes 2–5° E, covers approximately 356,861 hectares. It is bordered by Ogun State to the north and east, the Benin Republic to the west, and the Atlantic Ocean to the south [63,64]. About 47% of the land is designated for agriculture, although only 30% is currently under cultivation. The state has a tropical climate characterized by high rainfall and warm temperatures [63].
As shown in Figure 2, the study area is situated in the north-western part of Lagos State within Ifako Ijaiye Local Government Area, near the Lagos–Ogun state boundary.
The study centred on the Iju Waterworks Farmers’ Association, which manages 130 acres of farmland within the Lagos State Water Corporation premises in Iju [65]. The association was established in 2008 by Water Corporation staff and their families to cultivate surplus land, with access later extended to local farmers through a rental system. It comprises 149 registered small-scale crop farmers, most of whom cultivate similar crops on plots ranging from 1 to 3 acres, with some managing multiple plots depending on capacity. Land allocation is coordinated by the association’s executive committee, and farmers pay a monthly rental fee to the Lagos State Water Corporation.
Most farmers rely on basic or feature phones, with limited internet access mainly through mobile data. Farming practices are predominantly mixed cropping to manage risk and stabilize yields, with common crops including maize, cassava, pumpkin, pepper, and yams. Farmer cooperatives in Nigeria play an important role in supporting smallholder agriculture through resource sharing, market access, and knowledge exchange [30], and participation is often linked to government support programmes for small-scale farmers [20]. The Iju Waterworks Farmers’ Association was selected for its accessibility, members’ willingness to participate, and organized structure, providing a suitable context for examining climate and information-related dynamics among small-scale farmers.
3.2. Sampling and Participants Access to registered farmer groups was facilitated by the Lagos State Agricultural Development Authority, the state agency responsible for farmer extension and technical support. For the quantitative survey, simple random sampling was employed. A roster of all eligible members of the Iju Waterworks Farmers’ Association was compiled, and respondents were randomly selected to participate [66]. Eligibility criteria required participants to be registered association members, be at least 18 years old, and have at least 3 years of farming experience to ensure adequate exposure to climate-related challenges.
The survey included 128 of the association’s 149 registered members, representing approximately 86% of the total membership. Sample size adequacy was assessed using the Qualtrics sample size calculator, which indicated that a minimum of 108 respondents was required to achieve 95% confidence for the target population [67]. The final sample exceeded this threshold, and comparable sample sizes have been reported in similar studies of Nigerian crop farmer associations [21,64,68]. To complement the quantitative findings, semi-structured interviews were conducted to contextualize and support the interpretation of the quantitative results. Nine participants were purposively selected, comprising one extension agent, two government officials, and six farmers who had participated in the survey. Participants were selected based on their ability to articulate experiences related to climate change adaptation.
3.3. Instrument Development and Pilot Testing The survey instrument was developed following in-person consultations with farmers who identified the most utilized climate change adaptation practices within the group, including crop rotation, cover cropping, improved seeds, fertilizer and pesticide use, timing adjustments, and irrigation. Prior to full administration, the questionnaire was pilot-tested with eight respondents to assess clarity and relevance. The survey was administered in English using the Qualtrics XM platform (Qualtrics, Provo, UT, USA), version available at the time of data collection, between May–June 2023.
3.4. Data Collection Procedures Participants were first asked to identify their main sources of agricultural and climate-related information, and then to indicate whether they had received agri-food misinformation from those sources. Agri-food misinformation was defined as agricultural or climate information that can be misleading without deliberate intent. During the survey, participants reflected on previous experiences and situations, such as when they relied on advice that was no longer applicable, encountered inconsistent information across sources, or relied on explanations that lacked sufficient detail. Participants were then asked to indicate whether such information had influenced their decisions to adopt climate-smart practices. The key explanatory variable, agri-food misinformation, was operationalized as a binary measure coded as 1 and 0, to indicate its influence on adaptation decision-making. Separate binary logit models were estimated for nine climate change adaptation practices, each treated as a dependent variable: crop rotation, cover cropping or mulching, use of improved seed varieties, effective fertilizer use, effective pesticide use, adjustment of planting and harvesting dates, cultivation of short-duration crops, changes in planting depth, and irrigation. Each model estimated the likelihood that a farmer would adopt the specific adaptation practice in the presence of agri-food misinformation.
The selection of explanatory variables was based on previous studies on the factors influencing climate change adaptation among farmers [7,17,43,52,69,70]. Climate change challenges faced by farmers, including crop diseases, pest infestations, land shortages, and funding shortages, were identified, and farmers were asked to rank them in order of severity. The following table lists the explanatory variables and their measurements. (Table 1).
Socio-economic characteristics included age, gender, experience, education, number of farms owned by farmers, and number of crops planted per farmer. These were used to estimate the likelihood that a farmer would adopt each adaptation practice when faced with agri-food misinformation.
3.5. Data Analysis 3.5.1. Model Goodness of Fit Quantitative data was analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 30.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Descriptive statistics, including frequencies, means, and percentages, were used to analyse major information sources and severity of climate change challenges. A binary logit model examined the influence of agri-food misinformation on farmers’ climate change adaptation. The dependent variable was coded as 1 if a respondent reported adopting at least one climate-smart agricultural practice and 0 otherwise. Model results are reported using regression coefficients (β), odds ratios [Exp(B)], and associated p -values. A positive coefficient indicates a higher likelihood of adaptation, while a negative coefficient indicates a lower likelihood, suggesting that exposure to agri-food misinformation may be associated with reduced adaptive response.
Separate binary logistic regression models were estimated because each adaptation practice represents a distinct binary outcome variable (adopted/not adopted). However, the same set of independent variables was used across all models for consistency and comparability. Each adaptation practice reflects a different farmer decision-making process and may therefore respond differently to the explanatory variables included in the analysis. The goodness-of-fit and predictive strength of the binary logistic regression models were assessed using log-likelihood values, likelihood ratio (LR) chi-square statistics, associated probability values, and pseudo-R 2 estimates. The LR chi-square test evaluates whether the full model provides a better fit than a null model without predictors. Models with lower probability values (Prob > chi2 < 0.05) indicate stronger overall statistical significance.
Among the estimated models, the cover cropping model demonstrated the strongest overall fit (LR chi2 = 27.75, p = 0.023; Pseudo R 2 = 0.203), followed by the irrigation practices model (LR chi2 = 32.95, p = 0.005; Pseudo R 2 = 0.196). The planting dates model also showed acceptable model significance (LR chi2 = 25.30, p = 0.046). Other models showed moderate explanatory power but weaker overall statistical significance, which is common in household-level cross-sectional adaptation studies where behavioural and environmental decisions are influenced by multiple unobserved factors. (Table 2).
Pseudo R 2 values ranged from approximately 0.10 to 0.20 across models, indicating modest but acceptable explanatory capacity for social science and agricultural adoption research. The negative log-likelihood values further suggest improvement of the fitted models over intercept-only models. All models were estimated using 128 observations, ensuring consistency across analyses.
3.5.2. Thematic Summary Interviews were conducted and transcribed for analysis using Microsoft Teams (Microsoft Corp., Redmond, WA, USA), version available at the time of data collection in May–June 2023. Qualitative data was analyzed using NVivo 2020 (Release 1.6) (QSR International Pty Ltd., Melbourne, Australia) through a combination of deductive and inductive coding. Categories were identified from codes and quantified to capture recurring patterns. These were then synthesized into concise theme labels and aligned with the research questions. Table 3 summarises the categories, their frequencies, and corresponding themes. Identified themes include reliance on informal farmer networks where extension engagement is limited, peer-based circulation of agri-food information, and the consideration of such information in farmers’ decisions to adopt or not adopt climate change practices. These themes provide an understanding for discussing how agri-food misinformation operates within the study context.
4. Results 4.1. Demographic Characteristics of the Participants The study population is primarily composed of highly experienced, older female farmers. While the average farming experience is 18 years, the demographic is aging, with nearly 68% of respondents aged 56 or older. Education levels are low, as 44% have no formal schooling.
Nearly half (45%) of specialized small-scale farmers cultivate a single crop variety, such as maize, cassava, or pumpkin. According to the mean rankings in Table 4, financial and biological factors are the most severe impediments to productivity. Specifically, farmers identified a shortage of funds (Mean = 7.37) as their most critical challenge, followed by crop disease (6.35) and land shortages (5.74). Environmental events like drought (3.64) and flooding (3.15) were perceived as less impactful. Income distribution and farm ownership are concentrated in mid-range categories, with a similar number of farmers in the lowest (18%) and highest (17%) income brackets. Most farmers manage two farms (35.2%) or three farms (25%), while smaller proportions operate four or more farms (10.1%). (Table 4).
4.2. Primary Sources of Information and Climate Change Adaptation Practice Participants reported the highest level of reliance on public extension agents across all climate change adaptation practices. Figure 3 shows that farmers relied on the radio for inquiries regarding the effective use of pesticides. While fellow farmers are most frequently consulted for information on the effective use of fertilizers. Social media is reported as one of the least-utilized sources across all domains, although it is used for inquiries about changes in planting and harvesting and the effective use of fertilizer.
The interviews revealed that the Iju Farmers Association’s information system operates as a multi-actor ecosystem with a defined knowledge flow. Formal guidance originates from the government, primarily the Lagos State Agricultural Development Authority (LSADA). Public extension agents provide technical advice and transmit innovations from the government and research institutions.
Farmers 1–6 explained that information flows from group leaders to individual farmers, with occasional direct communication from extension agents. Farmer-to-farmer interaction via face-to-face contact and a group WhatsApp channel complements formal meetings and training sessions. Private extension services are largely underutilized in this system, as the associated costs make them a less preferred option for the group. Consequently, while public agents are identified as the most trusted actors, farmers are the most active and immediate sources of the group’s daily information exchange.
4.3. Frequency of Receipt of Agri-Food Misinformation Fellow farmers were identified as the primary source of agri-food information within the group, with 92 respondents reporting receipt of misinformation through their peer network. In contrast, public extension agents exhibited the highest reliability, with only 16 farmers reporting misinformation from this source. Social media, newspapers and radio were also identified as sources of agri-food misinformation, though minimal compared to extension agents and television.
Qualitative interviews clarified that while inaccurate information frequently spreads within these peer networks, it is typically shared without the intent to deceive. As seen in Figure 4, respondents explained that such misinformation originates primarily from fellow farmers rather than official extension agents. Farmers also highlighted the non-malicious nature of peer-to-peer information sharing, with 55.5% of respondents believing that sources had no intent to mislead.
4.4. Determinant of Climate Change Adaptation The binary logit regression analysis highlights the interaction among socio-economic characteristics, climate-induced challenges, and the quality of information received.
Cover cropping and mulching showed the most significant fluctuations in the model. It was positively associated with drought (β = 2.198, p < 0.001) and floods (β = 1.431, p < 0.05) but was negatively associated with age (β = −1.001, p < 0.001) and pest infestation (β = −0.913, p < 0.05). The decision to irrigate was positively associated with pest infestation (β = 1.855, p < 0.05) and the shortage of land (β = 0.883, p < 0.05). However, it showed a negative relationship with the shortage of funds (β = −0.660, p < 0.01). While flood (β = 0.925, p < 0.05) is positively associated with the likelihood of practicing crop rotation, drought (β = −1.390, p < 0.05), pests (β = −0.761, p < 0.01), and age (β = −0.756, p < 0.05) were negatively associated with the likelihood of adoption. For the change in planting and harvesting dates, significant negative coefficients were observed for infestation of pests (β = −1.023, p < 0.05), income (β = −0.242, p < 0.01), and age (β = −0.282, p < 0.01), suggesting that higher income or older farmers are less likely to alter seasonal timing when faced with agri-food misinformation (Table 5).
Socio-economic variables, including age, experience, and gender, demonstrated more consistent but smaller consequences than climate challenges. Age was the most consistent predictor, showing a significant negative relationship with every practice measured. There were mixed results for farming experience, which was positively associated with irrigation adoption (β = 0.021, p < 0.05). Yet, it had a significant negative relationship with the cultivation of short-duration crops (β = −0.020, p < 0.05) and with the effective use of pesticides (β = −0.008, p < 0.05). Finally, gender was a significant determinant for specific practices. Being male significantly increased the likelihood of adopting cover cropping/mulching (β = 0.584, p < 0.05) and changing planting depth (β = 0.346, p < 0.05), while showing no significant relationship with other practices such as irrigation or crop rotation.
4.5. Revisiting the Conceptual Framework Guided by the framework in Figure 1, the study identified that the main actors in this information system were government extension agents and farmers. Participants’ interviews revealed that limited engagement with extension is associated with greater reliance on informal farmer networks. Agri-food misinformation circulates largely through peer-based platforms and messages, and these information patterns negatively influence the adoption of climate change practices. Messages originated from pre-existing beliefs and incomplete information, while the platforms used ranged from traditional face-to-face interactions to digital spaces like WhatsApp. By revisiting the framework, the study identified how these dynamics directly influence climate change adoption in a local context. 5. Discussion 5.1. Socio-Economic Vulnerability and Adoption 5.1.1. Climate-Induced Challenges Crop diversification is widely recognized as a key adaptation strategy, as it enables farmers to manage production risks and limited land resources [21,22,71]. At the plot level, mixed cropping further reduces vulnerability by spreading risk and maximizing land-use efficiency, while monocropping exposes farmers to the impact of climate change [72]. The findings reveal some vulnerability among farmers due to limited crop diversification and financial insecurity. These challenges are potentially intensified by the circulation of agri-food misinformation. About 45% of the group’s farmers practice monocropping. This is significant from a land-use perspective. Monocropping reduces soil organic matter over time, increases vulnerability to pests and drought, and accelerates land degradation outcomes that are well-documented in the Nigerian smallholder context [15,21]. Where misinformation discourages crop rotation or cover cropping, the land-use consequences extend beyond a single growing season. Farmers who abandoned cover cropping due to misleading peer advice effectively reduced their soil’s protective cover, increasing erosion risk during the heavy rainfall events that are becoming more frequent under climate change [53]. The study’s finding that pest infestation (β = −0.913, p < 0.1) was negatively associated with cover cropping adoption is consistent with misinformation about integrated pest management leading to the over-reliance on chemical inputs, which in turn degrades soil structure and reduces long-term land productivity [40].
Financial constraints further intensify this vulnerability, as fund shortages were negatively associated with the effective use of fertilizer (β = −0.5560 ** p < 0.05). This suggests that farmers who lack funds were less likely to use fertilizer effectively. Interviewees explained that the government-supplied fertilizer was never sufficient, forcing farmers to source additional input from local markets. Farmer 6 noted, “It is cheap… that is why many of them will go there to buy a lot of it.” Participants further explained that less-literate farmers frequently relied on input suppliers to explain product labels and instructions, thereby exposing them to misinformation.
Evidence from India shows that around 85% of farmers lack the financial capacity to cope with climate-related impacts [72]. Similar financial fragility is reported in Kenya, where most smallholder farmers lack the collateral and savings needed to access credit or invest in adaptation measures [73]. In Nigeria, financial constraints also reduce the likelihood of adopting resource-intensive adaptation practices, including drought-response strategies [18,20]. This pattern aligns with our findings, where a shortage of funds (β = −0.660 ** p < 0.05) significantly reduced the likelihood of irrigation adoption. Scarce resources made farmers hesitant to adopt practices perceived as expensive or unfamiliar. As farmer 1 explained, “You just have to be sure before you invest your money.” This reflects broader evidence where high-cost strategies, such as irrigation and greenhouse construction, were difficult to adopt due to financial strain [18].
5.1.2. Age, Gender, and Experience The findings suggest female farmers are less likely to engage in adaptation practices when faced with agri-food misinformation, highlighting the need for equitable access to reliable agricultural information [74]. The significant negative coefficients for age and pest infestation suggest older farmers may lack access to modern data on the benefits of certain practices. Farmer 4 explained that many farmers continue to follow traditional planting calendars that no longer align with changing pest breeding cycles.
Older farmers rely more on long-standing routines and indigenous land-management practices, making them less responsive to new agronomic information [20]. Misinformation studies show a similar pattern in which familiar beliefs are reinforced by increased reliance on experiential heuristics [50,75]. Age has also been found to reduce both productivity and the likelihood of adopting climate-smart practices [7], with younger and better-educated farmers being more inclined to adopt technology-based adaptation strategies [55].
The interaction between agri-food misinformation, climate change adaptation, and experience appears complex, especially where established routines guide decision-making. The findings show that experience was positively associated with the adoption of irrigation practices (β = 0.021, p < 0.05). Yet it had a significant negative relationship with the cultivation of short-duration crops (β = −0.020, p < 0.05) and with the use of effective pesticides (β = −0.008, p < 0.05). This suggests that experience encourages some practices, such as irrigation, while reducing engagement with others. As familiar routines feel accurate, individuals continue to make judgments based on years of practice and experience [76]. While experience often protects against some misinformation, it can also create rigid, pre-existing beliefs that lead to the rejection of new information. For instance, pre-existing beliefs regarding the health risks of Genetically Modified Organisms (GMOs) often clash with scientific data. Farmer 4 reported that some experienced farmers often reject drought-resistant seeds in favour of traditional varieties, which are perceived as safer.
This reliance on personal experience can also lead to dangerous practices. During the interviews, an experienced farmer, farmer 6, described using neem biopesticides on vegetables and noted that the produce was consumed shortly after application. This reflects incomplete information about appropriate application intervals and safe methods of use. He also shared this method with peers, which may have contributed to the spread of misinformation within his local network. Islas [77] caution that toxicity levels for neem are often uncertain, rendering such approaches unsafe.
5.2. Evaluating Actors’ Roles Farmers rely on extension agents for guidance on climate adaptation practices, reflecting their role as major intermediaries within agricultural information systems [7,17,43]. Local soil conditions require soil conservation practices and careful input management, so farmers depend on government extension agents for guidance, especially on crop rotation, pesticide use, and cover cropping. Other sources, like the radio, provide technical information on effective pesticide use and improved seed cultivation. However, for routine day-to-day decisions, farmers rely on their peers, who are their most accessible source.
Peer-to-peer information sharing is central to the group’s functioning but is also where misinformation frequently emerges. Farmers described the inaccuracies as unintentional, which is consistent with definitions of misinformation [23,41,78]. Social connections can outweigh accuracy, leading people to overlook errors even when they intend to share correct information [79]. Peer-to-peer misinformation often arose from genuine attempts to help each other. Farmers regarded misinformation as an honest mistake and were reluctant to consider deliberate harm. For example, farmer 3 said, “I don’t think anybody wants to do that deliberately”. As noted by [75], peer networks operate as real-time support systems, making misinformation harder to detect and more likely to influence behaviour. However, cooperative membership does not necessarily translate into effective action, due to poorly tailored information and limited coordination [80].
Agri-food misinformation manifested as flawed technical guidance. For example, Farmer 3 reported applying fertilizer too close to the roots of leafy greens based on peer advice, leading to crop loss. The group’s common belief that Urea is superior to NPK for loamy soil contradicts FAO guidance on gaseous ammonia loss and soil degradation [81]. These errors may explain why the coefficient on Fertilizer is significantly negative.
Farmers 1–6 noted that farmers had easy access to support and information during meetings, through the group’s WhatsApp platform, or by visiting a nearby farm for guidance from a peer. This reflects a core characteristic of farmer groups where membership facilitates collective access to knowledge, credits, and information [55,82]. The psychological drivers of misinformation show that people are less likely to critically evaluate information from familiar platforms or spaces due to confirmation bias [33,35,48]. Users’ behaviours, preferences, and platform-specific norms also influence how agri-food misinformation is created and circulated [31]. For example, collectivist orientations prioritize conformity and social cohesion, which can override analytical thinking [83,84]. High power-distance cultures, where information from authority figures is rarely questioned, amplify these patterns [83]. Furthermore, in Africa, misinformation is observed to spread in both online and offline spaces [85].
In agricultural information systems, extension agents and farmers jointly serve as the primary conduits for information and knowledge transfer [17,29,43,86,87,88]. Beaman [89] observe that the distance between an individual and the original information source influences the accuracy of the information. Findings indicate that the extension agents may have played an indirect role in the spread of agri-food misinformation. Extension agent 1 explained that information could have been misconstrued, as it flows from extension agents to farmers. “When you pass that information through the leadership, and he is not able to gather all farmers, or he gathers some farmers… by the time the people who initially got the information from him disseminate it to others. There will surely be addition and subtraction.” This suggests that as information moved from formal to informal networks, personal interpretations often distorted original messages. Official communication can be susceptible to misinformation, especially when messages are ambiguous or open to multiple interpretations [36,90]. The use of overly technical language may also prompt farmers to replace original wording with alternative interpretations to make messages more accessible.
5.3. Broader Implications Misinformation carries wide societal costs by reducing compliance with systems, eroding trust in political institutions, and weakening climate action [44,91]. The implications of agri-food misinformation extend to rural land governance and the sustainability of smallholder land systems. Land management practices such as soil conservation, irrigation scheduling, and fertilizer management are highly information-sensitive; small errors in technical guidance can have disproportionate and lasting effects on land productivity [20]. Peer networks serve as the primary conduit for such guidance. As documented in this study, these networks are susceptible to unintentional misinformation, resulting in a systemic vulnerability in how land-use decisions are made at the farm level. This has wider implications for rural land systems: across Lagos State, where peri-urban agricultural land is under pressure from both climatic stress and urban encroachment [64]. The quality of farmers’ decision-making directly influences whether smallholder land remains productive or falls into degradation. Policy responses should therefore extend beyond communication campaigns to include the institutionalization of verification mechanisms within agricultural extension systems and farmer cooperatives structures that can serve as safeguards for sound land management practice [30,82].
Climate misinformation is especially harmful because it weakens confidence in climate science and lowers support for adaptation policies [92]. The interaction of misinformation with cultural beliefs and resource constraints has also reduced the uptake of preventive measures such as vaccines [93]. In agriculture, we highlight the need for stronger agricultural information systems, consistent extension contact, robust verification processes, and improved farmer capacity to assess information from informal channels [75,94,95]. Enhancing extension service delivery, digital literacy, and verification skills is central to reducing misinformation and improving adaptive decision-making [75]. Mapping information flows from source to recipient further clarifies where lapses arise and how feedback loops can be strengthened [95]. Addressing these barriers requires coordinated action from governments, researchers, extension systems, and farming communities. When structural constraints and information quality are tackled together, crop diversification can scale more effectively and support resilient, productive agricultural landscapes [71]. Farmers can also pursue other forms of diversification through broader marketing channels and participation. This include non-farm activities that address income and market risks rather than solely production risks [82]. Recognizing these pathways reinforces the need for integrated information systems that build resilience to climate change.
6. Conclusions Misinformation arises through diverse social interactions, which require frameworks that account for its different forms and the ways it spreads within communities. Agri-food misinformation reduces the uptake of climate adaptation practices among smallholder farmers in Lagos, Nigeria, with direct consequences for land management quality, soil health, land-use sustainability, and food security. Decision-making is shaped by the combined effects of structural vulnerabilities, individual characteristics, and information quality. This extends earlier work that examined these factors separately or focused solely on information availability.
The study has several limitations. Using perception-based measures for agri-food misinformation may introduce subjectivity because responses reflect participants’ personal interpretations and experiences. The cross-sectional design captures only a single point in time, limiting the ability to assess changes or establish causal relationships. The online survey format may have constrained the ability to identify some emotional nuances in participants’ responses. Since the farmers belong to a close-knit association, survey perspectives may be similar; however, selecting interviewees from this group helped explain and interpret patterns observed in the data. Focusing on a single farmers’ association narrows the range of contexts represented and calls for caution in applying the findings more broadly. While the interviews enriched the analysis, a broader sample would have strengthened the generalizability of the results. The framework applied in this study was originally developed to analyze agri-food misinformation in online settings. It offered a useful structure for this study, but it can be adapted to better account for offline information flows. Future research should adopt longitudinal designs to examine how agri-food misinformation shapes climate change adaptation decisions over time and consider how emerging digital tools can support more effective information delivery to farmers.
