1 Introduction Not long after the World Health Organization (WHO) identified COVID-19 as a Public Health Emergency of International Concern (PHEIC) in 2020, the WHO’s Director-General Dr. Tedros Adhanom Ghebreyesus warned of an equally dangerous “infodemic” [1] . An “infodemic” describes the proliferation of information spanning a spectrum from credible to misleading to intentionally deceptive, inundating our everyday communication and making it difficult to discern accurate, reliable knowledge. The continuum of pernicious false health information includes several categories broadly encompassing misinformation (information that is false but shared without intention of causing harm), and disinformation, (false information disseminated with an intent to deceive and is often designed to serve an agenda or political goal) [2] . Concerns about the implications of misinformation on public health, and in particular anti-vaccine sentiment, is not a new phenomenon [3–5] . Online misinformation has been attributed as the primary cause for real-world harms ranging from acts of violence committed against healthcare workers to the proliferation of home ‘cures’ that can lead to medical harm, including death, l during the 2014 Ebola outbreak [6] . The implications for vaccine-preventable diseases could be even more significant, as vaccines are a predominating topic of health misinformation found online and in social media platforms [2] . Infodemics and false information have had an array of effects on population health, including reducing willingness to vaccinate [7] .
Healthcare workers play an incredibly important role in combatting and responding to misinformation for vaccine communication; they routinely emerge as a trusted messenger for vaccine information for community members [8] including in communities with lower levels of trust toward the government broadly, and the health care system specifically, as well as and and vaccine acceptance [9] . Often, healthcare workers are the primary point of contact for guidance and advice about vaccines [10,11] , and for caregivers of young children specifically, the healthcare worker is the primary stakeholder in negotiating a recommended vaccine schedule [12] . Despite this critical role, healthcare workers may lack formal training on best practices for discussing vaccine acceptance and responding to misinformation. Additionally, prior research has identified the issue of healthcare workers themselves as being swayed by health misinformation. During the COVID-19 pandemic, several studies found that healthcare workers expressed hesitancy around critical preventative safety measure such as masking and vaccination [13–15] . Narratives about “injuries” and harm caused by vaccines largely dominate anti-vaccine misinformation pages on various social media platforms [16] , but healthcare workers may not feel adequately prepared to respond to such information. One survey of U.S.-based medical residents found that vaccine safety communication training during their medical training program was either insufficient or absent: only 30.9 % felt they had sufficiently learned vaccine safety communication during their residency program, with 20.6 % reporting that they had not learned at all [17] . Additionally, parents who believe in misinformation, may also believe, for example, that there is a link between autism and vaccines, and may refuse and/or delay vaccinations [15] . In a survey of pediatricians, participants reported that the strategies they used to respond to delayed vaccination schedule requests were not successful in leading to vaccine acceptance [12] . There is a felt need amongst healthcare workers for support and trainings to be able to confidently respond to questions and growing concerns about vaccines, including responding to vaccine misinformation [19] .
In support healthcare workers, our team developed a brief training which aims to equip healthcare workers with evidence-based strategies to identify and reject misinformation. The training employs participatory, problem-based learning so learners can practice and apply techniques presented in the training and simulate real-world encounters with patients and other community members. The goal of the training is to provide learners (in this case, healthcare workers) with concise, evidence-based practices to respond to patients and aid community members with tackling vaccine misinformation through prebunking, debunking, and interpersonal communication strategies.
This article discusses the design and implementation of a training program aimed at equipping healthcare workers with the skills to combat health misinformation, offering theoretical foundations for integrating evidence-based strategies into problem-based learning, and also shares examples and insights gained from its application across diverse learner groups. Details about this the design and implementation can serve both researchers and practitioners interested in developing approaches to mitigate vaccine misinformation in an ever-evolving vaccine misinformation landscape.

3 Training delivery Training took place from January through August 2023 Participants were recruited through convenience sampling, using existing networks with partner institutions and professional organizations. We sought to reach out to health organizations working in LMICs that worked in immunization. Our team liaised with known contacts across these organizations, who then conducted outreach to enroll learners.
Training occurred in eight countries (Cameroon, Guyana, Kenya, India, Mozambique, Nigeria, Philippines and the United States) and was translated from English into three additional languages (French, Spanish and Portuguese). See Table 2 for participant information. The training was delivered both online and in-person, based on the preference of the learners. Although the training was developed to be an online training, early conversations with liaisons felt that an entirely online training might be difficult for their learners due to factors such as digital literacy. Preference was decided based on consultation with liaisons prior to training delivery. Training that was held online lasted approximately three hours, whereas training that was held in-person was either a half-day (approximately four hours) or a full day (approximately six hours). For trainings that were held in-person, the core training content was elaborated with peer-to-peer exercises that mimicked the online activities. The difference between the full and half day trainings was whether lunch and mid-morning and mid-afternoon breaks were included, which, in some contexts, was identified as culturally appropriate for professional development opportunities such as these. We received ethical approval from the Johns Hopkins Bloomberg School of Public Health Institutional Review Board.
4 Curriculum development This training was built on findings from an initial pilot study that was also designed to equip healthcare workers with the skills and confidence required to effectively engage with their patients and community members about health misinformation [20] . The original pilot evaluation started with a landscape analysis of existing training resources to support healthcare workers in responding to health misinformation in low- and middle-income settings (LMICs). Although public health and health communication researchers have identified several effective intervention approaches for responding to vaccine misinformation, we found that few of these interventions were being incorporated into training resources specifically designed for frontline healthcare workers, or those who were engaging directly with beneficiaries. To inform curriculum development, systematic reviews of social media, misinformation, and vaccine acceptance were used to identify misinformation strategies that could be adapted into our training [21,22] . All of the strategies that we identified can be applied in both online and interpersonal settings, and problem-based exercises were developed across both contexts.
One theory that emerged with a robust evidence base in mitigating misinformation was psychological inoculation theory, which has decades of research supporting its application as an effective misinformation intervention. It has frequently been used in political science and combatting “anti-science” movements such as climate change denial [23] , with increasing application for health topics [19,20] . Inoculation theory has found that pre-exposing individuals to “weakened” versions of an argument can aid in building resistance toward real-world examples of the argument, similarly to how a weakened virus can stimulate the immune system to build resistance to the virus if exposed., Exposing individuals to mild doses of misleading or fabricated information can help fortify their existing beliefs against more potent efforts. In our training, we presented learners with “weakened” formats of common misinformation, alongside with refutations, to build their cognitive resistance and enhance their ability to effectively identify and counteract false health information.
Another strategy from psychological inoculation theory, prebunking (pre-emptive debunking), is a promising, scalable intervention based on training individuals to recognize common manipulative strategies used to spread false information. Rather than focusing on countering specific pieces of false information, prebunking provides a more generalizable skillset in identifying misinformation that extends beyond a given topic, providing a more general “immunity” effect [21] . Prebunking is generally considered to have two elements: using “weakened” examples of false information to identify common strategies and “debunking” or refuting false information, and has been found to improve confidence in spotting misinformation [22] . Prebunking was incorporated into this training by presenting healthcare workers with common misinformation techniques and debunking strategies, with the hopes of enabling them to recognize and dismiss false information before it takes hold.
The strategy of debunking, defined as correctly noting and refuting false information, [28] , is also an important intervention for preventing the ongoing spread of misinformation. Our training provided healthcare workers with the opportunity to refute false claims in simulated conversations by presenting factual information, correcting misconceptions, and providing clear evidence to disprove inaccuracies. There is a precedent for this training strategy for healthcare workers – training resources, for example, have adopted debunking interventions for climate change misinformation, tand can be applied to vaccine misinformation. [25,26] .
Feedback after our initial pilot training also identified the importance of including intervention strategies suitable for building trust and empathy in patient-provider interactions. The training included modules on best practices for evidence-based communication approaches for healthcare workers including presumptive communication, motivational interviewing, and information tailoring [27] .
As the landscape analysis identified few resources employing evidence-based intervention designed to meet the needs of healthcare workers, a stakeholder advisory group of client-facing healthcare workers and misinformation researchers [n = 6] was assembled to inform a pedagogical and training delivery approach that would center the needs of healthcare workers in LMICs. An initial pilot training was developed based on recommendations for accessibility, information needs, and confirmation that limited resources currently exist to meet healthcare workers’ need for support in responding to vaccine misinformation both in person and in digital contexts. The pilot training included n = 20 healthcare workers from 9 countries. Feedback from pre- and post-training surveys was used to provide refinements to the content and revision of the instructional approach.
The most common piece of feedback from the pilot trial’s testing was to use a different platform that allowed for more engaging content that was designed to be accessible on a portable device like a tablet or smartphone. Therefore, a key consideration was to optimize the learning experience for healthcare workers, both with respect to the instructional design as well as the duration of the training. Traditionally, evidence-based insights from other disciplines to optimize learner engagement have largely been absent from health professional education [28] , although this has been changing over the past decade [33] . Additionally, during the initial pilot process, healthcare workers and stakeholders had identified the challenge that although vaccine misinformation is a topic of great interest, learners had many restrictions on their time and opportunity for professional development. As healthcare workers are often confronting heavy workloads and many competing priorities, it can be difficult to undertake additional training opportunities [34,35] It was therefore critically important to design learning materials to be succinct. Stakeholders advised that training materials should be accessible on mobile devices like smartphones and that modules should be “chunkable” into short segments.
To accomplish this goal, our team employed principles from problem-based learning (PBL) to inform the instructional approach to enhance learner involvement and comprehension. This included a review existing PBL literature and an exploration of relevant sources to translate the existing curricular content from its formerly didactic, lecture-based approach into self-directed learning activities.
5 Instructional approach Problem-based learning (PBL) is a teaching paradigm well-supported by educational theories that shifts away from “teacher-centered” to “learner-centered” instruction, helping to nurture a more holistic learning experience that results in habit-building rather than fact memorization [36] . In medical education, PBL is considered an important technique to prepare learners for real-world communication and reasoning scenarios by immersing them in authentic challenges that require critical thinking and practical application of knowledge [37] . A common application of problem-based learning in medical education includes simulation trainings, in which learners are able to practice new skills and decision-making in a safe and controlled environment [38] . There are numerous frameworks and models used for problem-based learning, and applications of PBL vary across learning environments. We used an analytical framework developed to use problem-based learning for medical education, which included four criteria for effective student learning derived from cognitive theory [39] ( Table 3 ).
Below, we will further explore the principles of problem-based learning by explaining how we applied these principles in our training program to optimize learning outcomes.
5.1 Learners are active processors of information Whilst the pilot version of the training relied heavily on recorded lectures and readings, to incorporate PBL into our learning activities, we designed simulations that invited learners to be actively engaged in the learning process rather than passively receiving information. This involves critical thinking, analysis, and synthesis of knowledge, as well as applying what they have learned to solve problems, make decisions, and create new understanding. Active processing requires learners to interact with the material, ask questions, make connections to prior knowledge, and reflect on their learning experiences, thereby enhancing retention and comprehension.
One example of this principle was allowing learners to practice how to respond to a particular piece of misinformation using a card sorting activity designed to teach healthcare workers how to minimize the chances of the “backfire effect” in their communication by responding to a hypothetical piece of misinformation (about the HPV vaccine causing cervical cancer) from a patient empathetically and respectfully, and avoiding common pitfalls that might unintentionally reinforce false beliefs (e See Fig. 1 for an example). This dynamic approach allows learners to categorize and organize different responses and interact with materials – including feedback if the card was sorted into the incorrect pile (in which case it would return to the drawing pile). Learners could repeat this activity as many times as they wanted, with the responses continuing to emerge in random orders. This “gamification” approach had the added advantage of being enjoyable, an enabler of adult learnings which can help to motivate learners and encourage concentration [39] .
5.2 Prior knowledge is activated, and new knowledge is built upon it Another guiding principle for our PBL learning activities was grounded in the educational theory of constructivism, or allowing learners to build on their pre-existing knowledge and experiences to incorporate new information into their pre-existing knowledge [38] . In earlier iterations of the training, key concepts and definitions (e.g., “misinformation”) were presented in the very beginning of the training. In our training, the content was first grounded prior to providing definitions through a narrative series of historical depictions and personal reflection prompts ( Fig. 2 ), to present learners with high-profile, real-world examples and reflect on examples they have seen in their day-to-day work of the key concepts and definitions that would be introduced only later. Although a subtle shift, by activating prior knowledge, we were able to build a foundation upon which knew knowledge and strategies could be constructed, facilitating deeper comprehension and integration of new information.
5.3 Knowledge is acquired in a meaningful context Acquiring knowledge in a meaningful context means learning new information within a relevant and practical setting that mirrors real-world situations, enhancing understanding and retention by making the learning experience more relatable and applicable to everyday life or professional practice. To incorporate these principes into our training, we used realistic imagery and created experimental simulations to replicate real-world examples. In one exercise, learners had to discern different types of misinformation (e.g., “disinformation” versus “conspiracy theories”) by selecting among images and content created utilizing software to emulate the interfaces of prominent social media platforms ( Fig. 3 ). This was a variation from the formal training which relied on static animations (e.g., cartoons, clip art). By using real-world-like images, we aimed to connect learners to authentic situations and contexts and offer concrete examples relatable to their own experiences. Evidence suggests that the application of real-world imagery and experiential scenarios can help increase learners’ likelihood of engaging with materials, seeing relevance to their lives, and retain knowledge more easily [41–43] .
5.4 Learners have opportunities for elaboration and organization of knowledge Lastly, it was important for learners to be able to combine multiple skills and strategies and practice in a more complex scenario. When learners have opportunities for elaboration and organization of knowledge, it means they can engage in activities that encourage them to expand upon and structure their understanding of the material. Elaboration involves adding detail, making connections, and providing examples related to the information being learned. In our misinformation training, healthcare workers participated in simulated dialogue scenarios ( Fig. 4 ) to practice and reinforce multiple concepts that had been introduced over several modules. In a simulated conversation, learners could interact and practice concepts, with prompts affirming “correct” responses and providing additional context for “incorrect” answers. In these scenarios, learners are not assessed whether they responded correctly or not and have the opportunity to re-engage with the learning activities repeatedly. For in-person training sessions, learners had the additional opportunity to practice these dialogues with their peers.
6 Training evaluation Participants were asked multiple choice questions about the training including topics covered, usability of the platform, and opportunities for improvement. We also solicited detailed feedback during the post-training survey to encourage an extensive evaluation of the training content. While 407 participated in the trainings, not all consented to completing pre- and post-tests and having those data examined as part of a study; additionally, not all learners finished both tests, and incomplete surveys were not included. In total, 287 pre- and post- tests were completed and included for analysis, for a response rate of 70 %. Participants were unable to skip the Likert scale questions; consequently, for the 287 individuals who consented and took the post-test, there was a 100 % response rate to the Likert scale questions. As free text responses were primarily to solicit additional information, they were optional. 85 (64.5 %) participants provided free text responses to the survey.
Participants responded very positively to some of the key constructs about their training experience ( Fig. 5 ), with the overall utility of the training emerging as the top-ranking construct. Additional input was solicited whether objectives were met; the organization of the course; clarity of learning materials; the overall format of the course; enjoyability of the course; and ease use of the learning platform. Lastly, the accessibility of the training materials was the lowest-performing indicator, with two-thirds of participants responding “strongly agree.”.
In addition to the multiple-choice questions, we also invited participants to share free text responses about what they enjoyed about the training and how they felt it could be improved. Participants appreciated practicality, contextualization, and active learning. Continued learning was also a theme that emerged.
6.1 Evaluation themes
6.1.1 The value of active, engaging learning The participants appreciated that the training prioritized active learning, fostering an engaging and dynamic educational experience. By engaging in real-world problem-solving scenarios, trainees felt a deeper understanding and connection to the subject matter, making the learning experience both effective and enjoyable. This approach provided a refreshing departure from traditional methods, fostering a more dynamic and hands-on approach to skill acquisition. This was described by one participant from Nigeria as “ The training was engaging and interactive. This made the training fun and easy to understand .”.
Another trainee from the Philippines noted, “ The training was engaging and interactive. This made the training fun and easy to understand,” with another trainee from Mozambique agreeing the training was “ Muito inovadora e faciltou a aprendizagem. [Very innovative and easy to learn].” .
Even still, some participants felt that the training could have benefited from even more examples, with one from Guyana noting, “ I think there is a need for more simulations so that participants can fully grasp the information.” .
6.1.2 The importance of ongoing practice and learning opportunities Participants also mentioned that refresher trainings can help ensure that individuals maintain and update their skills. This was described by one participant from Nigeria, “ It should be done at least twice a year for trainees just to help refresh .” Another trainee from Nigeria agreed, “ Organize a refresher course after some time .” One participant from the Cameroon also thought the training would be more effective over the course of a few days, “ Il serait bien de continuer avec ce style de formation et également sur plusieurs jours. [It would be good to continue with this style of training and also over several days.” By offering learning opportunities may not only enhance professional competence but also foster a culture of continuous improvement and responsive in the ever-evolving landscape of vaccine misinformation.
6.1.3 Learning content is better received when it has been contextualized Examples representative from the real-world were appreciated by participants, as described by one trainee from Kenya, “ Citing examples from local events makes it more interesting and understanding .” Yet several participants felt the training could have been enhanced and made more valuable if it had been further contextualized to specific settings and scenarios. As one trainee from the Philippines noted, “ More specific examples [are needed] based on real scenarios in Philippine locale .” Similarly, another participant from Nigeria expressed, “Over time, this needs to be context-specific by country, sociocultural etc.” .
There was a sentiment that further contextualized examples would have better equipped them to apply the acquired knowledge in a variety of real-world situations, making the training more universally applicable, as a Mozambique participant described, “ Talvez explorer exercícios com exemplos ao nível local,de desafios na vacinação. [Maybe explore exercises with local-level examples of vaccination challenges.” Another participant from the Philippines agreed, “ For the effective communication part, would be helpful to have local examples to capture social and language nuances.” .
In addition, examples that appear familiar can help to make the training more appealing to the learner, as an Indian participant observed, “ Indian images and language option to make it more attractive to learning.” .
6.1.4 An appreciation for relevance and ease-of-use Relevance ensures that learners see the immediate applicability of what they are learning, which in turn fosters engagement and motivation, while ease of use minimizes barriers to accessing information, optimizing the learning process for efficient comprehension and retention. Many participants noted that the training delivery and content was both user friendly as well as applicable to their everyday experiences. As one trainee from Mozambique described, “ A plataforma e muito boa simples prática e rápida precisamos. [The platform is very good, simple, practical and quick].” The emphasis on practical application resonated with them, creating a more meaningful and enjoyable learning environment. As described by one participant from Nigeria, “ [I] gained valuable knowledge through the training. Learnt errors made in past times while attempting to debunk misinformation and feel better equipped to address such.” .
There seemed to be an appreciate for the immediate applicability of training content, which one participant from Kenya described as, “ The content was relevant and straight to the point ,” with another trainee from the United States noting, “ It was to the point and precise.” .
6.2 Evaluation themes and principles of PBL Many of the themes the emerged from our evaluation spoke directly to the principles we had intentionally considered in the design of the training and learning activities. This has been a useful exercise to explore how the principles of problem-based learning can be leveraged to address vaccine misinformation, whilst also providing learnings for future improvement.
The notion that learning was made easier by engaging activities showed that learners appreciated the opportunity to actively process information and “walk through” the learning content, rather than passively absorbing information. This supports PBL teaching practices that prioritize active learning over rote memorization of theoretical constructs, fostering an engaging and dynamic educational experience.
One challenge of our training was the heterogeneity of the learners, as “healthcare workers” could range from a community health worker to a medical doctor. It was therefore challenging to build upon and activate prior knowledge, as these foundations are variable. This was achieved in our instructional design by highlighting highly publicized global examples and encouraging internal reflections. However, the focus on continued learning and the need for refresher trainings identified by several participants supports the idea that learners are thinking forward about their current learning and implications for future learning, and how to work towards continuous improvement, retention, and mastery.
Given the high ratings in usefulness of the training, it may be interpreted that content of the course was introduced in a meaningful context. That said, there was considerable feedback that the training would have been even more useful if the examples were even more contextualized. This can be a difficult balance for trainers to balance - rather than trying to design a resource that fulfills the needs of all potential learners in any context, our goal was to create a robust tool that can be adapted to the needs of different healthcare workers in different contexts: ultimately maximizing the accessibility of the training resource as much as possible. Meeting the needs of all individuals globally poses considerable challenges, and it is important to consider the delicate balance between contextualization and meaningfulness.
The appreciation for relevant, practice-based learning demonstrated that activities designed to encourage trainees to elaborate upon and organize multiple skill sets. The recognition of the value of practice-based learning in the training highlighted that learners were provided with meaningful opportunities to both elaborate on and organize their recently acquired skills and knowledge. This emphasis on relevant, hands-on practice contributed to a more comprehensive and applied understanding of the training content.
7 Limitations There were several important limitations that should be considered with our findings. First, the self-reported feedback and evaluation measures are reliant upon subjective measures, which are influenced by personal biases, for example, the “halo effect” [44] of a reputable institution delivering the training may predispose learners to having a favorable experience. The immediate timing of the feedback also only offers a short-term focus, rather than assessing long-term impact of the training on job performance or behavior change. Lastly, our findings could be impact by self-selection bias of the individuals who initially signed up for the training, as these might represent individuals who were already interested or motivated, and therefore more enthusiastic or knowledgeable about the subject matter. This bias might distort the evaluation, as the characteristics of this self-selected group may not accurately represent the broader population.
8 Recommendations and conclusion We learned that principles of problem-based learning were well-received, and that trainings might be even more effective if they are contextualized to specific challenges such as localized immunization issues [e.g., specific vaccines or populations] or culturally relevant content [e.g., relevant social media sites, memes, or humor], although there is an inherent tension between the benefits associated with tailored content and the additional resources that customization requires. A possible middle ground could be an approach with an inherent “core” curriculum in a modular format that also allows for the possibility to tailor examples to resonate with different cohorts [45] . Though we provide examples generalizable to a global audience, these examples may be easily tailored with additional discussion prompts or examples to allow learners to provide their own more locally or personally relevant experiences or examples.
Healthcare workers may have significant responsibility to respond to misinformation and vaccine hesitancy, but they may not feel equipped with the skills to sufficiently respond. A Lancet commission identified misinformation as a new curricular topic for health profession education in the post COVID-19 context [46] .
The results of our training adds to the body of evidence that participatory, problem-based learning is effective and important for ensuring healthcare professionals [36,47,48] and shows its can also be replicated for vaccine communication and misinformation response training. A recent scoping review of educational interventions aimed at communicating skills with vaccine hesitant individuals highlights a need for guidance to select and tailor these types of trainings [49] . We hope that this publication can serve as a resource for practitioners and researchers to implement similar, contextualized trainings.
