Abstract
This article examines the impact of alarmist and fake economic news on consumer behaviour in the context of the growing prevalence of fake news. The study is structured into two components: a theoretical section addressing the mechanisms of misinformation dissemination and its effects, and an empirical section based on a survey conducted on a nationally controlled quota-based sociodemographic sample (N = 1,066). The research aims to investigate how media exposure, the perception of fake news, information credibility, and concern influence economic behaviour. The analysis employs structural equation modelling (SEM) and distinguishes three types of responses: behavioural reactions, consumption reduction, and saving behaviour. The findings highlight the central role of concern as a key determinant of economic behaviour, as well as the existence of distinct response mechanisms depending on the type of behaviour analysed. Overall, the study underscores the complex relationship between misinformation and economic decision-making.
Keywords: fake news, alarmist news, economic behaviour, survey, Romania, SEM, consumers.
JEL Classification: D12, D14, D91.
Introduction
Over the past decade, the fast development of the digital environment has profoundly transformed the production, distribution, and consumption of information, fostering the expansion of the fake news phenomenon. In this context, alarmist economic news has emerged as a distinct form of informational content, characterized by high emotional intensity and the ability to amplify perceptions of risk and uncertainty, with direct effects on consumer behaviour.
The literature highlights that the impact of information depends not only on its content but also on the interaction between cognitive, emotional, and contextual factors, with economic behaviour emerging as the outcome of an "exposure-evaluation-response" process. Although the body of research on fake news and misinformation has expanded considerably over the past decade, most studies have focused on the mechanisms underlying the detection, dissemination, and credibility of false information or on its effects within political and electoral contexts (Vishwakarma & Jain, 2020; Caled & Silva, 2022; Di Domenico et al., 2021). Significantly fewer studies have examined the economic effects of alarmist news and fake news on consumer behaviour, while research simultaneously modelling cognitive, emotional, and behavioural dimensions remains limited, particularly in Eastern European and emerging contexts (Pierri & Ceri, 2019; Zareie & Sakellariou, 2021).
In this context, the present study seeks to address this gap in the literature by developing an explanatory model based on data from Romania that integrates media exposure, fake news perceptions, information credibility, concern, and consumers' economic responses. The selection of Romania as the context of analysis is justified from several perspectives. First, Romania represents a setting characterised by intensive use of digital platforms and social media, combined with fluctuating levels of institutional trust and vulnerability to misinformation, features documented in recent literature on information ecosystems in Central and Eastern Europe (Helberger, 2020; Caled & Silva, 2022). Second, the socioeconomic environment marked in recent years by economic uncertainty, inflation, the ongoing war in the region, and the increasing circulation of alarmist news makes Romania a particularly relevant context for investigating the relationship between misinformation and economic behaviour. Finally, the international literature still provides limited empirical evidence on the economic effects of fake news in Eastern European countries, which grants the present study important contextual and comparative relevance.
The article is organized into several sections. The first section develops the theoretical framework concerning fake news, alarmist news, the dissemination of misinformation, and the mechanisms underlying information credibility and reception. The methodological section presents the research design, sample characteristics, measurement instruments, and the Structural Equation Models (SEM) employed in the analysis. Subsequently, the empirical findings related to the three explanatory models are presented and discussed. The final section summarizes the main conclusions, outlines implications for public policy, addresses the study's limitations, and proposes directions for future research.
This study contributes to the existing literature by integrating perspectives from misinformation research and economic behaviour studies, by developing an explanatory model that combines cognitive, emotional, and behavioural dimensions, and by providing empirical evidence regarding the effects of misinformation on consumption patterns, saving behaviour, and immediate behavioural responses. The novelty of the study lies in its differentiated analysis of these three types of economic responses within a nationally representative applied framework.
1. Theoretical benchmarks regarding the impact of alarmist and fake news on economic behaviour
1.1. Fake news and alarmist news - complex phenomena of the digital ecosystem. Specificity and mechanisms of action
The literature highlights the complex, multidimensional and intentional nature of fake news, defined as fabricated or distorted information created to mislead and influence public perceptions (Mosinzova et al., 2019; Narwal, 2018; Di Domenico et al., 2021).
A relevant synthesis of the current state of research on fake news detection is provided by Vishwakarma and Jain (2020), who examine the main methodological approaches and challenges associated with this field. The authors highlight that the transition of information consumption toward social media platforms and search engines has diminished traditional control over information flows, facilitating the rapid dissemination of false information and increasing the risks of misinformation at both social and economic levels (Vishwakarma & Jain, 2020). A notable example discussed by the authors is the impact of a false news report in 2013, which generated losses of approximately USD 130 billion in the stock market, illustrating the vulnerability of economic systems to misinformation (Vishwakarma & Jain, 2020). Furthermore, the study draws attention to existing limitations, such as the lack of standardised datasets and the difficulty of validating information in real time, emphasising the need for interdisciplinary approaches and more robust detection systems (Vishwakarma & Jain, 2020).
Fake news is characterised by manipulative intentionality, rapid dissemination through digital networks, and a strong emotional impact on audiences (Hu et al., 2025). Hu et al. (2025) propose an integrative perspective on the entire process of information dissemination and identify three essential characteristics of fake news: intentional creation, heteromorphic transmission, and controversial reception.
This perspective aligns with other contributions that define fake news as fabricated or distorted content designed to mislead the public and generate economic, political, or ideological effects (Narwal, 2018; Pierri & Ceri, 2019; Di Domenico et al., 2021). Existing studies further emphasise that false information circulates within a digital environment characterised by high accessibility, rapid dissemination, informational fragmentation, and the weakening of traditional journalistic validation mechanisms (Bean, 2017; Caled & Silva, 2022; Elhadad et al., 2019; Vishwakarma & Jain, 2020). Under these conditions, fake news becomes not merely a form of false informational content but also a product of the economic and structural logic of digital platforms, where visibility, virality, and content monetisation incentivise the dissemination of sensationalist and emotionally charged messages (Bean, 2017; Helberger, 2020; Khan et al., 2019).
In recent years, social media platforms and alternative information sources have become dominant channels for accessing information, enabling users to produce and disseminate unverified content. This transformation has fundamentally altered the processes of information production and consumption, shifting users from passive recipients to active participants in response to an increasing demand for real-time information (Andrews et al., 2016; Shklovski et al., 2008).
However, the dynamic and viral nature of these platforms facilitates the rapid spread of misinformation, while the large volume of content makes verification increasingly difficult, even for professional journalists (Tacchini et al., 2017; Liu et al., 2015; Starbird et al., 2014). The pressure to publish information rapidly further contributes to the dissemination of incomplete or insufficiently verified information, thereby increasing the vulnerability of the media to manipulation (Marwick & Lewis, 2017).
These processes contribute to the erosion of public trust in mainstream media and encourage the use of alternative sources of information, thereby increasing the risk of exposure to misinformation and negatively affecting both individuals' critical evaluation capacities and the functioning of democratic systems (Ardèvol-Abreu & de Zúñiga, 2017; Bennett & Livingston, 2018).
Within this context, the literature emphasises that effectively addressing misinformation cannot rely on unidimensional approaches but instead requires interdisciplinary perspectives integrating journalistic, educational, institutional, and technological dimensions (Caled & Silva, 2022; Niță & Ilie, 2015).
Consequently, fake news research has experienced substantial growth since 2016, being increasingly approached from interdisciplinary perspectives encompassing communication studies, psychology, computer science, political science, and economics (Ha et al., 2021). Similarly, Mosinzova et al. (2019) and Pherson et al. (2021) argue that fake news should be understood within the broader framework of information disorder, which includes misinformation, disinformation, and malinformation, all of which have the potential to shape perceptions, influence behaviours, and undermine public trust.
On the other hand, alarmist news represents a particular form of misinformation constructed around emotional appeal, perceived risk, and uncertainty, with direct effects on individual behaviours, including economic decision-making (Tucker et al., 2018; Pherson et al., 2021; Dumitrescu & Dan, 2025). Unlike fake news, alarmist news is not defined exclusively by the falsity of its content but rather by the manner in which information is framed and presented, characterised by the amplification of risk, uncertainty, and negative emotions.
Alarmist news may include both false and factually accurate information; however, such information is presented in an exaggerated, selective, or decontextualised manner with the aim of eliciting intense emotional responses, such as fear, anxiety, or panic (Narwal, 2018; Pierri & Ceri, 2019). In this regard, alarmist news can be considered a functional subcategory of misinformation-related content, although it cannot be reduced solely to this category.
The literature suggests that the effectiveness of alarmist news stems from the interaction between cognitive, emotional, and structural mechanisms. Ideological congruence and repeated exposure to information consistent with pre-existing beliefs increase the likelihood of its acceptance and dissemination (Bauer & Clemm von Hohenberg, 2020), while cognitive biases, such as confirmation bias and peripheral information processing, diminish the critical evaluation of messages (Caled & Silva, 2022; Singh et al., 2020). At the same time, the digital environment facilitates the circulation of emotionally charged and potentially viral content (Bean, 2017; Helberger, 2020), whereas the use of simplified language, negative framing, and evocative visual elements enhances both the attractiveness of such content and its likelihood of being shared (Horne et al., 2019; Parikh & Atrey, 2018).
A particularly important aspect concerns the impact of alarmist news on economic behaviour. Alarmist news may trigger disproportionate responses, such as precautionary consumption, capital withdrawals, and panic-driven behaviours, thereby influencing both market dynamics and individual economic decision-making (Pierri & Ceri, 2019; Vishwakarma & Jain, 2020; Zareie & Sakellariou, 2021).
Based on the reviewed literature, we conclude that fake news involves intentionally fabricated information aimed at misleading audiences, whereas alarmist news may encompass either factual or false information presented in an exaggerated manner, with the primary effect of eliciting emotional activation and amplifying risk perception. Although these two phenomena may overlap, they should not be regarded as synonymous.
1.2. The Propagation of Misinformation
A second important theoretical pillar of this research concerns the manner in which false information spreads within digital environments. The literature consistently indicates that fake news propagates more rapidly, extensively, and deeply than verified information, particularly when the content carries a strong emotional appeal, is perceived as novel, or activates pre-existing fears and beliefs (Ha et al., 2021; Mosinzova et al., 2019; Wang et al., 2018). In this regard, analyses of "information cascades" demonstrate that the scope and intensity of dissemination depend on the sequence of network connections and the dynamics of online content circulation (Chen et al., 2019). Similarly, Mishra (2020), Torgheh et al. (2021), and Xie et al. (2020) emphasise that the spread of fake news is influenced not only by message content but also by social relationships, actors' positions within networks, user characteristics, the temporal dynamics of dissemination, and the perceived credibility of those involved.
Several studies further suggest that digital platforms encourage this type of dissemination through their very operational logic. Helberger (2020) argues that platforms no longer function merely as channels for transmitting information but have become actors that shape public attention through algorithmic mechanisms. Along similar lines, Tucker et al. (2018) highlight the cyclical relationship between social media, polarisation, and misinformation, while Pherson et al. (2021) and Piazza (2022) demonstrate that algorithms, micro-targeting practices, and automation contribute to the reinforcement of echo chambers, the radicalisation of opinions, and the amplification of emotional responses. Consequently, alarmist news spreads not only because of its intrinsic content characteristics but also due to its adaptation to a digital infrastructure that favours the visibility of emotionally charged and controversial messages.
1.3. Information Credibility and News Reception
A third theoretical dimension concerns the mechanisms through which individuals assign credibility to information and come to believe or disseminate alarmist content. Bauer and Clemm von Hohenberg (2020) demonstrate that, within online environments, distinguishing between genuine and false sources becomes increasingly difficult, particularly when misinformation sources replicate credibility markers typically associated with legitimate sources. The authors further highlight that information that is ideologically congruent with pre-existing attitudes is perceived as more credible and is more likely to be shared. Moreover, repeated exposure strengthens trust even in false sources, thereby confirming the role of motivated reasoning mechanisms in shaping information acceptance and dissemination (Bauer & Clemm von Hohenberg, 2020).
This conclusion is supported by several studies emphasising the role of confirmation bias, selective exposure, and repeated exposure effects in maintaining inaccurate beliefs (Caled & Silva, 2022; Di Domenico et al., 2021; Ha et al., 2021; Pierri & Ceri, 2019). Tsfati et al. (2020) further suggest that even mainstream media may inadvertently contribute to reinforcing the perceived credibility of false information through the repetition of such content during fact-checking processes, thereby activating psychological mechanisms such as the illusory truth effect. Van Heekeren (2019) complements this perspective by arguing that the impact of fake news is amplified by the general perception of an unreliable information environment, which erodes trust and intensifies uncertainty.
In addition to source characteristics and informational congruence, credibility is also influenced by the form in which messages are presented. The interaction between textual and multimedia elements-including images, headlines, and other visual cues-may enhance both perceived credibility and emotional responses (Parikh & Atrey, 2018). From a multimodal perspective, Singh et al. (2020) argue that fake news exploits peripheral information processing through the use of visual and emotional stimuli, while Horne et al. (2019) highlight the use of simplified, negative, and emotionally charged language that increases the attractiveness of messages and facilitates their acceptance.
These findings are particularly relevant for the analysis of alarmist news, as they demonstrate that the reception of such content is not solely a rational process but is mediated by the perceived credibility of the source, congruence with pre-existing beliefs and fears, and the emotional and stylistic characteristics of the message (Bauer & Clemm von Hohenberg, 2020; Horne et al., 2019; Parikh & Atrey, 2018; Singh et al., 2020; Calderaro et al., 2025).
1.4. Methodological Approaches in the Detection of Alarmist and Fake News
The literature on fake news and alarmist news detection highlights that the identification and evaluation of false information involve complex methodological mechanisms centred on content analysis, social context, and dissemination patterns, with hybrid approaches demonstrating the highest levels of performance (Hu et al., 2025; Mahid et al., 2018; Mosinzova et al., 2019; Rana et al., 2018; Vishwakarma & Jain, 2020). Within this context, machine learning and deep learning techniques have emerged as dominant tools, although the dynamic nature of misinformation and the absence of common methodological standards continue to limit the stability and generalisability of findings (Manzoor et al., 2019; Meneses Silva et al., 2021).
Studies focusing on content analysis indicate that linguistic style, semantic structure, and the emotional characteristics of messages constitute relevant indicators for identifying false content and help explain the high appeal of alarmist news (de Oliveira et al., 2020; Gereme & Zhu, 2019; Jadhav & Thepade, 2019; Kumar et al., 2019). Highly emotional language, the extensive use of stylistic devices, and dramatic framing increase the likelihood that information will be accepted and shared (Kumar et al., 2019), whereas corroboration from multiple and diverse sources may function as an indicator of perceived credibility (Dementieva & Panchenko, 2020; Kumar et al., 2021).
Furthermore, the literature emphasises that the veracity and impact of information depend not solely on content characteristics but also on the relationship between the message, its source, and its audience. Source credibility, reputation, engagement levels, and the structure of social relationships all influence the acceptance and dissemination of information (Ramkissoon & Mohammed, 2020; Rasool et al., 2019; Reis et al., 2019; Wang et al., 2018; Xie et al., 2020; Mishra, 2020; Torgheh et al., 2021). Consequently, mitigating the effects of misinformation requires not only its detection but also efforts aimed at reducing its spread (Zareie & Sakellariou, 2021).
For the purposes of the present study, the relevance of these contributions lies not in the automated detection of fake news but rather in demonstrating that the processing of economic information involves successive stages of exposure, evaluation, and response, simultaneously influenced by perceived credibility, information verification processes, and the emotional impact of messages (Di Domenico et al., 2021; Bauer & Clemm von Hohenberg, 2020; Petty & Cacioppo, 1986). From this perspective, the Structural Equation Models (SEM) employed in this study enable the operationalisation of these mechanisms by integrating variables related to fake news perceptions, information credibility, information verification, and analytical rationality, as well as the emotional and behavioural responses associated with alarmist economic news (Kline, 2023; Hair et al., 2022).
Moreover, recent literature indicates that the investigation of fake news and related behaviours is no longer limited to automated detection approaches or machine learning techniques. Increasingly, researchers employ Structural Equation Modelling (SEM) and Partial Least Squares Structural Equation Modelling (PLS-SEM) to examine the relationships among information exposure, credibility assessments, cognitive processes, and behavioural responses (Tahat et al., 2022; Oanh et al., 2023; Obadă & Dabija, 2022; Mi & Apuke, 2024; Mombeuil et al., 2025; Melchior et al., 2026).
1.5. Fake News, Alarmist News, Uncertainty and Economic Behaviour
A central element of our study is that the literature does not treat fake news solely as a communication issue, but also as a phenomenon with direct economic effects. Several studies explicitly show that false information can influence markets, generate disproportionate collective reactions, and alter both individual and aggregate economic decisions (Elhadad et al., 2019; Narwal, 2018; Pierri & Ceri, 2019; Vishwakarma & Jain, 2020; Zareie & Sakellariou, 2021). Di Domenico et al. (2021) emphasise that fake news can lead to confusion, declining trust, and the adoption of economic behaviours based on erroneous information, while Bauer and Clemm von Hohenberg (2020) demonstrate that messages aligned with the public's fears and beliefs can quickly gain credibility and subsequently influence the acceptance and dissemination of similar information.
Narwal (2018), Pherson et al. (2021), and Piazza (2022) emphasise that misinformation and alarmist content amplify uncertainty and anxiety, generating panic reactions and disproportionate perceptions of risk. In addition, Mosinzova et al. (2019), Khan et al. (2019), and Kumar et al. (2021) show that the virality of false information is closely linked to its emotional nature and its ability to trigger rapid affective responses. Under these conditions, alarmist economic news can influence consumption behaviour, saving decisions, the postponement of purchases, and other forms of defensive behaviour, particularly in contexts of volatility and uncertainty.
Similarly, Tsfati et al. (2020) and Van Heekeren (2019) highlight that the effects of misinformation are not limited to the factual falsity of content, but also relate to the broader climate of distrust and uncertainty it generates. Thus, even when information is subsequently corrected, audiences may retain a sense of risk or instability, which in turn affects economic decision-making. This perspective is also consistent with Helberger (2020), who argues that digital platforms influence not only access to information but also the processes of opinion formation and the prioritisation of issues perceived as urgent or threatening.
From an economic and behavioural perspective, the effects of alarmist news can be understood in relation to the literature on uncertainty and risk perception. Akerlof and Shiller (2009), as well as Shiller (2017, 2020), argue that economic decisions are influenced not only by objective indicators but also by economic narratives, emotions, and collective expectations, all of which have the potential to amplify either optimism or panic. At the same time, the behavioural economics literature suggests that individuals tend to respond disproportionately to information perceived as threatening, exhibiting loss aversion and a preference for defensive strategies under conditions of uncertainty (Kahneman & Tversky, 1979; Kahneman, 2011). From this perspective, fake news and alarmist news may intensify risk perceptions and transform informational uncertainty into concrete economic responses.
1.6. Empirical Model Development
The impact of fake news and alarmist news on economic behaviour can be explained through a sequential process of exposure-evaluation-response (Hu et al., 2025; Di Domenico et al., 2021). The proposed model integrates cognitive, emotional and behavioural dimensions, highlighting the role of the digital environment in amplifying exposure (Khan et al., 2019; Elhadad et al., 2019), as well as the role of evaluative and emotional mechanisms in shaping economic responses (Bauer & Clemm von Hohenberg, 2020; Kumar et al., 2019).
At the same time, information verification, rationality and trust in institutions contribute to moderating the effects of misinformation (Gereme & Zhu, 2019; Tucker et al., 2018), while the use of distinct dependent variables reflects the heterogeneous nature of economic responses (Vishwakarma & Jain, 2020).
Therefore, the relationships examined within the SEM models do not represent mere statistical associations, but rather operational representations of validated theoretical mechanisms that describe how alarmist economic information is processed and translated into economic behaviour.
The empirical model proposed in this study aligns with an increasingly prominent line of research within the literature on fake news and information-related behaviours, in which Structural Equation Modelling (SEM) and Partial Least Squares Structural Equation Modelling (PLS-SEM) are employed to simultaneously investigate the relationships among perceptions, credibility, information evaluation, and behavioural responses. The literature emphasises that structural models enable the testing of complex explanatory mechanisms while allowing for the simultaneous integration of both latent and observed variables (Hair et al., 2022; Kline, 2023). Similarly, recent studies have employed SEM to model the dissemination of fake news, the identification of false information, and information-sharing behaviours across diverse digital and social contexts (Tahat et al., 2022; Oanh et al., 2023; Obadă & Dabija, 2022; Mi & Apuke, 2024; Mombeuil et al., 2025; Melchior et al., 2026).
2. Research methodology
The research conducted for this study was quantitative, based on the administration of a questionnaire to the general population of Romania.
Carrying out such a study at national level is justified by the particularities of the current informational context, marked by fragmentation and low levels of trust in the media (Reuters Institute, 2023). At the same time, difficulties in recognising misinformation, particularly among younger populations, indicate a relevant level of informational vulnerability (European Parliament, 2025). Moreover, the need to strengthen media literacy is institutionally acknowledged, including through initiatives of the European Commission targeting Romania (European Commission, 2026).
The study was conducted on a non-probability sample of 1,066 respondents, structured using sociodemographic quotas based on gender and area of residence. These quotas were established according to the distribution of the adult resident population in Romania, aged 18 years and over, totalling 15,405,320 individuals, based on data extracted from Tempo Online of the National Institute of Statistics. Although the snowball sampling procedure does not allow for probabilistic representativeness in the strict sense, the quota control approach aimed to obtain a sample structure closely reflecting the characteristics of the target population.
Initially, 1,126 individuals completed the questionnaire. Following the assessment of response completeness and consistency, 1,066 valid cases were retained for the main analysis. The quotabased sample yielded a maximum margin of error of ±3% at a 95% confidence level. The final sample included 513 men, of whom 257 resided in urban areas and 256 in rural areas, and 553 women, including 297 from urban areas and 256 from rural areas.
The effective number of observations used in the Structural Equation Models (SEM) varied depending on data availability for the variables included in each model and on the estimation procedures applied, particularly listwise deletion for handling missing values. Consequently, the sample size reported for individual SEM analyses may be smaller than the overall study sample.
Respondents were selected through a non-probability snowball sampling procedure controlled through sociodemographic quotas. Initially, adult participants from all counties of Romania, representing both urban and rural areas and including both women and men, were invited to complete the questionnaire. Subsequently, participants were asked to share the survey link with other individuals presenting similar sociodemographic characteristics until the predefined sample structure was achieved.
The sampling framework was based on data available through Tempo Online, the official database of the National Institute of Statistics, accessed in February 2026 and updated with 2024 population estimates. Specifically, the study used data from the dataset entitled "Resident population as of July 1, 2024, by age groups and age, sex, area of residence, macroregions, development regions, and counties" (National Institute of Statistics, 2026).
3. Research Objective and Hypotheses (SEM Framework) Research Objective
The primary objective of this study is to examine how media exposure, the perception of fake and alarmist news, the evaluation of information credibility, verification behaviours and analytical rationality influence individuals' economic reactions in the context of alarmist and fake economic news.
To capture the multidimensional nature of these reactions, the analysis is structured around three distinct Structural Equation Models (SEM), each with a different dependent variable:
* behavioural reactions (BEH_REAC)
* consumption reduction (SUS_REDUCE)
* saving orientation (ECON_SAVE)
This approach enables the identification of both common psychological mechanisms and specific differences across types of economic behaviour.
The choice of Structural Equation Modelling (SEM) is justified by the complexity of the proposed theoretical framework. The study does not aim merely to identify direct associations between observable variables, but to simultaneously test an explanatory mechanism in which media exposure, fake and alarmist news perception, information credibility, concern, and economic behaviour are integrated into a unified relational structure. In this context, SEM is more appropriate than classical linear regression, as it allows for the simultaneous estimation of relationships among multiple dependent and independent variables, as well as the analysis of direct, indirect, and mediating effects. Moreover, several dimensions analysed in this research are latent constructs, operationalised through multiple observed indicators.
Specific objectives
The research pursues the following specific objectives:
O1. To examine the relationship between media exposure and perceptions of fake news and alarmist economic news.
O2. To assess the role of cognitive and emotional mechanisms (information credibility, information verification, analytical rationality, and concern) in the processing of alarmist economic news.
O3. To investigate the influence of perceived economic impact and levels of concern on consumers' economic behaviour.
O4. To analyse differences among the three types of economic responses (behavioural reactions, consumption reduction, and saving orientation), including variations associated with institutional trust and sociodemographic characteristics.
Research Hypotheses
A. Hypotheses regarding the common mechanism
H1. Media exposure influences perceptions of the frequency of economically themed fake news.
H2. A stronger perception of fake news is associated with higher levels of credibility attributed to alarmist economic news.
H3. The credibility attributed to alarmist economic news positively influences the level of concern generated by such news.
H4. The perceived economic impact of alarmist news (CRISIS_IMPACT, BANK_IMPACT, and NEWS_EFFECT) significantly influences individuals' economic behaviours.
B. Hypotheses regarding the dependent variables
Model 1 - Behavioural Reactions (BEH_REAC)
H5a. Concern generated by alarmist economic news positively influences behavioural reactions.
H6a. The perceived impact of the economic and financial context positively influences behavioural reactions (BEH_REAC).
Model 2 - Consumption Reduction (SUS_REDUCE)
H5b. Concern generated by alarmist economic news positively influences consumption reduction.
H6b. The perceived impact of the economic and financial context positively influences consumption reduction (SUS_REDUCE).
Model 3 - Saving Orientation (ECON_SAVE)
H5c. Concern generated by alarmist economic news positively influences saving orientation.
H6c. The perceived impact of the economic and financial context positively influences saving orientation (ECON_SAVE).
C. Hypothesis regarding institutional trust
H7. Higher levels of trust in economic and public institutions are associated with reduced defensive economic reactions, with the magnitude of these effects varying according to the type of economic behaviour examined.
D. Hypothesis regarding sociodemographic variables
H8. Sociodemographic characteristics (age, gender, and education) significantly and differentially influence economic responses triggered by alarmist news.
4. Conceptual Model
The conceptual model proposed in this study (Figure 1) integrates cognitive constructs (fake news perceptions and information credibility), behavioural mechanisms (information verification practices), and emotional responses (concern) within a Structural Equation Modelling (SEM) framework. Estimating three separate structural models allows for a more nuanced understanding of the heterogeneity underlying individuals' economic responses to alarmist economic news, avoiding the reduction of these responses to a single behavioural pattern.
The overall configuration of the Structural Equation Model reflects an integrated approach to understanding the mechanisms through which economic information, filtered through media exposure and fake news perceptions, is transformed into individual behavioural responses. The model is structured around three interconnected dimensions: an exposure and perception component (ME and FN_PERCEP), a cognitive-evaluative component (CRED and VR), and an impact and response component comprising the perceived impact of the economic crisis, the banking system, and the effects of alarmist news (CRISIS_IMPACT, BANK_IMPACT, and NEWS_EFFECT), alongside the emotional dimension (WAR_WORRY) and the final behavioural outcome variables.
The latent structure of the variables is supported by multiple indicators, enabling the capture of complex constructs such as media consumption, information credibility, and analytical rationality. The proposed structural paths reflect a logical "exposure-interpretation- response" sequence, whereby fake news perceptions influence credibility assessments, credibility contributes to the generation of concern, and concern, together with the perceived impact of the economic crisis, the banking system, and the effects of alarmist news, helps explain individuals' economic responses.
The inclusion of institutional trust variables and sociodemographic characteristics as exogenous factors allows for the examination of contextual and individual influences on behaviour, thereby reinforcing the comprehensive nature of the proposed model. Overall, the SEM configuration is theoretically coherent and methodologically appropriate for investigating the complex relationships among cognitive, emotional, and behavioural factors in the context of exposure to alarmist economic news.
5. Results and discussion
The SEM models were estimated using the Maximum Likelihood Estimation (MLE) method, with the obtained results presented in Figures 2-4 and Table 2. The robustness tests applied are reported in Table 2 and support the overall adequacy of the estimated SEM models. The low RMSEA values (0.012-0.016) indicate a very good fit between the models and the observed data, while the coefficients of determination (CD = 0.657-0.696) demonstrate satisfactory explanatory power. In addition, the relatively large sample sizes employed (N = 679 and N = 1,054) contribute to the stability of the parameter estimates and the robustness of the findings.
Although the Comparative Fit Index (CFI) and Tucker-Lewis Index (TLI) values fall below the thresholds commonly recommended in the literature, this situation is frequently encountered in complex SEM models involving multiple constructs and structural relationships. Overall, the consistency of the relationships identified among the analysed variables provides additional support for the validity of the proposed conceptual framework.
Model 1
The results of the SEM model, with behavioural reactions (BEH_REAC) as the dependent variable, highlight the predominance of the emotional dimension in explaining immediate economic behaviours. Specifically, the level of concern generated by alarmist economic news emerges as the primary determinant of behavioural reactions, with the relationship between the two variables being positive and highly statistically significant (β ≈ 2.064, p = 0.001). The magnitude of the coefficient suggests that variations in the intensity of concern are associated with substantial changes in behaviour, indicating a high sensitivity of economic reactions to emotional stimuli.
At the same time, the perceived impact of the economic crisis (β = -1.299, p < 0.05) and the perceived impact of the banking system (β = -1.166, p < 0.05) significantly influence behavioural reactions, suggesting that the way individuals evaluate the economic consequences of events and the stability of the financial system contributes to shaping their behavioural responses. These findings indicate that individual reactions are influenced not only by the level of concern experienced but also by perceptions of the economic impact associated with alarmist information.
A noteworthy aspect of this model is the absence of statistical significance for the relationships between fake news perceptions and information credibility, as well as between credibility and concern. The estimated coefficients for the pathways FN_PERCEP → CRED and CRED → WAR_WORRY are not statistically significant, indicating that, in the case of immediate behavioural reactions, cognitive mechanisms based on credibility assessments are not decisive. Thus, reactive behaviours appear to result primarily from rapid, heuristic processing, in which the emotional component prevails over the analytical one.
Regarding the cognitive dimension of the model, the findings suggest that information verification and the critical evaluation of media content contribute indirectly to the formation of behavioural reactions; however, their influence is comparatively weaker than the effect exerted by the emotional component. Consequently, behavioural reactions appear to be determined primarily by the manner in which individuals perceive and internalise the risks associated with the economic and geopolitical context, with concern generated by alarmist news representing the principal mechanism through which these effects are translated into behaviour.
Furthermore, the perceived impact of the economic crisis (β = -1.299, p < 0.05) and the perceived impact of the banking system (β = -1.166, p < 0.05) significantly affect behavioural reactions, suggesting that individuals who perceive more severe economic consequences are more likely to adopt stronger behavioural responses. This finding confirms that the evaluation of the concrete economic effects of events constitutes an important determinant of individual reactions.
In addition, sociodemographic variables, particularly gender, exhibit a significant effect on behavioural reactions, with the estimated coefficient being positive and statistically significant, indicating the existence of structural differences in responses to alarmist news. By contrast, educational attainment demonstrates only marginal significance, while age does not emerge as a significant predictor, suggesting that immediate reactions are less dependent on formal cognitive capital.
Overall, the BEH_REAC model indicates that immediate behavioural reactions are governed primarily by emotional mechanisms, with the large coefficient associated with the WAR_WORRY variable confirming its central role. The lack of significance for the intermediate cognitive relationships suggests that these behaviours are only weakly mediated by deliberative processes and are more closely related to rapid responses influenced by risk perception and the emotional intensity of the messages.
Model 2
The SEM model associated with consumption reduction (SUS_REDUCE) reveals a more complex mechanism, in which the interaction between cognitive and emotional dimensions becomes more pronounced. Unlike the previous model, the relationships among fake news perception, credibility, and concern are statistically significant, indicating that decisions to reduce consumption involve a more elaborate process of information evaluation.
Thus, the perception of a high prevalence of fake news influences the credibility attributed to alarmist news (positive and statistically significant coefficient), and this credibility, in turn, contributes to an increase in the level of concern, with the CRED → WAR_WORRY relationship also being statistically significant. This pathway confirms the existence of a coherent cognitive mechanism through which information is evaluated and internalised before generating a behavioural response.
Concern remains a significant predictor of consumption reduction (β ≈ 0.121, p < 0.01); however, in this case, its effect is more moderate compared to the model explaining behavioural reactions. This difference in magnitude suggests that consumption reduction is not an impulsive response but rather a more tempered reaction embedded within a broader process of economic risk assessment.
The findings indicate the presence of a more clearly defined cognitive mechanism than that observed in the case of general behavioural reactions. Perceptions of fake news influence the credibility attributed to alarmist information, which subsequently contributes to heightened concern, suggesting that decisions regarding consumption reduction are preceded by processes of information evaluation and interpretation. In this context, individuals do not respond exclusively to emotional stimuli; rather, they incorporate information perceived as credible into their decision-making processes concerning the management of financial resources.
Furthermore, the positive and statistically significant effect of the NEWS_EFFECT variable (β = 0.032, p < 0.001) indicates that exposure to alarmist economic news contributes to the adoption of consumption reduction behaviours. At the same time, the perceived impact of the banking system exerts a significant negative effect on consumption reduction (β = -0.097, p < 0.01), suggesting that the manner in which individuals assess the stability of the financial sector directly influences decisions regarding current expenditures. In contrast, the perceived impact of the economic crisis does not exert a statistically significant effect on consumption reduction, implying that general perceptions of the economic context are less relevant than evaluations related to the functioning of the banking system and the information disseminated through media channels.
With regard to institutional trust, the findings reveal a significant negative effect of trust in banks on consumption reduction, suggesting that higher levels of trust in the financial system are associated with a lower likelihood of adopting defensive behaviours characterised by spending restraint. This result underscores the role of trust as a factor contributing to behavioural stability, although its influence appears to be domain-specific.
Sociodemographic variables play a more limited role in this model, with age exhibiting a positive and statistically significant effect (β = 0.066, p < 0.01). This finding suggests that consumption reduction is primarily a cross-cutting behaviour influenced by risk perceptions and psychological mechanisms rather than by individuals' structural characteristics.
Overall, the SUS_REDUCE model reflects a decision-making mechanism in which the emotional component is mediated by cognitive processes, while the estimated coefficients confirm the existence of a more coherent and rationalised pathway compared to immediate behavioural reactions. The findings also highlight the complementary roles of perceived economic impact and institutional trust in explaining consumption-related behaviours, suggesting that behavioural adjustments are shaped both by the evaluation of alarmist information and by the ways in which individuals assess the stability of the economic and financial environment.
Model 3
The SEM model for saving orientation (ECON_SAVE) highlights a distinct type of economic response, characterised by a combination of cognitive evaluation, anticipation, and moderate emotional influences. Similar to the consumption reduction model, the relationships among fake news perception, credibility, and concern are statistically significant, indicating an active cognitive pathway in the formation of saving behaviour.
Perceptions of fake news positively influence the credibility attributed to alarmist news, while credibility, in turn, contributes to increased levels of concern, with both relationships being statistically significant. This pathway suggests that individuals who attribute greater credibility to alarmist information are more likely to internalise economic risk and adjust their behaviour accordingly.
Concern positively influences saving orientation; however, the magnitude of this coefficient is smaller than that observed in the other models, indicating that saving behaviour is less reactive and more dependent on longer-term evaluative processes. This difference in effect size suggests that saving is not merely an emotional response but also involves a degree of planning and anticipation.
A particularly relevant finding concerns the direct effects of perceived impact on saving behaviour. Both the perceived impact of the economic crisis (β = 0.067, p < 0.01) and the perceived impact of the banking system (β = 0.042, p < 0.05) positively influence saving orientation, suggesting that perceptions of heightened economic risk encourage prudent behaviours aimed at accumulating financial resources. In contrast, the effect of alarmist news (NEWS_EFFECT) exerts a negative and statistically significant influence on saving behaviour (β = -0.012, p < 0.001), indicating that repeated exposure to alarmist information does not automatically lead to intensified saving behaviours but may instead generate differentiated responses depending on how individuals interpret and internalise such information.
Regarding institutional trust, the findings reveal a significant negative effect of trust in government on saving behaviour, suggesting that higher levels of confidence in the institutional capacity to manage the economic context reduce the perceived need to accumulate financial resources. This result underscores the role of trust as a mechanism for reducing uncertainty.
Furthermore, sociodemographic variables, particularly age and gender, exhibit significant effects, indicating that saving behaviour is more sensitive to individual characteristics than the other types of behaviour examined in this study. Age positively influences saving behaviour (β = 0.090, p < 0.001), suggesting that older individuals are more likely to adopt prudent financial behaviours in the context of economic uncertainty. At the same time, gender exerts a negative and statistically significant effect (β = -0.240, p < 0.001), highlighting the existence of structural differences in the management of financial risks and the accumulation of economic reserves. This finding suggests that saving decisions are influenced not only by the informational environment but also by structural factors and accumulated life experience.
Overall, the ECON_SAVE model reveals a more sophisticated decision-making mechanism in which the estimated coefficients reflect a balance between emotional and cognitive influences. Saving orientation appears to be shaped not only by the level of concern generated by alarmist news but also by evaluations of perceived economic impact and confidence in institutions' ability to manage the economic environment. These findings suggest that saving behaviour represents one of the most complex forms of behavioural adaptation to economic and informational uncertainty.
The results of the Structural Equation Models indicate varying degrees of support for the research hypotheses. Thus, H1 is supported at the structural level across all models, with the relationship between media exposure and fake news perceptions being consistently integrated into the mechanism under investigation. H2 and H3 receive partial support, as they are confirmed in the models related to consumption reduction and saving behaviour, where the estimated coefficients for the FN_PERCEP → CRED and CRED → WAR_WORRY pathways are statistically significant, but not in the behavioural reactions model. This finding suggests that cognitive mechanisms operate in a context-dependent manner. More specifically, cognitive processes involved in information evaluation appear to become more relevant in economic behaviours requiring more deliberate decision-making and less relevant in the case of immediate behavioural reactions. H4 is partially supported.
The findings further indicate that information verification and analytical rationality contribute to shaping the cognitive mechanisms underlying the models through their relationships with information credibility and the evaluation of economic risks. However, the effects of these factors are not directly or uniformly translated into final economic behaviours. Regarding the hypotheses related to the dependent variables, H5a, H5b, and H5c are supported, with concern exerting a positive and statistically significant effect on all three types of behaviour. The estimated coefficients indicate a stronger influence on general behavioural reactions (β = 2.064, p < 0.001) and more moderate effects on consumption reduction (β = 0.121, p < 0.01) and saving behaviour (β = 0.069, p < 0.01). Hypotheses H6a- H6c receive partial support, as the results suggest that the perceived impact of the economic and financial context influences the behaviours examined, although these effects vary according to the nature of the economic response. Specifically, the perceived impact of the economic crisis and the banking system significantly influences behavioural reactions and saving orientation, whereas, in the case of consumption reduction, the effects are more selective and dependent on the specific type of impact considered. H7 is also partially supported, with institutional trust selectively influencing economic behaviours, exhibiting significant effects only in certain models and for specific institutions. In particular, trust in banks reduces the likelihood of adopting consumption-restriction behaviours (β = -0.174, p < 0.01), whereas trust in government is associated with a lower propensity to engage in saving behaviour (β = -0.084, p < 0.001), highlighting the contextual nature of institutional influences on economic behaviour.
Finally, H8 is supported, although in a differentiated manner, as sociodemographic variables exhibit selective and non-uniform effects across the behaviours analysed. The findings indicate that age positively influences both consumption reduction and saving behaviour, whereas gender exerts significant effects on behavioural reactions and saving behaviour. In contrast, educational attainment does not demonstrate consistent effects across all models examined. Overall, the findings reveal a complex explanatory mechanism in which the emotional dimension is robustly supported, whereas cognitive and institutional dimensions exhibit effects that depend on the specific type of economic behaviour considered.
Moreover, the results suggest that economic reactions generated by exposure to alarmist news are determined not exclusively by levels of concern but also by the ways in which individuals evaluate the economic consequences of events and by the degree of trust they place in institutions responsible for managing the economic context.
6. Discussion of Findings in Relation to the Existing Literature
The findings support the literature emphasising the central role of emotions and risk perception in responses to fake news and alarmist news. Across all three models examined, concern (WAR_WORRY) emerges as a significant determinant of economic behaviour, supporting the argument that misinformation influences not only perceptions but also economic decisions through the amplification of uncertainty and anxiety (Narwal, 2018; Pherson et al., 2021; Piazza, 2022). These findings are also consistent with the behavioural economics literature on loss aversion and defensive responses under conditions of risk and uncertainty (Kahneman & Tversky, 1979; Kahneman, 2011; Shiller, 2017, 2020).
Furthermore, the models related to consumption reduction and saving behaviour confirm the literature concerning the role of credibility and cognitive information processing. The significant relationships between fake news perceptions, credibility, and concern support the existence of a mechanism involving information evaluation and internalisation prior to economic responses, a finding consistent with Bauer and Clemm von Hohenberg (2020), Di Domenico et al. (2021), and Pierri and Ceri (2019).
However, the results do not entirely support the existing literature. In the model explaining immediate behavioural reactions, the relationships among fake news perceptions, credibility, and concern are not statistically significant, suggesting that rapid responses are predominantly emotional and less dependent on deliberate information evaluation. This finding is consistent with heuristic processing theories and dual-process models of cognition (Petty & Cacioppo, 1986; Kahneman, 2011; Singh et al., 2020).
A distinctive contribution of the present study lies in highlighting the specific characteristics of the Romanian context and differentiating among three types of economic responses: immediate behavioural reactions, consumption reduction, and saving behaviour. The findings suggest that fake news and alarmist news do not generate uniform economic responses but instead activate different cognitive and emotional mechanisms. This aspect remains insufficiently explored in the international literature and has rarely been investigated empirically within Eastern European contexts.
7. Study Limitations and Future Research Directions
The findings of the present study should be interpreted in light of several methodological limitations associated with the research design employed. First, respondent selection through a non-probability snowball sampling procedure combined with sociodemographic quota controls was intended to achieve a sample structure closely aligned with the characteristics of the adult population in Romania. Within this context, the results provide valuable insights into the relationships under investigation; however, they should be interpreted in accordance with the specific features of the sampling design used.
Second, the analysis captures respondents' perceptions of the prevalence of fake news and alarmist news rather than actual or objectively measured exposure to misinformation. Consequently, the relationships identified reflect cognitive and emotional mechanisms associated with the perception and evaluation of information, without allowing for the direct quantification of the actual volume of fake news consumed by individuals.
Furthermore, the cross-sectional nature of the study limits the ability to draw definitive causal conclusions and captures the investigated relationships within a specific informational and economic context. Accordingly, the findings should be interpreted as evidence of associations and explanatory mechanisms modelled through SEM rather than as conclusive demonstrations of causality.
Future research could extend the present analysis in several directions. First, longitudinal studies could be conducted to examine the stability and evolution of the relationship between exposure to alarmist news and economic behaviour across different socio-economic contexts.
A second direction for future research involves the development of cross-national comparative studies aimed at assessing the role of cultural, institutional, and media contexts in shaping economic responses to misinformation.
Additionally, the use of experimental or quasi-experimental designs could enable a more precise assessment of the causal effects of exposure to fake news and alarmist news, including comparisons between responses to verified information and manipulative content.
Finally, integrating objective indicators of media consumption and digital exposure could complement the perceptual measures employed in the present study and contribute to the development of more robust explanatory models regarding the relationship between misinformation and economic behaviour.
Conclusions
The results of the three SEM models highlight the central role of concern (WAR_WORRY) in explaining economic responses generated by alarmist news and economic misinformation, although with varying levels of intensity depending on the type of behaviour examined. The effect is strongest in the model of immediate behavioural reactions (β ≈ 2.079), moderate in the consumption reduction model (β ≈ 0.109), and weaker in the saving orientation model (β ≈ 0.061).
These findings suggest that economic responses do not constitute a unitary phenomenon but are instead generated through distinct information-processing mechanisms. Immediate behavioural reactions are primarily driven by emotional processes, whereas consumption reduction and saving orientation reflect a combination of cognitive information evaluation, perceptions of economic impact, and concern associated with the context under consideration. Thus, the findings support the theoretical "exposure-evaluation-response" framework, according to which the effects of fake news and alarmist news on economic behaviour are mediated by both emotional and cognitive processes.
The originality of the study lies in the integration of cognitive, emotional, and behavioural dimensions within a unified framework, as well as in the comparative analysis of three distinct forms of economic response: immediate behavioural reactions, consumption reduction, and saving behaviour.
The empirical value of the study is supported by the analysis of a nationwide sample within a socio-economic context that remains insufficiently explored in the international literature. Consequently, the article contributes to the growing body of research on fake news and economic behaviour by highlighting the need for integrated and differentiated analytical approaches.
The findings also have important implications for public policy, economic stakeholders, and the media ecosystem. Specifically, they underscore the need to combat misinformation, strengthen media literacy, and develop communication strategies capable of reducing the destabilising effects of alarmist news on consumer behaviour and public trust.
