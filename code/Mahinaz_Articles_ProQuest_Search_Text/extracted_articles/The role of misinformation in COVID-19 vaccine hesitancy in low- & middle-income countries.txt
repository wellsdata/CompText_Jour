1 Introduction
In late 2019, SARS-CoV-2, a novel coronavirus, emerged and rapidly escalated into a global public health emergency. On March 11, 2020, the World Health Organization (WHO) declared COVID-19 as a global pandemic, reflecting sustained worldwide transmission and the urgent need for coordinated action across countries and sectors [1] . The pandemic's health, social, and economic disruptions accelerated major scientific efforts, resulting in the rapid development and deployment of multiple COVID-19 vaccines [2] . Despite these efforts, the benefits of vaccination have not been equitably realized globally due to differences in access and uptake worldwide [3] . To address inequitable access, global initiatives such as COVID-19 Vaccines Global Access (COVAX) were launched as part of a collaborative effort by various organizations (e.g., WHO, UNICEF, and several others) to promote fair vaccine distribution, particularly in countries facing constrained supply and delivery capacity such as low and middle-income countries (LMICs) [4] .
Long before the COVID-19 pandemic, vaccine hesitancy has been recognized as a significant public health concern. Vaccine hesitancy is commonly defined as a delay or refusal in the acceptance of a vaccine despite adequate supply, and it is often shaped by factors like insufficient knowledge, low risk perception, as well as socio-demographic and cultural backgrounds [5] . In 2019, the WHO identified vaccine hesitancy as one of the ten threats to global health, highlighting that vaccine refusal or delays in vaccine acceptance can undermine efforts to mitigate the spread of vaccine-preventable disease [5] . During the COVID-19 pandemic, vaccine hesitancy emerged across diverse populations, ranging from the general public to healthcare workers, and became particularly consequential given the need for high coverage to reduce severe disease and protect health systems. In response, the WHO articulated global vaccination targets as part of its strategy to increase population level immunity, underscoring the urgency to improve vaccine coverage across all regions [6] .
Much of the research surrounding high-income countries (HICs) has examined COVID-19 vaccine hesitancy and the information environments that shape it, including the relationship between misinformation exposure and vaccination intentions [7] . Misinformation is commonly defined as false or inaccurate information shared inadvertently, without the intent to cause harm [8] . In addition, an infodemic refers to an excessive volume of information including misleading or false information that can spread during public health crises like disease outbreaks, one result of which can be prolonged outbreaks [9] . Studies have also highlighted the influence of social media on shaping vaccination attitudes, both by facilitating the rapid dissemination of misleading claims and by influencing perceived social norms surrounding vaccination [10] . Although insights from HIC settings are informative, they may not translate directly to LMICs, where health system constraints, historical inequities, political instability, and local trust dynamics can interact differently with vaccine decision making and information credibility. In many LMICs, limited access to healthcare providers and trusted institutional communication channels may increase reliance on social media as a primary source of health information, potentially increasing the impact of vaccine-related misinformation.
In this scoping review, we focus on misinformation as a core concept contributing to COVID-19 vaccine hesitancy. During the COVID-19 pandemic, misinformation circulated widely as part of the broader “infodemic,” described by the WHO as an overabundance of information, including false or misleading content occurring in both digital and physical environments, capable of undermining public health responses [11] . Vaccine-related misinformation has included false claims regarding safety, effectiveness, ingredients, fertility, and long-term health effects, alongside conspiracy narratives that frame vaccination as harmful or politically motivated. These narratives are not limited to a single medium: they can spread through interpersonal communication, community networks, and traditional media [9] . Notably, the COVID-19 pandemic has been described as the first pandemic in which technology and social media were used at massive scales to communicate and connect, while also enabling the rapid amplification of misinformation [11] . Given this context, a more explicit focus on misinformation sources, including but not limited to social media-based misinformation, is warranted.
The structural features of social media platforms facilitate the rapid diffusion, repeated exposure, and social reinforcement of misleading claims, which may increase the perceived credibility of misinformation even when it conflicts with information from trusted institutions [10] . Existing literature focuses on the broader role of misinformation arising from multiple information outlets, with their specific influences on shaping vaccine hesitancy, particularly in LMIC settings, remaining understudied. Preliminary literature searches suggest that relevant evidence exists but remains fragmented across disciplines and geographic settings. To date, no scoping review has comprehensively mapped this body of literature. Therefore, a scoping review is warranted focusing on the role of misinformation as it relates to vaccine hesitancy in LMICs. We considered various misinformation outlets in our study and given the context of the broader infodemic during the COVID-19 pandemic as defined by the WHO, included an emphasis on social media-based misinformation sources. This review seeks to (1) explore how misinformation contributes to COVID-19 vaccine hesitancy in LMICs, and (2) identify the key misinformation themes, sources and dissemination channels that shape vaccine-related attitudes and behaviors in these settings.
2 Methods
This scoping review was conducted in accordance with the Arksey and O'Malley framework for scoping reviews and Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses - extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR) guidelines [12,13] .
2.1 Search strategy
A comprehensive literature search was conducted on June 15, 2025, across various databases, including Scopus, Embase, PubMed, CINAHL, and PsycINFO. Detailed search strings encompassing study criteria were created for each database, and keywords relating to the study such as “LMIC”, “COVID-19”, “vaccine”, “hesitancy”, “misinformation”, and “social media" were used. Search strings can be found in Appendix A.
2.2 Article screening
Upon the comprehensive literature search, articles were imported into Covidence, a systematic review tool, Veritas Health Innovation, Melbourne, Australia (available at https://www.covidence.org/ ) [14] . Covidence was used to conduct title/abstract (stage one) and full-text (stage two) screening. All articles were screened by two independent reviewers (AYS, ZUK, TR, ZK, AM, MH, LP, MAI, AA, JZ, LI, DLT, NB, NN, ED). Conflicts were resolved by a third reviewer to minimize bias in the screening process. Routine team meetings were held to discuss any conflicts that arose during this process and were evaluated to maintain reviewer consistency.
2.3 Inclusion and exclusion criteria
A rigorous set of inclusion and exclusion criteria was applied to effectively classify articles as relevant or irrelevant to the study. Studies were included if they focused on populations in LMICs, as classified by the World Bank [15] , regardless of age group. Articles also had to examine COVID-19 vaccine-related misinformation as a central topic, investigate its association with vaccine hesitancy, refusal, or low vaccine uptake, and be conducted within the context of the COVID-19 pandemic (i.e., from November 2019 onwards). Empirical studies (quantitative, qualitative, or mixed methods), reviews, commentaries, peer-reviewed journal articles, or pre-prints were included article types.
Articles were excluded if they exclusively focused on HICs without any reference to LMIC populations or comparisons did not address misinformation as a central theme or failed to connect it to COVID-19 vaccine hesitancy; or if they focused exclusively on vaccine efficacy, logistics/supply chains, clinical trials, or public health campaigns, without linking to public perception or misinformation narratives. Editorials or news articles without empirical data or substantive analysis were also excluded. Additionally, the study was restricted to English-language publications only.
2.4 Data acquisition and analysis
Data extraction was conducted using Microsoft Excel. A standardized data extraction spreadsheet was developed to note key variables relevant to the study. Included articles were imported and systematically distributed among team members (AYS, ZUK, TR, LP, JZ, NB, AM, ZK, LI, MDI, MH, DLT). Extracted variables included: Covidence study #, name of study, country, type of study, study population, demographic factors (if applicable), prevalence/incidence of vaccination, primary sources of misinformation, institutional trust, conspiracy/alternative beliefs, religious/cultural beliefs, social media use/trust, beliefs on vaccine efficacy/post-vaccination symptoms, and perception of COVID-19 health risks. Following data extraction, thematic data analysis was conducted to identify and summarize key themes across the included studies.
While study characteristics are presented and discussed throughout the paper, additional details and syntheses have been provided in the form of supplementary tables. Supplementary Table 1 provides the complete data extraction format used for this scoping review, consisting of all included studies and extracted variables. Supplementary Table 2 provides a concise overview of key variables like the countries that a study applied to, major misinformation types, media sources, and whether the study showed a negative relation with vaccine uptake. This was divided into three categories: Yes, No, and Not Applicable (N/A). “Negative relation with vaccine uptake” was coded as "Yes" when a study reported that misinformation, misinformation-related beliefs, or identified misinformation source(s) were associated with lower vaccine uptake, lower vaccination intention, greater hesitancy, or refusal; “No” when such a negative relation was not supported or when findings were neutral/positive; and “N/A" when the study did not permit this determination.
3 Results
In total, 2583 articles were identified across all relevant databases, including Scopus (1147), Embase (592), PubMed (568), CINAHL (213), and PsycINFO (63). 1102 automatic duplicates were removed, and 1481 title/abstracts were screened, of which 553 full-text articles remained and were screened for eligibility in stage two. Ultimately, 119 articles met the inclusion criteria and were included in the review ( Fig. 1 ). With the exception of geographical location and study type, not all categories of our results are mutually exclusive, and individual studies could be classified into multiple categories. Given the diverse nature of the studies included in this review, restricting the classification of studies into a single category would have misrepresented the complexity of the data. As such, total study counts may exceed the total number of studies due to overlap across categories.
3.1 Study characteristics
A total of 119 studies were included in the final analysis, representing diverse geographic and population contexts across LMICs (Supplementary Table 1). The largest number of studies were conducted in Nigeria (18) [16–33] , Pakistan (13) [34–46] , India (11) [47–57] , and Ethiopia (6) [58–63] , with additional representation across Sub-Saharan Africa, South Asia, the Middle East, and Southeast Asia. Notably, over 15/119 (12.61%) of studies [64–78] were conducted across multiple countries, reflecting a growing interest in cross-national perspectives on COVID-19 vaccine misinformation.
Cross-sectional studies were the most common study design among the included articles (69/119; 57.98%) [18,20–23,25–28,33,38,39,41,42,44,47–51,53,54,56–61,63–66,68,69,72,75,77,79–110] , followed by qualitative and descriptive studies (28/119; 23.53%) [16,17,19,29,31,32,35–37,43,46,52,55,67,78,111–123] , mixed-methods studies (12/119; 10.08%) [24,40,62,124–132] , reviews and commentaries (7/119; 5.88%) [34,45,70,71,73,74,76] , as well as content analysis studies (3/119; 2.52%) [30,133,134] .

The study population included in this review represents a diverse sample of LMICs. The most commonly observed population was the general public, represented in 95 of 119 studies (79.83%) [16–18,20–24,26,27,29–38,40–43,45–48,50–52,54–57,60,62,64–73,75,77–85,89,91–95,97–100,102,104–124,127,128,130,131,133,134] , including non-healthcare students, social media users, and individuals employed in education or business sectors. Healthcare workers and healthcare students constituted the second-most common study population (31/119; 26.05%) [19,25,28,32,42–45,52,53,55,58,61,63,71,73,86,87,89,90,96,103,106,108,112,115,123,125,128,129,132] . Additionally, 10/119 studies (8.40%) [32,39,49,55,59,88,101,104,115,126] mentioned patients as part of the study population, while 2/119 studies (1.68%) [74,76] did not report relevant data regarding a study population.
Demographic characteristics were reported in most of the included studies (106/119; 89.08%) [16–25,27–29,31–44,46–68,71,72,75–92,94–118,120,121,124,125,127–133] , which were classified as follows: (1) age and sex/gender composition, (2) education level, socioeconomic indicators, and employment/occupation, (3) participants' residential environment, geographic setting, and/or marital status, as well as (4) religion or ethnicity. Most commonly reported was age and sex/gender composition (96/119; 80.67%) [16–22,24,25,27–29,32–36,38–42,44,46–64,66–68,71,72,75,77–90,93–116,118,121,124–127,129–132] , followed by education level, socioeconomic indicators, and employment/occupation (87/119; 73.11%) [16–20,22,24,27–29,31,33,35–44,46–53,55,56,58–63,65–68,71,72,75–85,87,89–92,95–102,104–113,116,120,121,125,127,129–132] . Many studies also reported participants' residential environment, geographic setting, and/or marital status (50/119; 42.02%) [22,28,29,32,34,35,38–44,48,50,54,56,57,61–65,67,68,71,76,81,82,84,85,91,92,98,99,101–104,107,109,115,117,118,120,127,128,130,131,133] . Lastly, fewer studies reported demographic characteristics regarding religion or ethnicity (31/119; 26.05%) [17,19,24,25,28,33,46,48,56,58–61,65,66,76,77,82,91,92,96,97,101,103,108,109,111,117,118,120,131] , while some studies did not report any demographic data [26,30,69,70,73,74,119,122,123,134] .
3.2 COVID-19 vaccine uptake and intention
Vaccination prevalence and incidence, such as current vaccination status, uptake/coverage, willingness/intention, and hesitancy/refusal were reported in 78/119 included studies (65.55%) [16,17,19,21,22,24,27,28,30,33–35,39,44,46,47,49–53,56,58–66,68,71–76,78,79,81,82,84–91,95–99,101–104,106–113,118,123–125,127–133] . Measures of willingness/intention/hesitancy/refusal were the most commonly reported vaccination outcomes (59/119; 49.58%) [17,21–24,27,28,33–35,46,47,51,53,56,58–66,68,71,72,75,76,78,79,81,82,84,86–91,95,96,98,101–104,106,109–113,118,124,125,127,131,133] . Fewer studies reported current vaccination status/uptake/acceptance/coverage (39/119; 32.77%) [16,17,19,24,33,39,44,47,49–51,53,64,71–74,76,88–90,96,97,99,101–104,106–108,118,123,125,127–130,132] .
3.3 Institutional trust in information sources
Institutional trust was discussed in the majority of included studies (96/119; 80.67%) [18–21,23–26,28–32,34,36–39,41–48,50–53,55–59,61,62,64,65,67,68,70,71,73–80,82–85,88–93,95,96,98–101,104–124,126–130,132–134] . Trust related to government/health authorities was the most referenced institutional domain (30/119; 25.21%) [20,32,39,41,43,44,48,50,51,53,58,59,64,75,77,82,98,104,105,109,110,115–117,120,121,123,128,132,134] . Across the included literature, distrust/low trust in one or more institutions was also frequently reported (54/119; 45.38%) [19,21,25,26,29,30,34,36–38,45,46,48,55,56,61,62,67,71,73–76,78–80,82,84,85,88,91–93,95,96,100,101,106–108,111–115,118,119,121,123,124,127,130,131,133] and several studies specifically described healthcare professionals, health systems, and the scientific community as trusted sources of vaccine information (16/119; 13.45%) [28,42,48,52,64,73,75,79,81,99,100,111,115,116,126,131] .
3.4 Primary sources of misinformation
Social media was identified as the most common source of COVID-19 vaccine-related misinformation in 97 of 119 studies (81.51%) [16,18–22,25–43,45,46,51–54,56–58,60,61,63–71,74–78,83–87,89–91,93–98,100–127,130–134] . Particularly, social media platforms included Facebook (41/119; 34.45%) [16,19,20,31,35,37,40,43,51,63,64,66,71,76,78,84,87,89,91,94,95,97,98,100,102,108–113,115,116,118,121,124–127,130,134] , WhatsApp (28/119; 23.53%) [16,19,20,31,37,40,43,51,52,64,71,76,84,87,89,94,97,98,100,112,113,115,117–119,121,125,127] , X (21/119; 17.65%) [16,20,31,37,40,41,43,51,57,64,66,69,76,78,87,89,97,108,115,124,130] , YouTube (11/119; 9.24%) [30,40,41,54,71,89,109–111,114,118] , Instagram (9/119; 7.56%) [16,19,31,41,51,89,98,125,130] , TikTok (3/119; 2.52%) [16,40,133] , Telegram (2/119; 1.68%) [31,63] , Pinterest (1/119; <1%) [89] , LinkedIn (1/119; <1%) [89] , and Zalo (1/119; <1%) [109] . Other misinformation sources, such as mainstream media, television (TV), radio, the internet, family relatives, word-of-mouth, healthcare workers, Google, or friends, were mentioned by 37 of 119 studies (31.09%) [18,21–23,25,26,32,33,38–40,42,45,46,51,53,54,67,73,76,85,88–91,97,99,103,104,114,116,118,119,127–129,131] . Contrastingly, 22 studies (18.49%) [17,23,24,44,47–50,55,59,62,72,73,79–82,88,92,99,128,129] did not identify social media to be a major source of misinformation or did not report relevant data.
Social media use and trust was a key data element in exploring the role of misinformation in COVID-19 vaccine hesitancy. While the vast majority of studies (107/119; 89.92%) [16,17,19–23,25–47,49–71,73–85,87,89–94,96–103,105–119,121–123,127–134] reported social media usage among their respective study populations, findings differed with respect to the influence of social media on vaccination outcomes. Furthermore, 73 studies (73/119; 61.34%) [16,17,19,20,23,26–30,33–40,42,43,45,46,52,53,55–58,60–62,64–67,70,71,74–76,78–80,83–85,87,89,90,93,96,100,101,103,105,108,111–114,116–118,121–123,127,128,130–134] reported a high degree of negative influence of social media-induced misinformation on vaccine-related outcomes, whereas 14 studies (14/119; 11.76%) [31,32,41,47,50,51,59,81,82,94,98,99,106,110] reported a minimal negative influence. Notably, 27 studies (27/119; 22.69%) [21,22,25,28,40,44,49,52,54,57,63,67–69,73,77,91,92,97,99,102,107,109,111,115,119,129] reported a neutral or positive influence of social media on vaccination outcomes and specified instances where misinformation did not directly lead to vaccine hesitancy. Lastly, 12 studies (12/119; 10.08%) [18,24,48,72,86,88,95,104,120,124–126] did not report specific data in this regard.
3.5 Key themes of misinformation
Several prominent themes of misinformation were identified, with many studies reporting multiple themes: (1) malicious intentions, (2) fear of side effects, (3) concerns about vaccine development and safety, (4) religious and cultural beliefs, and (5) natural immunity. Few studies (9/119; 7.56%) did not explicitly report themes of misinformation [16,20,48,69,72,94,110,115,125] . (1) “Fear of side effects” was the most prominent theme, reported in 80/119 studies (67.23%). This included beliefs such as vaccination causing illness, COVID-19, autism, infertility, death, DNA changes, blood clots, or that humans turn into zombies [22,24,26–30,32,34–37,41–44,46,47,49–53,55–57,60–64,67,68,70,71,73–77,79,80,83–86,88–92,96–98,100–104,106–108,111–114,116–120,122–124,126–130,132,133] .
(2) “Concerns about vaccine development and safety” was another common theme, reported in 77/119 studies (64.71%). This included skepticism surrounding vaccine safety, the short development period, the idea that vaccine ingredients are dangerous, or that vaccines are saline water. Furthermore, this also included beliefs regarding the quality, efficacy, and degree of protection from vaccines [17–19,21,22,24–26,29,30,32–39,41,42,44–46,49,51–55,57–60,62–64,66,68,70,71,73,74,76,78,80,81,83,85–88,90–92,95,96,98,100,103,104,108,109,111,112,114,116,118–121,123,124,127,130–134] .
(3) “Malicious intentions” was a theme reported in 57/119 studies (47.90%). This included beliefs such as capitalist industries controlling the vaccines, vaccine developed to achieve commercial, financial, and economic aims, or deployed as part of broader conspiratorial efforts, including population reduction schemes, the establishment of a “new world order”, or covert research initiatives. Additional narratives within this theme included claims that COVID-19 is a man-made virus, that vaccines functioned as weapons of war, or enabled 5G microchipping [21–26,29,31,32,34,35,37,40,41,45–47,56,62,64,66–68,71,74–76,78,80,82–84,86,88,90–93,100,102,106–109,112,113,116–123,127,129,130] .
(4) “Religious and cultural beliefs” were reported in 50/119 studies (42.02%). This includes faith or cultural-based objections, moral/religious symbolism (such as the “mark of the beast”) and concerns about vaccine permissibility [17–19,26,28,29,32,34,35,40,42,45–47,49,50,55,56,60,62,64–67,70,71,73,74,76,78,79,82,88,91,93,99–101,103,108,117,118,120,122,123,127,128,130,132,134] .
(5) “Natural immunity” was the least common theme, reported in 15/119 studies (12.61%). Beliefs revolved around a preference for natural remedies, trust in natural immunity, and an overall false sense of being immune to COVID-19 [28,46,47,51,67,70,85,95,101,103,105,108,119,120,128] .

4 Discussion
Across the analyzed literature, misinformation related to COVID-19 vaccination in LMICs consistently appeared to operate within a broader information ecosystem, where the channel through which vaccine narratives spread often mattered just as much as the content itself. Many studies positioned social media and messaging platforms as central pathways for encountering and sharing vaccine-related claims, including both misinformation and debate about vaccine credibility [16,18–22,25–43,45,46,51–54,56–58,60,61,63–71,74–78,83–87,89–91,93–98,100–127,130–134] . Additionally, the findings of this review suggest that non-social media information sources including (but not limited to) TV, radio, the internet, family relatives, word of mouth, and friends were found to spread misinformation in far less instances [18,21–23,25,26,32,33,38–40,42,45,46,51,53,54,67,73,76,85,88–91,97,99,103,104,114,116,118,119,127–129,131] . This underscores the influential role of social media misinformation on public attitudes towards COVID-19 and COVID-19 vaccine hesitancy, and more broadly towards public health crises.
In terms of the content of misinformation and related beliefs, the included studies most frequently emphasized narratives that directly lower confidence in vaccines by reframing them as harmful, unsafe, or not worthwhile [17–19,21,22,24–30,32–39,41–47,49–64,66–68,70,71,73–81,83–92,95–97,100–104,106–109,111–114,116–124,126–134] . While many sentiments in the included studies found that many individuals had concerns regarding the overall effectiveness of vaccine benefits (often including perceptions that vaccines do not work or are unnecessary), one prominent pattern involved concerns of side effects, post-vaccination symptoms, and safety (often due to skepticism surrounding vaccine development). This included believing that the vaccine may cause severe harm or even death.
These concerns often coexisted with conspiracy-oriented frames that offered an “explanation” for why vaccines might be dangerous, such as narratives involving malicious intent, profiteering, population control, or politically motivated vaccine campaigns. Technological or biomedical-related frames are examples of these themes (such as microchips, 5G, or DNA-related narratives), which can further intensify safety fears by adding a sense of hidden, irreversible risk [17–19,21,22,24–30,32–39,41–47,49–64,66–68,70,71,73–81,83–92,95–97,100–104,106–109,111–114,116–124,126–134] . In addition, some studies highlighted “alternatives to vaccination” framing, including natural immunity or traditional remedies, which can reduce the perceived need for vaccination even when safety concerns are not the main driver [28,46,47,51,67,70,85,95,101,103,105,108,119,120,128] .
Many of the included studies repeatedly pointed to institutional trust, such as trust in governments, health authorities, healthcare professionals, and the scientific community [20,28,32,39,41–44,48,50–53,58,59,64,73,75,77,79,81,82,98–100,104,105,109–111,115–117,120,121,123,126,128,131,132,134] . These themes pointed to the influences that shape how people interpret competing messages. In addition, several studies documented distrust in official institutions [19,21,25,26,29,30,34,36–38,45,46,48,55,56,61,62,67,71,73–76,78–80,82,84,85,88,91–93,95,96,100,101,106–108,111–115,118,119,121,123,124,127,130,131,133] . Religious and cultural framing appeared less consistently than safety or conspiracy themes, but when present, it sometimes shaped vaccine interpretation through leader influence, perceived religious permissibility, or religiously framed narratives about vaccines [17–19,26,28,29,32,34,35,40,42,45–47,49,50,55,56,60,62,64–67,70,71,73,74,76,78,79,82,88,91,93,99–101,103,108,117,118,120,122,123,127,128,130,132,134] . Taken together, these findings suggest that misinformation in LMIC contexts is not simply a random set of incorrect claims, but rather a pattern of claims that compound into larger narratives within the infodemic.
It is important to note that a small subset of the included studies reported a minimally negative, neutral or positive influence of social media on vaccination outcomes. This can be attributed to a few different factors. First, the study populations examined by these studies held varying degrees of medical knowledge or digital literacy. For instance, studies that focused on healthcare workers as their target population were less likely to believe circulating misinformation regarding vaccines than chronic disease patients. Second, varying degrees of religious or cultural beliefs may have also played a role in determining the influence of social media on vaccination outcomes, especially due to messages propagated by religious leaders. Similarly, these factors also apply to the positive outcomes observed, as study populations who did not believe misinformation were more likely to recognize vaccination campaigns. In addition, varying levels of government/institutional trust were observed across the included studies, and contexts where institutional trust was higher may help explain more positive outcomes.
4.1 Future implications
Important lessons can be learned from the role of misinformation in COVID-19 vaccine hesitancy and applied to future pandemics. The rampant impact of misinformation, especially the type found circulating on social media platforms during the COVID-19 pandemic, influenced the trends of poor vaccine uptake in LMICs. The idea of misinformation serving an influential role in driving populations to vaccine hesitancy is not unique to COVID-19 and can therefore affect numerous infectious diseases that can be controlled by vaccination. One example is measles, a highly contagious disease caused by the measles virus, which requires herd immunity to control spread [135] . In 2025, the rise of measles in HICs like the United States and Canada resulted from a poor uptake of vaccines caused by vaccine hesitancy, similar to COVID-19 in the sense that herd immunity is a key concept in controlling spread [136,137] . Similar to COVID-19, themes of misinformation regarding the measles vaccine are prevalent and not exempt from affecting LMICs in a similar or even more significant manner. Lessons from social media misinformation and COVID-19 vaccine hesitancy in LMICs should be utilized in developing interventions to combat the circulation of inaccurate health information to the public [138] . If left unaddressed, the battlefield remains open for misinformation to circulate regarding infectious diseases in LMICs, and vaccine-hesitant outcomes may be observed similarly to during the COVID-19 pandemic, resulting in major public health repercussions. Therefore, it is strongly suggested that LMICs and governing influential authorities consider the potential impact of misinformation on vaccination outcomes within the population and implement appropriate measures to combat its widespread dissemination. Additionally, it is especially crucial to consider heterogeneity in the forms of misinformation as they pertain to vaccine hesitancy. While misinformation sources may include mainstream media, public outlets, and social media, the findings of this review highlight the overwhelmingly unfavorable influence of social media misinformation on COVID-19 vaccine hesitancy in LMICs, underscoring the need for targeted misinformation-specific interventions to counter infodemics. The lessons learnt from the COVID-19 pandemic in relation to misinformation and vaccine hesitancy, both as highlighted in this review and in the vast literature, are pivotal in ensuring that LMICs are better prepared for future pandemics by implementing robust misinformation countermeasures [139,140] . As seen from the developments post-pandemic in various HICs, lessons from the COVID-19 pandemic are crucial in preparing for future pandemics. Various studies have noted system-level factors like healthcare delivery strain, funding, and workforce constraints as major inhibitors which, although historically always present, were brought to light during the COVID-19 pandemic [141,142] . As such, it is paramount to invest in the development of robust pandemic preparedness frameworks as part of key systemic reforms to the public health sector. HICs lead the way in incorporating these changes, allowing LMICs to use them as exemplars in their own advances.
As such, future research should focus on addressing specific literature gaps to help formulate a cohesive plan-of-action that can be applicable to future infectious diseases or pandemics. Based upon the results of this review, there are several evident research gaps. First, a disproportionately high number of studies were conducted in a small subset of countries, which resulted in some LMICs being over-represented. Notably, Nigeria, Pakistan, and India accounted for a disproportionate share among the overall number of included studies, with several other countries accounting for a much lower proportion of the included studies. Countries with particularly low representation (i.e., only one study per country) included Zambia, Sierra Leone, Nepal, Papua New Guinea, Palestine, Egypt, Togo, and Yemen. This discrepancy in the overrepresentation of certain countries and underrepresentation of others, can be attributed to various factors, but most importantly their differing socio-political and media contexts. This can considerably influence overall narratives towards vaccination, access to media, and social media literacy. As such, this inconsistency may lead to gaps in information pertaining to these underrepresented countries, preventing the development of appropriate interventions. The findings of this review indicate that the adequacy of formal research studies across all LMICs is not equal, and is an important literature gap worth addressing. This also suggests that our findings, based off the overrepresentation of some countries over others, may not be truly generalizable to all LMICs. This issue warrants future research to focus on minimizing the disproportionate representation among LMICs in relation to misinformation and vaccine hesitancy, to ultimately more homogenously represent the various LMICs. Moreover, our review found that not all social media use is harmful and that, in some cases, social media use can have a beneficial or neutral impact on vaccine uptake [49,52,67,73,77,91,92,102,107,111] . While several social media platforms were examined in our dataset, there was a lack of evidence on which platforms led to better vaccine uptake outcomes, if any. Therefore, future research in this direction is essential to more adequately understand the misinformation infodemic in LMICs, allowing for more effective interventions to sustain future diseases and pandemics.
4.2 Strengths & limitations
Several measures were taken into consideration during the completion of our scoping review. First, a comprehensive mapping of the literature regarding the role of misinformation in COVID-19 vaccine hesitancy in LMICs has not previously been conducted, making it an essential literature gap worth addressing. Second, this scoping review was conducted in accordance with frameworks like Arksey and O'Malley and PRISMA-ScR, allowing for enhanced transparency and reproducibility. Furthermore, search strategies were created for multiple databases, enabling broad identification of relevant studies. Moreover, several independent reviewers were involved in the screening stages, ensuring minimal selection bias at all stages. Finally, an exhaustive data extraction template was curated, resulting in a rich dataset allowing for the identification of key themes, which can be used to effectively highlight further literature gaps.
Despite these strengths, our study is not exempt from limitations and should thus be interpreted with caution. While this review examines the role of misinformation in COVID-19 vaccine hesitancy, it is important to note that many included studies were cross-sectional and therefore cannot establish causal relationships. As a result, the findings should be interpreted as demonstrating associations between misinformation exposure and hesitancy, rather than definitive causal effects. Nonetheless, the consistency of these associations across studies suggests that misinformation may play an important contributory role. First, only articles published in the English language were included in our review. Importantly, English is not the primary language in the vast majority of the LMICs included in this study, and excluding non-English publications may mean that locally published health communication and research were excluded from our analysis. Therefore, this may reflect a language bias from the studies included and could result in decreased generalizability of the results across different LMIC contexts. Second, given the scope of this review and the large volume of published literature identified, a grey literature search was not conducted for this study, which may cause the results to overwhelmingly represent data present in peer-reviewed literature only. Organizations like the WHO, UNICEF, and government health agencies have produced substantive reports on vaccine misinformation in LMICs. Excluding this may represent a publication bias and have potentially caused us to miss unpublished data or data with null or negative results, affecting the comprehensiveness of this review. Third, as this is a scoping review, the quality appraisal stage is optional and was not conducted. Given the heterogeneity of the studies included in this review, no single quality appraisal tool exists that can apply to all the included study types, further limiting our ability to formally conduct a quality appraisal of the included articles. Therefore, the methodological rigour and risk of bias of the studies included in this review cannot be determined. As a result, the conclusions drawn reflect the breadth of literature rather than the strength of the evidence and should thus be interpreted with caution. Fourth, as this study included a small number of review articles, there is a possibility of minimal overlap with primary studies. However, overlap was limited and did not meaningfully affect the breadth or conclusions of the review. Lastly, this review included a small number of pre-print articles ( n  = 5), which are identified explicitly in the supplementary files. Despite their non-peer-reviewed status, the small number of pre-prints relative to the total study count (5/119) is unlikely to substantially affect the overall confidence in the findings of this review.
5 Conclusion
Our review suggests that misinformation relating to COVID-19 and COVID-19 vaccines is most frequently encountered and disseminated through social media and messaging platforms, which were identified as a major source of misinformation in most included articles. Likewise, these were also commonly associated with negative vaccination-related outcomes. Misinformation themes most often centered on malicious intentions, conspiratorial motives, fear of side effects, and concerns about vaccine development and safety, while religious and cultural narratives and natural immunity beliefs appeared less frequently. Consistent with these patterns, beliefs concerning vaccine efficacy and safety, including post-vaccination symptoms and side effects, were reported in nearly all included studies, reinforcing the central role of safety and effectiveness concerns as key drivers of vaccine doubt in LMIC contexts. Finally, institutional trust was discussed in most studies, with findings spanning both high trust in official sources and distrust in institutions such as governments, healthcare systems, and vaccine manufacturers, highlighting that misinformation related to vaccine decision-making occurs within a broader trust landscape. From a public health standpoint, it is crucial to apply the lessons from misinformation and vaccine hesitancy during the COVID-19 pandemic, towards interventions aimed at combating the social media misinformation infodemic in LMICs. Moving forward, engaging in research aimed at filling existing research gaps is important in developing resilient pandemic preparedness strategies for LMICs, ultimately resulting in more optimal population health outcomes.
