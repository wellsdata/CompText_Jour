Headnote The issue of fake news as it has risen to the fore in public and political discourse provides folklorists with an opportunity to not only weigh into the discussion with significant expertise, but also to reconsider our approach to the study of legend. In this paper, I propose a reorientation from a truth-centered approach to the study of legend to a doubt- centered one. Such an approach has the dual benefit of reorienting legend scholarship in new and productive ways while being particularly well-suited to the study of fake news.
Keywords
AFS ethnographic thesaurus: Contemporary legend; news; truth; politics; research, theory, and methodology
as a folklorist who studies contemporary legend, and a citizen who is deeply concerned about the attack on evidence-based arguments at the highest level of our government, the issue of fake news was one I felt obligated to address. Once taken up, I found myself moving in two directions. On the one hand, the issue of fake news directed my attention outward to how the field of folklore can and perhaps should engage more actively and aggressively in the political sphere. On the other, it directed my attention inward to some fundamental questions about how we approach contemporary legends. The two overlap in what I see as a need to be brutally honest about what we label legend, and how that acknowledgment opens up space for greater engagement in the public square. To do so, I would like to advocate for a doubtcentered approach to legend. Such an approach has the benefit of not only reorienting our analysis in new and productive ways but, in this case, of being particularly well-suited to the study of fake news.
To be clear, I do not equate legend with fake news, but I do see them as interrelated. In contemporary usage in the United States, there are at least three distinct definitions for "fake news." One is news that is intentionally fabricated, stories manufactured by people who know them to be false but peddle them as true. When such stories are created by governments, they are often called disinformation or propaganda (see Ellis 2018). When they appear online, shared through social media or other means that anonymize the source and blur intent, the more generic "fake news" is more commonly applied. A second definition is news that is intentionally fabricated as satire, such as stories in the Onion and other humorous media outlets. The third has been one popularized by President Donald Trump for any news he does not like or agree with. He has also labeled entire news agencies, including CNN, NBC, the New York Times, and the Washington Post, as "fake news" for reporting stories he declares to be false. He rarely provides evidence for such declarations, employing "the bully pulpit" exactly as the term suggests.
My primary interest is in the first definition of fake news as intentionally false information disseminated as true, though all three can enter the folk tradition when picked up by others and shared. My starting point for understanding fake news is shaped by my current research into the narratives told about public assistance in the United States. Consider the story that has circulated through social media both as a video and as an email, that in Michigan, Muslim men can get welfare benefits for up to four wives. The fact that the customer service phone line for Michigan's food stamp program provides instructions in Arabic is offered as evidence that the entire story is true (it is not). The story was originally created by CleanTV, a conservative online media source, and was posted to YouTube. By September 2014, it was circulating via email, referencing the video but framed as a personal experience narrative, beginning with the following:
How many wives can a Muslim have on welfare in the state of Michigan? This is
based on a conservative video currently "making the rounds" on the internet. I actually called the Michigan Dept. of Human Services to check this out and it is true.
("Muslim Men" 2015)
What began as fake news created by a known actor was ultimately taken up by the public-shared through social media, email, and likely word of mouth-and became part of a folk tradition of stories that were credible and plausible. Or was it? As researchers discovered, the story was used by a Russian company to fuel anger and outrage among Americans by creating multiple Facebook pages to disseminate this and similar stories, magnifying its reach (Confessore and Wakabayashi 2017). Distinguishing foreign companies, hackers, computer-savvy ideologues, cyborgs, and bots from the general public is not easy (Timberg 2017), reminding us that we cannot assume that online dissemination equals folk tradition (see Noyes 2018). Yet considering how a story, true or false, can move into the folk legend tradition, and why it does, is the bread and butter of contemporary legend scholars and provides a useful avenue for the study of fake news.
As a quick summary, then, I see the critical distinction between fake news and legend to hinge on credibility and adoption. Fake news describes a story intentionally and knowingly made up. Legend requires credibility and the possibility that the story could be true. Fake news created as false cannot be a legend because there is no doubt about its truth. Only when picked up and shared by others who see the story as credible, even if they doubt its accuracy, can it be considered legend. The distinction is an important one because we can, and should, call out the intentional manufacture of false information for what it is: fake. But we also can and should recognize that if that story finds purchase among us as the folk, we can and should attend to it as legend.
And so to the issue of credibility and truth. The hallmark of the legend is not that it is true, nor that it purports to be true, but rather that its truth is questioned and debated (see Marsh 2018). Based on these assumptions, Elliott Oring challenged folklorists to put their money where their mouths were. If legends were stories shared as true, then we should embrace all stories told as true or with "the rhetoric of truth" as legend (Oring 2008). In doing so, Oring argued for what amounts to a truth-centered approach to legend. I want to keep that approach as an important avenue of analysis, but I also want to suggest a parallel approach that refocuses analysis on doubt rather than truth.
A doubt-centered approach to legend is not just politically expedient for the types of politically charged narratives I have been studying around the issue of welfare, and not just attractive because I am deeply distressed at politically motivated attacks on journalism, verifiable facts, and democracy. A doubt-centered approach to legend is initially attractive simply by more honestly and accurately labeling much of the work we are already doing.
Consider how we as folklorists identify an appropriate topic of study in the first place. We do not tend to hear a story we believe to be true and think "I'm going to study that as a legend." Rather, we encounter a story that has traditional patterns, that we have heard before, that seems too good (or bad) to be true, that we disagree with, or that we do not want to believe to be true, and we dive in (Oring 2008:159; Fine and Turner 2001:3-12, 56). We can beat ourselves up about the hypocrisy of demonizing "the folk" who dismiss legends as stories told as true that are not, or we can admit that vernacular definitions of legend provide an emic approach to the genre that can be useful for some types of legends.
So, where would we start?
First, a doubt-centered approach to legend would require us to consider whose doubt matters. Obvious inclusion goes to anyone in the performance event, audience as well as performer. In face-to-face interactions, that group might be small; shared on social media, the group swells. Shared online, not only do the numbers swell even larger, but the chance for doubt and disagreement also rises exponentially. comment sections become excellent arenas for studying audience response.
These are no-brainers. But what about news pundits? What about folklorists? Oring (2008) and Carl Lindahl (2012), among others, have warned against folklorists serving as judge and jury, selecting those stories we believe to be false and studying them accordingly. They levied this warning as a critique, not a compliment. I agree, but it may be time to recognize that folklorists as a group are well positioned to address the issue of fact or fiction not as the arbiters of the truth, but as one of them.
This is where the distinction between mundane and supernatural legends becomes particularly relevant. Mundane legends offer the possibility of verifiability; supernatural legends do not. Focusing on doubt in the realm of the supernatural risks reifying elitist and Western approaches to belief, which David Hufford so effectively argued against with his distinction between cultural source and experiential source theory (1982). A truth-centered approach to legend seems both ethically and intellectually productive for supernatural legends. For mundane legends, however, analysis can focus on access to information, rhetorical strategy, and personal ideology without questioning a person's religious, spiritual, or supernatural views of the world. Doubt about mundane legends may question logic, but doubt about the supernatural may question rationality and sanity.
In either case, it is insulting, inappropriate, and incorrect to suggest that people are ignorant or irrational if they believe something that our research suggests is not true. However, we should make sure we do not fall prey to one of the most common logical fallacies: the either/or fallacy. The options are not either liar or lunatic, as we too often hyperbolize. You can be smart but wrong. You can be rational but construct an argument without sufficient information to lead to a supportable conclusion. Further, avoiding the issue of verifiable truth can do great damage. As we regularly remind our students in introductory folklore classes, folklore is not always pretty. Stories and jokes often explore the darkest corners of our beliefs, espousing racist, classist, sexist, misogynistic, homophobic, xenophobic, Islamaphobic, and jingoistic ideologies. Sometimes those dangers are mortal, as when legends encourage behaviors and actions that seem appropriate and justified but are not, such as when Edgar Welch drove to Washington, DC, and shot up a pizza restaurant that online stories said was home to a child sex-trafficking ring organized by high-ranking Democratic Party officials (it was not).1
Of course, not all stories set in the mundane world are verifiable. In fact, much of what folklorists have studied as legend is not verifiable. But we cannot ignore the distinction between verifiable fact and unverifiable opinion, perception, or belief. We are protected from analysis guided too heavily by our own ideologies not by ignoring the truth question, but by tackling it.
Before I started my research on welfare narratives-in fact, decades before I became a folklorist-I had heard the story of a woman who tried to buy dog food with her food stamps. When told she could not, she marched to the meat section, grabbed two steaks, and threw them on the conveyer belt, declaring: "If I can't buy dog food, then my dog will eat steak instead." Invariably, she loaded all her groceries into a brand-new Cadillac. I cannot remember whether I believed the story or not, but years later, when I read about Reagan's "welfare queen" story of "a woman in Chicago" who was using 80 names, 30 addresses, and 12 Social Security cards to collect veterans' benefits from four non-existent "deceased" husbands and garnering $150,000 a year, I dismissed the story as false. I did so primarily because journalists had labeled the story as a gross exaggeration ever since Reagan started telling it in 1976, but no doubt also because such race-baiting, classist arguments ran counter to my belief system, a clear example of confirmation bias. But here's the thing: when Josh Levin set out 40 years later to track down Linda Taylor, the real-life woman in Reagan's story, he found evidence that suggested Reagan's story was closer to the truth than the newspapers or the courts were (Levin 2013).
Ignoring this information turns a blind eye to how stories work. We need little explanation for understanding why true stories are told as true or false stories as false. But false stories told as true and true stories told as false raise far more interesting avenues for exploration and analysis. Further, ignoring the issue of truth risks reifying our own ideologies and assumptions because sometimes stories we assume to be false turn out to be true or include true elements. Consider stories of mice found in Coke bottles (Fine 1979) or organ theft legends (Campion-Vincent 2005) that may be false in detail and scope but reference real cases of soda contamination and organ trafficking.
Here again, the either/or fallacy risks snaring us, this time into declaring a story either true or false. There are many kinds of truths. We should explore them all, including the factual and verifiable. Michigan residents can hear information about applying for public assistance in Arabic; they cannot apply for aid for multiple wives. Reagan was right that Linda Taylor committed fraud. He was wrong that she was representative of the majority, or even a sizeable number, of aid recipients.2
This is where a doubt-centered approach is particularly useful. When I analyzed online comments about welfare, I found that some people clearly doubted the veracity of the welfare fraud stories on a factual level, labeling them legends that had been debunked years ago. For example, a commenter on a Yahoo Answers thread responded to a story about a woman on food stamps buying steak, alcohol, and ice cream for her dog by wondering:
You didn't run into that lady who posted pretty much the same thing . . . thirty years ago? These things make the rounds, the steak with food stamps, the illegal alien getting into the Escalade, the tired white woman working hard and seeing welfare leeches walk away with cigarettes and beer. Did I leave anything out? These aren't true stories, they are urban legends meant to make the poor look like thieves. They are meant to fan the flames and ignore reality.3
But I also found people locating doubt in other places, most often in the interpretation and generalizability of the stories, as with these comments posted in 2012 on the website titled "My Biggest Complaint":4
There are people who do abuse the system, but a majority do not. Why can't people look and listen before they judge? Btw, I can't even buy toilet paper with my food stamps, so how are others buying booze and cigarettes with theirs? Liars. (August 19, 2012)
While I won't deny that welfare abuse obviously DOES happen, can anyone give me any PROOF to back up "ME's" unsourced claim that "Half of people on welfare falsely apply"? Oh, really? Where did you hear that load of crap? The Heritage Foundation?
Lol. (September 2, 2012)
Question how the F·CK do any of you actually know who is or isn't on welfare? when you see someone with a luxury car do you ask them hey are you on welfare? or do you actually personally know many not a couple MANY people really abusing the system? because many people on welfare either don't have a car or are dependant on family or friends for transportation. I own a 1991 Acura NSX valued around 37,000 dollars and my former girlfriend who was on welfare drove my car on occasion and I drove her on occasion to the welfare office so when you idiots see a decent vehicle at DHS maybe it's not a clients car! now did you rocket scientist ever consider that?? HELL NO! About the iphones deal! boy you idiots are lost! anybody with afunctioning brain knows that you can go to most cell phone stores and usually get a iphone with a contract very cheaply! HELL I could have gotten a iphone for free at my cell phone provider! with no contract at Metro pcs and their month to month! Boy you people are so incredibly ignorant! (August 2, 2012)
Listening to people express their doubts, not just argue for their truths, reminds us that people are deeply engaged in conversations about what is real and what is not, what is fair and what is not, what is representative and what is not, proactively, not just defensively. Further, doubt is not always just the opposite side of belief. For example, few narrators explicitly evoked the "instance of a class" argument to suggest their story was one of many and therefore emblematic of a common, even typical experience, but a lot of people challenged each other on the generalizability of their stories and claims.
A reorientation from a truth-centered approach to legend to a doubt-centered one does more than reveal rhetorical strategies that may otherwise go unexamined. For one, it reorients our focus from a performer-centered approach to an audiencecentered one, a move folklorists continue to take too little advantage of. For another, it can flip perspective so that narrative becomes counter-narrative and vice versa. Consider the stories about Obama's birth certificate (see Turner 2018). According to a 2016 poll, the majority of Republicans (72 percent) continue to doubt Obama's US citizenship, despite incontrovertible evidence to the contrary (Clinton and Rousch 2016). For "birthers"-people who question the validity of Obama's birth certificate- the story that Obama was born in Hawaii is the story to be doubted; the story that he is secretly a Muslim born in Kenya is the story to be believed. For many others- including most Democrats, the mainstream media, and investigative journalists-the reverse is true.
Shifting to a doubt-centered approach also encourages us to consider how Trump's concept of fake news-stories he does not like or agree with-can move from individual declaration to folk tradition. How might the news Trump declares to be fake be taken up by his supporters and transformed into a vernacular legend tradition? Similarly, how might stories that begin as intentionally manufactured falsehoods be taken up by people as possibly true? The first transforms the true into the false, the latter the false into true. In both cases, a doubt-centered approach to legend provides a means for analyzing fake news if and when it moves into the folk tradition.
Finally, a doubt-centered approach to legend centers analysis on those places of disagreement rather than agreement within a folk group. Initially, such a move may seem antithetical to productive civic (and civil) discourse, particularly in these politically partisan and polarized times. But even within the echo chambers developed within friend groups and in our choices of media, people do not always agree. Such disagreement may be particularly explicit (and profane) online, but it also appears in conversations among friends, family members, and neighbors. Among a group of businessmen in my town who gather every Friday for coffee, for example, consensus rules the day. But after a barrage of criticism against aid recipients who use their cash to buy alcohol, one of the men gently challenged his peers on whether the poor should ever enjoy a beer or a cigarette, doubting the interpretations they had drawn from their stories of welfare abuse (Mould 2016). Paying close attention to how doubt is raised and against which claims it is levied can not only reveal those areas in narrative open for interpretation, but can also highlight avenues for addressing divides in ideology within groups that too often go unexamined by scholars. Attending to doubt makes it clear that "the folk" are testing the boundaries of what should be believed and what should be questioned not just across political divides, but within seemingly homogenous groups (cf. Bauman 1971; his concept of differential identity). Attention only to the rhetoric of truth risks erasing or ignoring these differences, leading scholars to reify the view that all conservatives or all liberals operate as monolithic groups. Doubt is driving much of the discourse around both contemporary legends and "fake news"; it's time we reoriented our analysis and paid attention.
