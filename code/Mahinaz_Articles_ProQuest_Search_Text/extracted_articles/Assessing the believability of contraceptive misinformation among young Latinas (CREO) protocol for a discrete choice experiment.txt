Strengths and limitations of this study - This study applies a discrete choice experiment (DCE) approach to contraceptive misinformation, aiming to generate direct evidence on which specific message features drive believability.

- Instagram story-style ‘posts’ with animated text and voiceover enhance ecological validity relative to the static text vignettes or written attribute description.

- The bilingual design, developed for fidelity rather than literal translation, allows the same message features to be tested in both English and Spanish.

- Recruitment through targeted social media advertising may introduce self-selection bias, since people who already engage with health content online may differ from those who do not.

- The forced-choice format, which requires selecting one post per pair with no opt-out, may introduce response pressure when both options are equally believable or unbelievable and does not mimic real life social media interactions in which a person is not forced to engage.


Introduction
Health misinformation circulates widely on social media platforms. 1 Contraceptive misinformation is especially prevalent, particularly on TikTok and Instagram, where emotionally charged personal narratives exaggerate side effects and misrepresent how methods work. 2 3 A 2024 content analysis found that 74% of YouTube influencers discussing birth control promoted discontinuing hormonal methods, and nearly half of top TikTok posts discouraged the pill, Intrauterine Device (IUD) or shot. 4 Some women switch to less effective methods, or stop using contraception altogether, driven by side-effect concerns based on social media narratives rather than clinical guidance. 5 6 Young Latinas are disproportionately exposed and more likely than their White peers to internalise fertility-related misinformation, a pattern shaped by language barriers, limited institutional access and a documented history of coercive reproductive practices that continues to erode medical trust. 6 7
Accurate contraceptive knowledge is crucial for reproductive autonomy while inaccurate knowledge changes both how a method is perceived and how much control a woman feels she has over choosing it. Knowledge and logistical barriers are among the most commonly cited reasons women report not using their preferred contraceptive method. 8 When patients are asked what information matters most for contraceptive decision-making, understanding how a method actually works to prevent pregnancy ranks as the top priority. 9 Pill misperceptions are associated with worse attitudes and weaker descriptive norms toward use and lower knowledge about a method is independently associated with lower autonomy in contraceptive decision-making. 10 However, baseline knowledge of contraceptive methods is not evenly distributed across race/ethnicity: Latina women report lower awareness of available contraceptive methods than their peers, which widens the gap between what women want from a method and what they end up using. 11 Contraceptive misinformation on social media distorts the link between knowledge and control over the decision. A post that misrepresents how a method works presents a false premise that might not ultimately reflect someone’s actual preferences.
Despite the impact and how widespread misinformation is, it is not known why certain messages are more believable than others. Existing studies document the prevalence and content of contraceptive misinformation, and separate work has linked influencer credibility, specifically perceived trustworthiness and expertise, to contraceptive behaviour. 3 12–14 What remains unknown is which individual topical, rhetorical and structural characteristics of a message drive believability. This distinction matters for understanding how misinformation affects credibility and to inform intervention design for disseminating accurate contraceptive information on social media and otherwise. Counter-messaging strategies require knowing not just that misinformation spreads, but which specific features make a post persuasive to a given audience.
Discrete choice experiments (DCEs) are an underused but well-suited approach to answer this question. By presenting participants with systematically varied pairs of stimuli and asking them to choose between them, DCEs decompose the relative contribution of each attribute to a stated outcome. 15 16 This approach isolates attribute-level effects that social media-based experiments, surveys and content analyses cannot. We have previously applied DCE methodology to identify preferred features of social media vaccination posts in a California sample, demonstrating its feasibility for health communication research. 17 To our knowledge, DCEs have not been applied to contraceptive misinformation specifically, where the outcome of interest is believability rather than preference for accurate content.
This protocol describes the design and planned methods for a DCE that’s part of the Choice and Reaction to Evaluate Online Opinions on Family Planning (CREO) Study, a two-phase investigation using a visual DCE and a behavioural reaction task to examine which features of contraceptive misinformation influence believability among young Latinas in the USA. The aim of this study is to estimate how young Latinas make trade-offs between message features when deciding which influencer posts on contraception they find believable, isolating the relative contribution of each feature to that judgement.
Methods and analysis
Study design
This is a cross-sectional online study using a visual DCE to examine which features of contraceptive misinformation posts influence perceived believability among young Latinas in the USA. The study is grounded in the Online Misinformation Engagement Framework, which conceptualises misinformation influence as operating across four sequential stages: source selection, information selection, evaluation and behavioural reaction. 18 Phase I, the DCE described in this protocol, targets the evaluation stage, where believability judgments are formed. Phase II will use a behavioural reaction task to examine how participants engage with posts in a simulated social media environment; its design will be informed by DCE findings and will be described elsewhere.
Attribute and level development
Attributes and levels were selected through a systematic literature review, expert consultation and operationalisability. We searched PubMed, the UCSF library catalogue and Google Scholar using terms related to contraceptive information sources, social media messaging, misinformation characteristics and health communication among Latinas. 23 studies were identified and reviewed to identify features of contraceptive content that influence engagement, trust and believability among young women. This process generated an initial list of 13 candidate attributes.
Attributes were retained if they met three criteria: theoretical grounding in the misinformation or health communication literature, visual operationalisability within a simulated Instagram post format and non-collinearity with other retained attributes. Applying these criteria reduced the set to six attributes ( table 1 ); the full table with definitions and sources is presented as online supplemental table 1 . Two exclusions warrant specific explanation. First, fertility awareness methods (FAM) were removed from the contraceptive method attribute because the absence of hormonal side effects made it impossible to construct misinformation content that varied meaningfully on the construct attribute (eg, FAM made me gain weight). For the same reason, we specified in the post language that the IUD was hormonal (as opposed to a copper IUD that does not have hormonal side effects). Second, three of the original framing levels (benefits, adverse effects, neutrality) were replaced by misinformation constructs: false certainty, exaggeration and omission to be more grounded in the misinformation literature. 3 19

The final DCE includes six attributes across 19 levels, yielding 864 possible profile combinations ( table 1 ).
Experimental design
The full factorial design yielded 864 possible attribute-level combinations (3×4×2×4×3×3). Presenting all combinations is not feasible, so a fractional factorial design was used to generate a statistically efficient subset. The design was generated in Stata V.16 using the dcreate command, which applies a modified Fedorov algorithm to identify profiles that maximise D-efficiency, the determinant of the information matrix. The algorithm converged at iteration 5, producing 32 profiles organised into 16 forced-choice pairs. Each participant will complete all 16 choice tasks. Task order will be randomised within participants to reduce order effects.
In each task, participants will view two simulated Instagram posts and be asked: “Which of these two posts would you be more likely to believe?” The response is forced-choice with no opt-out. Believability was selected as the outcome because it is the most direct, measurable indicator of whether a specific post is convincing.
Stimulus development
32 posts were drafted to serve as DCE stimuli ( online supplemental table 2 ). Initial drafts were produced by a research team member (SMM) and refined using an Artifical Intelligence (AI)-assisted Claude drafting process, with the attribute-level matrix and attribute definition provided as input. Two team members (LAM and SMM) reviewed all posts independently, then met to compare and consolidate versions. Drafts were then reviewed by a team member with health communication expertise (EP) along with the rest of the team. One additional round of edits was completed before the lead investigator reviewed and approved the final set. Revisions focused on removing clinical language, eliminating references to specific duration of contraceptive use and ensuring posts reflected the register of social media content.
Post formats are held constant across all 32 profiles: identical structure, approximate word count and Instagram-style visual layout. To simulate a controlled social media environment, each post will be produced as an Instagram story-style stimulus: a static influencer image with animated text overlaid and a voiceover reading the post content in English and Spanish. The full post text will also be displayed in the survey to ensure comprehension is not limited by accessibility, audio quality or reading pace. Influencer images were sourced and purchased from iStock under open licences, representing three influencer types: beauty, lifestyle and fitness. Structural consistency across all stimuli will ensure that participants respond to attribute-level variation rather than differences in format or production.
All posts were drafted in English and translated into Spanish. Translations prioritised register fidelity over literal equivalence, for example, personal narrative posts required colloquial, emotionally direct language, while informational claim posts used a flatter, more clinical tone. This distinction matters because ecological validity depends on posts sounding like content participants encounter online.
Participants and recruitment
Eligible participants will be self-identified Latinas or Indigenous women from Latin America, aged 18–29, residing in the USA, with regular use of at least one social media platform. The survey will be administered in English and Spanish via Qualtrics. Participants will be recruited through targeted ads on Instagram, Facebook and TikTok. Ads will direct users to a brief screening survey to assess eligibility. Individuals who meet criteria and express interest will be asked to provide contact information. A trained research assistant will follow up by phone or text to confirm eligibility and, if confirmed, distribute a one-time personalised survey link via email or phone to avoid fraudulent and automated bot data.
From initial recruitment, participants will be informed that the study involves two sequential surveys. Participants who complete survey 1 will be recontacted for survey 2 once the DCE findings have been analysed and phase II stimuli developed. To support retention across both phases, participants will receive 40% of the study incentive (US$20) on completing survey 1 and the remaining 60% (US$30) on completing survey 2.
Sample size
The sample size was estimated using the Johnson-Orme rule of thumb for DCEs, which calculates the minimum number of respondents needed to estimate main effects based on the largest number of attribute levels, the number of choice tasks per respondent and the number of alternatives per task. 15 With a maximum of four levels for any attribute, eight choice tasks per respondent and two alternatives per task, the minimum sample size required to estimate main effects is 125 participants (500×4)/(8×2)=125. To provide additional power for testing interaction effects, this estimate was conservatively doubled to 250 participants, consistent with common DCE practice. 20 Our target sample of 500 participants exceeds both thresholds and provides capacity for planned subgroup analyses by survey language and nativity, contingent on the final distribution of respondents. The enrolment target of 500 also allows for anticipated attrition between phase I and phase II of the study.
Data collection instrument
The survey will have two sections, the first will collect demographic information and assess trusted sources of health information and social media use patterns, which will serve as covariates and moderators in the analysis ( online supplemental file 1 ). The second will present the DCE. The survey will be administered via Qualtrics, optimised for mobile, and available in English and Spanish. The survey will be piloted with a small sample prior to full deployment to assess comprehension, task completion time, and data quality.
Statistical analysis
DCE data will be analysed using a conditional logit model as the primary specification, estimated in Stata using the clogit command. 21 This model estimates the relative contribution of each attribute level to believability by treating each forced-choice response as a comparison between two profiles. A mixed logit model will be estimated as a secondary specification using mixlogit, treating attribute-level coefficients as randomly distributed across participants. 22
Planned subgroup analyses will examine whether attribute-level effects differ by language of survey completion and nativity (US-born vs foreign-born). Covariates that will be included in all models are age, educational attainment, prior contraceptive experience and social media use frequency. Results will be reported in accordance with the DIRECT checklist for DCEs in health. 23
Patient and public involvement
Community members were not involved in the initial design of the study or the selection of DCE attributes and levels. However, social media influencers were consulted during stimulus development to assess whether posts reflected realistic social media content. Additional community input will be incorporated at two subsequent stages: during pilot testing of the survey instrument prior to full deployment, and during the design of phase II, where community perspectives will inform the development of the behavioural reaction task. Findings will be shared with participants and community partners before the close of the project.
Ethics and dissemination
This study was deemed exempt by the University of California, San Francisco Institutional Review Board (IRB #25-45383). Although exempt, all participants will provide informed consent prior to survey completion and will have access to contact information for the research team throughout the study period.
All stimuli used in the DCE are clearly labelled as research materials within the survey. Given that participants are exposed to deliberately misleading health content, a structured debriefing protocol will be implemented at the end of the survey. The debriefing will clarify that all posts were designed for research purposes and do not reflect accurate health information. Corrective information regarding side effects of each contraceptive method featured in the stimuli will be provided, directly addressing the specific misinformation claims participants encountered ( online supplemental table 3 ). 24 25 Accurate summaries of the common and rare side effects associated with each method will also be displayed ( online supplemental table 3 ). Charts were composed on Canva using information from bedsider.org and the American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists. To ensure exposure to the debriefing, participants will be required to review this information before receiving their incentive.
Findings will be disseminated through peer-reviewed publication and conference presentations targeting reproductive health, health communication and misinformation research audiences. Results will also be shared with community-based organisations serving Latinas in California, through community forums, and via social media in accessible formats in both English and Spanish. The DCE stimulus library and experimental design will be made available to support peer review, replication and extension by other research teams.
Discussion
The study described in this protocol seeks to address a gap in the misinformation literature by isolating the specific message features that make a post believable. Existing work documents the prevalence, content and style of social media disinformation descriptively. Identifying which characteristics drive believability in social media family planning posts can inform counter-messaging design and help identify which topics and trends are most likely to gain traction.
Findings from this DCE will directly inform the design of phase II’s behavioural reaction task, to connect stated attribute preferences from the DCE to observed engagement. More broadly, this study extends DCE methodology into misinformation research, an application that remains rare despite the method’s capacity to decompose message-level effects that survey and content-analytic approaches cannot isolate. If this design can reliably identify which features drive believability for contraceptive misinformation, the same approach extends to other domains, health-related or not, where message-level drivers of belief remain similarly underspecified.
This study has several strengths. To our knowledge, it is the first to apply DCE methodology to contraceptive misinformation, generating empirical evidence on which message features drive believability rather than relying on descriptive or self-reported exposure data. More broadly, it advances the application of DCEs in health communication research, 26 an area where DCEs have been underutilised despite their capacity to decompose the relative contribution of specific message characteristics to audience responses and the subsequent impact that could have in health communication in general. 17 The bilingual design and targeted recruitment of young Latinas in California address a population that is disproportionately affected by contraceptive misinformation but remain underrepresented in reproductive health communication and intervention research. 27 The use of Instagram story-style stimuli with animated text and voiceover enhances ecological validity, producing stimuli that more closely resemble content participants encounter in practice than static text vignettes. The sequential two-phase design that combines stated preferences from the DCE with behavioural engagement data from the reaction task will generate a more complete picture of misinformation susceptibility than either component alone.
Several limitations should be noted. Recruitment via social media ads may introduce self-selection bias, as individuals who engage with health content online may differ systematically from those who do not. As with all DCEs, stated believability in a controlled survey environment may not perfectly predict responses to misinformation encountered organically on social media, where algorithmic curation, peer networks and repeated exposure shape how content is received. The forced-choice format which requires participants to select one post per pair with no opt-out may also introduce response pressure in cases where both options are perceived as equally believable or equally unbelievable and not capture more nuanced responses. Finally, community members were not involved in the initial design phase, which may limit the extent to which attribute selection reflects lived community priorities.
