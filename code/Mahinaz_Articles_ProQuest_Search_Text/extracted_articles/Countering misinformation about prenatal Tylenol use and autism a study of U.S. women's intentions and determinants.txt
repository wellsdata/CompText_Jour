Introduction
On September 22, 2025, U.S. Secretary of Health and Human Services Robert F. Kennedy Jr. held a White House press conference alongside President Donald Trump urging pregnant individuals to avoid using Tylenol (acetaminophen) during pregnancy, warning that it “could contribute to autism in children” (Looi and Bowie, 2025). Although both officials acknowledged that existing studies on the relationship between acetaminophen and autism spectrum disorder in children are conflicting and that no clear causal link has been established, they nevertheless recommended that physicians prescribe the lowest possible dosage of Tylenol to pregnant individuals experiencing fever. This recommendation contradicts established medical guidance advising pregnant individuals to use Tylenol as needed for pain and fever during pregnancy (American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists, 2025). Subsequently, the U.S. Food and Drug Administration issued a notice to physicians and initiated a safety label update for acetaminophen warning of potential neurodevelopmental risks, while also emphasizing that a definitive causal relationship between acetaminophen use during pregnancy and autism has not been established (U.S. Food and Drug Administration, 2025a).
Public statements by high-level government officials can strongly influence health beliefs and behaviors, particularly during pregnancy when perceptions of risk are heightened. Following the Trump administration's announcement regarding the use of Tylenol during pregnancy, unverified claims about the safety of acetaminophen rapidly circulated on social media (Kekatos and Singh, 2025). The spread of these claims contributed to increased anxiety and fear among the U.S. public, while also generating frustration among clinicians and public health advocates concerned about potential harms of misleading claims for maternal and fetal health (Looi and Bowie, 2025). These reactions were especially pronounced among women of reproductive age, as messages that conflict with established medical recommendations can intensify fear, uncertainty, and confusion surrounding health decision-making during pregnancy.
In response to these claims, women physicians, women's health advocates, and social media influencers used their digital platforms to challenge and correct misleading narratives about the relationship between prenatal Tylenol use and autism in children (Gale, 2025;Landry, 2025). For example, some women physicians and researchers used platforms such as TikTok and Instagram to clarify that current scientific evidence does not establish a causal link between prenatal acetaminophen use and autism, while also encouraging pregnant women to seek guidance from health professionals instead of relying on viral claims circulating online. This response reflects a broader and growing phenomenon in which women actively leverage digital communication channels including social media to raise awareness, share expertise, and counter misinformation related to women's health issues (Figueroa et al., 2021;Khosla et al., 2023).
Against this backdrop, the present study examines how women in the United States perceived the controversy surrounding Tylenol in late 2025 and how their digital information competence and civic mindedness are associated with their intentions to correct misinformation on the topic. It also explores how digital health advocacy is reflected in women's perceptions of the issue. Empirical data for the study come from a national survey of women ages 18–54 living in the United States conducted in December 2025.
Understanding women's perceptions of the Tylenol controversy and their motivations to correct related misinformation is critical for assessing how ambiguous or misleading health messaging shapes health decision-making and emotional well-being. The findings can inform strategies to mitigate unintended public health consequences of politicized or imprecise health communication while also contributing to a broader understanding of why and how women engage in correcting misinformation related to women's health issues.
Literature review
Women’s use of online health information
Digital communication technologies have fundamentally transformed how people access and engage with health information. As digital tools have become more accessible and widely integrated into the health sector, the proportion of U.S. adults who go online to research potential medical conditions or connect with healthcare providers has increased significantly over time (Pew Research Center, 2013;2024). Web search engines and social media platforms now serve as primary channels through which people obtain information about diseases, medications, and other health-related topics (Huo et al., 2019;Pew Research Center, 2024). The COVID-19 pandemic further intensified public reliance on online and social media sources for health information (Chen and Wang, 2021). During this period, a substantial share of individuals reported using social media to obtain health-related content, with one report indicating that nearly 60% accessed such information at least once a week (Neely et al., 2021).
Younger generations, particularly Millennials and Generation Z, report higher levels of trust in social media platforms such as Reddit and TikTok, which have become increasingly prominent sources of health information (Blandi et al., 2022). Additionally, research suggests that women are more likely than men to seek health-related information online (Fox and Duggan, 2013;Wang and Cohen, 2023). For instance, a 2024 survey found that a vast majority (92.4%) of young women in the United States encounter health information on TikTok through algorithmically curated content, while 65.5% actively search for such information on the platform (Kirkpatrick and Lawrie, 2024). National survey data from the Kaiser Family Foundation further indicate that approximately 14% of women aged 18–25 have made or considered changing their contraceptive method based on information encountered on social media (Frederiksen et al., 2024). These findings highlight the substantial influence that digital platforms can exert on women's reproductive decision-making and bodily autonomy. More broadly, emerging technologies such as artificial intelligence and social media are increasingly shaping how women, girls, and gender-diverse individuals access and manage their health information. They offer expanded access to information and educational resources while also presenting new challenges related to information quality and reliability (Khosla et al., 2023).
Social media platforms can provide emotional support and help normalize reproductive experiences that have historically been stigmatized or overlooked, particularly when healthcare systems fail to adequately address these concerns (Faro et al., 2024). These platforms may also offer relatively anonymous environments where individuals can seek information about sensitive topics they may feel uncomfortable discussing with healthcare professionals or others in their personal networks. Algorithmic recommendations, peer experiences, and narratives of empowerment can further encourage women to engage with health content online, particularly when they perceive their healthcare providers as dismissive or when they seek validation regarding stigmatized reproductive health concerns (Kirkpatrick and Lawrie, 2024). While online platforms can empower women to become more actively engaged in their healthcare, they may also increase susceptibility to misinformation when social media algorithms reinforce existing beliefs through repeated exposure to similar content and when anecdotal experiences are perceived as more credible than evidence-based medical information. Therefore, understanding the intersecting demographic, socioeconomic, psychological, and experiential factors that influence women's online health information-seeking behavior is essential.
Women's online health information-seeking behavior is influenced by a combination of demographic, socioeconomic, psychological, and access-related factors, as well as their prior healthcare experiences and personal beliefs. Research shows that characteristics such as age, race/ethnicity, income, education, employment, health status, caregiving responsibilities, and access to a regular healthcare provider significantly shape whether and how women seek health information online (Ajayi et al., 2023;Jia et al., 2021). In addition, the availability of online communities, privacy features, and opportunities for real-time interaction encourage online information seeking, whereas limited health literacy, poor information-search skills, and information overload act as barriers (Jia et al., 2021). These factors contribute to unequal access to reliable health information and may ultimately influence health outcomes.
Beyond demographic and access-level factors, psychological and communication-related factors further explain why and how women engage in online health information seeking. Drawing on the Planned Risk Information Seeking Model (PRISM),Hovick et al., 2014demonstrated that subjective norms, attitudes toward seeking, perceived knowledge insufficiency, and affective risk responses were among the strongest predictors of health and cancer information seeking, framing the behavior as a deliberate, planned response to perceived risk and uncertainty rather than a purely reactive one. When this model was applied specifically to gender differences in web-based health information seeking,Link and Baumann, 2023found that subjective norms were the strongest overall predictor, but this varied by gender: men's information-seeking behavior was more strongly shaped by subjective norms, whereas women's was more strongly associated with perceived seeking control.
These psychological factors interact with women's experiences with healthcare providers and their personal belief, shaping their confidence, trust, and reliance on online health information sources (Ajayi et al., 2023). For example, experiences of dismissal, inadequate communication, or feeling unheard by healthcare professionals may motivate women to seek information from digital platforms that provide empathy, validation, and shared lived experiences (Hildenbrand et al., 2022;Kirkpatrick and Lawrie, 2024). In addition, women often interpret new information through the lens of their existing beliefs and experiences. Confirmation bias and the persuasive nature of personal narratives can increase women's trust in information shared by peers with similar lived experiences, particularly for stigmatized or poorly understood conditions such as reproductive health concerns, as these narratives provide emotional reassurance, normalize health concerns, and promote self-advocacy (Seo et al., 2025).
Recent reports suggest that primary reproductive health topics women encounter online include contraception and abortion, fertility, chronic diseases, breast cancer, maternal health, and vaccines (John et al., 2025). However, despite the abundance of available information, the use of social media for health purposes also carries significant risks. Online health content may include misinformation or disinformation, such as misinterpretations of scientific findings or information presented without appropriate context. These inaccuracies can contribute to confusion, mistrust, stigmatization, and potential harm, particularly in the context of Sexual and Reproductive Health and Rights (SRHR) (Purnat et al., 2025). For example, claims linking the use of Tylenol (acetaminophen) during pregnancy to autism often disregard substantial scientific evidence supporting its safety when used as directed (Qadir, 2025). Such misleading narratives can generate unnecessary anxiety, foster misinterpretation of scientific evidence, and lead to potentially harmful health decisions among women seeking information about reproductive or maternal health.
Given the widespread reliance on digital platforms for health information, understanding how misinformation about health topics emerges, spreads, and influences public perceptions has become an increasingly important area of research. The following section reviews the growing body of literature on health misinformation and its implications for public health communication, with particular attention to the recent Tylenol controversy.
Health misinformation and the Tylenol controversy
Health misinformation, defined as false or inaccurate information about health-related issues, appears in approximately 82% of adults’ social media feeds (Stimpson et al., 2025). Much of this misinformation concerns topics such as vaccines, drugs or smoking, noncommunicable diseases, pandemics, eating disorders, and medical treatments (Suarez-Lledo and Alvarez-Galvez, 2021). On social media platforms, misleading claims are frequently amplified through anecdotal experiences shared within users’ networks (Chou et al., 2018). The consequences are both harmful and costly. Health misinformation can undermine public trust in scientists and medical professionals while also generating economic burdens through increased hospitalizations and lost productivity among individuals who avoid necessary medical care (Stimpson et al., 2025).
The spread of misinformation during the COVID-19 pandemic was particularly pronounced, prompting the World Health Organization (WHO) to characterize the phenomenon as an “infodemic” (Schlicht et al., 2024). During this period, individuals were exposed to overwhelming volumes of both accurate information and misinformation, making it difficult to identify reliable sources. Pregnant individuals were especially vulnerable to misinformation during the pandemic, particularly regarding decisions about receiving the COVID-19 vaccine (Malik et al., 2024).Malik et al., 2024found that misinformation about vaccine safety during pregnancy spread rapidly due to limited monitoring of health misinformation on social media platforms and the proliferation of online messaging promoting vaccine skepticism.
A more recent example of health misinformation that has generated concern among pregnant individuals is the Tylenol-autism controversy (Kekatos and Singh, 2025;Looi and Bowie, 2025). Following a press conference by President Donald Trump and U.S. Health and Human Services Secretary Robert F. Kennedy Jr., in which pregnant individuals were urged to avoid Tylenol because it “could contribute to autism in children,” discussions about acetaminophen use during pregnancy spread widely on social media (Gale, 2025;Kekatos and Singh, 2025). This recommendation conflicted with established clinical guidance indicating that acetaminophen is safe for managing pain and fever during pregnancy when used as directed. As a result, it contributed to heightened fear and uncertainty among pregnant individuals, emotional responses commonly associated with exposure to health misinformation (Sathianathan et al., 2025;Swire-Thompson et al., 2020).
The framing of these claims further amplified public concern. Statements suggesting that Tylenol use during pregnancy “could contribute to autism” and references to “mounting evidence” conflicted with established medical consensus, yet their delivery by authoritative political figures lent them credibility (Looi and Bowie, 2025). More broadly, the administration's messaging illustrates the challenges of communicating emerging scientific evidence in a politicized environment. Prior research demonstrates that misinformation framed in emotionally charged language is often more persuasive than nuanced scientific explanations, particularly when it evokes fear or uncertainty (Loeb et al., 2019;Sathianathan et al., 2025;Swire-Thompson et al., 2020). Consequently, political messaging surrounding Tylenol may have overshadowed more cautious interpretations of the scientific evidence.
Although some observational studies have reported associations between prenatal acetaminophen exposure and autism diagnoses, more rigorous research has found no causal relationship. For example, an analysis of 2.5 million Swedish births using sibling comparison methods found no increased autism risk after accounting for shared genetic and environmental factors (Ahlqvist et al., 2024). Similarly, a Japanese cohort study involving more than 200,000 pregnancies reported no association between prenatal acetaminophen use and neurodevelopmental disorders (Okubo et al., 2026). These findings highlight the important distinction between correlation and causation and suggest that previously observed associations are more likely explained by underlying maternal health conditions or shared genetic factors rather than a direct effect of the medication. Clinicians therefore continue to recommend acetaminophen as the safest option for managing pain and fever during pregnancy when used as directed and at the lowest effective dose (Gale, 2025;Kekatos and Singh, 2025). The Tylenol-autism controversy illustrates how health misinformation, particularly when promoted by authoritative figures and framed in emotionally resonant language, can spread rapidly, conflict with established medical guidance, and shape public perceptions of risk.
Women often rely on their own lived experiences, instead of institutional sources, when evaluating the credibility of online health information. For example,Malki et al., 2024found that women frequently encountered emotionally charged health misinformation on social media and evaluated it using ad-hoc strategies such as drawing on personal experience and reading social media comment sections, which served as a form of collective sensemaking in the absence of trusted guidance. This reliance on experiential knowledge is particularly evident in reproductive and maternal health contexts, where personal narratives can both inform decision-making and facilitate the spread of misinformation. Similarly,Simas et al., 2021reported that although pregnant women viewed healthcare professionals as trusted experts, many perceived gaps in provider communication and instead relied on search engines, digital media, and the experiences of other women when making vaccination decisions. Consistent with these findings,John et al., 2025concluded that misleading reproductive health narratives frequently use personal stories to erode trust in guidance from public health agencies, highlighting how experiential evidence can be strategically leveraged to spread misinformation.
Beyond lived experience, prior beliefs, emotional responses, and perceived susceptibility also influence how women evaluate health misinformation.Jheng et al., 2025found that preexisting vaccine skepticism shaped women's responses to corrective messages: among women with low skepticism, harm-focused narratives evoked negative emotions that reduced the effectiveness of corrections, whereas among skeptical women, narrative framing itself lowered engagement and intentions to seek additional information. Similarly,Kirkpatrick and Lawrie, 2024found that although young women widely recognized the prevalence of health misinformation on TikTok, they consistently viewed themselves as less vulnerable to its influence than others.
These findings suggest that women's evaluations of online health information are shaped not only by source credibility but also by the interplay of personal experience, prior attitudes, emotional responses, and perceived susceptibility to misinformation. Although recent research has increasingly examined how these individual factors influence the evaluation of health misinformation, little attention has been given to how they affect women's willingness to correct misinformation related to the Tylenol case. The present study addresses this gap. While misinformation can spread rapidly through digital platforms, these same platforms also provide opportunities for individuals and communities to challenge misleading claims. Women, in particular, increasingly use digital media to advocate for reproductive health, share knowledge, and correct misinformation on women's health issues (Figueroa et al., 2021;Khosla et al., 2023). The following section examines women's engagement in digital health advocacy and misinformation correction.
Women’s digital health advocacy and misinformation correction
Although women increasingly rely on social media for information about medications and reproductive health, greater use of these platforms does not necessarily correspond to higher levels of online health information literacy. Studies indicate that many users do not verify health information encountered on social media with healthcare professionals or other credible sources (Neely et al., 2021;Paul and Headley-Johnson, 2025). Because a substantial share of social media health content lacks an evidence-based foundation, users may encounter misleading or inaccurate information that can contribute to confusion, anxiety, or unsafe health practices (Lim et al., 2022). Consistent with this concern, reliance on social media for health information has been associated with increased exposure to misinformation and potentially harmful health decisions (Paul and Headley-Johnson, 2025).
As social media becomes a central source of health information for women, it is important to understand how they evaluate credibility, establish trust, and respond to misleading claims online. Assessing the validity of health information often requires complex cognitive processing to determine whether claims should be accepted or rejected (Jia et al., 2021;Liu et al., 2025;Palmer and Gorman, 2025). Research frequently draws on motivated reasoning and other dual-process frameworks to explain how individuals interpret health information (Liu et al., 2025;Maguire et al., 2022). For example, during the COVID-19 pandemic, factors such as political partisanship, emotional strain, and a desire for certainty contributed to the rapid acceptance and spread of misinformation (Vu and Chen, 2024;Zhao et al., 2020). In general, individuals are more likely to accept information that aligns with their preferences or expectations, whereas analytic processing can encourage verification and more deliberate evaluation.
Another important consideration is whether individuals choose to correct misinformation when they encounter it. Increasingly, women have used social media platforms to challenge misleading narratives about women's health (Gale, 2025;Landry, 2025;Seo et al., 2025). The expansion of digital platforms has enabled women from diverse backgrounds to share lived experiences, mobilize attention through hashtags, and advocate for issues affecting women's health and well-being (Dixon, 2014). These developments reflect broader patterns associated with fourth-wave feminism, which builds on earlier intersectional movements and uses digital media to advance gender equity and public awareness (Cobble et al., 2015;Strauss, 2023). For example, women physicians, health advocates, and influencers used social media to challenge claims linking Tylenol use during pregnancy to autism (Gale, 2025;Landry, 2025).
Women's participation in correcting online health misinformation can also be understood through the concept of civic mindedness, which refers to individuals’ sense of responsibility toward others and their willingness to support collective well-being (Doolittle and Faul, 2013). In digital environments, civic orientations are often expressed through routine communicative practices such as sharing, evaluating, and responding to information that may influence others’ decisions (Kahne et al., 2016;Mihailidis and Thevenin, 2013). Within health contexts, concern for others can motivate advocacy, preventive behaviors, and peer-to-peer communication (Rimal and Lapinski, 2009). These motivations may become particularly salient when individuals encounter misleading health information that could affect others’ treatment choices or risk perceptions.
However, recognizing misinformation does not always lead to corrective action. Research identifies several barriers that discourage intervention, including fear of negative social reactions, uncertainty about factual accuracy, and perceptions that correcting misinformation is not one's responsibility (Bode and Vraga, 2018;Vraga and Bode, 2020). Civic-minded orientations may help overcome these barriers by reinforcing a sense of responsibility for others’ well-being (Doolittle and Faul, 2013;Kahne et al., 2016;Mihailidis and Thevenin, 2013). When such norms are salient, individuals may be more willing to intervene publicly or privately to mitigate the potential harms of misleading health information.
Recent debates linking Tylenol use during pregnancy to autism illustrate how unverified health claims can spread rapidly online and shape public perceptions (Kekatos and Singh, 2025). At the same time, women physicians, health advocates, and social media influencers have used digital platforms to challenge these claims and promote evidence-based information (Gale, 2025;Landry, 2025). These dynamics provide an opportunity to examine how women's civic mindedness and digital information competence affect their intentions to correct misinformation related to Tylenol.
Research questions & hypotheses
H1: Individuals with higher levels of digital information competence (H1a) and higher levels of civic mindedness (H1b) would report stronger intentions to address misinformation about the relationship between Tylenol and autism, even after controlling for demographic and social psychological characteristics.
H2: Individuals with greater concerns about the Tylenol-autism discussion (H2a) and higher levels of exposure to such discussions (H2b) would report stronger intentions to address misinformation about the relationship between Tylenol and autism, even after controlling for demographic and social psychological characteristics, digital information competence, and civic mindedness.
RQ1: How do U.S. adult women interpret and evaluate misinformation about a purported link between prenatal Tylenol use and autism in children?
Method
We conducted an online survey of adult women aged 18–54 residing in the United States in December 2025 to examine how their digital information competence and civic mindedness are associated with their intensions to correct misinformation linking prenatal Tylenol use to autism in children. The survey instrument contained a combination of closed-ended and open-ended questions, allowing the study to collect both quantitative and qualitative data related to the hypotheses and research question. Participant recruitment and survey administration were conducted through Qualtrics, a professional survey sampling platform. Previous studies have shown that samples drawn from Qualtrics research panels are comparable to community-based samples in terms of reliability and demographic representativeness (Belliveau et al., 2022;Miller et al., 2020). All research procedures described in this study were reviewed and approved by the Institutional Review Board at the authors’ university.
Sampling
To obtain a sample that reflected key demographic characteristics of the target population, the study employed a quota sampling strategy. Quota sampling is a non-probability method that aims to mirror the expected distribution of characteristics within a population by establishing predetermined demographic targets (Babbie, 2020). In practice, researchers set quotas for variables such as age, gender, race/ethnicity, or geographic region to ensure proportional representation. For this project, quotas were established for age, race/ethnicity, and geographic region among women aged 18–54 in the United States so that the sample would align with national demographic benchmarks reported by theUnited States Census Bureau, 2024.
Quantitative analysis
The survey questionnaire was developed based on themes and constructs identified in the literature review. After drafting the initial instrument, the research team conducted a pilot test with 30 U.S. adult women to assess clarity, question wording, and survey flow. Feedback from the pilot test informed revisions that led to the final questionnaire, which was implemented using the Qualtrics platform. The final instrument contained 29 closed-ended questions and two open-ended questions. Measurement items for the study's key constructs were adapted from prior research and are summarized below by topic area.
Measurement items
Digital information competence
Digital information competence was evaluated through seven items that assessed participants’ confidence and skills in locating and evaluating online information, drawing on prior research on digital literacy (Van Deursen et al., 2014). The items measured participants’ perceived ease in finding information online, their familiarity with different search strategies, their ability to choose effective search terms, their confidence in selecting relevant search results, their ability to verify information sources, their confidence in judging website credibility, and their tendency to cross-check information across multiple websites. Responses were measured using a five-point Likert-tyle scale, and the resulting index demonstrated strong reliability (Cronbach's α = .88).
Civic mindedness
Civic mindedness regarding health information was measured as participants’ sense of responsibility toward the well-being of others in the context of health information. Informed by the civic engagement framework validated byDoolittle and Faul, 2013, the survey included three items assessing respondents’ perceived obligation to help others remain informed about accurate health information, their concern for individuals who might be harmed by misleading health claims, and their belief that people should intervene or take action when misinformation could negatively affect public health. The resulting index variable demonstrated strong internal reliability (Cronbach's α = .85).
Concern about tylenol
Participants’ concern about Tylenol was assessed using a four-item measure developed to capture key dimensions of perceived medication risk identified in the health risk perception and medication safety literature. Research on risk perception suggests that individuals evaluate health products across multiple dimensions of potential harm, uncertainty, and societal implications rather than as a single global construct (Brewer et al, 2007;Slovic et al., 2007). Accordingly, participants indicated the extent to which they were concerned about each of the following aspects of Tylenol: (1) safety and potential side effects, (2) overdose risk, (3) the ingredients contained in Tylenol, and (4) legal and policy issues related to Tylenol. Responses were combined to form a composite index, which exhibited high internal consistency (Cronbach's α = .87). These four domains were selected because they reflect the principal concerns emphasized in acetaminophen safety research and regulatory risk communications. Specifically, theU.S. Food and Drug Administration, 2025bidentifies medication safety, adverse effects, accidental overdose, and awareness of acetaminophen-containing ingredients as major public health concerns associated with acetaminophen products. In addition, regulatory actions, product labeling requirements, and ongoing policy discussions surrounding acetaminophen contribute to public concern regarding legal and regulatory issues. Collectively, these four items provide a multidimensional assessment of participants’ concerns regarding Tylenol that is consistent with established theories of risk perception and medication risk communication (Brewer et al, 2007;Slovic et al., 2007;U.S. Food and Drug Administration, 2025b).
Tylenol information: exposure and familiarity
Informed by previous research on health information exposure (National Cancer Institute, 2022;Seo et al, 2025), exposure to discussions about a potential association between Tylenol and autism was measured using four items. Specifically, participants were asked to report how much they had seen or heard about such discussions from each of the following sources: (1) news article, (2) social media posts, (3) friends or family members, or (4) healthcare professionals. Responses were combined into an index, which demonstrated strong internal validity (Cronbach's α = .83). Based on previous research on health topic familiarity (Puspitasari et al., 2015), the survey also asked respondents to what extent they believed there is a connection between Tylenol use and autism and how familiar they were with recent debates concerning this issue.
Social psychological characteristics
In terms of participants’ social psychological characteristics, self-efficacy was measured using seven items adapted from previous studies of adolescent social competence (Zullig et al., 2011). The items captured participants’ perceived ability to communicate their opinions in situations of disagreement, initiate and maintain friendships, interact with unfamiliar peers, collaborate effectively with classmates, express discomfort when others behave in undesirable ways, speak confidently in group situations, and sustain peer relationships over time. Responses were recorded on a five-point scale, and the index showed a high level of internal consistency (Cronbach's α = .90).
Self-esteem was assessed using six indicators adapted from earlier research on adolescent group identity (Barker, 2009;Seo et al., 2014). These items evaluated how participants viewed their membership in social groups or organizations, including perceptions of their own value within those groups, pride in belonging, the importance of group membership to their identity, cooperative participation in group activities, and perceptions of how others view the groups to which they belong. Responses were measured on a five-point agreement scale, and the combined index showed good reliability (Cronbach's α = .92).
Demographics
Finally, the survey included questions assessing demographic characteristics, including age, gender, parental status, marital status, household income, and education level. In addition, participants’ levels of social media use were measured.
Quantitative analysis
In analyzing quantitative survey data, multiple regression models were used to examine effects of digital information competence and civic mindedness on individuals’ intentions to address misinformation about Tylenol and autism, controlling for demographic and social psychological variables. Additional models examined effects of issue-related engagement—measured as exposure to and concern about the Tylenol-autism controversy—on intentions to correct misinformation, while controlling for demographic and social psychological variables, digital information competence, and civic mindedness. Diagnostic procedures were performed to assess potential multicollinearity among predictors; tolerance values and variance inflation factor (VIF) statistics indicated that multicollinearity was not present. All analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics (version 29.0.2.0).
Qualitative analysis
To address the second research question, the survey included two open-ended questions that asked participants to describe their thoughts about recent public discussions regarding Tylenol and autism and challenges they encounter when evaluating the accuracy of information about Tylenol. Participants’ qualitative responses were analyzed using thematic analysis informed by grounded theory principles (Strauss and Corbin, 1994). This inductive approach allows patterns and conceptual categories to emerge from the data rather than imposing predetermined classifications.
Qualitative data were organized and analyzed using Dedoose (version 10.0.59). Two trained communication researchers conducted the coding. Before formal analysis, both coders completed training sessions to familiarize themselves with the study objectives and ensure consistency in coding procedures. To establish intercoder reliability, the coders independently performed open coding on an initial subset of the data, allowing codes to emerge inductively rather than relying on a predetermined codebook. They then compared their coding, discussed similarities and differences, merged overlapping codes, and refined code definitions to develop a shared preliminary codebook. This codebook was subsequently applied to a second subset of responses from 55 participants, and intercoder reliability was assessed using Cohen's kappa, yielding a value of.75, which indicates acceptable agreement (O’Connor and Joffe, 2020). Remaining coding discrepancies were resolved through discussion before coding the full dataset.
This iterative process of independent coding, comparison, refinement, and consensus strengthened the credibility of the analysis by ensuring that the coding framework was grounded in the data rather than a single researcher's interpretation. Using the finalized codebook, the coders independently analyzed the remaining qualitative data while meeting regularly to address questions and maintain consistent application of the coding framework throughout the analysis.
Results
Demographic characteristics and media
A total of 556 adult women in the United States completed the survey. Participants’ demographic characteristics are summarized inTable 1. All participants self-identified as female. With respect to age, 19.8% of the participants were between 18 and 24 years old, 24.8% were between 25 and 34 years old, 30.0% were between 35 and 44 years old, and 25.4% were between 45 and 54 years old. Regarding race/ethnicity, 55.2% of the participants identified as White or Caucasian, 18.5% as Hispanic or Latino, 13.1% as Black or African-American, 6.5% as Asian, 2.3% as “other,” and 0.4% as Native Hawaiian or other Pacific Islander. In terms of educational attainment, 22.1% reported having completed a bachelor's degree, 21.9% completed high school, 19.8% had some college credit but no degree, 14.7% held an associate degree, 8.8% held a master's degree or professional degree, 5.2% reported some high school education without a diploma, 4.1% completed trade, technical, or vocational training, 1.6% held a doctoral degree, 1.3% reported no schooling completed, and 0.4% reported education ranging from nursery school to eighth grade.

Among specific social media sites, YouTube ( M = 2.91, SD = 1.48) was the most commonly used for health information, followed by Facebook ( M = 2.62, SD = 1.50), TikTok ( M = 2.43, SD = 1.50), Instagram ( M = 2.33, SD = 1.45), and X ( M = 1.87, SD = 1.33).
H1: digital information competence, civic mindedness & intentions to correct Tylenol misinformation The first hypothesis proposed that individuals with higher levels of digital information competence (H1a) and higher levels of civic mindedness (H1b) would report stronger intentions to address misinformation about the relationship between Tylenol and autism, even after controlling for demographic and social psychological characteristics. The respondents demonstrated strong levels of digital information competence ( M = 3.86; SD = .85) and civic mindedness ( M = 3.53; SD = 1.06), measured on index scales ranging from 1 to 5, with higher values indicating greater competence and civic mindedness.
The overall level of misinformation correction intention was lower ( M = 3.31 and SD = 1.04) than the levels of digital competence and civic mindedness. A one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) revealed significant differences in misinformation correction intention across parental status groups, F (4, 551) = 3.02, p < .05. Participants who were parents of at least one child under the age of 18 reported the highest level of misinformation correction intention ( M = 3.50, SD = 0.99), followed by those who were expecting a child ( M = 3.30, SD = 0.86), whereas participants without children reported the lowest level ( M = 3.18, SD = 1.06). Scheffé post hoc tests were conducted to examine pairwise differences among the groups and indicated that parents reported significantly higher misinformation correction intention than non-parents.
The regression analysis revealed statistically significant effects of digital information competence and civic mindedness on intentions to correct misinformation regarding Tylenol ( ΔR 2 = .26, F = 122.46, p < .001). Specifically, digital information competency ( β = .19, t = 4.53, p < .001) and civic mindedness ( β = .48, t = 12.25, p < .001) were both positively associated with intentions to correct misinformation regarding the relationship between Tylenol and autism. Therefore, H1a and H1b were supported. These findings suggest that both digital information competence and civic-mindedness play important roles in motivating individuals to engage in efforts to address health-related misinformation.
Participants’ responses to the open-ended survey questions provided additional valuable insights. For example, some participants who reported high levels of digital competence indicated that they were hesitant to comment on social media posts related to the topic because they were afraid of offending friends or family members. One participant explained, “On Facebook, I am connected with a diverse group of people. I worry about negatively affecting relationships if I comment on these issues or try to correct misinformation.” Others noted that the polarized nature of the current political environment makes engaging with such content on social media even more “tricky.” These responses suggest that the relationships among digital competence, civic mindedness, and intentions to correct misinformation should be interpreted with caution. Although individuals with higher levels of digital competence and civic mindedness may possess the knowledge and motivation to address misinformation, concerns about potential social consequences and the polarized nature of online discourse may discourage them from acting on these intentions in social media environments.
H2: effects of concern and exposure on intentions to correct Tylenol misinformation
The second hypothesis posited that individuals with greater concerns about the Tylenol-autism discussion (H2a) and higher levels of exposure to such discussions (H2b) would report stronger intentions to address misinformation about the relationship between Tylenol and autism, even after controlling for demographic and social psychological characteristics, digital information competence, and civic mindedness. Overall, participants reported moderate levels of concern about the Tylenol-autism debate ( M = 2.71; SD = 1.21) and moderate exposure to related discussions ( M = 2.50; SD = 1.05). Both index measures range from 1 to 5, with higher scores indicating greater concerns and exposure.
A one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) revealed significant differences across parental status groups in both Tylenol-related concern, F (4, 551) = 8.42, p < .001, and exposure to related discussion, F (4, 551) = 16.89, p < .001. Participants who were parents of at least one child under the age of 18 reported the highest level of concern ( M = 3.07, SD = 1.19), followed by those who were expecting a child ( M = 2.87, SD = 1.18), while participants without children reported the lowest level ( M = 2.46, SD = 1.15). Regarding exposure to Tylenol-related information, participants who were expecting a child reported the highest level of exposure ( M = 2.96, SD = 1.01), followed by parents of at least one child under the age of 18 ( M = 2.90, SD = .99), whereas participants without children reported the lowest level ( M = 2.25, SD = .98). Scheffé post hoc tests were conducted to examine pairwise differences among the groups and indicated that parents with at least one child under 18 reported significantly higher Tylenol-related concern than non-parents, and that expecting parents reported significantly higher information exposure than non-parents.
The regression results indicated that concerns about the Tylenol-autism discussion and exposure to such discussions had statistically significant effects on intentions to correct misinformation regarding Tylenol ( ΔR 2 = .05, F = 17.32, p < .001). Specifically, greater concern about the Tylenol-autism discussion ( β = .10, t = 2.37, p < .05) and higher exposure to such discussions ( β = .09, t = 2.1, p < .05) were both positively associated with intentions to correct misinformation regarding the relationship between Tylenol and autism. Therefore, H2a and H2b were supported. These findings suggest that individuals who are more concerned about and more frequently exposed to the Tylenol-autism debate are more likely to report intentions to address related misinformation.
RQ1: interpretation of the Tylenol-autism discussion
The first research question asked how U.S. adult women interpret and evaluate misinformation about a purported link between prenatal Tylenol use and autism in children. The following three key themes emerged in participants’ responses: (1) navigating scientific authority and medical expertise, (2) confronting misinformation in digital media environments, and (3) maternal responsibility and risk management in health decision-making.
Negotiating scientific authority and medical expertise
A central theme emerging from participants’ responses was the negotiation of scientific authority when interpreting information about the potential relationship between Tylenol and autism. Many respondents emphasized the importance of relying on medical expertise and established scientific research when evaluating claims circulating in public discourse. For example, one participant said, “I believe that there is no link,” while another stated that the issue should be evaluated based on credible evidence, noting that “Doctors know best and have been advising pregnant women for decades.” These responses suggest that participants often frame their interpretations of the debate within existing structures of medical authority.
Other respondents explicitly rejected claims about a causal link between Tylenol and autism by invoking scientific credibility and peer-reviewed research. For instance, one participant commented that the claim “is a hoax and has been disproven by medical studies,” while another asserted that “there is no proof that Tylenol causes autism.” Several participants who voluntarily disclosed having autistic children stated that they did not take any acetaminophen during pregnancy and described claims linking prenatal acetaminophen exposure to autism in the absence of robust scientific evidence as “infuriating” and “frustrating” because such claims place blame on parents. These statements reflect an effort to counter misinformation by appealing to scientific consensus. In this way, participants engaged in forms of digital health advocacy by reinforcing the importance of evidence-based knowledge when discussing health risks.
At the same time, some respondents expressed uncertainty about how to interpret conflicting scientific findings. One participant explained that “some observational studies suggest potential links…while others find no association,” noting that differences in methodology and sample size make it difficult to draw clear conclusions. These comments highlight the challenges that lay audiences encounter when interpreting complex scientific information in digital environments. Within digital health advocacy frameworks, such uncertainty reflects broader challenges for individuals, particularly women navigating health decisions, to access clear and trustworthy health information.
Taken together, these responses illustrate how participants actively engaged with scientific authority when discussing the Tylenol-autism debate. By invoking medical expertise, emphasizing evidence-based research, and acknowledging scientific uncertainty, respondents positioned themselves as critical interpreters of health information. This engagement reflects a form of digital health advocacy in which individuals assert the importance of credible knowledge in shaping public discussions about women's health (Figueroa et al., 2021;Khosla et al., 2023;Seo et al., 2025).
Confronting misinformation in digital media environments
The next prominent theme involved participants’ recognition of the challenges posed by misinformation circulating across digital media platforms. Many respondents identified social media and online environments as key spaces where misleading or inaccurate claims about Tylenol and autism are amplified. For example, one participant noted that “if there's a way to share…misinformation becomes widely spread rather quickly, mostly on Facebook.” This observation reflects a broader awareness among participants that digital platforms can facilitate the rapid diffusion of unverified health claims.
Participants also described difficulties distinguishing reliable information from misleading or politically motivated messages. One respondent mentioned that assessing the accuracy of information involves navigating “fake news or fake information sources online,” while another emphasized that “there is so much fake information on the media.” These comments illustrate how individuals perceive the digital information environment as saturated with competing narratives, making it challenging to identify credible health guidance.
In response to these challenges, some participants emphasized the importance of information literacy and critical evaluation of sources. For instance, one respondent stated that “if you know how to use search engine properly, it is not difficult to find reliable information.” This perspective suggests that participants view digital literacy skills as essential tools for navigating misinformation. Within the framework of digital health advocacy, such comments highlight how individuals seek to empower themselves and others to critically assess health information circulating online.
Overall, participants’ comments reveal an acute awareness of the role digital media plays in shaping public debates about health risks. By identifying misinformation, questioning the credibility of sources, and emphasizing the need for critical evaluation, respondents engaged in forms of digital advocacy aimed at promoting more accurate health communication. These efforts demonstrate how individuals actively negotiate the complexities of digital information environments when forming opinions about controversial health topics.
Maternal responsibility and risk management in health decision-making
The final theme centered on how participants framed the Tylenol-autism debate in relation to maternal responsibility and health decision-making during pregnancy. Several respondents discussed the issue in terms of risk management, emphasizing that decisions about medication use should involve careful consideration by pregnant individuals and their healthcare providers. For example, one participant stated that “it most likely has to do with other factors involved, and it's up to the pregnant woman and her healthcare providers to determine if it is ok for her to use it.” This comment reflects an emphasis on shared decision-making between patients and medical professionals.
Other respondents emphasized the broader context of uncertainty surrounding pregnancy-related health risks. One participant noted that “no matter what you take, they are going to find something wrong with it,” highlighting the perception that pregnant individuals are frequently confronted with conflicting guidance about health behaviors. Such statements illustrate how public discussions about medication safety can place significant pressure on women to navigate complex and often contradictory health information.
Participants also referenced generational knowledge and family experiences when discussing the issue. For example, one respondent wrote that “my mother told me about some issues,” indicating that personal and familial experiences can shape how individuals interpret emerging health debates. These narratives illustrate how digital health discussions intersect with everyday experiences and social networks, influencing how individuals evaluate potential health risks.
A participant who voluntarily disclosed having an autistic daughter drew parallels between the current controversy surrounding prenatal acetaminophen use and the historical “refrigerator mother” theory, which incorrectly attributed autism to mothers who were perceived as “cold” and “unaffectionate.” She expressed concern that claims linking acetaminophen use during pregnancy to autism continue this pattern of assigning responsibility to mothers. She emphasized that parents of autistic children already face numerous challenges and described such unsupported theories as “hurtful,” noting that they add an unnecessary emotional burden to families. She added that she uses an online support group for mothers of autistic children to counter misinformation and advocate for evidence-based discussions.
Collectively, these responses demonstrate how discussions of the Tylenol-autism controversy are embedded within cultural expectations surrounding maternal responsibility. Participants’ comments reveal how women and families navigate uncertainty, evaluate risks, and seek reliable guidance when making health decisions. In this context, digital health advocacy emerges through efforts to promote informed decision-making and ensure that discussions about maternal health risks are grounded in credible evidence and supportive public dialogue.
Discussion
This study examined the factors associated with U.S. adult women's intentions to correct misinformation about the relationship between prenatal Tylenol use and autism in children, while also exploring how these women perceived and interpreted this debate. The quantitative findings indicate that digital information competence, civic mindedness, and issue-related engagement play meaningful roles in motivating individuals to address misinformation in digital environments. The qualitative findings provide additional insight into how women interpret and respond to health misinformation within the context of women's health debates. Taken together, the results highlight the complex ways individuals evaluate and respond to contested health information in digital communication environments.
Scholarly implications
This study contributes to several areas of scholarship, including health communication, misinformation research, and women's health advocacy. First, the quantitative findings reinforce the importance of perceived digital information competence in shaping individuals’ self-reported willingness to intervene in misinformation discussions. Prior research has emphasized the role of digital literacy in helping individuals evaluate online information (Blomberg et al., 2021;Schuster et al., 2024), and the present findings extend this literature by demonstrating that individuals’ perceptions of their own digital competence are also associated with their stated intentions to correct misinformation. It is important to note that this study did not assess participants’ actual knowledge or ability to critically evaluate misinformation; rather, it captured self-reported beliefs about one's own competence and self-reported willingness to engage. Thus, the findings suggest that individuals who perceive themselves as capable of evaluating online information may also report greater willingness to engage with others on misleading health claims, though whether this perceived competence corresponds to actual evaluative skill, or translates into real-world confidence and engagement, remains an open question that future research incorporating behavioral or experimental measures should address.
Second, the strong association between civic mindedness and corrective intentions highlights social and normative dimensions of misinformation correction. Civic-minded individuals may perceive correcting misinformation as part of their broader sense of civic responsibility, regardless of whether their own understanding of the topic is accurate or complete. This finding aligns with scholarship suggesting that misinformation correction is not only a cognitive process but also a form of civic engagement in digital public spheres (Kahne et al., 2016). By linking civic mindedness with misinformation correction intentions, this study suggests that the motivation to address health misinformation may be understood as an expression of participatory digital citizenship, though further research directly assessing participants’ knowledge and evaluative accuracy is needed to determine whether this motivation translates into corrections that meaningfully improve the accuracy of public discourse.
Third, the qualitative findings contribute to the growing body of scholarship on women's digital health advocacy (Figueroa et al., 2021;Khosla et al., 2023;Seo et al., 2025). Participants’ responses illustrate how women engage with contested health information by evaluating scientific authority, reflecting on the credibility of online sources, and discussing maternal responsibility in health decision-making. Importantly, these accounts reflect participants’ own perceptions and reasoning processes rather than a demonstrated or verified command of the underlying science; what the data show is how women talk about and make sense of contested health claims, not whether their evaluations are factually accurate. Even so, these responses demonstrate that digital health discussions are shaped not only by claims to scientific knowledge but also by social expectations surrounding women's roles in managing family health. In this sense, digital health advocacy emerges as a form of everyday discursive engagement in which individuals position themselves as critical interpreters of health information and express a desire to advocate for credible, evidence-based knowledge in public conversations about women's health, even though this study cannot confirm whether such advocacy, if enacted, would in fact improve the accuracy of these conversations.
Finally, the qualitative findings help explain why this expressed motivation to advocate does not always translate into a stated willingness to correct misinformation directly. Prior research indicates that recognizing misinformation does not necessarily lead to corrective action, pointing to barriers such as fear of negative social reactions, uncertainty about factual accuracy, and the perception that correcting others is not one's responsibility (Bode and Vraga, 2018;Vraga and Bode, 2020). Participants’ accounts echoed these concerns, suggesting that even individuals who feel civically motivated to protect others’ well-being may hesitate to intervene publicly given the interpersonal risks and polarized tone of online health discourse. This is consistent with the broader literature suggesting that civic-minded orientations can reinforce a sense of responsibility for others (Doolittle and Faul, 2013;Kahne et al., 2016;Mihailidis and Thevenin, 2013), but that this sense of responsibility competes with anticipated social costs. Collectively, the findings suggest that women's digital health advocacy may be better understood as a spectrum of engagement, ranging from private evaluation and discussion to more hesitant or selective public correction, rather than as a straightforward pipeline from perceived knowledge and civic motivation to actual corrective behavior. Future research incorporating behavioral or experimental measures would be valuable for clarifying whether and how these self-reported orientations correspond to observable correction attempts in real-world social media environments.
Practical implications
The findings of this study also offer several practical implications for public health communication and misinformation mitigation strategies. First, the strong relationship between digital information competence and corrective intentions suggests that efforts to strengthen digital health literacy may help empower individuals to respond to misinformation more effectively. Educational initiatives that focus on evaluating online sources, interpreting scientific evidence, and identifying misleading health claims may encourage more active participation in correcting misinformation.
Second, the results of this study highlight the importance of fostering civic responsibility in addressing misinformation. Public health campaigns may benefit from framing misinformation correction as a collective social responsibility rather than an individual task. Encouraging individuals to view the sharing of accurate health information as a form of civic engagement may increase their willingness to intervene when encountering misleading claims in online discussions.
Third, the qualitative findings suggest that health communication strategies should acknowledge concerns and responsibilities that individuals, particularly women, experience when making health decisions during pregnancy. Providing clear, accessible, and evidence-based information about medication safety and maternal health may help reduce confusion and uncertainty surrounding controversial health topics. In addition, public health organizations should recognize that social media platforms often serve as key spaces where individuals encounter and discuss health information. Developing communication strategies that directly address misinformation within these digital environments may improve the reach and effectiveness of public health messaging.
Limitations and future research
The cross-sectional survey design limits the ability to draw causal conclusions about the relationships among digital information competence, civic mindedness, and intentions to correct misinformation. Future studies could employ longitudinal or experimental approaches to examine how changes in digital competence or civic mindedness shape individuals’ responses to misinformation over time. In addition, future research examining related topics among parents of autistic children could provide valuable insights. Finally, the considerable variability in parent-status subgroup sizes in our sample limited our ability to conduct advanced multi-group analyses. Future studies with larger and more balanced subgroup samples—particularly of expecting parents—would be better positioned to test how life-stage-specific contexts (e.g., pregnancy, parenting young children vs. adult children, no children) shape the extent to which issue engagement, digital information competence, and civic mindedness translate into corrective intention, potentially through moderated regression or multi-group structural equation modeling (SEM).
Conclusion
Health misinformation surrounding widely used medications has important implications for public understanding of medical risk and for individuals’ health decision-making. This has been made especially clear by the acetaminophen-autism controversy that followed the Trump administration's September 2025 announcement warning pregnant women against Tylenol use. In the months after that announcement, emergency department orders of acetaminophen for pregnant patients declined by roughly 10% (Lupkin, 2026), even as multiple large-scale studies published in the following months found no credible evidence linking prenatal acetaminophen use to autism or ADHD (D’Antonio et al., 2026). This gap between the strength of the scientific evidence and the behavioral response to a single high-profile claim illustrates how quickly contested health information can shape real-world medication decisions, often before the underlying science has been fully evaluated or communicated.
The results of this study highlight the critical roles that perceived digital competence and civic mindedness play in shaping how individuals report responding to contested health information in online environments. More broadly, the findings underscore the importance of recognizing women not only as recipients of health information but also as active participants in public conversations about women's health and medication safety. Through everyday practices of evaluating scientific claims, reflecting on source credibility, and discussing health risks and responsibilities in digital spaces, women express motivation and willingness to participate in public conversations about women's health. Future research directly assessing knowledge and observed corrective behavior is needed to determine how these self-reported orientations translate into practice.
