Introduction
As social media continue to play a substantial role in adolescents’ digital saturated lives, attention has been drawn to the role of the platforms in the diffusion of inaccurate information, or what is commonly called fake news. Young people rely on social media such as Instagram, YouTube, Snapchat, TikTok and a plethora of other digital platforms to communicate, socialize, as well as find, and share information. Social media are therefore important sources of news for young people all over the world (Newman 2022; Medietilsynet 2022; Robb 2017). However, the same platforms also play a substantial role in fueling the spread of fake news (Silverman 2016a; Allcott and Gentzkow 2017; Grinberg et al. 2019; Allcott et al. 2019). The outbreak of a global pandemic in 2019/20 followed by Russia’s invasion of Ukraine in early 2022 did not only wreak havoc for humanity but further highlighted the severity of the fake news problem (Wesolowski 2022). The world witnessed pervasiveness of misleading claims about the coronavirus and the war in Ukraine, amplified by social media and other digital platforms. The gravity of fake news spread about the COVID-19 pandemic prompted the head of the World Health Organisation to coin the term “infodemic”, thus: “we are currently fighting an infodemic in the same way we are fighting a pandemic1.” Misleading information about the coronavirus was used as evidence to support false claims and to impersonate officials (Brennen et al. 2021). Social media had a strong hand in enabling instantaneous spread of such content about the virus’ the origin, treatment, prevention and vaccination. The repercussions were grave including violence, loss of lives, destruction of property and resistance towards vaccines (Hern 2020; Neuman 2020).
Among minors, fake news has been found to negatively affect children’s self-esteem and create skepticism towards news (National Literacy Trust 2018a; Robb 2017). Young people often mention social media as arenas where they come across fake news (Medietilsynet 2020b; Ofcom 2018), and worry about fake news and their inability to spot it (National Literacy Trust 2018a). In Norway, two studies paint two somewhat conflicting pictures. In one such study, participants considered themselves to be highly competent in determining the credibility of online information (Medietilsynet 2018). Two years later, young people in a similar survey (Medietilsynet 2020b) communicated a lack of confidence to deal with fake news. We ought, however, to take self-reported competences with caution considering that studies in other contexts show that adolescents lack relevant competences to assess online information and struggle to distinguish between fake and credible news (Nygren and Guath 2021; Head et al. 2018; McGrew et al. 2018; National Literacy Trust 2018b and Wineburg et al. 2016). I must emphasize, however, that these studies usually test youth’s abilities in controlled settings. Therefore, they may not sufficiently reflect young people’s real everyday practices when they come across fake news.
To extend our understanding of teenagers’ media practices in relation to fake news, it is imperative to interrogate their views and practices. This article contributes a youth-centered perspective to research about young people’s experiences and perspectives on fake news on social media. Adolescents’ voices, as Vissenberg and d’Haenens (2020) observe, are critical even as researchers, journalism institutions, educators and nation states seek for solutions to combat fake news and the challenges the phenomena pose. The current study2 takes a bottom-up approach, and foregrounds Norwegian teenagers’ views, their experiences, and practices when they encounter fake news on social media.
Fake news
Fake news has existed since time immemorial but it has since 2016 become a subject of discussion in both scholarly and non-scholarly circles. Earlier deliberations concentrated on defining the term (e.g. Tandoc et al. 2018; Allcott and Gentzkow 2017; Lazer et al. 2018). As discussed in the next section, several studies aimed at understanding: a) the term “fake news”; b) studying its origin, c) its impact, among other topics. Despite the widespread use, contentions emerged about the use of the term fake news (see Molina et al. 2021 for a detailed discussion of disagreements about application of the concept). Specifically, the word fake news has been dismissed as unhelpful, problematic in application, ambiguous, inadequate and misleading (Wardle 2017; DiFranzo and Gloria-Garcia 2017; Albright 2017; European Commission 2018). The word fake news as later discussed, has been associated with a range of information formats and as a report by the high-level group rightly observes, the concept encompasses all “low-risk” and “high-risk” types of information (European Commission 2018).
There are arguments that the term does not capture the complexity of today’s “information disorder” as it mixes up misinformation (information created with no intention of causing harm), malinformation (information based on reality and used to inflict harm) and disinformation (Wardle and Derakhshan 2017). Instead of fake news, a group of experts preferred the word disinformation, defining it disinformation as “all forms of false, inaccurate, or misleading information designed, presented and promoted to intentionally cause public harm or for profit” (European Commission 2018: p.10). Baptista and Gradim’s (2022) definition of fake news includes online disinformation, which coheres with the European Commission’s definition above. Subsequent scholarly publications use either “misinformation” or disinformation” or use fake news synonymously with misinformation.
Despite the contention, this study consciously adopted the term fake news. The notions “misinformation” and “disinformation” were treated as relatives of fake news. As Tandoc et al (2020, p.383) wrote, “fake news is a form of disinformation…”. Also, the current study did not aim or attempt to pin a definition, but rather the author regards fake news as any false information created or disseminated in online spaces regardless of the motive. This article aims at examining adolescents’ understanding of the concept, thus, the notion fake news was deemed relevant to allow and at the same time bring to the fore adolescents’ knowledge, views, reflections around different forms of problematic content in online environments. Such insights or the lack of them are important to a discussion about necessary interventions to mitigate such content.
Defining fake news
Whereas a simple dictionary definition has been coined3, explaining the word fake news is an uphill task, considering the plethora of existing definitions (e.g Tandoc et al. 2018; Allcott and Gentzkow 2017; Lazer et al. 2018). A simple review of literature shows that fake news has mainly been defined in terms of its nature (i.e content), its source, and motive for sharing or creating it. Fake news is, for example, described as “fabricated information that mimics news media content in form but not in organizational process and intent” (Lazer et al. 2018, p.1094). In their paper about the impact on elections in 2016, Allcott and Gentzkow (2017, p.213) defined fake news as “news articles that are intentionally and verifiably false, and could mislead readers.” These two versions assume that “fake news” has to be related to news media content.
Tandoc et al. (2018) found that the word fake news has been used to refer to among others, news satire, news parody, fabrication, manipulation, advertising and propaganda. Others relate it to counterfeit news (Fallis and Mathiesen, 2019); false content packaged to look like real news (Tandoc et al. 2018). Others such as Klein and Wueller (2017) introduce the world wide web medium element and define fake news false content that is intentionally published online.
Intent, sources and consequences
The motive for creating or distributing fake news is pertinent in understanding the different forms of unreliable information (Frank, 2015). The phenomena are, thus, associated with economic and ideological factors (Hunt 2016; Allcott and Gentzkow 2017; Tandoc et al. 2018). Other motives for propagating fake news include deception and manipulation (Baptista and Gradim 2022; Gelfert 2018; McGonagle 2017).
Fake news has life threatening consequences as was the case with mob killings in India (Frayer 2018). Socially, fake news has been linked to deception and confusion (Barthel et al. 2016) and destabilizing social cohesion in interpersonal relationships (Duffy, Tandoc and Ling 2020). Fake news poses a threat to democracy as it is associated with manipulation of elections (Allcott and Gentzkow 2017) in addition to deteriorating trust in news (Lazer et al. 2018; Quintanilha et al. 2019; Hofseth 2017).
The phenomenon is attributed to political actors, and Internet sources including social media. Social media’s role has particularly been singled out with evidence showing that the platforms facilitate the spread of information to many, almost instantaneously (Lazer et al. 2018). Platforms such as Facebook and Twitter (now X) fuel and amplify the distribution of fake news due to the ease with which users can access and share content (Tandoc et al. 2018; Lazer et al. 2018; Vosoughi et al. 2018; Allcott and Gentzkow 2017). Social media’s role is reflected sentiments that regard fake news as any false, content inadvertently or deliberately shared on social media regardless of the intent (see Mena et al. 2020; Allcott and Gentzkow 2017).
The rapid spread of fake news on social media is disconcerting due to extensive use and overreliance on platforms such as TikTok, Instagram, YouTube and Snapchat for news (Newman 2022; Medietilsynet 2022; Eddy 2022; Notley et al. 2020; Grinberg et al. 2019).
Combating fake news
The concerns about the pervasiveness of fake news are exacerbated by reports showing that youth are vulnerable to fake news and some lack the capacity to spot it (Notley and Dezuanni 2019). As such efforts to mitigate fake news are in high gear. For example, Shu et al. (2020) suggested education, research and collaboration as pertinent approaches to combating problematic content online. Education relates to media literacy and media education, which are being implemented as antidotes for fake news. Scholarly deliberations underscore media literacy interventions to improve the skills of citizens (McDougall et al. 2018; Santos et al. 2017). Understood as “all technical, cognitive, social, civil and creative capacities that allow citizens to access, have a critical understanding of the media and interact with it” (MLEG4), media literacy is regarded as an important competence for the constantly connected young generation. And as Rodríguez-Hidalgo et al. (2023) observe, the approach is essential for equipping youth with requisite skills to assess media content.
The current article is a part of a project whose goal is to explore adolescents’ understanding of and experiences with fake news. Therefore, the researcher did not operationalize or attempt to define fake news, allowed the definition(s) to emerge from participants narratives, viewpoints, and experiences.
Young people and news
Younger people define news differently and their understanding of news media’s role differs from that of adults (Marchi 2012; Eddy 2022). But in some contexts, young people have been found to associate news with ideals associated with news values such as objectivity and timeliness (Swart and Broersma 2024). As this researcher established, youth understand news in broad terms to include online news, news apps for established news organisations, social media news content, news on radio and TV as well as in newspapers (see also Tamboer et al. 2022). Social and digital media have become important drivers of news consumption among young people (Newman et al. 2022; Pew 2022).While studies about news consumption point to a lack of trust in news media (Park et al. 2020), disconnection with news (Newman 2022), and decline in interest in news among youth (Eddy, 2022), young people are in some contexts depicted as interested in news and engage in it (Selnes 2024; Medietilsynet 2022; Head et al. 2018).
Competences to spot fake news
Young people are exposed to fake news due to their attachment to social media platforms which they appropriate for self-expression and content sharing (Medietilsynet 2020a; National Literacy Trust 2018a; Robb 2017). This group of social media users consume inappropriate information or what Herrero-Diz et al (2020, p.3) describe as content that “fits under the umbrella of fake news”. The motives for sharing such content include entertainment and for informational purposes. Sharing is a means to engage in online debate for some youth (Zozaya-Durazo et al. 2024), but in the process, youth get exposed to fake news yet some of them lack adequate skills to distinguish between false and reliable news. The fake news phenomenon has, therefore, prompted research that examines young people’s ability to assess online information (Dumitriu 2020; Nygren and Guath 2021, 2019; Loos et al. 2018; Wineburg et al. 2016; Pilgrim et al. 2019). One such surveys US found that almost 3,000 high school students who participated in the study could not evaluate online content (Wineburg et al. 2016). Following a similar methodology and theoretical approach, two Swedish researchers tested Swedish teens’ (17–19) civic online reasoning skill (Nygren and Guath 2019, 2021). The duo also found that adolescents struggled to distinguish news from ads and fell short in determining the credibility of online. In an experiment that replicated a study that was earlier conducted in the Netherlands (Loos et al. 2018), a researcher in Romania arrived at the same conclusion that participants (10–19 years) lack the requisite skills and the confidence to navigate and evaluate content that they encounter online Dumitriu (2020). These studies have helped to identify gaps in young people’s competencies and skills that are important for spotting fake news. But the studies, conducted in controlled settings, do not portray a complete picture of youth’s practices in real-life or everyday encounters with information in online settings. As McGrew et al. (2018) point out, the tasks administered to participants in such studies are “somewhat artificial” because the contents that are evaluated are impose on participants by the researchers. Young respondents may be “more motivated to evaluate information if they (themselves) come across it out of personal interest” (McGrew et al. 2018). In fact, personal interest determines whether and how one responds to suspicious content (Tandoc et al. 2020).
Additionally, there is a paucity of studies that consider young people’s views on practices related to fake news. Swart (2021) studied how young people evaluate news on social media and found that teens validate the credibility of online content through cross-verification with online and offline sources (see also Marchi 2012; Mercenier et al. 2021). An online focus group investigation found high levels of a) awareness of disinformation and its pervasiveness on social media, b) strategies for verifying such content and c) confidence in participants’ ability to spot fake news (Zozaya-Durazo et al. 2024). Irrespective of the limitations, such studies are important in building evidence and solutions to the phenomena.
So far, little is known about how people generally react to fake news except for Tandoc et al. (2020) whose survey indicated that adult social media users mainly ignored unreliable content. Qualitative inquiries into how youngsters respond in their day-to-day encounters with fake news on social media is still missing in the literature. Moreover, the question of whether young people verify fake news or not remains unaddressed, yet reacting to such content can contribute to propagating or combating it. Building on some of the research outlined above, the current paper explores adolescents’ awareness of tactics and strategies relevant for navigating and sieving the loads of content that they experience on social media. Do they share it? Do they verify it? It is unclear for example whether adolescents are familiar with and use social media functions such as “delete”, “flag” or block when they come across fake news. This study, therefore, asks the fundamental question:
RQ: How do adolescents react to fake news in their everyday use of social media? In other words, what actions or inaction do young people take when they encounter suspicious news content on social media?
Research context
This study was conducted in Norway, a relatively small country with a high level of media consumption. While research in other contexts show dwindling levels of news consumption, news consumption remains high in Norway with indications of high levels of trust in news coupled with a high willingness to pay for news among the Norwegian population (Bjørgan and Moe 2024). State broadcaster, NRK, is the most trusted media brand for news among the population and other news brands including VG (a leading tabloid), Aftenposten, Dagbladet, and TV2, are also widely consumed (Bjørgan and Moe 2024). Statistics show close to universal access to the Internet (Medietilsynet 20185; SSB 20246). According to the Norwegian Media Authority7 (Medietilsynet 20248), the majority of children and young people (9–18) own and use a mobile phone, have access to a computer and or a tablet. Many young people express interest in news and use different media formats for regional, national and international news, sports news, crime, entertainment and celebrity news, as well as news about politics, climate and fashion (Selnes 2024; Medietilsynet 2022: p.76). Although research shows a reduction in the number of teens who use social media from 90% in 2020 to 86% in 2024 (Medietilsynet, 2020a9; Medietilsynet 2024), platforms such as Snapchat, Instagram, TikTok, YouTube, Facebook and messenger remain a large part of adolescents’ media repertoire (Medietilsynet 2022). Social media, thus, play an important role as a source of news for adolescents (Medietilsynet 2020b).
As is the case in other contexts, fake news poses a challenge with many citizens expressing concern about the phenomena (Bjørgan and Moe 2024). Norwegians associate fake news with social media and worry mostly about misleading information from activists (Medietilsynet 2020b). Those under people under 45 years are more likely to come across fake news than their older counterparts (Medietilsynet 2021b). Respondents reported that they had seen news they suspected to be false on social media, but they rarely cross-checked it (Medietilsynet, 2020a). The youngest respondents in the same study (16–24 years) thought that it was difficult to deal with fake news and disinformation. Yet in an earlier survey, teenagers considered themselves to be highly competent in determining the credibility of online information (Medietilsynet 2018: p.7). Moreso, whereas teenagers frequently encounter fake news on social media, a few check it closely (Medietilsynet 2020b).
There is evidence of high media literacy among the population including children and adolescents. This is reflected in surveys (Medietilsynets 2018, 2020a) in which the majority of respondents (9–18) expressed competence in a) finding and understanding information and b) distinguishing between false and reliable information. Nevertheless, there are rigorous efforts to improve critical media literacy ( kritisk medieforståelse ), which is described as “important for democracy” (Medietilsynet 202110) and is regulated by policy. For example, the national education policy underscores media literacy and critical thinking, and these are integrated in school curricula. The curricula underscore source criticism – that is critiquing sources (the author, their affiliation and motivation) to cultivate youths’ news and media literacy skills (Bråten et al. 2019). As such pupils learn about such topics as fake news and deepfakes in school (Medietilsynet 2021b). Moreover, fake news and disinformation are some of the themes surveyed every other year when the Norwegian Media Authority (Medietilsynet) maps critical media literacy in the country (Medietilsynet 2018; 2021a).
Methods
This study adopted a qualitative approach in order to gain a nuanced understanding of how adolescents aged between 13 and 18 interact with and react to fake news on social media. The article draws on views from 18 focus groups and six semi-structured interviews convened in 2022 in two regions in Norway (i.e, south Trøndelag and Oslo, the capital). Participants were selected from three lower secondary schools, three upper secondary schools and one after-school youth program. The focus groups comprised of 85 participants while the interviews, which consisted of six adolescents (two males and four females), were conducted with teens who wished to participate in the study but could not do so in groups. Sixteen focus groups and five interviews were conducted during school hours while two focus groups and one interview were conducted out of school. Two focus groups were conducted at an after-school program in the capital Oslo, while 16 focus groups were conducted at three lower and three upper secondary schools in Trondheim, Orkland and Heim municipalities.
Only participants who were familiar with the word fake news were selected. It was assumed that adolescents had been exposed to perspectives of media literacy since this is part of the school curricula. The total sample (N = 91) varied in terms of age and gender. The purposively selected sample consisted of 32 participants aged between 13 and 15 years and 59 teenagers aged between 16 and 18 years. A little over fifty six percent (56.7) of the participants were female while the rest were male. About 35% of participants were 13–15 years old while about 65% were within the 16–18 age range. Ethnic background is important in relation to fake news, but the study did not seek to understand how such a factor influence youths’ practices and was therefore not included in the application for research approval. The focus groups and interviews were conducted face-to-face in Norwegian and were translated to English after transcribing. The focus group duration was between 45 and 60 min while the interviews took between 15 and 25 min. The number of focus groups participants varied between three to seven participants.
Considering the small size of the sample, the findings here are not meant to represent the entire population of adolescents in Norway.
Since many studies about young people and fake news draw on surveys and experiments (e.g. Nygren and Guath 2019 and 2021; Wineburg et al. 2016; Head et al. 2018), the current study contributes a qualitative perspective. Focus groups and interviews as methodological approaches have potential limitations when studying young people in relation to fake news on social media. One of the limitations relates to the potential for self-reporting bias or the so-called Dunning-Kruger effect in which study participants overestimate their competencies (see Mahmood 2016). This researcher was conscious of evidence that adolescents’ self-reports may not accurately reflect actual behaviors. The study is, therefore, cognizant of the view that young people tend to overestimate their capabilities vis-à-vis their use of digital technologies (Herold 2012) and their ability to search and evaluate online information (Davidsson and Thoresson 2017; Martínez-Costa et al. 2023). The study did not aim to determine teens’ news evaluation skills or to test their ability to detect fake news. Its purpose is to provide insights into how young people deal with fake news as they navigate social media. The narratives and examples provided by adolescents are useful to the extent that they provide insights into what teenagers do, or not do) and ought to do when they come across fake news on social media.
Focus groups and interviews have previously provided useful empirical insights on news, social media use as well as audience perspectives on fake news (Craft, Ashley and Maksl 2016; Nielsen and Graves 2017; Swart 2021; Swart and Broersma 2021; Gondwe 2023). Focus groups were particularly regarded as an appropriate technique to elicit descriptions of experiences as well as perceptions in a group setting (Krueger and Casy 2000). In the current study, the researcher was more interested in adolescents’ views and discussions on fake news in addition to their experiences with such content in their day-to-day use of social media.
Analysis
All interviews were recorded and transcribed. Using inductive thematic analysis (see Strauss and Corbin 1994), themes and categories were developed and compared for relevance to the study’s research objective.
Consent
The study was conducted in accordance with the national research guidelines (NESH 2022) and after approval from the then Norwegian Centre for Research Data (NSD)11, the body which approves research. Consent was obtained from both parents and the participants before they were interviewed. Adolescents under 16 years were only interviewed upon receiving signed consent forms from their parents or guardians.
Findings
Each session started with general questions about news and thereafter fake news. Participants were asked to share examples of fake news they had encountered on social media; and what they did with it; whether they had knowingly or unknowingly shared fake news on social media; fallen for fake news; or knew anyone who had shared or fallen for fake news. Such prompts got teenagers to reflect on what they know; and how they react to fake news when they encounter it. An analysis of teens’ exchanges during the focus group discussions brought to the fore the strategies and tactics they employ to identify and cope with fake news.
Teenagers and fake news
This paper sought to explore how adolescents respond to fake news. In so doing, the paper presents tactics and strategies that participants reported to employ as they use social media. Each group and individual interview started with participants reflecting on their news consumption practices, social media use in relation to news, as well as their understanding of and experiences with fake news. For purposes of the current paper, the following questions were posed to participants: Have you come across fake news on social media? Those who answered in the affirmative were asked to share examples of fake news they had encountered; how they knew that it was fake and what they did with it. They were further asked whether they or someone they knew had knowingly or unknowingly shared what they perceived to be fake news.
Discussion about fake news automatically emerged as participants explained their news consumption practices, highlighting the relationship between fake news and social media. The researcher wished to examine whether factors such as gender and age have a bearing on how young people respond to fake news, but the findings indicate that the two did not appear to play any such a role. They viewed social media as facilitating the spread of fake news. Almost by definition, teens regarded Facebook as the most prone to fake news. As exemplified in the excerpts below, teenagers’ views suggest that fake news thrive on social media due to the absence of control or gatekeepers coupled with large numbers of users and the ease with which anyone can create and disseminate information. There are quite so many different things posted and shared further. It (content) quickly spreads around to many. I have Facebook, but I am just there because i know that there is a lot of fake news on the platform (Interview, Heim, 2022).
Facebook is the worst because one can easily share posts on the platform. A lot of people can see the (fake) posts. (Interview, Orkland, 2022).

An analysis of some of the examples of fake news as shared by participants reflects news interests of adolescents. The examples are related to sports news, entertainment, politics, COVID-19, the war in Ukraine, among others. Female participants regardless of age had come across celebrity news they considered false or simply dismissed as “celebrity drama” and “celebrity gossip”. For example, the two female participants cited content about the Johnny Depp and Amber Heard court case. Male participants on the other hand considered fake news in relation to sports news. But as evident in the excerpts below, the most shared examples of fake news were about the COVID-19 pandemic and the war in Ukraine. Adolescents regardless of age and gender had come across unreliable information about the Coronavirus, which was pervasive on social media. Some of the examples shared were about claims of how to prevent and cure the Coronavirus (Goodman 2020) in addition to conspiracies related to lockdown measures. I read stories saying, for example, that one can protect themselves from the Coronavirus using [the] mouthwash liquid. I also read that smoking can help prevent Coronavirus. But I knew that that was fake news (FG, Trondheim, 2022).
At the start of the pandemic, someone posted that everyone would get Corona. That everyone would die. It was everywhere on social media, so it was difficult not to see that kind of news (FG, Heim, 2022).
There was (fake) news that COVID-19 was not dangerous. It stated that authorities should not be trusted. That they are after us. That authorities exercise control over us if we stay home due to the restrictions. That authorities use the restrictions to hold us in bondage. That they want to make decisions for us (Interview, Trondheim, 2022).

In addition, participants had encountered fake news about the war in Ukraine which portray some of the fake news that circulated online especially at the start of the war including claims that the war was a hoax (Coleman and Sardaruzadeh 2022). There was a video in which the president of Ukraine was made to say things that he had never said. And I think that Ukrainians believed that their president had given up an area to Russia (Interview, Trondheim, 2022).

‘There was a video on Instagram which showed a plane attack on Ukraine […] But I found out that it was fake.’ (FG, Heim, 2022). The teenager explained that he found out that the video was not related to the ongoing war in Ukraine after checking and reading more about the war in mainstream media. When prompted to explain more, the participant indicated that he turned to conventional media, which they considered to be more trustworthy than social media. Another teen also shared: ‘It was about the war in Ukraine. There was news that Putin was also threatening Sweden and Finland […]’ (FG, Heim, 2022).
Responding to fake news
The narratives above exemplify young people’s awareness of and experiences with fake news. The next section is concerned with how teenagers respond to fake news. Participants who shared examples of fake news, were asked follow-up questions such as ‘how did you know that it was fake?’ and ‘What did you do with it?’ Their responses provide us with insights into the strategies that are relevant for verify online news.
Ignoring
The findings show that adolescents are aware that any actions (or inaction) taken on social media can fuel or limit the spread of fake news. It appears in the findings that young people ignore (potentially) suspicious content. Participants reported scrolling past and avoiding acting on fake news on social media. They intentionally ignore suspicious information as captured in this sentiment: ‘I just continue browsing social media and ignore the fake news…’ ‘I just scroll past [the fake news] (FG, Orkland, 2022). While the analysis shows participants regardless of age or gender ignore fake news, older teenagers (over 16 years) communicated awareness of influence of social media algorithms and how acting on content creates “echo chambers” and leads to generation of similar content as noted by a 17-year-old participant: “When you click on a news item, you get similar content” (Interview, Trondheim, 2022). By consciously ignoring social media content due to algorithms, the teen seems to apply what is called “the do-not-feed-the -trolls heuristic”, a critical ignoring strategy, which is useful in guarding against undesirable information online (Kozyreva et al. 2023). Whereas teenagers seem to be aware of the view that ignoring reduces the visibility of unverified information online, participants generally expressed skepticism towards news on social media as they regard the platforms unreliable sources of information. Ignoring may therefore be applied not just to fake news but to all social media content, which as earlier observed (Selnes 2024), can lead to passive consumption of digital media and news, and leads to disengagement with news altogether. Among teens under 15, disregarding social media news was partly due to lack of confidence in dealing with fake news as exemplified in sentiments such as “with social media, you are never really sure what is real and what is fake…”
Verifying and cross-checking
The belief that social media content is unreliable drives some teenagers into engaging in practices related to verifying and cross-validating of sources and news. Some adolescents reported validating source(s), a practice that involves cross-checking authors and verifying the actual source of the news. The findings indicate that participants understand the importance of checking news sources as exemplified in this sentiment: ‘The most important thing is to check the source of the news…’ (FG, Trondheim, 2022). The findings also show that checking reliable and multiple sources is a significant step in verifying news for the adolescents. Moreover, it was evident that participants stressed engaging in acts such as “double-checking” and “investigating” of suspicious content. For example, a teenager explained how he reacted to news about his favorite football player: I believed news on Snap about the transfer of Neymar. I believed that he was moving to Manchester City. But I checked with the main news channels and discovered that it was not true (FG, Oslo, 2022).

In the same focus group, two other teens narrated how they reacted to what they called rumors on Snapchat and TikTok (FG, Oslo, 2022): - There was a rumor on Snapchat that someone we know had died. Everyone who knew him was worried. I searched with credible sources and found that it was false.

- I saw on TikTok a post that a celebrity was dead. I checked and found out that it was fake news.


The young people in the above examples react to untrustworthy news by cross-checking with other sources, which sometimes involves navigating away from social media. They cross-check with mainstream news channels, which they regard to be trustworthy, in addition to using Google and triangulating with other social media platforms. Teenagers’ views further underscore the importance of verifying news with multiple sources as a key step to identify fake news as exemplified in the following quote: ‘We search multiple sources, checking to be sure that what we read is genuine. I usually read credible sources’ (Interview, Trondheim, 2022).
Two adolescents (FG, Trondheim, 2022) also explained thus: - If I see a news story on Facebook or Instagram, I go to NRK or VG to check if they have said the same thing

- I check with multiple sources. I Google. I can disconnect from social media and check in other places


Similar to Wenzel’s (2019) study, the findings show that adolescents are familiar with practices such as triangulating, critiquing and checking mainstream sources, asking trusted adults as well using search engines. By searching across different media, teens look for repetition away from social media where first encounter with a news item occurred. The perspectives above confirm the view that social media users validate news content through cross-verification, which as Swart (2021, p.9) found among young people in Netherlands, involves checking other online and offline sources. Additionally, participants’ views also signal triangulation of news sources with conventional and non-conventional media sources (see Wagner and Boczkowski 2019, p.878 for similar findings).
When faced with suspicious news on issues such as COVID-19, some teenagers emphasized the need to cross-check with government sources such as Folkehelseinstituttet (FHI) and the ministry of health officials in addition to mainstream media as they regard these institutions to be reliable as reflected in this quote: ‘I use trustworthy sources such as Folkehelseinstituttet to hear what the government says’ (Interview, Heim, 2022). Another teenager also said: ‘I check what is posted (on social media) with credible sources’ (FG, Trondheim, 2022). While the above quotes do not confirm actual behavior, they hint at awareness of the importance of authenticating news. Reports of checking with main news channels such as the public broadcaster NRK and VG, one of the largest commercial media, and government sources, exemplify trust in institutional sources and in mainstream media. Despite reports of lower levels of trust in the media among young people in Norway (Kalleid and Veberg 2021), reports of engaging with the public broadcaster; NRK, for verification demonstrate strong trust in professional journalism and trust in news (Newman 2021). The Norwegian Broadcasting Corporation (NRK) is the most used and reliable news media in Norway (Bjørgan and Moe 2024).
Social media system’s prompts
Social media offer users several options for dealing with content through functions such as sharing, commenting, deleting, blocking, and reporting. Several teenagers especially those in the 16–18 age-bracket reported employing one or multiple social media functions to deal with undesirable content as illustrated in following excerpts: ‘I report or block those who share fake news’ (Interview, Trondheim, 2022); ‘I report the fake news post’ (FG, Orkland, 2022); “I block, remove them, and stop following it’ (FG, Orkland, 2022); ‘Blocking and using the report button … and deleting users who post fake news’ (FG, Orkland, 2022).
The study found that even younger teens (aged 13–15) are familiar with tools that can be used for managing content on social media. Proving whether the participants frequently use these functions was beyond this study’s scope since several adolescents reported disregarding and avoiding fake content. Nevertheless, employing social media affordances is a proactive way of dealing with unreliable content.
A discussion around content sharing as afforded by social media revealed that participants avoided sharing any content openly on social media. But some sentiments suggests that adolescents sometimes share such content with classmates, friends or a family when they are uncertain for purposes of cross-verifying news: ‘I send and discuss together (with someone) whether a news story is credible’ (FG, Heim, 2022). It is noteworthy that sharing for such purposes mainly takes place offline or in online private spaces. Moreover, participants’ views point to relation and social ways of acting on fake news, which suggest social verification of news, a tactic in which social media news consumers rely on people to verify news (Swart 2021).
Further, the findings suggest that the decision to react to perceived fake news among young people is determined by the relationship between the sharer and the recipient. Participants easily correct a fake news item if the sharer is someone close to them as can be seen in these excerpts: ‘If someone like my dad comes to me with such (fake) news, I ask him, where did this come from? […]’ (FG, Trondheim, 09.03.2022). ‘I talk with my parents that they should not believe in everything they find on social media’; ‘I have talked to my friends about fake news. We also talk about how we can avoid it’ (FG, Orkland, 2022). These sentiments point to preference for verbal and face-to-face communication about unreliable content with relatives and friends as opposed to doing so in online public spaces. This is further illustrated in this excerpt: ‘I do not often discuss whether news is fake or real in the comments section on social media. But I can discuss it with my mom. It is much easier to just tell someone face-to-face that it is fake news […].’ (FG, Trondheim, 2022).
Taken together, participants’ views on sharing of (potentially) fake content hint at intentional and interpersonal effort for verification, and exemplify the notion that social networks, despite their many shortcomings, contribute to finding, evaluating, and verifying information online (Tandoc et al. 2017).
Discussion and conclusion
The study shows that youth are aware of the prevalence of fake news and underscored the role of social media in propagating the phenomena.
As Tandoc et al. (2020) observed, the findings in this study show that how and whether adolescents respond to fake news depends on whether a) the topic is of interest and relevance to them, b) the news has what they consider to have serious consequences. However, this study found that whether and how youth react depends on their individual understanding of what fake news is. The analysis brought to the fore three different ways of responding: a) ignoring; b) verifying; and c) deploying social media functions.
A report from a survey showed that (young) social media users do not check fake news even though many reported frequent encounters with unreliable content (Medietilsynet 2020b). Participants adopt ignoring as one of the strategies for consuming news and evading fake news while browsing social media. Inaction amounts to allowing unreliable news content to spread unabated as Tandoc et al. (2020) noted. But participants’ views such as “it (fake news) stops with me […]” suggest that some participants deliberately use ignoring as a tactic to prevent the spread of fake content.
Three categories of participants who reported ignoring fake news emerged from the analysis: a) teens who deliberately “scroll past”; b) teens who distrust content on social media and c) adolescents who expressed uncertainty in dealing with fake news. The first category comprised older teens (16–18 years) whose views demonstrate understanding of how algorithms influence content on social media users’ news feeds. This is in tandem with the notion that reaction through actions such as clicking, liking, following, commenting, and sharing legitimizes social media content (Mercenier et al. 2021). For youth in this category, ignoring is a calculated tactic for navigating social media, which is now regarded as “core competence for digital citizens” (Kozyreva et al. 2023). Despite frequent use of social media, teens in the second category do not act on any online content regardless of its veracity. A detailed analysis shows that teens in this category, many of whom male are skeptical about news in general and associate fake news with authorities, politicians as well as journalists and some new media. This is interesting to further examine and could be understood in relation to observations that highlight falling levels of trust in the media among young people in Norway (Kalleid and Veberg 2021; Raabe 2023). This author argues that skepticism is healthy as it (may) drive (some) news consumers into active engagement with news and contribute to managing fake news, but in the case of some of these teenagers, skepticism appears to alienate them from news. The last category consisted mainly of teens (under 15 years) who reported ignoring probably due to uncertainty about social media news. The findings show that some of these teens ignore social media news because they do not trust in their ability to confirm whether such news was fake or not. It appears that age is an important factor regarding how and whether teens react to fake news through ignoring.
Reports of scrolling past unreliable information also relate to the view that young people’s encounters with and consumption of news (reliable or fake) on social media is incidental. As previous research established (e.g Boczkowski et al. 2018), consuming news on social media is a secondary activity or something (many) young people do alongside other activities. In the current study, views such as “I use social media for other things… not for news” exemplify the idea that some teens do not log onto social media primarily for news. They come across news as they navigate different platforms for other reasons and combine social media use with several other online and offline activities. Thus, scrolling past suspicious content becomes an easier and lesser time-consuming option than engaging with it. It is also important to note that whereas young people first consume news via social media (Medietilsynet 2022; Newman 2022), reports of checking other media for cross-verification indicate that adolescents engage with news content off social media (see also Selnes 2024).
Similar to Swart (2021) and Zozaya-Durazo et al. (2024) works, this investigation established that young people undertake critical evaluation practices only when a) the news in question relates to themes that they are deeply interested in and b) when it is shared by people they know. The verification practices as reported in the current study, are in tandem with information evaluation tactics discussed in earlier studies (Metzger et al. 2010; Tandoc et al. 2017; Swart 2021). For instance, youth’s accounts reflect practices connected to “searching for repetition across different media and non-media platforms as a signal of credibility” as employed by adult news consumers in the US (Wagner and Boczkowski 2019). The perspectives in the current study emphasize the role of sources in evaluating news coupled with the importance of authenticating a single news item with multiple sources, which is considered as critical in spotting fake news (Galan et al. 2019). Checking sources of news in addition to Googling is an “effective verification strategy” (Leeder 2019) and a way to authenticate news (Tandoc et al. 2017). But as argued elsewhere (e.g Vaagsholm 2023), search engines ought to be employed for news verification with caution because they mix advertisement content with independent journalism.
The participants communicated awareness of the importance of looking for evidence, which is one of the pillars of civic online reasoning (McGrew et al. 2018). Moreover, as others observed in relation to young people’s engagement with news (Head et al. 2018; Galan et al. 2019), this study demonstrates that reaction to fake news is (sometimes) a group activity that takes place as youth interact with one another. This relates to the view that young people’s interaction with reliable or otherwise is socially mediated in online and offline spheres (Notley et al. 2023). In the current study, some youth mentioned parents as their point of reference when engaging with fake news, which exemplifies the view that parents (can) contribute significantly to shaping their children’s news consumption habits (Swart and Broersma 2021).
Further, reports of checking non-institutional sources such as interpersonal sources (friends and parents) echo the idea that “socially proximate sources” or interpersonal sources play an important role in verifying of news on social media (Mercenier et al. 2021; Tandoc et al. 2017, p.136).
This study shows that social media platforms despite posing enormous challenges, offer users opportunities for managing fake news. Deploying social media affordances allows users to contribute to combating fake news. The views in this study suggest knowledge of social media affordances such as flagging and reporting, blocking, deleting, and unfollowing suspicious content and users who share it. But commenting on open social media forums was not common as teens expressed preference for correcting or discussing news related issues in face-to-face settings or using private messaging platforms. But they share (potentially) fake news in private forums and discuss it orally for cross-verification and correction purposes.
Conclusion
Drawing on narratives of Norwegian adolescents, this paper explores young people’s awareness of and reactions to fake news on social media. Based on material obtained through semi-structured interviews and focus groups, the study shows widespread awareness and exposure to fake news among the surveyed adolescents. The exposure is connected to levels of access and use of social media, including using the online platforms for news. The study also points to participants’ awareness of solutions to fake news, some of which underscore the role of news and media literacy education. The study’s findings reinforce previous research from other contexts (e.g Herrero-Diz et al. 2020; Zozaya-Durazo et al. 2024; Tandoc et al. 2020, etc).
Moreso, the study found that youth’s act by a) ignoring fake content; b) engaging in authentication practices and c) employing social media to manage fake news. Ignoring was the most common strategy against fake news because participants applied the concept to different types of content.
Whereas social media users in other contexts have been found to ignore fake news (Tandoc et al. 2020), this study to the best of the researchers’ knowledge is the first to explore ignoring as a tactical measure against fake news from the perspective of adolescents. This study suggests that “ignoring” should be emphasized as a technique for tackling fake news among youth. In addition to reflecting participants awareness of ignoring as an important media literacy technic, the article found that age is an important factor in understanding the practice of ignoring as an antidot to fake news. The study, thus, underscores the importance of media literacy skills such as assessing and verifying information.
Whereas the study could not ascertain whether participants are motivated to undertake, in practice, the activities towards suspicious social media content, this study highlights strategies that are pertinent to identifying and distinguishing between fake and credible news sources on social media. Participants indicated acquiring this knowledge in school, which underscores the role of media education in preparing young people for exposure to reliable and fake news online and for engaging with such content. Further, participants are aware of existing systems embedded in social media platforms to control the spread of fake news. Their perspectives contribute to our understanding of how responding actively (through such small actions as deleting, reporting, blocking) and passively (e.g. not liking and not commenting) contribute to controlling the spread of fake news. Social media, thus, serve as tools for managing the diffusion of unreliable news. This also means that users ought to have skills and knowledge to navigate social media in addition to understanding the technological affordances of the platforms and how these relate to spreading and controlling unreliable content.
Lastly, this article underlines the necessity enduring relevance of reliable news media as providers of verifiable news content since young news consumers highly regard legacy media as their go to sources for verifying social media news.
This study is not without limitations. Firstly, this is a qualitative study based on a small sample of adolescents. This means that the findings are not generalizable because the views and experiences may be limited to the teens who participated in the study or the young people in Norway, a country where media literacy is integrated in the education system. Secondly, the views presented in this article are self-reported. Self-reported practices can diverge from actual behavior. Research participants are known to overestimate their abilities (e.g Mahmood 2016) and youth in particular sometime overstate their ability to search and evaluate online information (Davidsson and Thoresson 2017). Similar to what Martínez-Costa et al. (2023) observed, younger study participants (13–15 years) appeared to lack confidence in dealing with fake news while older teens 16–18 expressed more confidence in dealing with such content.
Although the study’s scope did not include testing teens’ skills as confirmation of their responses, participants were encouraged to illustrate their reported actions with examples of fake news they had encountered. Future research could combine focus groups or one-on-one interviews with digital ethnography to observe behavior in relation to news practices on social media.
The limitations notwithstanding, teens’ perspectives underscore the role of media literacy and information evaluation practices in mitigating fake news. The views and experiences shared help us to shed light on: a) teens’ knowledge about fake news; b) tactics for evaluating news and online information; and c) the role of social and conventional media in shaping young people’s news consumption habits.
The study has social implications. First, long time ignoring can reduce or result in disengagement with information or news or social media altogether. At the same time, if incorporated in teaching, deliberate ignoring can contribute to combating fake news. It should be taught as a core media literacy skill. Interventions that target young people should be reinforced to equip them with necessary tools and skills to actively champion the fight against fake news. The findings only confirm theoretical knowledge about fake news. If reported strategies for verifying information are put in practice, fake news and its impact on individual young news consumers will ebb in the long run. Thus, media literacy interventions ought to a) emphasize practical news evaluation skills and b) nurture positive skepticism and mitigate cynicism towards news among young social media users.
