It is not uncommon these days to hear someone ask, in anguished punctuation to a news story, and as if the question may never be answered: " What is going onnn? " While pundits have explained the outcome of the 2016 election in various ways, the two most prominent explanations, each carrying different implications for strategic responses, have to do with a basic question of whether the election represented the genuine will of a significant portion of the electorate or whether an insidious propaganda effort by a foreign power can explain why the president of the United States is now the man Keeanga-Yamahtta Taylor described as a "racist, sexist megalomaniac." 1 Four new books about the history of American politics and media offer insights relevant to both sides of this argument. Ranging chronologically from the early twentieth century's Committee on Public Information to 1960s and 1970s conspiracytheory fueled mobilizations against the civil rights movement and women's liberation, to websites and message boards dedicated to discussing and preparing for an imminent apocalypse, this collection of books provides a historical perspective on what many of us are experiencing as an unprecedented attack on reality itself. Lucidly written, informative, and often entertaining, they each map a constellation of ideas across the writing and actions of relatively small groups of activists and opinion makers to make larger claims about American political culture.
In Weapons of Democracy: Propaganda, Progressivism, and American Public Opinion , Jonathan Auerbach analyzes writings of early twentieth-century Progressive US political leaders and public intellectuals about the nature of publicity and persuasion in democratic government. Edward Miller's Nut Country: Right Wing Dallas and the Birth of the Southern Strategy describes the dialectical relationship between moderate conservatives and ultra-right conspiracy theorists in the Dallas Republican Party in the years leading up to the Kennedy assassination. Also set partly in Texas, Marjorie Spruill's Divided We Stand: The Battle over Women's Rights and Family Values That Polarized American Politics describes a year of dramatic conflict between second-wave feminist activists and their opponents, placing a cadre of ultraconservative women ideologues at the heart of the story of the birth of the New Right. Departing from party politicking to explore a virtual community on the left, Matthew Schneider-Mayerson's Peak Oil: Apocalyptic Environmentalism and Libertarian Political Culture guides us through the media landscape of the present day, with a detailed analysis of the Left's version of doomsday preppers, "peakists" who prepare individually to survive the anticipated fall of modern civilization. In a variety of ways, all four works come back to the question, once again relevant to our own time, that the inability to know exactly what is going on? has presented a perpetual sense of crisis for democracy.
Auerbach opens with Walter Lippmann, famous for his concern about the threat of commercially circulated news to the practice of democracy. In his extended reading of Lippmann's work in the fourth chapter, Auerbach notes that this early critic of mass media was not simply an elitest but deeply concerned about the difficulty of getting accurate information in a mass society. Lippmann wrote, for instance in the Phantom Public , a comment foreshadowing today's common news-reader complaint: "I cannot find time to do what is expected of me in the theory of democracy; that is, to know what is going on and to have an opinion worth expressing on every question which confronts a self-governing community" (124). Lippmann is only one of the thinkers to have wrestled with this problem. Beginning in the 1880s and concluding in the 1930s, Auerbach surveys Progressive theories attempting to define the public, the "common good," and to avoid the capture of truth's definition by ominous "'special interests.'" George Creel's Committee on Public Information, which infamously promoted World War I using advertising techniques, takes up two chapters as the most highly organized state-run propaganda attempt, but the book also explores earlier government-sponsored information campaigns, as well as the parallel history of corporate public relations. Concerned with the power both of industry and of the state, thinkers of the time, he argues, "struggled to distinguish between modes of influence that encouraged citizens to partake in the life of the polis and those that more directly organized their beliefs, attitudes and values for them" (2). Both the terms propaganda and social control once had positive connotations, as they aimed to create "cohesion" in the public in opposition to "fragmentary individualism" understood as chaos or, in Lippmann's term, "drift" (11). Auerbach's nuanced readings help clarify shifts in the national understanding of ethical dilemmas related to the practice of persuasion.
Three utopian novels become touchstones: Edward Bellamy's Looking Backward , Mark Twain's Connecticut Yankee in King Arthur's Court , and Philip Dru: Administrator , a lesser-known novel by Woodrow Wilson's "most trusted adviser," Edward M. House. All three address the problems human fallibility and fractiousness have presented for would-be social reformers. For Bellamy, the utopian society of the year 2000 spoke as one whole without the need for persuasion, making the ideal future nation into a "post-political bureaucracy" (20). By contrast, Twain's Hank Morgan is a master-manipulator of news and education, but still cannot reduce the population to homogeneity until he slaughters them (21–22). Philip Dru, a "muckraking journalist" who moves into politics to fight Senator Selwyn, head of a cabal of multimillionaires whose goal is to "mislead" the public, winds up in a civil war before he can set up a new republic in which dissenting voices are easily drowned in a "chorus of approval" (25). The last novel will have particular significance for contemporary readers, as Auerbach notes that it is now sometimes discussed by right-wing pundits such as Glenn Beck, as "a revealing example of liberal Progressivism run amok" (27). Removing Philip Dru from this context, Auerbach describes Wilson's goal of "giving direction to opinion" as being related to his notion that the common interest would be too vulnerable in the face of organized "special interest"—which for the Progressives often meant big business. Wilson's concerns about organizing public opinion led to the creation of the position of the White House press secretary as well as the first presidential press briefings. Auerbach notes also that both Theodore Roosevelt and William Howard Taft organized administrative publicity campaigns for policy initiatives: Gifford Pinchot led Roosevelt's publicity for forest preservation and Julia Lathrop promoted the Taft administration's new Children's Bureau.
Auerbach tracks the details of Roosevelt's own "intimacy" and "mutual trust" with Progressive journalists through the prism of his falling out with Ray Stannard Baker, following a series Baker wrote about railroad companies, including one that targeted the shady practices of corporate PR. It was in response, Auerbach argues, that Roosevelt coined the term muckraker in a 1906 speech at the Gridiron Club, where he "worked himself to a fever pitch over hysterical sensationalism" (40). The next two chapters detail the activities of the Committee on Public Information (CPI), including a detailed analysis of Creel, who had been famous for passionate attacks on J. D. Rockefeller following the Ludlow massacre—and who had himself written an exposé of Rockefeller's publicity man, later to be dubbed "Poison" Ivy Lee (65). While Auerbach refers to his fourth chapter, reassessing the Progressive ideology through the work of John Dewey and Lippmann, as the "theoretical core" of the book, the final two chapters tell the darkest story of PR history in the United States, and the one most relevant to contemporary discussions about the meaning of relationships between US political operatives, media, and foreign agencies or actors. These chapters return to the parallel story of corporate and state propagandists at the end of World War I, when CPI agents Edward Sisson and Carl Byoir as well as private PR agents Edward Bernays and Ivy Lee were all active in postwar Europe. Showing the flexibility of PR's technology as well as its ethics, Auerbach writes, "Since the CPI had essentially served as a publicity bureau for the United States, why not begin an enterprise to represent the interests of other nations, an endeavor that might be just as lucrative as representing corporations?" (153). Thus Sisson came into contact with a group of anti-Bolshevik activists who turned "the CPI representative into an intelligence agent" (155). The CPI later published this early example of "fake news" under the title The German-Bolshevik Conspiracy , a pamphlet that continues to circulate today, although its status as a forgery was revealed by George Kennan in 1956. 2 Auerbach connects these documents to better-known public disinformation campaigns of the Cold War era, noting that Bernays represented United Fruit in Guatemala and Lee, notoriously, became a publicity agent for the Nazi regime. For all the descriptive strength of intellectual connections in Weapons of Democracy , much of the analysis turns around abstract and formal questions, so that the argument rarely breaks from a liberal framing. For this reason, while Auerbach's skillful analysis of rhetoric, methods, and styles of argument is rewarding, the analysis of material interests is less developed. Perhaps there are so many contradictory points in Progressive ideology about information and propaganda because the Progressives were engaged in a contradictory project: to effectively manage and contain capitalist interest while advocating for the "common good."
The Cold War reshaped the public's understanding of government propaganda, filtering into the national view of the domestic Left, and ultimately led to a reevaluation of the Progressive vision of government regulation as well as a progressive press— tout court . Miller's Nut Country and Spruill's Divided We Stand chart the rise of this transformation. Miller's story of the rise of the Right begins in the 1950s, as Dallas conservatives demonstrated a marked distrust in the truthfulness of the federal government and growing criticism of public education, and developed their own partisan media. Both Miller and Spruill emphasize the early importance of the John Birch Society's ideological influence on the right wing of the Republican Party. Opening and closing with the Kennedy assassination, Miller's Nut Country is a sharp analysis of how the combination of economic interest, political calculation, racism, and radical anticommunist ideology helped lead to electoral successes for a Dallas Republican Party that maintained an influential circle of prolific ultraconservative conspiracy theorists in its constituency, a group to which, Miller argued with foresight, the modern party is now "manacled." After a decade of radical right organizing, led by local activists such as H. L. Hunt, he argues that by 1961 "Dallas was a 'national epicenter' for the John Birch society" (90). It became national knowledge after President John F. Kennedy's assassination, when influential ideologues there speculated that JFK might rise from the grave as the anti-Christ (1).
Miller argues that many recent histories of the Right have gone so far in their quest to wrest conservative history from the characterization of "the paranoid style" that they now understate the influence of the radical Right on the Republican Party's history. Furthermore, since most historians of resistance to Jim Crow have emphasized Southern Democrats, they have missed the story of the first utilization of the Republican "Southern strategy"—which he argues was perfected in Texas. This strategy was most evident at the national level in the actions of John Tower, Texas's first Republican senator since Reconstruction, who as a Goldwater backer had helped write the national RNC platform in 1964 (118). Through the microcosm of the Dallas GOP, Miller argues that the strategy began even earlier, and that despite opposition by moderate Republicans, Dallas's activist ultraconservatives became "catalysts for the diffusion of conservative ideas across the entire right wing of the American political spectrum," simultaneously providing cover for moderates who criticized them and radicalizing the right wing of the national Republican Party (3–4). At times it is difficult to see what distinguishes the "moderate" wing of the Dallas GOP from the ultraconservatives, especially as Miller notes, since the Dallas moderates were considerably to the right of the national party in the Keynesian 1950s. Both moderates and ultras opposed civil rights activism, celebrated militant, religiously inflected anticommunism, and claimed social welfare policies should be blocked as socialism. Dallas congressman Bruce Alger was even corresponding with Ayn Rand (35–36). However, the ultras went farther, as the chapter title describing their ideology suggests—they were "heralds of apocalypse."
Here a group of radical right media figures stand out. Dan Smoot ran a direct-mail newsletter and later created his own right-wing television talk show The Dan Smoot Report . Ultraright oil millionaire Hunt circulated conspiracy theories nationwide through his multimedia Facts Forum ; women's club activist Ida Durden ran the Southern Conservative , in which she called for the revival of the KKK and argued that the Democratic Party had been conspiring against America in favor of communism since the time of Woodrow Wilson (2, 59, 62). All three Texas-based publications pushed an absolutist ideology that refused to distinguish between liberalism and communism; called for the abolition of the income tax; and referred to the Brown v. Board of Education decision as leading to a plan to "'mongrelize America,'" suggesting that Earl Warren should not just be impeached but hung (53, 56). While both moderates and ultraconservatives opposed the UN, it was the ultras, whose premillennial dispensationalism undergirded arguments that the UN was a vehicle for the anti-Christ, who suggested that even Dwight D. Eisenhower might be part of a communist conspiracy to create a one-world government (55). Miller explains the broad circulation and popularity of such bizarre theories through the city's economic transformation and religious culture.
Whereas other Southern states continued much longer under the one-party dictatorship of segregationist Democrats, Dallas's shift to the Republican Party developed through a white-collar migration. With its rapidly growing economy based in oil financing, Dallas was by the 1940s a destination of choice for upwardly mobile professionals from many surrounding states, infusing the city's political class with Midwestern Republicans, such as Minnesota-born Fred Agnich, executive vice president of Texas Instruments, chief fundraiser for Richard Nixon's campaign in Texas in 1960, and eventual Dallas representative to the state legislature (42). Local institutions also included the increasingly conservative newspaper the Dallas Morning News , edited by E. M. "Ted" Dealey, who after inheriting the paper from his New Dealer father, supported the anticommunist crusades of an east Texan Democrat, Martin Dies, and a Wisconsin Republican, Joseph McCarthy, during the 1940s. McCarthy visited the state so frequently, Miller notes, that some described him as "Texas's third state senator" (31). In addition, the Dallas area was home to influential charismatic Protestant congregations, such as the segregationist Scofield Memorial Church, the Word of Faith movement, and the Baptist General Convention of Texas, led by the radio evangelist and anticommunist ideologue W. A. Criswell (24–26). Miller first shows the influence of the more radical activists on moderates when Bruce Alger, while fighting for election to Congress against a segregationist in 1956 as a supporter of Eisenhower, finally buckled to segregationist pressure from his Democratic opponent and adopted "subtle" support for segregation, distancing himself from more extreme activists. Miller argues that Alger's conversion to segregation was the "earliest incarnation of the Southern strategy" (85). The strategy became his gateway to the Far Right, as Alger later wound up as a favorite of Hunt and the only US congressman to vote against "socialized milk" for schoolchildren. By the early 1960s, a protest against Lyndon Johnson that backfired and created bad press for the Dallas GOP and the Republican gubernatorial campaign of John Birch Society hero General Edwin Walker, who had moved to Dallas after being institutionalized by John F. Kennedy, caused the Dallas Republican Party to develop a national reputation as "a bizarre band of nut-jobs" (100, 111). Because of this embarrassment following the Kennedy assassination and the rise in Black voter turnout against the GOP after 1964, the Texas party was not able to succeed electorally until the 1970s, with the rise of Reagan and the New Right. In conclusion, Miller argues that the Dallas GOP marked the most extreme version of Republicans' Southern strategy, and that the national party learned from it, "fine tuning" appeals to states' rights in such a way as to avoid alienating moderates by the time of the Nixon 1968 presidential campaign (143). In Miller's analysis, the most egregious propaganda could only take hold in a climate already prepared for it—that is, more moderate people would meet "fake news" with skepticism, so that the rise of influence of the Far Right cannot be explained simply by the fact that bizarre newspapers circulated. Conspiracy theories could either win adherents or, as with the early repudiations of the John Birch Society by the GOP establishment, come to be seen as embarrassments that backfired outside the environment that birthed them.
It was near this storm surge of growing ultraright conservative activism that the final conference of the International Women's Year of 1977 (IWY), a federally funded program of conferences, would occur. Spruill's Divided We Stand describes the road to the final national conference, held in Houston, where nearly twenty thousand feminists gathered to see the two thousand delegates produce a platform "to tell Congress and the president what women wanted" (2). In the tale of the IWY, Spruill recasts the history of second-wave feminism, identifying a broader coalition of women than appears in more simplistic representations shared by so much popular discussion of second-wave feminism today. She opens with a description of a meeting that sounds both nearly impossibly bipartisan while being much more inclusive of a variety of women than contemporary popular histories might have us believe. She notes that among the women who attended were three first ladies, Rosalynn Carter, Betty Ford, and Lady Bird Johnson, along with Republican activists and representatives Jill Ruckelshaus, Margaret Heckler, and RNC cochair Mary Crisp. Also on the program were prominent Democrats including Elizabeth Holtzman, Barbara Jordan, and Bella Abzug, who was the "presiding officer" of the commission on the IWY, as well as leading activists in women's organizations such as Betty Friedan, Maya Angelou, and Coretta Scott King. Among the most significant events for the history of feminism to occur at the conference were King's reading of the minority caucus plank and Friedan's public acknowledgment of her own previous failure to understand the need for lesbian rights (8). But, as Spruill notes, after describing this amazing scene, an equally large number of antifeminists calling themselves "Pro-Life and Pro-Family" met the same weekend in Houston to denounce feminism.
The rest of the book tells the story of the formation of the bipartisan feminist establishment, the organized antifeminist movement against it led by Phyllis Schlafly, and then, most dramatically, their clashes in state conferences on the road to Houston, as right-wing activists came "out of the kitchen and into the counter-revolution," as one aptly named chapter puts it. The early opposition to the feminist movement could be summarized by the antifeminist activist Rosemary Thomson's belief that the Comprehensive Child Development Act of 1971 was "a horrifying first step on the slippery slope toward a godless government invasion of family like that in the USSR." For this reason, another significant antifeminist organizer, Connie Marshner, used the direct-mail techniques of Richard Viguerie—ultimately succeeding when Nixon vetoed the bill, announcing his veto in a speech written by Patrick Buchanan (74). Like the conservatives of Dallas, the anti-ERA activists' arguments against the ERA were informed by religiously based anticommunist conspiracy theories. As Spruill puts it, "Population control, secular humanism, and feminism all seemed to be part of a communist plot" (105). Schlafly's political trajectory in the 1950s and early 1960s, speaking at John Birch Society events, working for Barry Goldwater, and running for Congress against a moderate Republican in the 1960s mirrors the journeys of the Dallas ultras that Miller describes, though she was based in Alton, Illinois. Like them, she ran her own publication, beginning in 1967, the Phyllis Schlafly Report . Like Schlafly, many other state-level STOP ERA leaders were associated with the John Birch Society, as well as the Mississippi-based segregationist group Women for Constitutional Government. Another leading figure, Lottie Beth Hobbs, came out of the Dallas milieu that Miller charts. She was part of the Church of Christ in Fort Worth and was the organizer of a national anti-ERA organization called Women Who Want to Be Women (WWWW) in 1975 and editor of a newsletter known as The Pink Sheet (86, 87).
Divided We Stand details the organizing drives of conservative women through a group called the IWY Citizens Review Commission (CRC), which adopted an anti-establishment approach. Having failed to stop the International Women's Year conferences, they decided to attend them, take notes to document "wrongdoing," argue their points to influence discussions, and get antifeminist women elected to state delegations for the national conference. Spruill's account explains how religion, racism and free market fundamentalism made an increasingly diverse feminist movement into a target. Anti-IWY activism unified formerly disparate religious opponents of abortion, gay rights, and the ERA, bringing members of the conspiratorial far right into the mainstream of the Republican Party, ultimately influencing the national platform. Friedan even worried about the "potential for a female Nuremburg fire," because of disruptions at state meetings led by Stop-ERA, the Eagle Forum, the John Birch Society, and, in Mississippi, the KKK (211). Even as the national mainstream media reported on the conference by acknowledging feminism as a majority political view, ultraright publications continued to hammer on the themes that we now see as standard right-wing arguments: the conference was dominated by militant lesbians; representing discipline as child abuse threatened the family; and of course, the whole thing was, in the words of JBS writer Susan Huck, "a monstrosity whelped by the United Nations with Bella Abzug as the midwife" (232). In addition to attacking liberals and moderate Republicans in government, this group of right-wing radicals also articulated their opposition to what they perceived as the liberal establishment media.
Schlafly's role in these events, and her continuing influence on national party leaders from Reagan to Trump, makes this book a vital contribution to history of the New Right, showing that Schlafly was every bit as important a policy influencer as both a propagandist and an organizer as male leaders like Lee Atwater or Karl Rove. The tactics and arguments of the CRC activists also indicate an early strategy by ultraright activists to make opposition to feminism a litmus test for "real" conservatism, as activists complained repeatedly after state IWY conferences that no "conservatives" were members of state delegations even when pro-ERA Republicans were among them. By the end of the summer's state conferences, when the antigay radical Anita Bryant and her allies had joined the CRC mobilization along with the conservative churches opposed to abortion, feminist activists were lamenting that the IWY had become "a vehicle for anti-ERA and abortion forces around the country" (189). Despite the evidence that the IWY and the ERA proved to be an important ground for Schlafly to build an antifeminist right-wing coalition that would ultimately shift the GOP's support away from the amendment and from abortion, Spruill argues that the growing field of studies of the Far Right has paid "little attention to the role of women and women's issues" in the process (2). Certainly, the role of abortion politics and the STOP the ERA campaigns have been a footnote in comparison with the attention paid to the Southern strategy in the history of the rise of the New Right, even as abortion remains one of the most significant dividing issues between the contemporary Democratic and Republican Parties, to the extent that many Republicans remain single-issue voters on the question of abortion. 3
Nonetheless, the story of the mobilization of Republican opposition to the women's year through Schlafly's CRC and the women's conference itself has remained, as Gloria Steinem is quoted in the book's opening, "the most important event that nobody knows about" (2). Like Miller's study, Spruill's explains a major Republican policy shift and the growing influence of largely Southern ultra-right activists on the national party's mainstream, as moderates switched positions on the ERA and abortion rights in the 1970s. The Republican shift on ERA and abortion was much more divisive in the party than was its policy transformation on the question of segregation. Spruill describes Crisp, a leading Republican feminist, being fired over the ERA as Schlafly worked closely with the RNC 1980 platform team. Most significant, in what Ms. Magazine would refer to as "one of the most dramatic and cynical policy reversals in modern American politics," George H. W. Bush signed on as Ronald Reagan's running mate in 1980, despite having made his differences with Reagan on issues of abortion and the ERA a central part of his primary campaign, and when questioned by the press about his willingness to overlook these policy differences, declared, "I'm not going to get nickel-and-dimed to death with detail" (287). Ultimately, thousands of ERA supporters, including Ruckelshaus, a leading Republican, demonstrated in the streets to oppose the Republican Party's shift on the issue (288–89). As these policies spread in the 1980s, another Republican feminist, Tanya Melich, referred to the Southern strategy as producing "an impressive show of political power for the new partnership of racism and sexism in pursuit of Republican victory" (303). While Spruill's analysis of the Far Right remains clear-eyed to the end, including a discussion of Schlafly's influence on Trump and the president's relationship with Kellyanne Conway, Spruill's treatment of Hillary Clinton in the conclusion lacks the nuanced reading of internal conflicts that characterizes the book's earlier chapters. Spruill allows Clinton to stand too neatly as the contemporary voice and ultimate symbol of achievement of second-wave feminism, effectively avoiding feminist critiques of Clinton from both inside and outside the Democratic Party.
Both Spruill's and Miller's books present an interesting question about the traditional trajectory of the rise of the New Right and the birth of the Southern strategy itself. Miller presents Goldwater as a "moderate"—but only in comparison to the John Birch Society and the apocalyptic religious arguments of Hunt—who was one of Schlafly's funders. As Schneider-Mayerson explains in Peak Oil , they also suggest that the post-1970s left-wing distrust of government often attributed to Vietnam, Watergate, and revelations about COINTELPRO, as well as countercultural politics that emphasized libertarian values, ultimately "dovetailed with" a broader libertarian shift in the culture associated with the antiregulatory neoliberal politics (85). Read in conversation with Spruill's and Miller's studies, Schneider-Mayerson's analysis of peakist subculture also suggests that internet left subcultures have intersected not only with neoliberal framing that has rightly been a chief concern of cultural studies but also with media cultures of the more conspiratorial Far Right.
Peak Oil differs from the many histories of the rise of the right, as the author seeks to turn from "the various causes of the rise of conservatism to its effects." Thus, even the left-wing subculture, or disparate "social assemblage" of internet readers of Peak Oil forums, is in many ways "conservative" despite individual participants describing themselves as liberal, socialist, or anarchist. Schneider-Mayerson's wide-ranging interdisciplinary analysis is drawn from readings of Peak Oil books and websites through the cultural context of apocalyptic movies and television shows. The central source, though, is the author's own detailed online survey taken by over one thousand "peakists" he contacted through the web. A demonstration of the ability of American studies to illuminate broad cultural trends beyond self-conscious political ideology, Peak Oil provides a fascinating look into the development of a left subculture that grew in popularity and adherents largely during the Iraq War, beginning in 2004 and tapering off by 2011. Although he does identify key movers and shakers of the Peak Oil ideology, the book reaches beyond a small coterie of intellectual influences to explain how a group of believers can be a microcosm to understand broader currents of individualism and apocalypticism in American culture. The two most valuable chapters are in the middle. The third chapter describes how Peak Oil embodies the libertarian "network effect" and offers both a comprehensive analysis of internet technology's connection to libertarian ideology in the tech industry and a concise history of libertarianism and its connections to a broader neoliberal hegemonic ideology. Most important, Schneider-Mayerson is attentive to the architecture of the media itself and how it affects subcultural formation, as are his respondents, one of whom points out that "community means something different on the internet" (89–94). Moving away from formal considerations to a longer popular cultural discursive analysis, the fourth chapter, with its discussion of the 1970s fear of a "population bomb," locates Peak Oil in the broader ideological tradition of apocalypticism in American culture. Schneider-Mayerson makes it clear that the ideas behind Peak Oil—that fossil fuel is an inherently limited resource and that humans are affecting global transformations of the earth—is not "fake news." The peakists' problem is their belief that a civilizational collapse is imminent and inevitable, and their individualistic methods of responding to this belief through survivalist preparation. It is this aspect of the virtual movement that he argues is fundamentally conservative. Adherents reject the idea that alternative energy solutions will work, distrust government action as a viable solution, do not believe in collective politics, distrust the "mainstream media" as a source of truth, and rather than becoming more politically active, become less so. He finds that peakists are mostly well-educated white men in their forties, and their most conservative visions emerge in their imagining of the post–Peak Oil world in which elements of traditional manhood are restored, detailed in a chapter that Schneider-Mayerson titles "White Masculinity and Post-Apocalyptic Retrosexuality." Among the leading intellectuals of the Peak Oil movement is James Howard Kunstler, author of nonfiction books and articles on Peak Oil, but also the popular novel World Made by Hand . Read by the majority of peakists surveyed, the novel and other future scenarios indicate that, as Schneider-Mayerson argues, "the peak oil movement and similar survivalist phenomena provided one means of reinscribing 'traditional' (read 1950s) gender roles and revitalizing white masculinity" (131). Here, perhaps, is the real domain of the dreaded "Left-bro" of much centrist alarmism on the 2016 election trail.
Unlike the conservative movements, and unlike the actual Bernie Sanders supporters of 2016, however, the peakists are defined by their pessimism. Their acceptance of the "real" news, which causes them to be ostracized by much of society, also leaves them deeply depressed. Their isolation and connection to internet communities as their only means of connection to political allies also resembles the discourse of being "red pilled" on the internet's "manosphere." However, these peakists seek therapy for this depression and try to adjust rather than organize politically. In this sense they are examples of the very effect on the common interest of the public that earlier Progressive opinion-makers worried about: not only that the public would be organized for the wrong causes but that they would be disorganized and prostrate before the more organized special interests, in this case, the PR machine of global-warming denial sponsored by the massive oil industry and its political allies. Of all the fractious opinion and distrust of media, climate change denial, fostered by the combination of the corporate PR and suspicious approaches to establishment science and media, may be the greatest danger social reformers have yet faced. Indeed, all the Far Right conspiracy theories described by Miller and Spruill are easily applied to climate change, just as they were to civil rights and feminism. According to the contemporary far right figure Alex Jones, climate change is a hoax invented by the UN to impose a one-world government. And today, as in the 1900s, intellectuals are still trying to figure out, like the Progressives many years ago, how to most effectively persuade the public in the interest of the common good while also preserving democracy. These books, while they do not provide a solution to that problem, are helpful in giving readers a deeper understanding of the terrain of struggle that we face.
Rebecca Hill
Rebecca Hill is professor of interdisciplinary studies and director of the American Studies Program at Kennesaw State University in Kennesaw, Georgia, where she teaches American studies research methods, introduction to the field of American studies, and prisons and policing in American society. She is the author of Men, Mobs, and Law: Anti-Lynching and Labor Defense in U.S. Radical History (2009) and has published articles in the New Left Review, Labor: Studies in Working Class History of the Americas, Labour/Le Travail, American Quarterly, The History News Network, and most recently at the blog Three Way Fight.
