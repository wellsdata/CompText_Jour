Over the past two decades, while the Internet has replaced traditional media, becoming the first or the preferred source of health information [42, 45] health misinformation has been prevalent on the Internet globally [6, 23]. Exposure to misinformation fosters misperceptions of health issues, which subsequently lead to risky health behaviors [11, 22, 26, 46]. Facing the growing concerns about health misinformation, scholars find it necessary to reduce the harm it brings about.
Scholars have examined health misinformation from various aspects. Kata [23], for example, surveyed a typical form of health misinformation online: anti-vaccination discourses. Jolly and Douglas [22] examined the effects of misinformation on individuals’ vaccination intentions. While existing literature has provided deep insights into health misinformation, not much research has compared the immediate effects of different kinds of health misinformation. This study attempts to address this research gap.
Learning about individuals’ responses to misinformation allows scholars and health educators to understand what kinds of misinformation are likely to generate the worst impact and deserve the most attention. In this way, they can develop tailored education messages to enhance the effects of health misinformation correction. This project adopted an experimental design to answer one overarching question: How do Chinese young adults perceive the source credibility and message believability of social media messages that deliver misinformation about the human papillomavirus (HPV) vaccines? This study provides novel insights into how individuals respond to different types of health misinformation, including the uncertainty frame that exaggerates and distorts the scientific uncertainties about vaccinations [15], the profit-making conspiracy frame that argues that vaccination is promoted mainly to advance profit-making purposes [15], and the nationalist conspiracy frame that views the HPV vaccines as a form of Western imperialism [7], which in turn, demonstrates the unique dangers of each type of misinformation.
Controversies surrounding vaccines are a part of “a more general political or cultural struggle” ([54], p. 1924). People in developing countries, especially those being colonized in the past few centuries, tend to view emerging medical interventions as a new form of Western imperialism (e.g., [7]). In China, anti-imperialism has been a crucial component of nationalism [60]. The nationalist thoughts rooted in history bring about a new challenge for health educators to introduce emerging medical interventions to developing countries. We chose the HPV vaccines in China as the context to exemplify this trend. The introduction of the HPV vaccines to China provides a valuable lens through which to examine if and how nationalist sentiments shape the outcomes of health misinformation. Our research findings can help health educators develop tailored educational campaigns to reduce the negative effects of health misinformation in developing countries with rich historical legacies like China.
Literature review
Perceived source credibility (PSC) and perceived message believability
Scholars have identified three types of sources in computer-mediated communication: visible sources, technological sources, and receiver sources [47]. While all these sources may affect the outcomes of media consumption, visible sources have captured the most scholarly attention because the psychological effect of who presents a message is the most powerful on receivers [47]. Visible sources are “the sources seen by the receiver to be delivering the message or content” ([47], p. 58). In other words, visible sources are often labelled as the authors of media messages. This project focuses on the credibility of visible sources.
Source credibility consists of three elements, including expertise, trustworthiness, and intention toward the receiver [32]. Expertise is defined as the competence of a source in a given discipline, which is often operationalized as being experienced, knowledgeable, qualified, and skilled [39]. Trustworthiness refers to the character of a source being honest, reliable, sincere, and dependable [39]. A source’s intention also determines its credibility: having a “good will,” such as being understanding, empathetic, and responsive, often indicates higher source credibility [32].
One way of gauging source credibility is to measure PSC. PSC is “the judgments made by a perceiver (e.g., a message recipient) concerning the believability of a communicator” ([38], p. 181). Consistent with the original conceptualization of source credibility [32], the three dimensions of PSC are expertise, trustworthiness, and attractiveness [39].
PSC is a crucial component in health communication because it affects viewers’ acceptance of a message [39]. High PSC results in desired message outcomes, such as more positive perceived message quality [13] and higher perceived message effectiveness [30, 35].
Some message sources are perceived as more credible than others and PSC varies across social and cultural contexts. In China, for example, due to high trust in the central government, people tend to find a government source to be more believable than the mass media, scientists, popular science media, celebrities, and layperson sources of misinformation correction [57].
The news media used to be a reliable source of health information, but in recent years, the credibility of news media outlets has been declining across the globe [28, 41]. In China, state-owned and national media outlets contributed to the perpetration of misinformation [17]. Given their major influence in the media landscape, news media outlets also set the agenda of social media platforms such as WeChat and Weibo in advancing rumors [18].
Meanwhile, in China, doctors and scientists intensively use social media to disseminate health knowledge and voluntarily deliver science and health education [21]. Non-governmental popular science organizations have emerged, too. Guokr, for example, a popular science organization dedicated to the dissemination of scientific knowledge and the promotion of science culture, has obtained more than 10 million followers on Weibo [48, 57].
Given the prominent role of non-governmental popular science organizations like Guokr [48] in supplementing and enriching the health information provided by traditional health communication sources, we aimed to examine the PSC of popular science organizations and compare it with the news media. While the credibility of news media outlets was declining and that of popular science organizations was increasing, we were not able to rely solely on existing literature to predict which one had obtained higher PSC. We asked RQ1 to understand how individuals perceived the credibility of these two types of organizations differently.
RQ1. How do a popular science organization and a news media outlet score differently on PSC?
In addition to directly measuring attitudinal and behavioral changes to determine message effectiveness, scholars often use perceived message effectiveness or perceived message believability (hereafter “message believability” or “believability”), a variable predicting the likely effects, as an indirect measure to gauge the impact of persuasive messages [36, 58]. Dillard and Ye [12] defined message believability as “an estimate of the degree to which a persuasive message will be favorably evaluated by recipients of that message” (p. 150). Although message believability is self-reported [25] and is not theoretically the same as actual effectiveness [37], it remains a critical component of health communication research because it is a strong predictor of actual effectiveness [25, 36, 37]. Individuals are more likely to be convinced if they find health education messages (e.g., [31]) to be believable.
In recent years, scholars have applied believability to misinformation research. Agley and Xiao [1], for example, intensively studied how demographics, psychographics, and the narratives of misinformation about COVID-19 affected message believability. Their study showed that most people believed conspiratorial narratives the least but were more likely to be affected by other types of misinformation narratives [1]. Existing research also shows that information sources exert an influence on believability (e.g., [24]). Like RQ1, we were not able to predict which type of message source generated more believable messages, so we asked:
RQ2. How do a message created by a popular science organization and one by a news media outlet score differently on believability?
Misinformation about vaccines and the HPV vaccines
Misinformation refers to “the presence of or belief in objectively incorrect information” ([5], p. 621). It is a kind of false information that “can mislead people even if unintentionally promoted or mistakenly endorsed as being true” ([46], p. 3). Given the diverse origins of misinformation [26], misinformation often resonates with the cultural, political, economic, and media contexts in which it emerges.
Misinformation features diverse vocabularies and narratives [50]. Existing research shows that individuals’ intentions to believe in and share misinformation vary by rumor type [9] and misinformation narrative [1]. Given the impact of misinformation narratives on message outcomes, this study examined how different frames used in misinformation about the HPV vaccines might trigger different cognitive and affective responses.
Misinformation about the HPV vaccines centers on several themes/frames. The first frame is the uncertainty frame, which overemphasizes and distorts scientific uncertainties and vaccination risks [15]. A large number of anti-vaccination messages emphasize that the HPV vaccines cannot protect people from infectious diseases but lead to deadly adverse effects [7, 15, 23]. Through emphasizing potential scientific uncertainties and exaggerating the side effects of vaccination [15], people often use the uncertainty frame to mislead others to believe that the harms of vaccines greatly outweigh their benefits [7].
The second frame is the profit-making conspiracy frame. Conspiracy theories emphasize the systematic deception of the public by powerful and malevolent forces [51]. In the United States, due to the HPV vaccine mandate, conspiracists argue that HPV vaccine promotion is a conspiracy involving the government, pharmaceutical companies, and doctors [6]. Both the uncertainty frame and the profit-making conspiracy frame have been identified in a wide variety of anti-vaccination messages, including vaccine-critical websites [61], user-generated YouTube videos [6], and blog posts [10].
Since being introduced to China in 2017 [59], the HPV vaccines have become a popular topic on China’s major social media site, Weibo. Misinformation about a variety of health and science topics prevails on Weibo [20] Empirical research in the Chinese context [7] also confirms that these two themes are prominent in the narratives surrounding the HPV vaccines in China. Another content analysis of Weibo posts revealed that advertisement was the most common topic about the HPV vaccines, which might make people question whether the promotion of the HPV vaccines aimed to advocate for public good or to benefit pharmaceutical manufacturers only [20].
Vaccine criticism often resonates with broader political debates [54]. In China, the third prominent frame of the social media narratives surrounding the HPV vaccines has emerged. It is the nationalist conspiracy frame that resonates with nationalist sentiments [7]. Nationalism is the ideology that “values membership in a nation greater than other groups, seeks distinction from other nations, and strives to preserve the nation and give preference to political presentation by the nation for the nation” ([4], p. 520). Chinese nationalism emerged in the late 1800s, when China faced the intrusion of Western forces and became semi-colonized [60]. Rooted in the context of anti-imperialism, a key component of Chinese nationalism is resisting external invasions to avoid national annihilation [60]. Nationalist sentiments are rising in recent years [4, 14]. Due to a high level of vigilance against the West, especially the United States, many people have viewed the HPV vaccines as a form of “Western influence” and even “Western imperialism” since the first day the HPV vaccines were approved for sale in China [7]. The highly sensational rhetoric used by anti-vaxxers and nationalists makes nationalist arguments strong and convincing [7].
This kind of nationalist conspiracy frame imposes a new challenge for health educators to introduce emerging medical interventions to developing countries with strong historical legacies but is rarely discussed in North American or European contexts. Therefore, it is worthwhile introducing the nationalist conspiracy frame, a sequela of the colonial era, to health misinformation research. Existing research has described a variety of misinformation frames, but few studies have compared or predicted media consumers’ responses to different misinformation frames. Therefore, we asked the following RQs:
RQ3. How do different misinformation frames affect PSC?
RQ4. How do different misinformation frames affect message believability?
RQ5. How do misinformation frame and message source interact to affect (a) PSC and (b) message believability?
Responses to health misinformation
Empirical research has demonstrated the detrimental effects of health misinformation on various audience segments, especially those who are under-informed about a particular scientific issue [53]. Misinformation leads to misperceptions and discourages them from performing desired health acts [11, 46].
In addition to generating attitudinal and behavioral outcomes, misinformation and misperceptions can trigger affective and emotional reactions. For example, both the uncertainty frame and the profit-making conspiracy frame in vaccine misinformation caused negative attitudes toward vaccines and weaker vaccination intentions, but the conspiracy frame led to stronger fear and anger than the uncertainty frame [15]. Participants in the conspiracy frame conditions scored higher on the items asking to what extent they felt angry and afraid after being exposed to the misinformation [15]. In line with the study by Featherstone and Zhang [15], we further examined if and how message sources and misinformation frames evoked fear and anger differently.
RQ6. How do a popular science organization and a news media outlet evoke individuals’ (a) fear and (b) anger differently?
RQ7. How do different misinformation frames trigger individuals’ (a) fear and (b) anger differently?
RQ8. How do misinformation frame and message source interact to affect (a) fear and (b) anger?
Media use habit is another variable to determine misinformation acceptance. Active and frequent social media users are more likely to consume media content critically, because they develop higher media literacy through “accessing, managing, producing, and exchanging information on interactive platforms” ([56], p. 8). In this study, we sought to know the impact of time spent on social media on young adults’ responses to health misinformation.
RQ9: How does social media use affect individuals’ (a) PSC, (b) message believability, (c) fear, and (d) anger, after exposure to health misinformation?
Methods
Participants and procedure
Our study received the university’s institutional review board approval in July 2022. Data were collected via an online survey using the Qualtrics panel, which was hosted on wjx.com , from August 28 to August 30, 2022. Wjx.com has been proven to be a reliable crowdsourcing tool for recruiting participants in China (e.g., [27, 43]). Young adults between the ages of 18 and 35 who had not received the HPV vaccines were invited to complete the online questionnaire. The survey questions were adapted from literature and translated into Chinese. 621 responses were collected. After removing the data points due to failing attention checks and unanswering more than a third of the survey questions, we identified 498 valid responses. Each participant who contributed a valid response received a certain amount of wjx.com credit, which could be converted into a monetary reward in the future. In total, data from 498 participants were used for analysis. A priori power analysis indicated that a sample size of N  = 357 provides 95% power (1–β = 0.95) to detect a medium effect size ( f  = 0.25) in a one-way ANOVA with eight groups, assuming α = 0.05.

During the pretest, participants responded to survey questions measuring their time spent on social media in a typical day. With the completion of the pretest, participants were randomly assigned to one of the eight conditions in a 4 (misinformation frame) × 2 (message source) between-subjects design. Of the final sample, the number of observations in each condition was roughly even, ranging from 56 to 66 in each. Chi-square and ANOVA tests did not identify statistically significant differences in demographics or pretest variables between conditions.
During the posttest, participants responded to manipulation check questions and another set of survey questions asking about PSC, message believability, affective responses (fear and anger), and demographics.
To avoid misleading research participants, before they exited the questionnaire, we presented those who were assigned to the first six conditions (i.e., those who read a message delivering misinformation) a briefing statement claiming that the message content was made up and recommended sources for reliable information about the HPV vaccines.
Experimental stimulus materials and manipulation check
This study adopted a 4 (misinformation frame: uncertainty, profit-making conspiracy, nationalist conspiracy, and control group) × 2 (message source: media outlet and popular science organization) between-subjects experimental design. Weibo plays the most prominent role in facilitating the diffusion of online misinformation in China [18]. A big data analysis of the top 10 rumors in China’s cyberspace showed that among the three major online news platforms (Weibo, WeChat, and mainstream news sites), Weibo was the most likely to advance rumors [18].
Given the prominent role of Weibo in spreading misinformation, we used PhotoShop to produce our stimulus materials in the format of Weibo posts. Each post included the message and its source’s profile image, resembling an actual Weibo post to the best of our abilities. Eight Weibo posts were created. In each experimental condition, participants viewed one Weibo post.
We referred to the design and findings of similar research to manipulate misinformation frames. To create uncertainty and profit-making framed messages, we referred to the excerpts of experimental messages about MMR vaccinations by Featherstone and Zhang [15] and a mixed methods content analysis of Weibo posts about the HPV vaccines [7]. We wrote messages that reflected the prominent features of uncertainty and profit-making conspiracy arguments being described by these two studies. To create a nationalist conspiracy message, we wrote a message that resonated with the key components (e.g., “Western imperialism”) and adopted the rhetoric in a typical nationalist conspiracy message on Weibo [7]. In the control conditions where the Weibo post delivered reliable information, we downloaded several bullet points from the World Health Organization website. All the messages were written in Chinese. Appendix A shows the English translations of the Weibo posts.
Regarding message source, in the media outlet conditions, the author of the Weibo posts was claimed to be “China Youth Daily,” one of the state-owned and bestselling national daily newspapers in China [8]. China Youth Daily was chosen because it demonstrates the features of two major types of publications in China: an “official” press that aims to advocate government’s agenda and a “market-oriented” press that pursues “a news agenda to win readers and hence advertisers” ([52], p. 1204). Given its dual roles, China Youth Daily is a good example to represent a news media outlet in China. In the popular science organization conditions, the author of the posts was “Guokr” ( guokr.com ), an influential media platform dedicated to popular science communication since 2010 [19]. Scholars argue that Guokr “exemplifies the important role that non-governmental organizations can play in the dissemination of scientific knowledge and the promotion of science culture” ([48], p. 231).
Right after reading the designated Weibo post, participants responded to several manipulation check questions. They were asked to indicate if the source/author of the message was a media outlet or a popular science organization, χ 2 (1, N  = 497) = 406.07, p  < 0.001, V  = 0.90. 96.7% ( n  = 234) of the participants in the popular science organization conditions correctly identified the source of the media message, and 93.7% ( n  = 239) of the participants in the media outlet conditions correctly identified the source of the media message. These results showed that most participants correctly identified message sources.
Participants also indicated what was true about the central argument made by the message author. Chi-square results showed that most participants correctly identified misinformation frames, χ 2 (9, N  = 497) = 1122.20, p  < 0.001, V  = 0.87. 86.7% ( n  = 104) of the participants in the uncertainty conditions indicated that “the author of the post argued that the HPV vaccine was unsafe and its effectiveness remained unclear,” 84.4% ( n  = 108) of the participants in the profit-making conditions indicated that “the author of the post argued that the promotion of the HPV vaccine was driven by profit-making purposes,” 92.2% ( n  = 119) of the participants in the nationalist conditions indicated that “the author of the post argued that the promotion of the HPV vaccine was a conspiracy of foreigners,” and 95.8% ( n  = 115) of the participants in control conditions indicated that “the author of the post posited that the HPV vaccine was effective and safe.”
Primary measures
We identified the top nine most popular social media sites in China from public reports [2, 49]: Weibo, WeChat, TikTok, QQ, Xiaohongshu, Kuaishou, Bilibili, Zhihu, and Baidu Tieba. Adopting the measure from Zuniga et al. [62], we asked participants to indicate how much time they spent on each site in a typical day. The scale ranged from 0 (never), 1 (less than 30 min), 2 (30 min to 1 h), etc., to 7 (more than 3 h). We obtained the social media use scores ( M  = 2.09, SD  = 0.89) by calculating the average scores of participants’ responses to all nine survey questions.
We used a 7-point semantic differential scale from Ohanian [39] to measure two dimensions of PSC: trustworthiness and expertise. Participants indicated to what extent they thought the author of the social media post was “dependable,” “honest,” “sincere,” “trustworthy,” “an expert,” “knowledgeable,” and “qualified.” The average of all these items made the PSC scores. Higher scores indicated higher PSC (α = 0.96, M  = 4.03, SD  = 1.41).
Perceived message believability was measured using one question adapted from Maynard et al. [31] and Park et al. [40]: “to what extent is the social media post you read just now believable”? The scale ranged from 1 (not believable at all) to 7 (extremely believable), M  = 4.09, SD  = 1.66.
We adopted the measures from Nabi et al. [34] to measure two affective responses, fear and anger. Participants indicated their feelings after reading the social media post on a 6-point scale (0 = not at all, 5 = extremely). Fear was measured using “anxious,” “scared,” “afraid,” and “worried.” The average of all these items made the fear scores (α = 0.87, M  = 1.54, SD  = 1.15). Anger was measured using “angry,” “annoyed,” “irritable,” and “frustrated.” The average of all these items made the anger scores (α = 0.85, M  = 1.27, SD  = 1.20).
Data analysis
With our sample size exceeding 400 in a public health context, assumption of normal distribution was not required [29]. We conducted independent-sample t -tests to answer RQ1 and RQ2. We employed one-way ANOVA tests to answer RQ3 and RQ4. We employed two-way ANOVA tests to examine interaction effects (RQ5 and RQ8). If the two-way ANOVA yielded statistically significant results, we divided the dataset by independent variables and reran ANOVA to identify simple effects. Otherwise, we looked at the main effects only. To test for RQs 6–8, we first ran the correlation between fear and anger and found that these two dependent variables were moderately correlated ( r  = 0.46, p  < 0.001). Thus, we employed one-way MANOVA. We conducted multiple linear regression to answer RQ9.
Results
RQ1 asked how a popular science organization and a news media outlet scored differently on PSC. Participants perceived a media outlet to be more credible than a popular science organization. Independent-sample t -tests showed that those who were assigned to the media outlet conditions scored significantly higher on PSC than those assigned to the popular science organization conditions, M media  = 4.16, SD media  = 1.45, M popular  = 3.88, SD popular  = 1.36, t (1, 493) = 2.23, p  = 0.03, Cohen’s d  = 0.20. Regarding RQ2, no statistically significant difference was found between the impact of the two message sources on message believability, M media  = 4.22, SD media  = 1.68, M popular  = 3.95, SD popular  = 1.63, t (1, 495) = 1.82, p  = 0.07, Cohen’s d  = 0.16.
ANOVA tests showed that misinformation frames exerted different influences on PSC, believability, fear, and anger. To answer RQ3, participants in control conditions scored highest on PSC ( M  = 5.22, SD  = 0.87). There was no statistically significant difference between uncertainty ( M  = 4.05, SD  = 1.19) and profit-making conspiracy conditions ( M  = 3.74, SD  = 1.27). Nationalist conspiracy conditions scored lowest on PSC, M  = 3.19, SD  = 1.40, F (3, 494) = 62.85, p  < 0.001, η 2  = 0.28, Cohen’s f  = 0.23.
Believability scores demonstrated a similar trend (RQ4). Those who were assigned to control conditions rated highest on believability ( M  = 5.56, SD  = 0.85). There was no statistically significant difference between uncertainty ( M  = 4.11, SD  = 1.45) and profit-making conspiracy conditions ( M  = 3.75, SD  = 1.52). Nationalist conspiracy conditions scored lowest on believability, M  = 3.04, SD  = 1.58, F (3, 496) = 72.30, p  < 0.001, η 2  = 0.31, Cohen’s f  = 0.24. Among the three misinformation frames, participants were least likely to find a nationalist conspiracy frame to be believable.
For RQ5, no interaction effects were identified between misinformation frame and message source on PSC, F (3, 487) = 0.24, p  = 0.87, Cohen’s f  = 0.00, or believability, F (3, 489) = 0.08, p  = 0.97, Cohen’s f  = 0.00.
For RQ6, MANOVA tests showed that message sources did not have an impact on respondents’ affective responses to the experimental stimuli, F (2, 480) = 0.55, p  = 0.58; Wilk's lambda = 0.99, Pillai's Trace = 0.00, F (2, 480) = 0.55, p  = 0.58, η 2  = 0.31, Cohen’s f  = 0.05.
We used one-way MANOVA to test RQ7. Wilk’s lambda test and Pillai test results were significant, F (6, 956) = 31.98, p  < 0.001; Wilk's lambda = 0.69, partial η 2  = 0.17, Pillai's Trace = 0.32, F (6, 958) = 0.30.76, p  < 0.001, Cohen’s f  = 0.69. The follow up univariate ANOVA test showed that misinformation frames had a statistically significant effect on invoking both fear, F (3, 479) = 12.84; p  < 0.001; partial η 2  = 0.07, and anger F (3, 479) = 52.48; p  < 0.001; partial η 2  = 0.25.
To be specific, those in uncertainty conditions scored highest on fear ( M  = 2.01, SD  = 1.23). Within the three other conditions, nationalist conspiracy conditions ( M  = 1.56, SD  = 1.24) scored higher than control conditions ( M  = 1.12, SD  = 0.82). There was no statistically significant difference between profit-making conspiracy conditions ( M  = 1.46, SD  = 1.10) and nationalist conspiracy or control conditions, F (3, 491) = 13.04, p  < 0.001, η 2  = 0.07 (RQ7a). When it came to anger, control conditions scored lowest ( M  = 0.27, SD  = 0.47). Within the misinformation conditions, nationalist conspiracy conditions scored higher ( M  = 1.85, SD  = 1.29) than uncertainty conditions ( M  = 1.29, SD  = 0.96). There was no statistically significant difference between profit-making conspiracy conditions ( M  = 1.59, SD  = 1.21) and two other misinformation conditions (RQ7b), F (3, 483) = 52.28, p  < 0.001, η 2  = 0.25. RQ9 asked about the effects of social media use on dependent variables. Multiple regression analyses revealed differential effects of social media use across outcome variables. Null effects emerged for PSC (β = 0.004, SE = 0.075, t  = 0.05, p  = 0.961, R 2  < 0.001) and believability (β = − 0.020, SE = 0.088, t  = − 0.22, p  = 0.823, R 2  < 0.001). Discussion
Our online experiment yielded three major findings. First, young adults found a state-owned news media outlet to be more credible than a popular science organization, but the two message sources did not cause different message believability or affective responses. Second, uncertainty, profit-making, and nationalist conspiracy frames led to different PSC, believability, fear, and anger. Finally, social media use did not affect PSC or believability, but was associated with affective responses.
PSC and believability in the misinformation context
Compared to the messages that delivered misinformation, the reliable health messages presented in control conditions scored highest on PSC and believability, suggesting that participants were able to discern reliable and unreliable information on social media. This finding resonated with previous research: most misinformation on social media was often disbelieved [33]. Agley and Xiao [1] also found that 70% of their sample population believed the scientifically accepted narratives about the origins of COVID-19 but did not believe in conspiratorial narratives.
The results of RQ1 show that participants perceived a news media outlet to be more credible than a popular science organization. Even though the credibility of news media has been declining [28] and non-governmental popular science organizations have been increasingly active in China’s cyberspace [21, 48] news media outlets remain a highly credible source of information.
One possible reason for the news media outlet receiving higher PSC scores is the unique features of the example news media outlet in our study, China Youth Daily. China Youth Daily is a state-owned “official press” that is supposed to prioritize governments’ political agenda [52]. Chinese media consumers tend to trust a government source more than other information sources [57]. The nature of being an “official press” makes China Youth Daily an alternative government information source that is often perceived as credible. In addition to being endorsed by governments, China Youth Daily demonstrates typical features of a “market-oriented press” that caters to the interests of ordinary readers and adopts “techniques such as personalization, sensationalism, details of personal life and the display of emotion in its journalism” ([52], p. 1216). Its high readership [8] shows that China Youth Daily is well received by readers, which is an indication of prolonged high perceived credibility. In contrast, although Guokr has demonstrated increasingly prominent roles in science and health communication, its influence is not yet comparable to China Youth Daily, a well-established official and market-oriented media outlet.
Despite the small differences in PSC scores, participants perceived both the news media outlet and the popular science organization as relatively credible. This kind of credibility judgment could make individuals process information heuristically [55]. With heuristic information processing, participants relied more heavily on message content to judge message believability. As a result, message source did not play a role in determining message believability or triggering emotional reactions.
Misinformation frames
Featherstone and Zhang [15] found that uncertainty and profit-making conspiracy frames had a negative influence on vaccination attitudes and intentions, and our study showed that both frames scored higher on PSC and believability than the nationalist conspiracy frame. Two factors could have contributed to uncertainty and profit-making frames being considered more believable than the nationalist conspiracy frame.
First, the uncertainty frame exaggerates the scientific uncertainties about vaccinations, but the scientific uncertainties of vaccination, such as side effects [15], are acknowledged by the science community. Unlike the nationalist conspiracy frame that almost makes up a connection between vaccination and other social issues, the uncertainty frame is a distortion of “uncertainty,” a basic component of science. Since portions of the message are built upon a basic science component and look reliable, people are more likely to find an uncertainty framed message to be believable. This finding shows that compared to making up facts, distorting some facets of science can generate worse outcomes.
Second, since the HPV vaccines being introduced to China, there have been numerous advertisements on social media [20]. The profit-making conspiracy frame resonated with the prevalence of commercial narratives, making individuals think the profit-making framed message was believable. This trend shows that commercial narratives such as advertising messages could inadvertently contribute to the negative effects of health misinformation, suggesting that educators need to take into consideration various kinds of discourses in the media when they attempt to correct health misinformation.
Young adults found the nationalist conspiracy frame to be the least believable. This showed that despite the rising nationalist sentiments in China and across the globe [4, 14], young adults did not resonate with the messages that relied on nationalist sentiments to defy emerging medical interventions. One possible explanation is that our sample population had relatively high educational backgrounds, leading to stronger critical thinking skills and higher media literacy. They had the abilities to identify science knowledge and authentic forms of imperialism, so they were not likely to believe in the nationalist sentiments that were embedded in a health message only to support anti-vaccination conspiracy arguments. This finding shows that correcting nationalist conspiracy narratives may not be in urgent need, but more research is needed to determine their impact on other audience segments, such as older adults and those with lower health and media literacy.
Regarding affective responses, the uncertainty frame triggered stronger fear than two other frames. Our research findings partially resonated with previous research. Featherstone and Zhang [15] found that a profit-making conspiracy frame in a piece of vaccine-related misinformation evoked fear but an uncertainty frame did not. The differences might be explained by the different research contexts. Featherstone and Zhang [15] tested individuals’ reactions to misinformation about the MMR vaccine, a highly mature vaccine that had been widely adopted and proven to be effective for decades. Our study was conducted in the context of the HPV vaccines, a medical intervention that was not introduced to Chinese young adults until 2017 and few reliable reports had been generated to confirm their effectiveness and safety among the Chinese population. Since the HPV vaccines were relatively new, young adults cared more about their safety and were even more susceptible to any skepticism over their safety. This trend resonates with individuals’ lower tolerance of uncertainty when it comes to new vaccines. Gillman et al. [16], for example, found that lower tolerance of vaccination risks and ambiguity was associated with lower vaccination intentions. Both research findings indicate that an uncertainty frame that exaggerates the uncertainties about emerging forms of vaccines might generate stronger fear and thus is more likely to discourage people from making rational decisions.
When it came to anger, the nationalist conspiracy frame evoked stronger anger than the uncertainty frame and the reliable health message. This is perhaps because Chinese nationalism is rooted in anti-imperialism and resisting external invasion remains a key component [60]. Nationalist conspiracy arguments indicated that the introduction of the HPV vaccines was a new form of Western imperialism, and together with their sensational and exaggerated rhetoric [7], they triggered antagonism against the West, stirring young adults’ emotions and making them feel angry. Accordingly, the control conditions led to the weakest anger because the message was written in plain language and did not intend to stir emotions. Despite the aforementioned reasons, it is worthwhile noting that it remains unclear in this study what the object of anger is. It might be the specific foreign entity being mentioned in the message or the nationalist conspiracy comment itself. Therefore, more research is needed to determine the object that directly triggered anger.
Social media use
Previous research shows that active and frequent social media users develop higher media literacy so that they are more likely to consume media critically and are less likely to be misled by misinformation [56]. Our study showed that social media use did not predict PSC or believability, but was positively associated with fear and anger. The phenomenon has several possible explanations. First, since the study population had relatively high educational backgrounds, they could have developed high health and media literacy, resulting in high trust in science that protected them against misinformation [1]. Compared to trust in science, media use played a trivial role in shaping one’s evaluation of message quality. This could explain why media use did not positively predict PSC or believability. Second, when it came to affective responses, active social media use led to higher anger and fear. One possibility is that active social media users had encountered similar misinformation online numerous times. Repetitive exposure to similar misinformation could have led to stronger anger and fear. More methodological discussions on experimental design and manipulation check are needed to more precisely measure participants’ affective responses to health misinformation.
Conclusion
Adopting an experimental design, we studied Chinese young adults’ cognitive and affective responses to conspiracy social media messages about the HPV vaccines. Overall, health misinformation scored lower on PSC and believability than reliable information, which demonstrated young adults’ abilities to discern reliable and unreliable information.
The research findings have practical and theoretical implications. First, the high PSC of a news media outlet suggests that news media outlets remain influential. Given news media outlets’ prominent agenda-setting roles in advancing misinformation [17, 18] and young adults’ trust in news media, more attention and efforts are needed to correct the unreliable information disseminated by the news media. Second, compared to the nationalist conspiracy frame, the uncertainty and profit-making frames were found to be more believable. Therefore, while health educators might not be able to eliminate health misinformation, correcting those utilizing uncertainty and profit-making frames is in the most urgent need. Third, even though the nationalist conspiracy frame scored relatively low on PSC and believability, it evoked strong anger among young adults. Negative emotions such as anger might prohibit individuals from making rational decisions if they accumulate as time goes on.
We went beyond treating the HPV vaccines merely as a medical intervention and applying the research findings in the North American context to China, but placed this health controversy within the indigenous context. Controversies surrounding vaccines are a part of “a more general political or cultural struggle” ([54], p. 1924). Therefore, Chinese young adults’ reactions to multiple forms of misinformation about the HPV vaccines exemplified the combined effects of health myths and nationalist thoughts, providing deeper insights into the complexity and nuances of health communication in developing countries.
This study has several limitations. First, the sample population was not representative enough to represent the nation’s average, lowering its generalizability. Participants had relatively high educational backgrounds, which indicated high health and media literacy. It will be worthwhile studying to what extent our research findings are applicable to groups with lower educational backgrounds.
Second, the measures of some variables need further evaluations. For PSC, we adopted seven out of the ten items from Ohanian’s scale (1990). A meta-analysis of online health information literature, however, identified conceptual ambiguity of trustworthiness and credibility in empirical research [44]. Given the inconsistent conceptualizations, viewing trustworthiness as one dimension of source credibility might have weakened the internal validity of Ohanian’s scale. Recent research has developed more reliable measures of “trust” (e.g., [3]), which can be used in future research. Regarding the “social media use” variable, some participants might be aware of or had been exposed to similar health misinformation on social media, so they could have reduced their social media use to minimize their exposure to misinformation. We did not consider the impact of this kind of information avoidance on our research outcomes. It is also worth noting that the dependent variable of our study, message believability, measured the extent to which participants found the messages to be believable, but it did not indicate whether or not participants were likely to develop biased perceptions of vaccines or perform risky health acts. Future research could further examine if and how misinformation frames might mislead individuals over the long term.
Another limitation lies in the inconsistent granularity of the scales used in our questionnaire. Since the scales were adapted from existing research, some variables were measured on 5-point scales whereas others were measured on 7-point scales. The inconsistency of scale granularity could confuse respondents and add to response fatigue, which might negatively affect reliability. Regarding data analysis, we did not conduct a confirmatory factor analysis to validate the proposed model, weakening the power of research outcomes. In addition, this study adopted a posttest-only design. We were not able to measure attitude changes that were directly caused by exposure to misinformation. Future research can adopt a pretest–posttest design to more precisely measure the direct impact of misinformation on attitude changes.
Regarding future research on message sources and PSC, since existing literature suggests that sources affect message outcomes (e.g., [30, 35]), it will be worthwhile exploring the possible mediating effects of message source on PSC and other variables.
