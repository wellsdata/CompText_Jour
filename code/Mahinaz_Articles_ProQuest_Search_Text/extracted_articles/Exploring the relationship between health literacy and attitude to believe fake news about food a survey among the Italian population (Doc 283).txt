WHAT IS ALREADY KNOWN ON THIS TOPIC
- Health literacy influences the ability to evaluate health information, with lower levels linked to higher vulnerability to misinformation.

- Few studies have focused on food and nutrition fake news.


WHAT THIS STUDY ADDS
- Lower health literacy is associated with greater belief in and sharing of food-related fake news.

- Sociodemographic factors like age and education also play a role.


HOW THIS STUDY MIGHT AFFECT RESEARCH, PRACTICE OR POLICY
- Enhancing health literacy across the population could reduce food misinformation and guide public health strategies to improve critical evaluation of nutrition information.


Introduction
In a globalised and constantly changing world, digitisation provides new tools for social interaction that can strongly impact previously unknown areas and create opportunities to reorganise the information system. The advent of the internet has enabled unfettered access to mass content and information about health, wellness and nutrition, 1 and social media has radically changed the way we communicate, collaborate, consume and create content. 2 In this phenomenon of digital globalisation, information sharing is made easier but also uncontrolled, and real news spreads at the same speed as fake news, real news spreads at the same speed as disinformation, content intentionally crafted to mislead or deceive, which has become increasingly relevant. Currently, the term has taken on a meaning closely related to the world of Social Networks, emphasising the crucial role of the Web in their dissemination. Significantly, much of the news shared on social media is not even read by the user before being shared 3 ; news that sows doubt, ridicules a thesis or excludes minorities easily receives a like and is shared and commented on more quickly, spreading in a very short time despite the lack of any scientific support. 4
Food and nutrition are sectors particularly affected by this phenomenon and the spread of fake news is challenging the classic relationship through which consumers and companies have always dialogued. Indeed, food represents a universal language and a tool for connecting different cultures; at the same time, it is a protagonist of our lives, not only in the kitchen. In fact, a US survey found that American dietitians identified Facebook as the main source of nutrition information for citizens seeking a quick solution to their food dilemmas, and the collection of information on vitamins, diet, nutrition and supplements is the main motivation that leads people to use the internet and social media. 5 People are exposed to a variety of dietary information and lifestyle advice that may be contradictory and deviant from health standards, yet is often considered reliable and followed, 6–8 thereby encouraging unhealthy behaviours. 9 This system generates ‘nutrition fake,’ a widespread proliferation of fake nutrition information online, often unmoderated and highly engaging, which can dangerously influence the food choices of Web users. In Italy, which has always been the standard-bearer of the Mediterranean diet, the extent of this phenomenon and its impact on public health are still largely unknown, as in the rest of the world. 10 11 Food misinformation constitutes one of the challenges for health communication 12 and represents a crucial issue that affects all citizens and touches a cardinal principle of democratic society: the right to correct information.
Studying the phenomenon of online fake news regarding nutrition and food is certainly complicated because it implies several structural, individual and social factors. 11 In nutrition science, there is multiple evidence to support health literacy (HL) as an empowerment strategy for citizens to make informed and consistent choices in protecting health. The multifaceted aspects that the concept of HL takes on in the various social and health contexts are well encapsulated in the integrated model described by Sørensen et al . He defines HL as the set of people’s knowledge, motivation and skills to access, understand, evaluate and apply health information, in order to make judgements and decisions in everyday life regarding healthcare, disease prevention and health promotion to maintain or improve quality of life. 13 In these terms, HL appears to be closely related to socioeconomic and health determinants. On the other hand, scientific evidence testifies that individual health practices must be integrated with public health strategies 14 : possessing—or lacking—HL skills makes a significant difference in one’s ability to access, understand and respond to information in order to achieve established health goals.
The present study takes its inspiration from the integrated model of Sørensen et al 13 summarising the ‘evidence-based’ dimensions of HL 15 16 and combining it with salutogenic theory by Antonovsky et al . 17 By highlighting the social and individual factors, common to the two models, the model concurs to determine citizen empowerment for individual and collective health promotion (figure 1). Indeed, integrating HL models with health promotion models through education, social mobilisation and advocacy actions is essential to improve the nutritional well-being of the population. In the context of a global and multifactorial view of HL, this study aims to assess any potential correlation between consumers’ average HL (HL-mean) and their susceptibility to food-related fake news, highlighting the potential role of social media and influencers in shaping consumers’ food choices.

Methods
Data were collected through a structured self-administered online questionnaire from a convenience sample of 2284 customers from nine community pharmacies in northern, central and southern Italy. The pharmacies were selected through purposive (non-probabilistic) sampling, with the intent to ensure a geographically balanced distribution. Customers accessing the selected pharmacies during the data collection period (January–March 2024) were invited to participate voluntarily and anonymously via a QR code displayed at the entrance or distributed by pharmacists. All participants, aged 18–75 years, provided informed consent before accessing the questionnaire; those who did not were excluded from the database. The questionnaire was created and administered using Google Forms, chosen for its accessibility and ease of use. The structured questionnaire, consisting of 27 items, was divided into five sections: (i) demographic data (seven questions), (ii) measure of HL (six questions), (iii) attitude to believe and share food-related fake news (four questions), (iv) impact of social media and influencers in consumer’s food choices (six questions), (v) consumer’s awareness in discriminating fake news from verified news (four questions). A pilot study was conducted on a sample of 300 individuals (not included in the final analysis), selected to represent a range of age and education levels. The aim was to assess clarity, language comprehension and response option suitability. Based on feedback, we simplified the wording of some items—particularly those on fake news—added practical examples (eg, ‘gluten-free diet without coeliac disease’), and revised certain response options to improve clarity and balance. The structure of the questionnaire (see online supplemental materials 1) includes validated scales and items chosen ad hoc for the context under study:
- Demographic, socioeconomic and health context variables : geographic area, gender, age, educational level, occupation, financial status and self-rated health (SRH) were assessed. Specifically, age in years was calculated from the year of birth and divided into three age groups (18–39, 40–65, >65 years), the level of education of each participant was defined as the highest educational qualification attained (bachelor’s degree, high school diploma, middle school diploma). The financial situation was assessed by the item, “On a scale of 1 to 5, how do you rate your difficulty in making ends meet?” with 5 being the highest difficulty in making ends meet. Finally, the SRH was assessed as a valid measure of health status, which is widely used in general population health surveys. 18

- HL (HLS-EU-Q6) : the HLS-EU-Q6 is a shortened form of the original HLS-EU-Q47, developed by selecting six items. In this study, the Italian validation of the scale was used, 19 which records preference responses using a Likert-type scale (‘very easy’, ‘quite easy’, ‘quite difficult’, ‘very difficult’, ‘do not know’) and associates the responses with a final score that measures interaction, comprehension, information seeking, decision-making ability and critical thinking. 20 The same categories as the HLS-EU-Q47 score were considered to generate the HLS-EU-Q6 score. 21

- Attitude in believing and sharing fake news online : respondents’ self-reported experience of believing and sharing fake food news on their social profiles was assessed by four ad hoc items on a 5-point category scale from ‘every day’ (or ‘always’) to ‘never’. This model has previously been used in the Italian context in psychological studies of interpersonal influence. 11

- Impact of social media and influencers in consumer food choices : this behaviour was assessed by items specifically chosen to explore the impact of influencers and food bloggers on consumer purchasing power. The items recorded responses on a Likert scale from ‘never’ to ‘always’.

- Consumers’ awareness of distinguishing fake news from verified news : the final four items investigated the world of food fake news through concrete examples of popular fake news on the Web, in order to collect the general opinions of respondents, to assess the level of critical judgement and the ability to distinguish fake news from verified news, in agreement or disagreement with responses to the previous items.


Data analysis
Prior to analysis, data cleaning procedures were applied: incomplete questionnaires (with >25% missing responses) and logically inconsistent entries (eg, age <18 years) were excluded; duplicate records were removed; categorical variables were recoded as needed (eg, education into three levels, age into four groups). Missing data, which remained below 5% per variable, were handled using listwise deletion in regression models. Descriptive statistics (means, frequencies SD) were calculated for each item. For the HLS-EU-Q6 instrument, scale reliability was assessed using Cronbach’s alpha and McDonald’s omega, 22 both of which showed satisfactory internal consistency (α=0.81; ω=0.81). A composite HL-mean score was calculated for each participant by computing the arithmetic mean of their responses to the six HLS-EU-Q6 items, using a 5-point Likert scale (1=very difficult, 5=very easy). Participants with ≥2 ‘do not know’ responses were excluded from HL-mean analysis. The method used follows the recommendations of the European Health Literacy Consortium 21 and is consistent with procedures reported in literature. 19 HL levels were classified using standard thresholds proposed by Sørensen et al 23 : inadequate (≤2), problematic (>2 and ≤3) and adequate (>3). Associations between HL-mean and socio-demographic variables (eg, age, gender, education) were tested using Welch’s analysis of variance (ANOVA), suitable for heteroskedastic samples and non-normal distributions. Shapiro-Wilk tests confirmed deviations from normality, justifying the use of Welch’s robust procedure. Bonferroni correction was applied to all post hoc comparisons involving more than two categories to adjust for the risk of type I error. For all ANOVA tests, effect sizes (η²) were reported to indicate the magnitude of differences between groups. For categorical variables (eg, belief in food fake news, trust in influencers), χ 2 tests were used, and effect strength was discussed where relevant.
Binary logistic regression models were used to examine the association between socio-demographic predictors and the likelihood of sharing fake food news online. 24 Results were reported as ORs with 95% CIs. Multicollinearity was tested using variance inflation factors (VIFs), all below 2, confirming acceptable independence between predictors. Linearity of the logit for continuous variables (eg, HL-mean) was confirmed using the Box-Tidwell procedure and visual inspection of residual plots. All analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics V.29.
Prior to accessing the questionnaire, participants were presented with a detailed information sheet describing the study purpose, ethical safeguards, data privacy policy and the voluntary nature of participation. Only participants who confirmed having read this information and provided explicit informed consent by ticking a checkbox were granted access to the questionnaire.
Results
Sample characteristics
The sample consists of 2284 respondents, including 1338 women and 946 men aged 18–75 years. Of these, 36.2% belong to the 40–65 age group, 35.7% to the 18–39 group and 28.1% to the over-65 group. The distribution by geographic origin is balanced: 49.9% from the north/centre and 50.1% from the south/islands. Regarding education, 50.3% hold a high school diploma, 20.2% a university degree and 29.5% a middle school diploma. In terms of SRH, most respondents rated themselves 4 or above on a 10-point Likert scale. Detailed demographic characteristics are provided in table 1.

HLS-EU-Q6 analysis and association between health literacy and other context variables
The respondents’ level of HL was measured through the validated HLS-EU-Q6 instrument (figure 2). The scale reliability analysis, which assesses the internal consistency of the items, was satisfactory (Cronbach’s α=0.81 and McDonald’s ω=0.81). Overall, all items were found to be well correlated with each other; in particular, Pearson’s correlation is highest for the pairs of responses linking mental well-being issues to information reported in the media and to coping with stress and depression. The majority of respondents reported difficulty in handling most items, with the exception of item HL4, for which 38.5% of participants rated it as ‘quite easy’ or ‘very easy’. Notably, item HL5, addressing mental health management, was perceived as ‘quite difficult’ or ‘very difficult’ by 82.5% of respondents, while 61.6% reported challenges in assessing the reliability of health information from media sources (item HL6).

To explore differences in the mean HL score (HL-mean) across sociodemographic groups, Welch’s ANOVA was employed due to its robustness against heteroscedasticity and non-normality. Effect sizes were quantified using Eta-squared (η²), and Bonferroni corrections were applied to all post hoc comparisons to adjust for multiple testing. No significant differences emerged based on geographic origin (F(1, 2203)=1.72, p=0.19; η² = 0.01) or gender (F(1, 2282)=3.11, p=0.08; η²=0.05).
Conversely, age was significantly associated with HL-mean scores (F(2, 1418)=86.4, p<0.001; η²=0.14), with post hoc analyses revealing that participants aged over 65 years had a substantially lower mean score (M=1.76, SD=0.55) compared with those aged 40–65 years (M=2.58, SD=0.49) and 18–39 years (M=2.82, SD=0.45). Education level (figure 3) also significantly influenced HL (F(2, 1419)=74.2, p<0.001; η²=0.16), with university graduates exhibiting the highest mean HL score (M=2.83, SD=0.47), followed by high school graduates (M=2.56, SD=0.50) and those with only a middle school diploma (M=2.01, SD=0.53).

Fake news and related variables
A substantial portion of respondents reported encountering food-related fake news at least once a week (21.5%) or several times a month (52.7%). About 78% stated they ‘often’ or ‘sometimes’ believed such news, and 37.4% admitted to sharing it. A χ 2 test showed a strong inverse association between HL-mean and the likelihood of sharing fake news (χ 2 =1033, p<0.001).
A binary logistic regression was performed to identify socio-demographic predictors of sharing fake news. The model showed good fit, and multicollinearity checks confirmed acceptable independence (VIF <2). The analysis revealed that male respondents were significantly more likely to share misinformation (OR 2.11, 95% CI 1.53 to 2.64, p<0.001), as were individuals aged 40–65 years (OR 3.17, 95% CI 2.23 to 4.50, p<0.001) and especially those over 65 years (OR 19.36, 95% CI 12.47 to 30.06, p<0.001), compared with the youngest age group (18–39 years). Educational level was also a key factor: those with only a middle school diploma (OR 2.46, 95% CI 1.51 to 4.00, p<0.001) or a high school diploma (OR 2.45, 95% CI 1.60 to 3.73, p<0.001) had significantly higher odds of sharing misinformation than university graduates. Additionally, occupational status showed notable differences: compared with government employees, unemployed individuals (OR 7.01), housewives (OR 4.41) and workers (OR 2.04) were more likely to spread fake news online (table 2).

Exploring the topic of food fake news popular on the Web, it is noteworthy that 42.8% of respondents believe that, even without a gluten allergy or intolerance, it is healthier to follow a gluten-free diet; only 36.7% disagree, while 20.5% remain uncertain. To assess consistency in judgement, we cross-tabulated this belief with responses about sharing food-related fake news online (figure 4). Among those who reported never sharing nutritional fake news, 21.8% still believe in the gluten-free diet myth. Conversely, among those who admitted to having shared fake food news, 77.8% endorse this misconception. These results reveal that certain food-related myths may persist independently of general awareness or cautious behaviour online, indicating a form of cultural internalisation of misinformation that transcends individual intent or literacy.

Impact of social media and influencers on consumers’ food choices
Approximately 48.8% of respondents said they ‘often’ or ‘sometimes’ bought food or supplements due to social media advertising. Similarly, 49.6% admitted to following dietary advice from influencers or celebrities (figure 5). A χ 2 test revealed a strong inverse correlation between HL-mean and trust in influencer-promoted products (χ 2 =855, p<0.001), highlighting the protective role of HL against media influence.

Discussion
The results of this study suggest that HL may function as a mediator of socioeconomic, cultural, health and nutritional determinants. They support the original hypothesis that an individual’s HL level, strongly linked to both personal and social factors, can influence, in a multifactorial way, the tendency to believe and disseminate food-related fake news online. This aligns with prior findings indicating that less educated and socioeconomically disadvantaged individuals tend to exhibit inadequate HL, 20 and that lower HL is associated with poorer self-reported health status. 25 26 Our findings reinforce these associations, revealing that lower HL was significantly correlated with lower educational attainment, poorer SRH, older age, economic hardship and less stable or lower-paying employment. Conversely, individuals with more stable and better-paying jobs appeared somewhat less likely to fall for food misinformation online. However, it is important to note that the variables used reflect subjective perceptions of economic and health status, which may not always represent objective conditions. Interestingly, younger participants (aged 18–39 years) were less likely to believe in food-related fake news, potentially due to higher HL levels in this age group. This study adds a novel contribution to the field by proposing an integrated multifactorial model that links HL, health promotion and nutritional well-being with vulnerability to misinformation. Another significant finding is the increased reliance on social networks and influencers, especially among participants from disadvantaged health or socioeconomic backgrounds, who are perceived as trusted sources for dietary advice and food-related information. This trend is consistent with previous research, which emphasises the psychological and sociological mechanisms underpinning belief in fake news, 27 particularly the persuasive power of misinformation within cognitive processing. 28 The spread of food-related misinformation can lead to detrimental dietary behaviours, undermining efforts to promote healthy eating. Consequently, targeted interventions are needed, particularly for vulnerable demographic groups with lower HL and socioeconomic status. While this study focused on general HL, the role of digital HL warrants specific attention in light of the growing influence of online misinformation. Future research should examine whether distinct measurement tools are necessary to differentiate between digital and traditional HL, especially given the influence of social media platforms. Additionally, the long-term health consequences of following inaccurate dietary advice, particularly for individuals with chronic illnesses or specific dietary requirements, remain an urgent area for exploration. Cultural factors may also play a crucial role in shaping susceptibility to food misinformation; comparative cross-national research could shed light on how cultural norms influence HL and trust in online information. Moreover, the role of algorithm-driven content personalisation should be investigated, as such mechanisms can amplify exposure to misinformation and further deepen inequalities in access to reliable health information. These insights support the growing call for accessible, equitable strategies to promote food and HL across the population. 29 Education and training programmes that equip people with the knowledge and skills to critically evaluate nutrition-related content are essential. 12 Health and food literacy promotion, integrated into broader public health frameworks, can empower consumers to make informed food choices, mitigate the confusion caused by misinformation and rebuild trust among citizens, institutions and food system stakeholders.
Limitations of the study should be considered. The non-probabilistic sampling through pharmacies, although broadly inclusive, may limit the generalisability of the findings. Rural populations may be under-represented, and some participants may not have been seeking healthcare, introducing sample heterogeneity. The reliance on self-reported data also introduces the risk of recall or social desirability bias; however, anonymity and clear question design likely mitigated these effects.
While the questionnaire was administered online, pharmacists provided in-person support to individuals with limited digital literacy, helping reduce exclusion bias. The cross-sectional nature of the study limits causal inferences, and future longitudinal or interventional studies are needed to validate these findings. Moreover, no internationally standardised definition of food-related fake news currently exists, and while the items used were pretested for clarity, they were not formally validated.
Finally, future research should explore variables not covered in this study, such as the frequency and type of social media use, the role of specific health conditions (eg, obesity, anorexia, binge eating disorder) in susceptibility to nutrition-related misinformation and the specific impact of digital HL. Investigating content algorithms, personalised feeds and cross-cultural differences could deepen our understanding of the broader dynamics involved in the spread of food misinformation.
Conclusions
Several scientific studies highlight that HL is a dynamic and multifaceted construct, emerging from a complex interplay of cognitive abilities, life experiences, knowledge, opportunities and contextual factors. 30 A thorough analysis of these social, psychological, health, economic and cultural determinants is crucial to design effective collective initiatives and public advocacy campaigns aimed at enhancing HL and fostering clearer, more informed communication. In this context, our study confirms a strong correlation between the level of HL and the belief in and dissemination of nutritional fake news. A comprehensive understanding of how individual and social components influence HL can help elucidate the mechanisms behind the emergence and spread of misinformation, facilitating the development of targeted intervention strategies to curb its diffusion. This research underscores the importance of collaborative efforts among health professionals, educators and policymakers to promote the dissemination of accurate and reliable food and nutrition information. Educating consumers to make healthier dietary choices is essential to raise awareness of the challenges posed by online fake news. Moving forward, educational and public health initiatives should aim to improve general HL and prioritise the development of digital literacy skills, particularly related to nutrition information. Such interventions must be accessible, culturally sensitive and adaptable to different levels of socioeconomic status and digital access. Integrating these strategies into formal education systems and national communication campaigns could strengthen population resilience against misinformation, fostering more informed and health-promoting food choices. A multisectoral approach, including partnerships with digital platforms, will be essential to address the structural and behavioural drivers of food misinformation effectively. Through these efforts, food and HL have the potential to become powerful tools that enhance public health, reduce social and cultural disparities and promote healthy lifestyles, ultimately supporting the vital role of food in maintaining health and social well-being.
