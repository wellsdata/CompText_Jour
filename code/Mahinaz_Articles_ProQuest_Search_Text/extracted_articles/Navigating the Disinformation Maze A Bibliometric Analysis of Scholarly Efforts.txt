1. Introduction The problem of disinformation has become a major international challenge in an era marked by the extremely rapid spread of information in the online environment. Additionally, the occurrence of fake news has played an important role in manipulating the masses, creating propaganda, and changing mentalities based on false information. As has been observed, this type of news has an influence across various fields, producing negative effects both economically and psychologically.
There is an important difference between fake news and disinformation that must be addressed. Fake news, according to the UK Cambridge Dictionary, is a false narrative which could be used as news and distributed on the internet via social media platforms, with the purpose of influencing political views [1]. Disinformation focuses on misleading on purpose by manipulating stories based on a biased information, also known as propaganda [1]. Zimdars and McLeod [2] consider fake news to be intentional information that has been created in full in order to mislead and increase the emotions of readers, replicating mainstream news. Disinformation consists of false information that is intentionally promoted, with the aim of harming others [3]. In order to better understand the difference between disinformation and fake news, a Venn diagram has been created using Microsoft Visio, as presented in Figure 1.
Figure 1 thus highlights the differences between disinformation, misinformation, reality, fake news, and conspiracy theories. Misinformation’s definition is the distribution of false information without having an intention to emotionally damage people, while disinformation is the intention of sharing false information to influence public opinion and policies [4]. Malinformation is founded on partially real information that is promoted out of context in order to harm the public. Biased information appears at the intersection of reality and misinformation, while malinformation is at the intersection of reality and disinformation. At the intersection of disinformation and misinformation is fake news. At the intersection of misinformation, disinformation, and reality are conspiracy theories. Conspiracy theories focus on people’s secret activities and plans, with a refusal to accept existing evidence which denies the theory. Numerous conspiracy theories that are promoted are dangerous for society [4].
While the definition of disinformation is straightforward—the process of the voluntarily spreading misleading information which affects people in a direct or indirect manner [5]—the effects of disinformation can have a major impact on people’s lives.
At present, disinformation has affected various areas of our life [6]. As Wang et al. [7] identify in their research, there are four major areas closely related to the disinformation domain: the heterogeneity of misinformation in memory, the mechanisms of disinformation on social media, public health during the COVID-19 pandemic, and the use of big data technologies in the infodemic.
Through this bibliometric study, we aim to understand and gain a deeper insight into the current state of the disinformation that appears in the online environment, as well as to highlight the efforts being made to combat this phenomenon. This study discusses both the methodologies and the datasets that are being used, but also metadata from the papers included in this analysis. Considering the increasing number of papers published in the field and the growing interest in the fast development that artificial intelligence has had in recent years, we hope that the present study will be of interest to researchers in the disinformation field.
Considering that disinformation, misinformation, and fake news have reached a historical maximum, discussions regarding this field are also in the foreground. A major factor that has had a great impact in disseminating these types of information to the public has been social media platforms, as stated in Lewandowsky et al. [8] and Vosoughi et al. [9]. Another key factor in the spread of disinformation has been the advancement of artificial intelligence. In addition to the disastrous effects that it can bring to the economy, disinformation can also bring negative effects on a psychological level [4].
The spread of false information in political areas impedes individuals from creating their own opinions and affects democracies. Two of the most representative examples are the United States of America’s (USA) elections in 2016 and the United Kingdom (UK) Brexit referendum, which occurred in the same year, events that became key milestones for researchers who are becoming more and more interested in the disinformation and fake news domain [10]. Another period of exponential disinformation growth occurred during the COVID-19 pandemic, which led governments to adapt different policies, such as passing various laws to reduce infection rates, though these measures faced significant protests and opposition [10].
Various studies have been conducted in the field of disinformation throughout its history. For example, Belen et al. [11] argued that by sharing false or misleading news in the media, the most vulnerable individuals could easily be manipulated. The method that the authors developed learns the characteristics and weaknesses of individuals, but also points out the vulnerabilities that people have to deal with when they interact with fake news or misinformation. Chung and Kim [12] conducted two experiments and their results show that fact-checking information neutralizes social media’s impact and that without fact-checking, social media would have a greater impact, generating more intentions to share the information. Based on the scientific literature, it has been observed that some studies have focused on better delimitating the field of disinformation from other types of information [5,13] or on identifying types of disinformation [14], while other studies have considered context-specific situations in which disinformation has occurred, such as natural disasters [15], climate change [16,17], politics [18,19,20], or health issues [21,22,23]. The rise of social media and the disinformation associated with this type of media has been studied in the scientific literature, from the organization of social media disinformation campaigns [24] to issues related to combating disinformation in the social media age [25,26].
Researchers have also focused on identifying the best methods to mitigate the spread of disinformation. One of the best machine learning (ML) algorithms is the recurrent neural network (RNN). Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) and the Gated Recurrent Unit (GRU) are algorithms that are also relevant in disinformation’s detection [27,28,29]. During the COVID-19 pandemic, numerous pieces of disinformation were distributed. In order to combat this disinformation, bag-of-words or N-grams methods were developed, which are part of natural language processing [30]. Kumar and Geethakumari [31] explored the spread od disinformation, propaganda, and misinformation online and used multiple algoirthms that are able to categorize whether information is fake or not, such as the J48 learning method, RankSVM, Relevance, and PageRank. Soe [32,33] explored the PHEME-project, which detects whether information is the truth, disinformation, or misinformation. Galitsky [34] proposed an algorithm that detects disinformation, finding the source of text on the internet and comparing it with fake information using parse ticket technology.
The aim of our analysis is to synthesize the body of literature published in this field and to emphasize the most significant findings. In order to better understand the impact of researchers on the domain and how its interests have evolved in recent years, there is a focus on universities that have been releasing scientific articles, together with an investigation into the scientific production in this area, the collaboration network of the authors publishing, journals, and keywords. As Sierra et al. [35] explained in their paper, authors have a prominent role in work related to disinformation, which has a larger impact on their research career. The number of publications from universities demonstrates the complexity and the quality of this research. By employing a bibliometric approach, we seek to identify and extract the key elements that contribute to the evolution of disinformation, addressing the following scientific questions:
- SQ1: Which are the universities that have published the most scientific papers?

- SQ2: How has annual scientific production evolved?

- SQ3: Do any collaboration networks between authors exist?

- SQ4: Who are the authors with the most crucial impact, taking into account their number of publications?

- SQ5: Which are the most relevant journals related to misinformation?

- SQ6: Which are the most used keywords and those most used by authors?



In order to achieve the objectives described above, the first step is to extract the most relevant documents in the research area that will be investigated [7,36]. This process uses Biblioshiny, a library from the R programming language and a scientific tool used for bibliometric analysis, which offers a great variety of graphics and insights on the imported dataset, as Aria and Cuccurullo [37] noted in their article. Biblioshiny allows for the rapid extraction of information about authors, countries, and journals and visualizes the temporal evolution of the domain and its authors. Additionally, Biblioshiny offers a series of detailed visualizations such as thematic maps, co-occurrence networks, and three-field plot graphics [37]. The graphics in this study were generated using the Biblioshiny library and the selection of the figures align with other scientific papers related to the field of disinformation [38,39,40].
This paper is divided into multiple sections, as follows: Section 2 offers an overview of the methods used for the bibliometric analysis, presenting the main steps that have been applied. Section 3 presents the results of the research, explaining who the most relevant authors are, in which journals they publish, which countries they are from, how they collaborated, and which groups of words are most used. Section 4 focuses on limitations and a discussion, while Section 5 contains our concluding remarks.

3.1. Dataset Overview An exploratory analysis was conducted in this section to provide a comprehensive overview of the data. The field has developed rapidly, as indicated by its annual growth rate of 15.14%, showing a significant acceleration in recent years.
Table 2 includes an overview of the dataset. The analyzed period starts in 1980 and it ends in 2023, covering 711 sources and 1394 documents, with an average number of citations per document of 12.68 and an average of 3.4 years since these documents were published. In total, there 55,703 references were included.
Between 1980 and 2006, the publication rate in the field of disinformation was minimal, ranging from 0 to 5 papers per year, as depicted in Figure 2. For example, the first document on disinformation was published in 1980, followed by five papers in 1982, while 1985 saw no publications at all. Until 2014, when eight articles were published, 1982 remained the year with the highest publication rate, except for 2011, which also saw five papers published. However, beginning in 2018, there was a marked increase in publication activity, reaching a peak in 2023 with 429 papers published. Sandu et al. [55] conducted a similar analysis on misinformation, reporting comparable findings, with a positive trend in annual scientific output over the analyzed period.
Figure 3 presents the average yearly citation rate. The values between 1980 and 2003 were very small, averaging below one citation per year. In 2003, the average citation per year was 3.82, making this the first relevant year for the field of disinformation, which becaume very volatile in the next 20 years, having numerous spikes. The highest average per year was registered in 2018, at nine citations per year. Starting in 2020, the trend in the citation rate started to decrease, from 6 to 1.1 in 2023, but this trend might be explained by the increased number of papers which have been released recently. In 2018, at the peak of the citation rate, numerous papers related to the Brexit vote were published, describing the United Kingdom’s process of leaving the European Union and how its people were disinformed through the promotion of the fact that numerous immigrants would enter the country if they remained in EU, as Vasu et al. [56] explained.
Sommariva et al. [57] focused on the health domain, investigating the disinformation related to the Zika virus and grouping that information into three different categories, known also as rumors: false connections, which mislead people about the correlation between content and its title; fabricated content that contains only fake information, without any scientific evidence; and misleading content which promotes inaccurate stories. Out of the total 120 stories analyzed, 27 were considered rumors. The impact was significant, since the Zika virus appeared mainly in Brasil, in 2016, when the South American country organized the Olympics.
3.2. Sources Sources are analyzed in this section: the number of articles published, the number of keywords used, the most relevant sources, and their impact.
Table 3 summarizes the types of the documents included in this research. Most of the papers (1273 papers) are, as expected, in the “Article” category, while 65 papers are “Early Access Articles”, 50 papers are “Book Chapters”, 5 papers are “Proceedings Papers”, and 1 document is a “Book”. Table 4 presents the number of authors keywords and keywords plus used in all publications included in this research. There are 3118 authors keywords and only 1160 keywords plus. Garfield [58,59,60] explained that keywords plus can contain some of the content of a paper, presenting information at a higher depth and variety; they are used to extract research trends from various domains. The keywords plus are extracted from titles and articles, contain more terms than the authors keywords, and are recommended for use in bibliometric analyses. Keywords plus highlight the articles which might not have authors’ keywords and could include terms that are not included in the authors’ keywords [61].
Table 5 details information about the authors. The current database includes 3091 unique authors; 421 of them contributed to the creation of single-authored documents, and 467 papers in the database are single-authored papers. On average, there are 2.67 co-authors per article, and the international co-authorship rate is 20.16%.

The investigation of journals is a crucial step in bibliometric research, and the most important sources are presented in Figure 5 using Bradford’s Law, which focuses on the most cited journals, separating them from those with a smaller impact.
Bradford’s Law divides journals into three separate categories based on their total number of publications, with all three categories containing the same proportion of papers: one third. In the end, Bradford’s Law groups proportionally, with a ratio of 1:n:n 2 , as Yang et al. [62] explained. Meller et al. [63] explained the importance of Bradford’s Law, which is able to perform an online-based multi-sectional analysis while also providing a robust distribution of the journals into three different categories. Bookstein [64] demonstrated that the main use of Bradford’s Law is on the citations of journals, together with the number of articles appearing in journals, as is being tested in many domains. Profesional de la Informacion is ranked as the most important journal by Bradford’s Law, as it has, in total, 36 articles, followed by Social Media + Society with 35 papers and International Journal of Communication with 32 documents. Media and Communication published 26 papers related to the field of disinformation, Journalism Practice has published 19 papers, and New Media & Society has 16. Information Communication & Society , and Revista Icono 14-Revista Cientifica de Comunicacion Y Tecnologias each have 15 papers, while Political Communication and Revista Latina de Comunicacion Social each have 13 documents. All 10 journals that have been described above are in Zone 1, which is the most important [64].
Figure 6 illustrates the local impact of various academic journals within the disinformation field, measured by their H-index. This value reflects not only the number of published articles, but also their impact on the scientific world.
As can be observed, Social Media + Society leads, with an H-index of 13, followed by Information , Communication & Society , which has an H-index of 11. The top positions occupied by these two sources indicate their strong impact on the field. Furthermore, journals like Digital Journalism and New Media & Society also show notable influences, with an H-index of 8. Other journals such as Political Communication and the International Journal of Communication display a moderate impact, while sources like Journalism and Journalism Practice have lower H-indexes, indicating a more specialized influence. When considering all the journals included in this list, it can be observed that most of them specialize in communication and media studies, highlighting once more the connection between disinformation and the communications channels through which disinformation takes place.

3.6.2. Top 10 Most Cited Papers—Review In the first article, Bennett and Livingston [91] bring to light the problem of fake news in the field of politics, which is on the rise. The authors present the problem of political websites that try to imitate the format of news journals. Usually, these actions are associated with far-right parties, which try to manipulate supporters to turn against other political ideologies. As a result, the trust of many citizens is steadily decreasing, and the public is trying to seek alternative sources of information. These alternative sources of information play a crucial role in misinforming and manipulating voters, as they are predominantly associated with nationalist movements but could also represent foreign interventions. They are designed to destabilize governments and create propaganda, thus undermining the government’s authority. Among the most popular examples mentioned in the article are the UK’s Brexit from the European Union and the election of US President Donald Trump in 2016.
The study published by Zhou [92] discuss the urgent need for the detection of fake news, emphasizing the negative effects that fake news can bring to various fields, such as justice and politics. It analyzes the detection of fake articles from four different perspectives: the level of false information, writing style, propagation patterns, and the credibility of the sources. Another topic touched on in this article is the expansion of online news due to social networks and social media. These types of online sites have much faster dissemination rates and lower dissemination costs than traditional media. Fake news also has a major effect on stock markets and the global economy. One example of fake news that has influenced the stock market, causing losses of more than USD 130 billion involves Barack Obama, the 44th president of the United States, who was alleged to have died in an explosion. Another example would be the story of some young adults in Macedonia who made around USD 60.000 each, according to NBC, due to the creation of various fake news stories during the US presidential election.
Zubiaga et al. [93] discuss the impact of social media platforms on spreading false or misleading information. At present, news is spreading much more frequently online, and media platforms are the most common platform for spreading news. At the same time, we can use these platforms to collect data and validate it using natural language processing techniques. With this paper, the authors aimed to create a rumor classification system that consists of four components: rumor detection, rumor tracking, rumor stance classification, and rumor veracity classification. Also, another very important aspect in this paper is the division of rumors into two classes, namely, long-lived rumors and short-lived rumors. In the case of short-lived rumors, they are usually breaking news, and their sources are not verified. In contrast, long-lived rumors occur over long periods of time.
The study conducted by Scheufele and Krause [94] is specific to misinformation occurring at the U.S. level and encompasses several domains of interest, from politics to science-related fields. Their study highlighted that the detection of misinformation depends on everyone’s motivation to try and detect fake news. However, other external groups and societal factors could increase the chances of detecting it.
Databases are the main topic that Shu et al. [95] investigated. Although there are different shortcomings at the computational level, they consider one of the main problems that exists at the level of false content detection in the online environment to be the limited databases on which models are trained. At this level, two problems of major interest have been observed; datasets are often sparse and non-contiguous. Moreover, the datasets often lack a multitude of critical features, such as social context and various spatial–temporal information. Thus, the researchers of this paper created a data warehouse for training models called FakeNewsNet. This repository contains two datasets with social context and spatial–temporal information. The article describes this dataset, the benefits it brings to the scientific world, and potential applications that could utilize the newly created database.
The article published by Jones-Jang [96] is concerned with the media literacy of the population. The increasing number of fake articles that we find online encourages us to find various solutions in the fight against deepfakes. Thus, in the article they study whether a higher degree of public literacy will also help in recognizing fake news. After conducting various studies, it was concluded that a higher degree of information literacy (but not other literacies) would have a positive impact on the identification of fake news. Information literacy is defined as the ability of an individual to search for, critically analyze, and filter available information to avoid misinformation and other fake news.
Su et al. [90] studied how the communication of information during a major crisis happening across a whole society can cause problems for the mental health of individuals. In particular, they analyzed the COVID-19 pandemic and its global effects, which started in 2019. The authors argue that during the pandemic, the plethora of news that was published online about high death rates and exponentially increasing infection cases led to the imminent rise of the risk of mental health problems. Thus, due to the premature dissemination of information through media channels and clickbait news, major crises in society may also impact the mindset of all individuals. Thus, the need for research on improving communication between media channels and the public in moments of major risk for society is a must. A collaborative environment between media representatives is also desired so that information can be person-centered. In this way, crisis moments can be resolved as quickly as possible, and the wellbeing of individuals will not be affected.
Freelon and Wells [97] introduced us again to the world of disinformation in politics. The article is divided into four sections of interest in order to have a better understanding of disinformation from different perspectives. Section 1 discusses the disinformation that occurs in political communication. Section 2 deals with a historical analysis of the issues in communication, with a focus around the new ones that have been discussed from 2016 to the present. The third point of the list is the classification of the findings in two categories: studies on disinformation’s content and studies of disinformation’s reception. Section 4 presents suggestions for the scientific world for further research on disinformation.
Supran and Oreskes [98] study the case of the ExxonMobil Corporation, which is alleged to have misled society about global climate changes. In their analysis, they studied more than 187 communications, including advertorials published by The New York Times. Advertorials are paid advertisements that are written and published in an editorial style. The study concluded that 80 of ExxonMobil’s internal documents acknowledged that climate change is due to human intervention. In contrast, of the advertorials produced by The New York Times, only 12% recognize this, casting doubt on the subject. Thus, by looking at this case study it is possible to analyze the phenomenon of deception at the macro level and how it affects society.
Vaccari and Chadwick [72] explore the insecurity generated among the public by false online stories. In a study carried out in the United Kingdom, it was observed that among a large sample of the population, users tend to be insecure and their trust of social networks tends to be low. Political deepfakes play an important role in changing the public opinion of society and will become more and more crucial, as companies focus on creating AI software tools which generate synthetic representations of humans. Journalists must constantly check the authenticity of their information and inform the public about the potential danger. At the same time, AI can be used for education, demonstrating the potential of political deepfakes, and increasing trust in news or social media, as the authors presented. This is exemplified by two videos made by artificial intelligence. One of these videos depicts Barack Obama insulting his political rival Donald Trump, while the other depicts the kidnapping of a child in India.

The most cited paper was published by Humprecht et al. [99] and it focuses on online disinformation, which is becoming more and more dangerous for democracies. The initial assumption of the authors was that some countries are more resilient than others to online disinformation. A framework was used to compare different countries’ conditions, and then the importance of quantifiable indicators was measured in 18 Western countries and the results grouped the countries into two different categories based on online disinformation. The first group had a high resilience, and it included Northern European countries, while low resilience was found in Southern European countries and the USA. In the final part of their analysis, a comparative review of political speeches was conducted. In total, there are three authors from Switzerland and Belgium, who have a total of 158 citations, with a total citations per year of 31.60 and a normalized total citation of 5.31.
Farkas et al. [100] suggest that fake information has become more and more relevant, especially in politics, offering examples of Donald Trump when he was the president of the USA. They used data from social media between November 2016 and March 2017, as well as articles from various newspapers and magazines such as The Washington Post , The New York Times , The Wall Street Journal , and many others. The aim of the research was to point out the political struggles that fake information is promoting and what the consequences are. In total, two authors from Sweden contributed to the paper, which obtained a total of 152 citations, with an average yearly citation of 21.71 and a normalized total citation of 2.24.
Tandoc et al. [101] investigated the behavior of social media users responding to disinformation, analyzing 2501 responses from Singapore. The majority of the social media users in Singapore ignore social media disinformation and corrections appear only when the problem is relevant to them or their close friends. Other participants confirmed that they respond to disinformation only when they are interested in the topic, because they might not be emotionally connected to the news or they lack motivation. Social media platforms have started to implement fact-checkers in order to reduce the amount of disinformation on their sites. In total, three authors from Singapore contributed, with a total citation of 141, an average annual citation of 28.20, and a normalized total citation of 4.74.
Jaiswal et al. [102] detailed the problems that public health systems had during the COVID-19 pandemic. The lack of consistency, the fear, uncertainty, social media pressure, prevention methods, and treatments that were promoted increased people’s frustration level. Disinformation occurred mainly when the COVID-19 vaccine appeared. One of the main problems was the promotion of disinformation by the federal government, and this inequality created false opinions. Conspiracy theories, which constantly try to explain the cause of an event and are strongly correlated with disinformation, make it more and more difficult to change people’s opinions, even if evidence is presented, since sometimes experts also promote conspiracies. Upon analysis, three authors from the USA participated, obtaining a total of 140 citations, with an average yearly citation of 28 and a normalized total citation of 4.71.
Bruns et al. [103] explored the dynamics of the COVID-19 pandemic’s outbreak and its correlation with 5G technology in Wuhan and all around the world by performing a time series analysis combined with quantitative and qualitative investigations. The scope of the research included presenting rumors from Facebook groups. The analysis of disinformation could offer a perspective on new possibilities for mitigating the distribution of false information. The data that were used were collected from a Facebook platform called CrowdTangle. Multiple analyses had to be performed in order to create a pattern and identify specific processes that could be used to counteract the disinformation. In total, three authors from Australia contributed to the paper, which had a total of 137 citations, with an average yearly citation of 27.40 and a normalized total citation of 4.61.
Chadwick et al. [104] compared the tabloid news and disinformation and misinformation behaviors from social media platforms by taking into consideration four different datasets—17,989 web domains, 641 articles, 1,525,748 shared news, and 1313 Twitter users that responded to a survey—related to the 2017 UK election campaign. Using a novel method to investigate the data, the results obtained provided a perspective about tabloid news that is shared on social media which reflects a dysfunctional democracy, combined with disinformation and misinformation, this has a serious impact on society.
Keller et al. [105] analyzed the South Korean National Information Services (NIS) disinformation campaign that occurred during the 2012 presidential election, collecting information from Twitter. Using a methodology that had been defined during their research, the results confirmed that the NIS accounts had a limited impact on social media. The authors also developed a method that detects disinformation campaigns, identifying over 1008 Twitter accounts that were run by NIS agents. Thanks to the principal–agent framework, the accuracy of identifying disinformation campaigns increased. In total, four authors from Hong Kong, the UK, Germany, and the USA worked on the research, which obtained a total of 127 citations, with an annual citation of 25.40 and a normalized total citation of 4.27.
Ross and Rivers [106] focused on the 2016 US presidential elections, and especially on Donald Trump’s Twitter usage, where a large amount of disinformation and misinformation was spread. Using the Fire Ant software tool AMD Political Twitter Discourse Corpus, the data that were collected between 9 November 2016 and 7 August 2017 confirmed that numerous pieces of disinformation and misinformation were distributed via social media platforms. The numerous times Donald Trump accuses media platforms of things in his tweets force the viewer to observe his tweets from a different perspective. In total, there were two authors from Australia that contributed to the paper, which has a total of 111 citations, with an average citations per year of 15.86 and a normalized total citation of 1.77.
Basch et al. explored the disinformation and misinformation that was spread through TikTok during the COVID-19 pandemic about vaccinations. The scope of the analysis was analyzing the content that has been published on TikTok related to COVID-19 vaccines, with the videos filtered based on hashtags such as #covidvaccine. Over 100 videos which had 35 million views were included; only 36 of them promoted the vaccine, while 38 of them discouraged people from getting the vaccination. A total of 22 videos out of 38 shared, wrongly, that a vaccine was already available, even if the scientists did not start the research, which generally affected young people. There were five authors that contributed to the research, all from the USA, leading to a total of 98 citations, with a total citations per year of 24.50 and a normalized total citation of 7.16.
Zimmerman and Kohring [107] detailed the main factors that sustain disinformation, even if the problem did not raise serious concerns. A panel survey that focused on the 2017 German parliamentary election was performed, collecting a total of 989 responses. The results show that disinformation has a significant impact, driving the voters from main governing parties such as CDU or CSU to right-wing populists’ parties such as AfD. The lack of trust in politics and news means a higher trust in online disinformation. There were two authors from Germany that worked on the research, which obtained a total of 96 citations, with an average citations per year of 19.20 and a normalized total citation of 3.23.

4. Discussion and Limitations This bibliometric research offered a comprehensive evaluation of the disinformation domain, with its exploration starting with 1980, when the first paper was published, which had a very small impact. However, thanks to the evolution of technology, this area has evolved and grown exponentially in the last few years of the analysis, with a maximum of 429 papers being published in 2023. Over the 42 years that have been investigated, a positive trend was observed, which highlights the increasing interest of researchers, thanks to technological evolution.
Disinformation has a significant impact on society, especially in politics, and in order to reduce its spread, it is important that the government adopt laws which block the distribution of fake information, together with achieving better control over the information that is presented in the news. Pherson et al. [110] detailed what measures should be adopted in order to minimze disinformation: create a safe space in the cloud where information should be validated by real sources, adopt tools that combat online disinformation such as bot and spam detection—Botometer, FakerFact, and many others.
The domain of disinformation became more and more interesting to researchers. The most relevant country in terms of the number of papers it has published is the USA, which has published 313 papers which have 6758 citations. The impact of the USA is accentuated by the four universities which are in the top 10 most important affiliations: the University of Texas Austin, University of Washington, Harvard University, and the University of California System, which have together published 164 articles.
Journals play a crucial role in the evolution of a domain, and the most important ones in the disinformation domain are Profesional de la Informacion , Social Media + Society , International Journal of Communication , Media and Communication , Journalism Practice , New Media & Society , Information Communication & Society , Revista Icono 14-Revista Cientifica de Comunicacio , Political Communication , and Revista Latina de Comunicacion Social .
In terms of limitations, the most significant limitation is the database that has been chosen for extracting papers, as we have taken into consideration only papers indexed in the WoS database. Singh et al. [111] explained the differences between the Scopus and WoS databases, and the major difference is the coverability level, which has improved massively since 1990 for the WoS, which has the most cases papers written in English. The WoS had indexed around 13,610 journals with over 13 million articles as of June 2020, offering a higher impact for WoS publications. Scopus indexed a larger variety of journals, containing, in total, approximately 40,385 journals with over 18 million publications as of June 2020, but its impact is much lower compared to that of the WoS. The decision to exclude Scopus documents from this research could have altered the results but has provided interested readers with more papers with a well-respected database within the research domain.
Another important limitation that might have altered the results of this research is related to the process of selecting which articles are included in the database. In our case, the papers marked as “Article” by the WoS database were included in the analysis, which also included conference and journal publications. The filters that were used to extract the database limited the number of publications since specific words were included as filters. A restriction was added to the languages used; keeping only English publications and excluding the year 2024 are two other limitations that reduced the size of the database and altered the results.
5. Conclusions The purpose of this paper was to offer a comprehensive analysis of the disinformation domain in order to understand the evolution and potential development of the area. Using a bibliometric approach, this research offered a clear perspective on the main authors, the main topics of disinformation, what the collaboration network looks like, the most important journals, and the most relevant countries. Based on the findings discovered during this research, we can highlight the following results:
- SQ1 response: Based on the number of publications, most of the papers were published by universities in the USA, such as the University of Texas Austin , University of Washington , Harvard University , and the University of California System, with a total of 164 papers published by these institiutions. There are also important universities from the Netherlands ( University of Amsterdam) , the UK ( University of Oxford , University of London) , Spain ( University of Navarra , Complutense University of Madrid), and China ( Nanyang Technological University) .

- SQ2 response: The first paper was published in 1980 and had a very small impact, but during the timespan of this research, the trend was positive, and in the last few years the domain has grown rapidly, especially since 2018. The largest number of papers were published in 2023, at more than 429 documents.

- SQ3 response: The collaboration network between authors in the disinformation domain is complex and in the majority of cases it involves authors from same countries, based on the SCP values seen. The SCP value for the top 10 most important countries ranges between 63.1% and 90.2%, with the UK having the smallest value for SCPs and Spain having the highest value for SCPs. Regarding the collaboration between countries, the most fruitful collaboration was between the USA and UK, who have published 20 articles together. Another important collaboration between countries is that of the UK–Germany, with 15 documents published, and the USA–China with 14 articles, the USA–Australia with 12 articles, the USA–Germany with 12 articles, the UK–Australia with 11 articles, the USA–Canada with 10 articles, the UK–Canada with 9 articles, the UK–Netherlands with 9 articles, and the UK–South Africa with 8 articles.

- SQ4 response: Regarding authors, the most relevant author in the disinformation domain, based on their number of publications, is Hameleers M. , who has 29 papers published. The second author, Carley KM. , has only published 9 documents, which shows how important Hameleers M. is in the analyzed area. Other important authors include Farkas J. , Bastos M. , Chadwick A. , Herrero-Diz P. , Humprecht E. , Hurcombe E. , Ahmed S. , and Buchanan T.

- SQ5 response: The most important journals that publish scientific papers on the disinformation domain are Profesional de la Informacion (36 papers) , Social Media + Society (35 papers) , International Journal of Communication (32 papers) , Media and Communication (26 papers) , Journalism Practice (19 papers) , New Media & Society (16 papers) , Information Communication & Society (15 papers) , and Revista Icono 14-Revista Cientifica de Comunicacio (15 papers) .

- SQ6 response: Keywords are a crucial part of a bibliometric approach since they provide a clear perspective as to what each author is focusing on. The most used keywords plus in the disinformation domain are fake news (279 appearances) , social media (235 appearances) , communication (103 appearances) , information (96 appearances) , misinformation (85 appearances) , disinformation (45 appearances) , impact (44 appearances) , politics (41 appearances) , trust (41 appearances) , and science (37 appearances) . Regarding the keywords used by authors, the most relevant words are disinformation (991 occurrences) , fake news (339 occurrences) , social media (335 occurrences) , misinformation (315 occurrences) , COVID-19 (160 occurrences) , Twitter (79 occurrences) , fact-checking (73 occurrences) , journalism (57 occurrences) , infodemic (51 occurrences) , and propaganda (49 occurrences). These terms represent the disinformation domain, the platforms that are helping to share information rapidly, the impact on politics, and the challenges faced by journalists in verifying information.



These results offer a complete overview of the disinformation domain, how it evolved, and what the consequnces are if fake information continues to be distributed. Several examples of methods that mitigates disinformation have been presented during this research. Providing the most important universities, the collaboration networks between authors, the most relevant authors and journals, the yearly number of papers that have been published, and the main keywords that authors have used, together with an investigation into the collaboration between countries, the number of SCPs and MCPs, and the top 10 most cited documents that present the theorical and practical aspects of this field of research creates a foundation for researchers and governments to start to take into consideration the dangers of disinformation, promoting methods that mitigate the spread of fake information.
Future research could include more documents from other databases, analyzing non-English articles and papers that are not marked as “Article” in the WoS.
Last but not least, an evaluation of the impact of technology on the disinformation domain and the policies that should be adopted could represent other future approaches.
