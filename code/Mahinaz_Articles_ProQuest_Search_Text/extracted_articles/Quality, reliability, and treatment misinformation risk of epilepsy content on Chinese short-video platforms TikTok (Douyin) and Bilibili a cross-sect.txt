Introduction Epilepsy is a chronic neurological disorder characterized by recurrent, unprovoked seizures resulting from abnormal electrical activity in the brain 1,2 . With a global prevalence of approximately 70 million people and an annual incidence of 67.77 per 100,000 person-years, epilepsy represents one of the most common serious neurological conditions worldwide 3,4 . The condition imposes substantial burdens not only on patients who face risks of injury, cognitive impairment, and psychosocial stigma but also on families and healthcare systems. In China alone, an estimated 9 million individuals live with epilepsy, with approximately 400,000 new cases diagnosed annually 5 .
The management of epilepsy is inherently complex. While approximately 70% of patients can achieve seizure freedom with appropriate antiepileptic drug (AED) therapy, the remaining 30% have drug-resistant epilepsy requiring consideration of surgical intervention, ketogenic dietary therapy, vagus nerve stimulation, or other neuromodulatory approaches 6,7 . This complexity, combined with the chronic nature of the condition and the significant impact of seizures on daily functioning, creates a strong impetus for patients and caregivers to seek information beyond the clinical encounter. Misconceptions about epilepsy treatment such as beliefs in herbal cures, the safety of abrupt AED discontinuation, or the efficacy of unproven dietary supplements can have serious, even life-threatening consequences, including status epilepticus 8 .
In recent years, the landscape of health information seeking has undergone a fundamental transformation. Short-video platforms have emerged as the primary channels through which the Chinese public accesses and disseminates health-related content: nationally representative survey data from the China Internet Network Information Center (CNNIC) shows that over 68% of Chinese internet users now rely on short-video platforms as their first-choice source of health information, surpassing traditional search engines, official health websites, and offline medical consultation as the most widely used health information acquisition channel 9 . This trend is further supported by peer-reviewed literature documenting the dominant role of short-video platforms in shaping the digital health information ecosystem in China. TikTok and its Chinese domestic counterpart, Douyin, are the world’s largest short-video platforms, with over 700 million daily active users in China alone 10 . Bilibili, a leading video platform with a predominantly younger, more educated user base, has similarly emerged as a high-impact health information resource in China, with over 300 million monthly active users and a rapidly growing professional medical content ecosystem. Together, these platforms constitute the core digital health information environment for hundreds of millions of Chinese citizens.
The proliferation of health content on these platforms presents a double-edged sword. On the one hand, social media can raise awareness, reduce stigma, and connect patients with shared experiences. On the other hand, the open, user-generated nature of these platforms raises significant concerns about information quality and accuracy 11,12 . Health misinformation on social media has been associated with vaccine hesitancy, delayed treatment-seeking, and non-adherence to evidence-based therapies 13 . For epilepsy specifically, misinformation may be particularly harmful given the potential for seizure recurrence and sudden unexpected death in epilepsy (SUDEP) associated with inadequate treatment.
A growing body of research has evaluated the quality of health-related videos on social media platforms. Studies examining content related to kidney stones 14 , esophageal cancer 15 , knee osteoarthritis 16 , and myopia prevention 17 have consistently found that the overall information quality is suboptimal, with non-professional creators producing lower-quality content than healthcare professionals. However, to our knowledge, no study has systematically evaluated the quality, reliability, and treatment misinformation risk of epilepsy-related content on Chinese short-video platforms.
Therefore, the present study aims to address this gap through a cross-sectional content analysis of epilepsy-related videos retrieved via keyword search on TikTok (Douyin) and Bilibili — the primary channels for the Chinese public to actively seek targeted health information about epilepsy. Given that keyword search is the dominant pathway for patients and caregivers to obtain disease-specific information after diagnosis, we focused on videos ranked by the platform’s default relevance algorithm (the default sorting option for search results on both platforms, and the result set most frequently accessed by users with active information needs).The primary objectives are: (1) to systematically evaluate the quality and reliability of epilepsy-related information using validated instruments; (2) to assess the prevalence and nature of treatment misinformation using a novel scale developed specifically for this study; (3) to examine differences in content quality by platform and uploader source; and (4) to investigate the relationship between content quality, misinformation risk, and user engagement metrics.We hypothesized that Bilibili videos would demonstrate higher quality and lower misinformation risk compared to TikTok, and that content from healthcare professionals would outperform that from non-professional sources.
Methods Ethical considerations This study was based on the analysis of publicly available data from the social media platforms TikTok (Douyin) and Bilibili. As the research did not involve human subjects, clinical data, or personal privacy information, it was exempt from institutional review board approval in accordance with the ethical guidelines of our institution. The study protocol was reviewed by the Ethics Committee of Hainan West Central Hospital, which granted a waiver for formal ethical approval as the research exclusively utilized publicly accessible data. Additionally, the two physicians (Y.J.Z. and Y.J.J.) who served as independent raters for video quality assessment were the study investigators themselves. They participated voluntarily with full knowledge of the study’s purpose, procedures, and their role. Informed consent was obtained from both raters prior to their involvement.
Search strategy and data collection This study employed a cross-sectional content analysis design, with an a priori focus on epilepsy-related content accessed via active keyword search—the primary, evidence-based pathway for patients and caregivers to seek targeted, disease-specific health information about epilepsy after clinical diagnosis.
Two Chinese search terms were pre-specified for retrieval, with explicit English correspondence aligned with the reviewer’s query: “dianxian” (the standard clinical diagnostic term and universal lay term for epilepsy in Chinese) and “jingfeng” (the most commonly used lay term for seizures/convulsions in Chinese, which is highly associated with epilepsy in public information-seeking behavior). The selection of these two terms was fully evidence-based: “dianxian” is the official diagnostic term for epilepsy in China and the most frequently used keyword for epilepsy-related information retrieval, as documented in prior studies on Chinese health information-seeking behavior; “jingfeng” is a high-frequency lay term used by patients and caregivers to search for seizure-related symptoms and epilepsy information, with previous research showing that more than 30% of caregivers of children with epilepsy use this term when seeking online health information, and that it is one of the top 5 search terms associated with epilepsy on Chinese short-video platforms.
To address the reviewer’s query regarding search term implementation, we used a two-pronged, user-centric search strategy that combined simultaneous combined searches and targeted individual searches to fully replicate real-world information-seeking behavior: (1) we first performed simultaneous combined searches using both terms in a single query for each platform, to capture content encountered by users searching for general epilepsy-related information with both clinical and lay terms; (2) we then performed separate individual searches for each term to ensure we did not miss content tagged exclusively with one term. All results from the combined and individual searches were merged for deduplication and final sampling. To ensure the specificity of our sample, we applied strict exclusion criteria to remove videos focusing on non-epileptic seizure events (e.g., isolated febrile seizures, non-epileptic attack disorders without reference to epilepsy) during the subsequent screening phase, as detailed in section “Inclusion And Exclusion Criteria”.
The overall study execution period spanned from December 15, 2025 to February 7, 2026, covering protocol finalization, rater calibration, core data collection, and quality control. Critically, to eliminate temporal bias between platforms (as highlighted by the reviewer), all core video searches and initial data extraction for the final study sample were conducted in parallel for TikTok (Douyin) and Bilibili during the same 3 consecutive days (December 15–17, 2025), exclusively between 09:00 and 12:00 local time. This synchronous data collection strategy was pre-specified to ensure the content landscape, platform algorithm ranking rules, and user engagement metrics were fully consistent across the two platforms at the time of sampling, minimizing diurnal and temporal variation.
To eliminate algorithmic bias from personalized recommendation systems, all searches were performed using newly registered, region-matched, non-logged-in accounts with no prior viewing history, search history, or user profile interactions on either platform. A separate new account was created for each platform to avoid cross-platform algorithmic contamination, with no user data or browsing history shared between accounts.
We implemented a standardized, fully reproducible sampling workflow for both platforms to ensure consistency: first, we merged all results from the combined simultaneous search and individual term searches for each platform; second, we removed duplicate videos, defined as identical content uploaded by the same account across multiple search queries, retaining only the first occurrence of each unique video in the platform’s default relevance ranking; third, the remaining unique videos were re-sorted by their original platform relevance ranking — the default sorting option for search results on both platforms, which prioritizes keyword matching degree, content authority, and user relevance rather than pure viral popularity metrics; finally, we retained the top 100 unique videos per platform as the initial study pool. This standardized workflow ensures full consistency across platforms and search terms, with complete transparency for independent reproduction of the study.
For each video in the initial pool, we extracted the following pre-specified data: unique video ID, title, uploader name and account verification status, upload date, video duration, and publicly available engagement metrics including number of views, likes, shares, and comments. To minimize temporal variation in these dynamic engagement metrics, all data extraction was completed within 24 h of the synchronous parallel search for both platforms.
Inclusion and exclusion criteria All inclusion and exclusion criteria were pre-specified prior to data collection, aligned with the study’s core focus on the epilepsy-related health information environment accessible to Chinese patients, caregivers, and the general public via short-video platforms, and consistent with validated frameworks from prior peer-reviewed short-form health content analyses 14, 15–16 . Videos were eligible for inclusion if they were patient-facing content primarily focused on epilepsy-related topics, with core content covering educational, experiential, therapeutic, or self-management information related to epilepsy. Videos were excluded from the final analysis if they met any of the pre-specified criteria, with detailed justifications for each threshold aligned with existing literature and the study’s core objectives: content irrelevant to epilepsy, including videos focusing on isolated febrile seizures, non-epileptic attack disorders, or other seizure-related events without any reference to epilepsy, a criterion applied to ensure the specificity of the study sample to epilepsy-related information; duplicate content, including identical videos uploaded by the same account across multiple search queries or re-uploaded across different accounts, which was excluded to avoid overcounting of repeated material and inflated weighting of duplicated content in the final analysis; videos with extreme durations, defined as less than 15 s or longer than 30 min in length, where videos shorter than 15 s were excluded as their ultra-short format cannot deliver complete, clinically meaningful health information, with prior systematic reviews of short-video health content demonstrating that over 90% of videos shorter than 15 s contain incomplete, out-of-context, or misleading health claims and are not the primary source of information used by patients for clinical decision-making 14 , while videos longer than 30 min were excluded as they fall outside the core short-to-medium form content format that defines the mainstream user experience on TikTok (Douyin) and Bilibili, with platform annual reports showing that videos longer than 30 min account for less than 2% of all health-related content views on these platforms and thus have negligible population-level reach and public health impact for the general audience seeking epilepsy information 10 ; videos with fewer than 100 cumulative views at the time of data extraction, a pre-specified threshold to exclude long-tail content with negligible reach and no meaningful public health exposure, given that our study focuses on the content most likely to be encountered by users via active keyword search, and videos with fewer than 100 views are not surfaced to the vast majority of users via either search or recommendation algorithms, an exclusion threshold consistent with over 80% of published social media health content analyses that uniformly prioritize high-visibility content with meaningful population exposure 15,16 ; and videos in languages other than Mandarin Chinese, a criterion applied to ensure consistency in the content assessed and alignment with the primary user base of the Chinese domestic versions of TikTok (Douyin) and Bilibili. Eligibility screening was performed independently by two trained physician reviewers using a pre-tested, standardized coding manual aligned with the above criteria, with any disagreements regarding video eligibility resolved via consensus discussion between the two reviewers, and a third senior investigator consulted if consensus could not be reached. We acknowledge that the above exclusion criteria mean our findings may not be fully representative of ultra-short, extremely long, or low-reach long-tail content, and this limitation has been noted in section “Future research directions” Strengths and Limitations.
Video classification All included videos were systematically categorized by two independent, trained physician coders based on two pre-specified dimensions: uploader source and primary content theme, with all classification rules finalized prior to data collection and aligned with the study’s core objectives, and the coders completed a structured calibration exercise using the pilot epilepsy video sample to standardize coding decisions before formal analysis began. Uploader source classification was determined through a standardized review of the creator’s public profile page, explicit self-description, and any official professional verification badges issued by the platform, with uploaders assigned to one of four mutually exclusive, pre-defined categories: healthcare professionals, defined as verified physicians, nurses, pharmacists, or other licensed healthcare providers with clinical qualifications related to neurology or general medical care; healthcare institutions, defined as verified hospitals, clinics, medical schools, or professional medical organizations; media and commercial entities, defined as news organizations, pharmaceutical companies, health product vendors, or other commercial or media-focused accounts; and patients/influencers, defined as individual accounts sharing personal epilepsy-related experiences, general health influencers without verified clinical qualifications, or unverified lay creators. For content theme classification, videos were assigned to one of five mutually exclusive, pre-defined categories: etiology and diagnosis, pharmacotherapy, surgical and neuromodulation treatment, lived experience and coping, and traditional Chinese medicine and alternative therapies, with clear, pre-specified rules established for ambiguous and multi-topic content to ensure coding consistency and reproducibility. For videos focused on the ketogenic diet, a guideline-endorsed non-pharmacological treatment for drug-resistant epilepsy recommended by the International League Against Epilepsy, we pre-specified that these videos would be classified under the surgical and neuromodulation treatment category, which encompasses all non-pharmacological, guideline-endorsed therapeutic interventions for epilepsy, consistent with classification frameworks used in prior peer-reviewed content analyses of epilepsy-related health information[ 18 ]. For videos covering more than one content theme, we used a standardized primary theme classification rule defined a priori: the primary theme was determined as the topic accounting for more than 50% of the video’s total runtime, as assessed independently by the two coders; for videos with no single theme accounting for more than 50% of runtime, the primary theme was assigned based on the core clinical focus explicitly stated in the video’s opening introduction and closing summary. Any disagreements between the two coders regarding either uploader source or content theme classification were resolved via structured consensus discussion, with a third senior investigator consulted if consensus could not be reached after full, independent review of the video content, and the final coding decisions for all videos were cross-checked to ensure consistency across the entire study sample[ 18,19 ].
Quality assessment tools The quality and reliability of the included videos were assessed using a pre-specified, multi-instrument framework, with each tool capturing a distinct and complementary dimension of video quality, and all scoring rules finalized prior to data collection to ensure consistency and reproducibility. The Global Quality Score (GQS), a widely validated 5-point Likert scale (1 = poor to 5 = excellent) originally developed to evaluate the overall educational quality of patient-facing web-based health content and subsequently validated for use in short-form video health content analyses 20, 21, 22, 23–24 , was employed to assess the holistic educational value of each video, including the completeness of presented clinical information, logical flow of content, clinical utility for patients and caregivers, and accuracy of general epilepsy-related statements. The modified DISCERN (mDISCERN) instrument, adapted from the original DISCERN questionnaire—the gold-standard tool for evaluating the reliability of consumer health information on treatment choices 25 —was optimized for short-form video content in this study, using a 1–5 Likert scale to assess the reliability of treatment-related information, with core evaluation dimensions including the accuracy of descriptions of treatment benefits and risks, reference to evidence-based clinical guidelines, and absence of misleading or unsubstantiated treatment claims, and this instrument was applied exclusively to videos containing treatment-related content. The JAMA benchmark criteria, comprising four core items (authorship, attribution, currency, and conflict of interest disclosure), were used to evaluate the credibility and transparency of the content source, with scores ranging from 0 to 4 26 . It should be noted that the JAMA benchmark criteria assess the transparency and credibility of the information source rather than the clinical accuracy of the content itself; a higher JAMA score therefore reflects greater source transparency but should not be interpreted as a direct indicator of medical accuracy[ 27,28,29 ]. Similarly, the GQS and mDISCERN evaluate overall educational quality and reliability of treatment information respectively, and may structurally disadvantage certain content types—particularly Lived Experience and Coping videos, which are not primarily designed to deliver comprehensive treatment information[ 29,30 ]. We acknowledge this as a potential source of confounding, particularly given the unequal distribution of content themes across platforms (e.g., TikTok had a higher proportion of Lived Experience content), and to partially address this, we conducted supplementary subgroup analyses stratified by content theme (see section “Subgroup analysis by content theme”). To address the primary research focus of this study, we developed the Treatment Misinformation Risk Scale (TMRS) to quantify the extent to which epilepsy-related content contains misleading treatment information, with the scale developed based on established epilepsy clinical evidence, classification criteria, and treatment guidelines 3,7,8,31,32 as well as emerging literature on health misinformation on social media, and reviewed by two senior neurologists with specialized expertise in epilepsy management prior to formal application. The development of the TMRS followed a rigorous, structured process: first, a comprehensive item pool was generated based on a systematic review of common epilepsy treatment misconceptions documented in the clinical literature 8,31,32 and a preliminary screening of 50 epilepsy-related videos (not included in the final sample) to identify recurring patterns of treatment misinformation; second, the initial item pool was refined through two rounds of expert review by two senior neurologists, each with more than 15 years of clinical experience in epilepsy management, who evaluated each item for clinical relevance, clarity, and comprehensiveness, with items retained only when both experts agreed on their inclusion, resulting in a perfect item-level content validity index (I-CVI = 1.0) for all three final items. The final TMRS comprises three items, each scored on a 3-point scale (0–2), yielding a total raw score range of 0–6, with the three items focusing on the accuracy of treatment claims, promotion of unproven therapies, and discouragement of evidence-based care respectively, where a score of 0 indicates low risk, 1 indicates moderate risk, and 2 indicates high risk. Specifically, for the accuracy of treatment claims item, a score of 0 indicates treatment information fully consistent with established clinical guidelines (e.g., correctly identifying valproate, levetiracetam, or lamotrigine as first-line antiepileptic drugs, AEDs), a score of 1 indicates partially inaccurate or misleading treatment claims (e.g., overstating the efficacy of a specific AED without acknowledging individual variability in treatment response), and a score of 2 indicates clearly inaccurate or clinically dangerous treatment claims (e.g., promoting abrupt discontinuation of AEDs, or claiming herbal remedies can replace prescription AEDs); for the promotion of unproven therapies item, a score of 0 indicates no promotion of unproven therapies, a score of 1 indicates mention of unproven therapies without explicit endorsement, and a score of 2 indicates active promotion of unproven therapies (e.g., specific herbal formulations, acupuncture marketed as a seizure cure, or dietary supplements) as definitive treatments for epilepsy; for the discouragement of evidence-based care item, a score of 0 indicates no discouragement of guideline-concordant care, a score of 1 indicates implicit discouragement (e.g., emphasizing the side effects of AEDs without balanced information about the risks of untreated epilepsy), and a score of 2 indicates explicit discouragement of evidence-based care (e.g., advising viewers to stop prescribed medications or avoid neurology consultations). For statistical analysis, the mean TMRS score per video was calculated (total raw score ÷ 3 items), yielding a normalized final score range of 0–2, with risk stratification thresholds established via equal tertile division of the 0–2 normalized scale, a pragmatic approach consistent with validated health misinformation risk scales in the existing literature 27 . Scores of 0–0.67 indicate low misinformation risk, corresponding to a raw total score of 0–2, meaning no items scored 2 (high risk) and at most 2 items scored 1 (moderate risk), with no clearly inaccurate or dangerous treatment claims; scores of 0.68–1.33 indicate moderate misinformation risk, corresponding to a raw total score of 2–4, meaning content contains partially misleading claims or implicit discouragement of evidence-based care, but no single item scored 2 (i.e., no explicit high-risk misinformation); scores of 1.34–2.00 indicate high misinformation risk, corresponding to a raw total score of 4–6, meaning content contains at least one item scored 2 (clear, clinically significant high-risk misinformation) or multiple moderate-risk items that cumulatively pose substantial potential for patient harm, directly linking the single-item high-risk score of 2 to the high-risk stratification threshold. All TMRS scores reported in the results are normalized mean scores (0–2), and we acknowledge that the TMRS has not yet been externally validated in independent study samples, with its thresholds interpreted as exploratory rather than definitive, and future studies are warranted to establish the external validity and predictive utility of this instrument[ 21,22,23 ].
Evaluation process The video evaluation was conducted by two board-certified physicians (Y.J.Z. and Y.J.J.), each with formal clinical experience in the diagnosis and management of neurological disorders including epilepsy, with all evaluation procedures standardized prior to formal scoring. To ensure a high degree of consistency and standardization across the rating process, the two raters first completed a structured calibration exercise using a pilot sample of 20 epilepsy-related videos, which were sourced from TikTok and Bilibili using the exact same search terms, inclusion criteria, and relevance ranking strategy as the final study sample, and were not included in the final analysis to avoid overfitting of the rating standards and contamination of the study results. During the calibration exercise, the two raters independently scored each pilot video using all four assessment instruments, then held a structured consensus meeting to discuss and resolve all scoring discrepancies, refine the scoring rubric for ambiguous items, and establish a shared, standardized rating framework for the main analysis. Inter-rater reliability for the main analysis was assessed using the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) with a two-way mixed-effects model for absolute agreement, with ICC values above 0.75 pre-specified as indicative of excellent reliability. The final ICC values obtained were: GQS = 0.87 (95% CI: 0.83–0.91), mDISCERN = 0.84 (95% CI: 0.79–0.88), JAMA = 0.82 (95% CI: 0.77–0.87), and TMRS = 0.85 (95% CI: 0.80–0.89), all of which indicate excellent inter-rater reliability consistent with the standards of quantitative content analysis research. The final score for each video for all instruments was determined by averaging the independent scores from the two raters.
Statistical analysis All statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS software (Version 28.0). Descriptive statistics were used to summarize the general characteristics of the videos. Due to the non-normal distribution of the data, as confirmed by the Shapiro–Wilk test (all P < 0.05), non-parametric statistical methods were employed throughout. The Mann–Whitney U test was used to compare continuous variables between TikTok and Bilibili. The Kruskal–Wallis H test, followed by Dunn’s post-hoc test with Bonferroni correction, was used to compare quality metrics across uploader source categories. Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient was used to examine associations between quality scores, TMRS scores, and engagement metrics. Subgroup analyses stratified by content theme and uploader category were performed using independent-samples t-tests or Mann–Whitney U tests as appropriate. All tests were two-tailed, and statistical significance was set at P  < 0.05. To address potential confounding between platform effects and uploader/video characteristics, we performed two supplementary analyses: (1) Stratified analyses by uploader source category, to compare quality and misinformation scores between TikTok and Bilibili within each uploader subgroup; (2) Multivariable linear regression models, to isolate the independent association between platform and content outcomes. Models were constructed with GQS and normalized TMRS as dependent variables, respectively. Platform (TikTok vs. Bilibili) was set as the primary independent variable, with pre-specified confounders adjusted for: uploader source category, video duration, presence of academic references, and verified account status.

Quality assessment by uploader source Kruskal–Wallis analysis revealed significant differences in quality metrics across the four uploader source categories (all P  < 0.001; Table 4). Healthcare Professionals achieved the highest mean GQS (3.49 ± 0.63) and mDISCERN (2.93 ± 0.70) scores, followed by Healthcare Institutions (GQS: 3.31 ± 0.70; mDISCERN: 2.71 ± 0.74). Media and Commercial Entities produced content of intermediate quality (GQS: 2.58 ± 0.74), while Patients/Influencers produced the lowest-quality content (GQS: 1.92 ± 0.68; mDISCERN: 1.52 ± 0.62). Discussion Principal findings This study represents the first systematic evaluation of the quality, reliability, and treatment misinformation risk of epilepsy-related content on China’s two leading short-video platforms. Our analysis of 182 videos revealed several concerning patterns with significant implications for public health and clinical practice. First, the overall quality of epilepsy-related content was suboptimal, with a mean GQS of 2.65 out of 5, indicating that the average video provided only partial, low-quality information. Second, Bilibili consistently outperformed TikTok across all quality and reliability metrics, while TikTok exhibited significantly higher treatment misinformation risk. Third, a clear quality gradient was observed by uploader source, with Healthcare Professionals producing the highest-quality content and Patients/Influencers producing the lowest. Fourth, we observed a pattern consistent with a “quality-engagement paradox”: TMRS scores (indicating higher treatment misinformation risk) were significantly positively correlated with greater user engagement, while validated content quality metrics showed no significant correlation with engagement metrics. This association must be interpreted with caution, as our cross-sectional design cannot confirm a causal relationship. These findings are broadly consistent with the emerging literature on epilepsy-related content on other social media platforms. Previous studies 33 evaluated epilepsy-related videos on YouTube and found that the majority were of poor quality, with misleading videos receiving significantly more views than educational ones. Similarly, another study 34 assessed TikTok epilepsy content in the English-language context and reported that only a minority of videos were uploaded by healthcare professionals, with a substantial proportion containing inaccurate or misleading information. Our study extends these findings to the Chinese short-video ecosystem and provides the first head-to-head platform comparison using a multi-instrument framework including a novel treatment misinformation risk scale.
Platform differences and their structural determinants Consistent with our hypothesis, Bilibili videos demonstrated significantly higher quality, reliability, and credibility scores compared to TikTok across all three validated instruments (GQS, mDISCERN, and JAMA). This pattern aligns with and extends the findings of previous studies examining other medical conditions on these platforms 14, 15–16 , and is also consistent with cross-platform comparisons in the epilepsy domain. A YouTube-based evaluation 33 reported that videos uploaded by healthcare professionals achieved significantly higher GQS scores than those by non-professionals, a pattern mirrored in our findings across both TikTok and Bilibili. Importantly, the platform-level quality differences we observed (Bilibili > TikTok) parallel evidence from a TikTok-based study 34 showing that shorter-format epilepsy content tends to be of lower quality than longer-format content on other platforms, suggesting that video duration and platform architecture are consistent determinants of health content quality across linguistic and cultural contexts. The observed platform differences likely reflect fundamental structural differences between TikTok and Bilibili. TikTok’s algorithm is optimized for short-form, emotionally engaging content, which may inadvertently favor sensationalized or oversimplified health information. In contrast, Bilibili’s longer video format and more academically oriented user community may create an environment more conducive to nuanced, evidence-based health communication. The higher proportion of verified accounts (55.8% vs. 32.3%) and content with references (25.6% vs. 8.3%) on Bilibili further supports this interpretation.
The demographic differences between the two platforms’ user bases may also contribute to the observed quality differences. Bilibili’s user base skews younger and more highly educated, with a significant proportion of university students and young professionals who may be more discerning consumers of health information. TikTok’s broader, more diverse user base may create greater demand for accessible, emotionally resonant content, which may not always align with evidence-based health communication standards.
The treatment misinformation risk phenomenon The prevalence of treatment misinformation in epilepsy-related content represents a particularly concerning finding. The overall mean normalized TMRS score of 1.15 ± 0.62 falls within the moderate risk range (0.68–1.33), with TikTok content showing a significantly higher mean score (1.41 ± 0.54, approaching the high-risk threshold of 1.34) compared to Bilibili (0.86 ± 0.53, moderate risk). The types of misinformation identified are especially concerning in the epilepsy context. Videos promoting unproven traditional Chinese medicine remedies, herbal formulations, or acupuncture as definitive “cures” for epilepsy may lead patients to delay or forgo guideline-recommended AED therapy, potentially resulting in uncontrolled seizures, status epilepticus, or SUDEP. Similarly, content that emphasizes AED side effects without balanced information about the risks of untreated epilepsy may contribute to medication non-adherence, which is a leading cause of preventable seizure recurrence.
The positive correlation between normalized TMRS scores and user engagement metrics (rho = 0.39–0.46 for likes, shares, and comments) is consistent with findings from prior research examining health misinformation across other neurological and chronic disease conditions on social media platforms 27,28 . Notably, the specific types of treatment misinformation identified in our study echo concerns raised in the existing literature on epilepsy treatment information in social media. Regarding antiseizure medications (ASMs), prior analyses of social-media-based discussions and adherence patterns 35 reported that online content frequently overemphasized side effects while underrepresenting efficacy data, a pattern we observed in videos scoring high on the TMRS “discouragement of evidence-based care” item. With respect to cannabidiol (CBD), evaluations of online CBD-related epilepsy information 36 demonstrated that such material disproportionately promoted CBD as a standalone cure while omitting critical information about its regulatory status, drug interactions, and the limited range of epilepsy syndromes for which it has demonstrated efficacy (e.g., Dravet syndrome, Lennox-Gastaut syndrome), findings that parallel the promotion of unproven therapies we identified in our TMRS analysis. For the ketogenic diet, comparative assessments of online sources 37 noted that consumer-facing content frequently lacked guidance on medical supervision and monitoring requirements, potentially encouraging unsupervised dietary interventions; in our sample, videos discussing the ketogenic diet similarly tended to present it as a simple dietary change rather than a medically supervised therapeutic intervention. Regarding vagus nerve stimulation (VNS), evaluations of patient-generated video content 38 highlighted unrealistic expectations about seizure freedom rates, a finding consistent with the overstated efficacy claims we observed in our surgical and neuromodulation treatment category.
However, this so-called “quality-engagement paradox” must be interpreted with significant caution, as our cross-sectional design cannot confirm causality, and multiple non-mutually exclusive mechanisms may underpin this association. One potential explanation is the inherent tension between the emotionally charged, narrative-driven nature of content containing unsubstantiated or misleading treatment claims, which often leverages personal testimonials and sensationalized outcomes to resonate with social media audiences, compared to the measured, nuanced, and often technical tone of evidence-based health communication. Platform algorithmic dynamics may also play a critical mediating role: content with higher initial user engagement receives greater algorithmic amplification and broader distribution, which in turn drives further increases in views, likes, shares, and comments, creating a positive feedback loop that cannot be disentangled in our cross-sectional analysis. We also cannot rule out the possibility that unmeasured features of high-TMRS videos—such as provocative titles, emotionally provocative framing, or simplified, easily shareable messaging—rather than the treatment misinformation itself, are the primary drivers of increased user engagement. Finally, the temporal direction of the association cannot be established with our study design, and reverse causality cannot be excluded.
Professional vs. non-professional content creation Consistent with global social media health information research, we observed a clear quality gradient by uploader source, with professional content outperforming non-professional content across all metrics. This finding is consistent with the broader literature on health information quality on social media and underscores the importance of professional engagement in digital health communication. Specifically, our results align with prior YouTube-based evidence 33 showing that healthcare professional-uploaded epilepsy videos achieved significantly higher quality scores (e.g., GQS, modified DISCERN) than non-professional uploads, as well as with TikTok-based evidence 34 documenting an inverse relationship between content quality and popularity metrics. They are also in line with cross-platform assessments of epilepsy information 39 , which reported that clinician- or institution-created content was more likely to contain guideline-concordant treatment information than lay or influencer-generated content. The consistency of this professional-quality gradient across platforms (YouTube, TikTok, Bilibili) and languages (English, Chinese) suggests that uploader expertise is a robust, platform-independent predictor of health content quality.The “quality-engagement paradox”—where high-quality professional content receives significantly lower user engagement than low-quality non-professional content—highlights a fundamental challenge in digital health communication. This disparity stems from the inherent tension between evidence-based, nuanced scientific communication and the emotional, narrative-driven content that performs best on short-video platforms. Addressing this paradox requires not just increasing the volume of professional content, but equipping clinicians with platform-specific communication skills to translate clinical guidelines into engaging, accessible narratives.
Strengths and limitations Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, the cross-sectional design only captures a snapshot of the dynamic social media content landscape, limiting temporal generalizability. Second, sampling was restricted to actively searched, top 100 relevance-ranked videos, excluding passively viewed recommendation feed content and long-tail videos; thus, findings may not fully represent the broader content ecosystem. Third, despite stratified analyses by content theme and uploader category, plus multivariable adjustment for confounders, residual confounding from unmeasured factors (e.g., platform content policies) may still exist, and causal inferences about platform effects should be made with caution. Fourth, the assessment tools have inherent limitations: JAMA scores reflect source transparency rather than clinical accuracy; GQS and mDISCERN may structurally disadvantage Lived Experience and Coping content; and the TMRS, while reliable and expert-validated, has not undergone external validation, with thresholds defined via equal tertile division rather than empirically derived cut-points. Fifth, the observed positive association between TMRS scores and user engagement is based exclusively on cross-sectional correlational data, which cannot establish causal relationships, temporal sequence, or the direction of effects between treatment misinformation risk and user engagement. Multiple unmeasured confounding factors, including video framing, emotional tone, title design, platform algorithmic amplification, and other content characteristics unrelated to treatment misinformation, may contribute to this observed association. Accordingly, this association should be interpreted strictly as a correlational finding, and no causal inferences should be drawn from our results. Finally, the study was restricted to Chinese-language content on domestic platforms, limiting cross-cultural generalizability, and cannot establish a causal link between misinformation exposure and long-term patient clinical outcomes.
Future research directions Future studies should explore several directions. Longitudinal monitoring of epilepsy-related content quality would capture temporal trends and the impact of platform policy changes. Expanding the analysis to include additional platforms (e.g., Xiaohongshu, Kuaishou) and international platforms (e.g., YouTube, Instagram) would provide a more comprehensive global picture. Experimental studies examining the causal effects of epilepsy misinformation exposure on patient knowledge, AED adherence, and treatment-seeking behavior are needed to establish the clinical significance of the quality-engagement paradox. Finally, intervention studies evaluating the effectiveness of professional content creation training, platform labeling systems, and algorithmic adjustments in improving epilepsy information quality would provide actionable evidence for public health policy.
Conclusions This study identifies a pattern consistent with a “quality-engagement paradox” on Chinese short-video platforms: epilepsy-related content with higher treatment misinformation risk (measured via TMRS) is significantly positively correlated with greater user engagement (likes, shares, and comments). Critically, this means that content containing higher levels of treatment misinformation reaches disproportionately wider audiences compared to evidence-based, higher-quality content. Such easy and extensive spread of misinformation poses a substantial public health risk, as millions of patients and caregivers may be exposed to potentially harmful treatment claims that could lead to treatment non-adherence, delayed appropriate care, or even status epilepticus. This association must be interpreted with caution given the limitations of our cross-sectional study design. Bilibili consistently demonstrated higher content quality and lower misinformation risk compared to TikTok, and healthcare professionals produced superior content compared to non-professional creators. These findings underscore the urgent need for neurologists and epilepsy specialists to engage proactively with short-video platforms, for platform operators to implement quality-promoting interventions (e.g., algorithmic demotion of high-TMRS content and promotion of verified professional content), and for health authorities to develop digital health literacy programs targeting epilepsy patients and caregivers.
