Introduction
The etiology of the disease known as “Covid-19” is believed to have originated in the city of Wuhan, China [1]. It has since evolved into a global pandemic, with a cumulative total of more than 775 million confirmed cases and 7 million deaths reported worldwide as of July 2024 [2]. From 2020 to 2021, the repercussions of the pandemic were manifold, including but not limited to: exacerbation of preexisting challenges in health services and human resources, exacerbation of unemployment rates, and impoverishment of the population. These factors contributed to a marked deterioration in key performance indicators in the educational sector, while concurrently imposing stringent restrictions on commercial activities, artistic and entertainment enterprises, and other domains. This had the effect of further exacerbating preexisting inequalities and injustices within society [3, 4].
The mandatory use of masks, hand hygiene, and circulation restrictions constituted the core strategies to contain the virus spread until the availability of the first vaccines with proven efficacy and the achievement of reasonable vaccination coverage rates [5, 6–7]. Despite their evident importance, the implementation of these measures proved challenging due to their deleterious impact on mental health, demanding behavioral modifications for which many were not prepared [8, 9]. Furthermore, their repercussions on economic and political spheres, as governments were compelled to determine the respective priorities of nations, cities, and regions [10], further compounded the complexity of the situation. In nations governed by far-right politicians (e.g., USA, Israel, and Brazil), defenders of ideologies marked by radical conservatism, authoritarianism, ultra-nationalism, and nativism, the difficulties to implement evidence-based health care strategies were more pronounced [11].
In the case of Brazil, the impact of the pandemic was exacerbated by the scenario of strong political-partisan polarization, which mobilized the population and hampered the work of health authorities. This happened because many people tended to take a position for or against the adoption of measures to contain Covid-19 based on their identification with the discourse of politicians and celebrities [12, 13]. A series of actions taken by the federal government also contributed to Brazil being considered one of the most inefficient countries in the fight against the pandemic [14, 15], including delaying the purchase and distribution of vaccines, resisting the adoption of public health measures that restricted the movement of people, spreading false news about the lethality of the virus and the potential impact of the pandemic, encouraging the population to be exposed to the virus on the false premise that this would lead to so-called “herd immunity”, scientific denial and insistence on the adoption of ineffective treatments against Covid-19, the use of the resources of the Brazilian Health System (SUS) for ideological and electoral purposes, favoring supporters and persecuting political opponents, as well as the discourse that “the economy cannot stop” [16]. For this reason, the measures adopted by the then President Jair Bolsonaro to face the pandemic can be considered catastrophic, inhumane and even criminal from the point of view of international criminal law [17]. In this regard, it is estimated that hundreds of thousands of deaths could have been avoided if the federal government had followed the guidelines of health authorities and the recommendations of scientists [18, 19].
Research has demonstrated that adherence to physical distancing measures was higher among individuals identifying as left-leaning [20]. Furthermore, the discordance between politicians’ discourse and scientific recommendations influenced the behavior of Brazilians during the pandemic [21]. Additionally, it was observed that elevated scores on measures of authoritarianism and conservatism, coupled with stronger right-wing political identification, were associated with greater credibility in information disseminated through the WhatsApp social network. Conversely, these same individuals exhibited less trust in information disseminated via traditional news outlets, such as radio and television [22].
In addition to the ideological clashes resulting from political party polarization in Brazil, the dissemination of fake news and misinformation about the novel corona virus has been recurrent throughout the years of the pandemic [23]. This has meant that health authorities have had to deal not only with the lack of information from the general population, but also with the strong movement to discredit science, boosted by many sectors of society [24, 25].
Research has shown that the number of Google and social media searches for information about the virus increased significantly between 2020 and 2021, mirroring the emergence of the first cases and the subsequent spread of the disease worldwide [25]. However, a significant proportion of the accessed and widely circulated information was of a dubious nature, including claims such as the efficacy of hot water or the mineral supplement MMS (Miracle Mineral Supplement) in combating the virus, the alleged similarities between HIV and SARS-CoV-2 [26], and other content of a similar nature. This misinformation was disseminated not only by individuals but also by prominent figures, further aggravating the situation.
The dissemination of substantial quantities of disinformation during the pandemic engendered the phenomenon termed “disinfodemic”. This phenomenon is characterized by the accelerated and widespread propagation of information devoid of scientific credibility, with deleterious consequences for the efficacy of containment strategies for the virus [24]. For instance, health literacy and the capacity to discern authentic information from disinformation influenced decision-making processes regarding vaccination [27, 28]. Moreover, associations have been observed between perceptions of the severity of contracting the disease and the consumption and acceptance of fake news [29].
In the context of modifying population behaviors, effective communication is paramount. This communication should entail the dissemination of clear and precise messages regarding the desired behaviors, the reinforcement of these messages by leaders and authorities, and the reiteration of the new behaviors by other members of society. This multifaceted approach is instrumental in facilitating the adoption of new habits [30]. This process of change is more likely to occur when individuals perceive a sense of belonging to the group in which the actions are implemented, thereby fostering a sense of social responsibility toward their peers [31]. Evidence has demonstrated that the incorporation of audiovisual stimuli addressing the pandemic and the disease’s consequences, designed to evoke empathy for the victims’ family members, can enhance individuals’ motivation to adhere to care measures during the pandemic [32, 33–34]. Another study found that the intention to get vaccinated against the virus increased when participants read short excerpts from a pro-vaccination message emphasizing the individual risks and collective benefits for the population, regardless of whether or not it was preceded by information about strict control in vaccine production (pre-bunking) and whether the author was a lay person or a health expert. Conversely, when the message exclusively invoked arguments pertaining to the economic advantages of vaccination, there was no observed increase in vaccination intentions [35].
Thus, research conducted in the context of the pandemic demonstrated the impact of misinformation and political party identification on the attitudes and preventive behaviors of the population, as well as on the way they sought information about Covid-19. However, most of the studies were conducted in the early months of the Covid-19 crisis, when there were no vaccines available or when the access to them was very restricted, i.e., in a context where the adoption of non-pharmacological measures (hand hygiene, respiratory etiquette, mask use and physical distancing) was the most effective way of containing the number of infections and deaths from the disease [5, 6–7, 36].
The present study sought to determine whether susceptibility to accepting fake news about Covid-19, political party identification, and agreement with the public stance of prominent individuals on the disease would influence attitudes and intentions to adhere to the recommendations of health authorities during the third wave of the pandemic in Brazil. The study also examined the durability of the associations between empathy and the adoption of measures to combat the virus, as identified in previous studies [21, 32, 33], even in a scenario where the number of deaths and infections was significantly lower than those observed in the first two waves (https://covid.saude.gov.br/). Also, when the study was conducted, at least one dose of the vaccine had been administered to over 70% of the Brazilian population [37].
Furthermore, the government had authorized the majority of social activities, commerce was operating normally, and events had resumed. Consequently, this study’s data were collected during a period in which restrictions on circulation and crowding of people were lessened. At that time, the perception of reduced risk and increased control of the pandemic was widespread, because the so-called “second wave” of Covid-19, the most lethal of all (https://data.who.int/dashboards), had been overcome.
Method
Participants
This exploratory online survey employed a non-probabilistic convenience sample, consisting of 320 participants. Three protocols were excluded due to participants residing outside Brazil, and eight were excluded for failing to respond correctly to any of the checking items. Consequently, the analysis was conducted on the responses of 309 participants. The participants’ ages ranged from 18 to 65 years ( M  = 26.73; SD = 8.85), with the majority having completed high school (62.5%), residing in the Northeast (84.1%), and identifying as cisgender women (57.3%). Instruments
The data were collected using a Google Forms questionnaire, which was divided into four sections. The first section collected sociodemographic data, including gender, age, family income, education, and place of residence. The second section inquired about political party identification, using a scale from 0 to 5 to measure leftist and rightist identification, center-left, center, and other (0 = can’t identify what it means to be leftist or rightist; 1 = clearly leftist; 2 = center-left; 3 = center; 4 = center-right; 5 = clearly rightist) [38]. The survey also inquired about the participants’ health, including whether they belonged to a high-risk group, if they had been vaccinated, if they had already been infected with the virus, and if they had lost family members or friends to the disease. The respondents were also asked to rate their ability to practice social distancing, wear masks when in contact with people outside their family, and practice hand hygiene with soap and water or alcohol regularly. These ratings were made on a five-point Likert scale, ranging from 1 (no conditions) to 5 (total conditions). At the conclusion of the first part of the study, the participants were asked how they had sought information about the pandemic.
The second part measured the willingness to continue taking personal measures to prevent the spread of the virus in the following month ( ω  = 0.72 and α  = 0.69), assessed by three items (wearing a mask in environments with other people, avoiding crowds, washing hands with soap and water or hand sanitizer). The questionnaire was to be answered using five-point Likert scales (1 = not at all willing/I have already stopped taking this measure to 5 = absolutely willing). Respondents were also asked to rate the importance of taking all doses of the Covid-19 vaccine (‘not important’ to ‘absolutely important’) and to indicate their agreement (‘absolutely disagree’ to ‘absolutely agree’) with eight sentences related to vaccination (I. Make vaccination compulsory; II. Vaccinate all children with health problems; III. Require a vaccination pass to enter concerts, IV. Football stadiums; V. Restaurants and bars; VI. Prohibit unvaccinated people from entering closed environments; VII. Cancel major events in the country until minimum vaccination coverage is achieved; VIII. Allow attendance at face-to-face classes in universities and colleges only for students with a complete vaccination cycle). The scores assigned to these eight items were used to construct a variable ( ω  = 0.93 and α  = 0.94) representing the position on the use of the vaccine as a measure to combat the pandemic, such that the higher the score, the more in favor of vaccination the respondent was.
The third part of the form included the Interpersonal Reactivity Index by Davis (1982), in its version validated for the Brazilian context [39], with 26 items assessing four subdimensions (Fantasy: ω  = 0.81 and α  = 0.80; Empathic Consideration: ω  = 0.80 and α  = 0.81; Perspective Taking: ω  = 0.72 and α  = 0.71 and Personal Distress: ω  = 0.83 and α  = 0.82) and a general empathy dimension ( ω  = 0.89 and α  = 0.88).
In the fourth part, participants were asked to rate the degree of authenticity of 12 statements related to Covid-19 using five-point scales (I believe the information is absolutely true = −2 to I believe the information is absolutely false = 2). Four statements were true and  eight were false (Supplementary Material), widely circulated in social networks and already technically reviewed by experts from the Oswaldo Cruz Foundation (https://portal.fiocruz.br/noticia/fiocruz-esclarece-informacoes-falsas) and other Brazilian research centers, as well as publicly discussed by the consortium of press outlets formed in Brazil during the pandemic (https://projetocomprova.com.br/). The authenticity scores assigned to each statement were used to create a measure of susceptibility to accepting fake news related to the pandemic ( ω  = 0.62 and α  = 0.65), such that the higher the score, the greater the susceptibility. The values assigned to real news were inverted before calculating this variable. The fifth part of the form assessed the degree of agreement with statements made by the country’s public figures (politicians, scientists and doctors) on issues that permeate the pandemic: childhood vaccination, mandatory vaccination pass, effectiveness of mask protection, among others. To that, 10 statements made by politicians and celebrities (Supplementary Material), widely disseminated in the Brazilian media, five in favor and five against the adoption of measures to control the pandemic, were presented. The authors of the statements were not identified, and the respondent was asked to indicate his/her level of agreement with each statement on a five-point scale (“I absolutely disagree” to “I absolutely agree”). The scores assigned to these items were used to create a measure ( ω  = 0.82 and α  = 0.80) of the degree to which participants agreed with public figures who advocated the adoption of restrictive measures to combat the pandemic. The scores assigned to sentences representing opinions against the use of these restrictive measures were reversed. Thus, the higher the score on this measure, the more the respondent agreed with public figures who advocated the adoption of measures to combat the pandemic.
Data collection
Participants were invited via email and WhatsApp, and when they accessed the survey link, they were given general information about the study and read an Informed Consent Form (ICF) before deciding whether or not to proceed to the sessions of the form containing the study questions. Data were collected over a 19-week period between March and August 2022. This research is part of a project approved by the Ethics Committee for Research with Human Beings at the Alcides Carneiro University Hospital (CAAE: 33660820.2.0000.8267, opinion # 4.294.751). However, in order to include a third phase in the study, the results of which make up this manuscript, an amendment was submitted to the original ethical protocol, which was also approved by Opinion No. 5.259.694. This work adhered to the Declaration of Helsinki.
Data analysis
Descriptive and inferential statistics were used to analyze the data. A MANOVA was used to test whether there were differences in the willingness to continue to take personal measures to prevent the spread of the virus in the following month (1), the favorable position regarding the use of the Covid-19 vaccine (2), the agreement with public figures who advocated the application of measures restricting the movement of people (3), and the susceptibility to accept fake news related to the pandemic (4) as a function of gender, education, and belonging to a risk group for COVID-19.
Hierarchical Multiple Regressions were used to test whether the willingness to continue taking personal measures to prevent the spread of the virus in the following month (A), the position regarding the use of vaccines (B), and the agreement with authorities who advocate the adoption of restrictive public measures to control the pandemic (C) could be predicted by the susceptibility to accept fake news related to the pandemic (1), conditions of practicing social distancing (2), wearing masks (3), and practicing hand hygiene (4), political party identification (5), and dimensions of empathy (Fantasy, Empathic Consideration, Perspective Taking, Personal Distress and General Empathy = 6, 7, 8, 9, and 10). The models were built hierarchically—in the first block the variables (2), (3), and (4) were entered, in the second block the variables (1) and (5) were entered, and in the third block the empathy dimensions (6, 7, 8, 9, and 10) were entered.
All regression models were checked for the absence of collinearity and multicollinearity between the independent variables, and for the normal distribution of the residuals through the analysis of histograms and plots of standardized residuals versus adjusted values. The homoscedasticity of the data was checked by graphical analysis of the regression residuals versus the estimated values of the dependent variable. Cases with unstandardized residuals greater than 3 and less than −3 (outliers) were excluded from the analysis. In addition, the Durbin–Watson test, Tolerance Analysis, and Variance Inflation Factors (VIF) were used to confirm that the assumptions for the use of multiple regression analysis were met [40].
All analyses were performed using the Statistical Package for Social Sciences—SPSS (version 22) and Microsoft Excel 365.
Results
MANOVA (Pilai Tracking = 0.89; F  = 589.35; p  < 0.001; η 2  = 0.05) indicated that women were less likely to believe Fake News related to the pandemic [ F (1, 306) = 3.74; p  = 0.054; η 2  = 0.01], more favorable to the use of the vaccine as a strategy to control the virus [ F (1, 306) = 14.10; p  < 0.001; η 2  = 0.04], and were more likely than men to agree with public figures who advocated the adoption of restrictive measures to control the pandemic [ F (1, 306) = 3.77; p  = .053; 2 = 0.01] (Table 2). On the other hand, no differences were found in these three dependent variables and in the willingness to continue taking personal measures to combat the virus as a function of schooling or belonging to a risk group for COVID-19. Regression analysis yielded a statistically significant model [ F (9, 293) = 15.64; p  < 0.001; R 2  = 0.30] suggesting that the conditions of practicing physical distancing ( β  = 0.18; t  = 3.59; p  < 0.001), wearing a face mask ( β  = 0.33; t  = 5.74; p  < 0.001), and hand hygiene ( β  = 0.14; t  = 2.58; p  = 0.010), as well as perspective taking ( β  = 0.13; t  = 2.38; p  = 0.018), significantly predicted willingness to continue taking measures to control the virus in the month following data collection. The equation describing this relationship is: willingness to continue taking measures to combat the virus in the next month = 0.17 + 0.13 × (conditions of practicing social distancing) + 0.31 × (condition of wearing a face mask) + 0.16 × (condition of practicing hand hygiene) + 0.22 × (Perspective Taking).
The model was also significant for the position in favor of using vaccines as a measure to control the virus [ F (9, 263) = 5.74; p  < 0.001; R 2  = 0.13]. This variable was predicted by the conditions of wearing a face mask ( β  = 0.14; t  = 2.19; p  = 0.029), political-ideological identification ( β  = −0.23; t  = −3.86; p  < 0.001), and Personal Distress ( β  = 0.14; t  = 2.11; p  = 0.035). The equation corresponding to this model is: favorable use of vaccines = 4.16 + 0.04 × (conditions of wearing a face mask) + (−0.06) × (political identification) + 0.04 × (Personal Distress).
Finally, an additional significant model was identified regarding agreement with the position of public figures in favor of adopting restrictive measures to control the pandemic [ F (9, 289) = 28.41; p  < 0.001; R 2  = 0.45]. In this model, this variable was predicted by conditions to practice hand hygiene ( β  = 0.13; t  = 2.61; p  = 0.009), political identification ( β  = −0.16; t  = −9.89; p  < 0.001), susceptibility to believe Fake News related to the pandemic ( β  = −0.47; t  = −3.53; p  < 0.001), and Fantasy ( β  = 0.18; t  = 3.45; p  = 0.001). This model can be described by the equation: agreement with positions favorable to restrictive measures = 2.81 + 0.08 × (conditions to practice hand hygiene) + (−0.07) × (political identification) + 0.12 × (Fantasy).
Discussion
The results of this research are consistent with those of other studies conducted during the Covid-19 pandemic, with some differences. For example, there was a tendency for women and people with better economic conditions to be more supportive of the mandatory use of restrictive measures and to continue to take personal action against the Covid-19 virus, as has been observed in research in Brazil and other countries [14, 41]. In addition to confirming what is already known about the differences in the way men and women perceive risk and adhere to prevention and care behaviors in emergency situations [42, 43], these data highlight the impact of the pandemic in exacerbating inequalities in Brazilian society, affecting families of lower socioeconomic status more severely, who had greater difficulty adopting measures that would protect them from Covid-19 [34].
Regarding political identification, regression models suggested that this variable did not predict the willingness to follow the recommendations of the health authorities in the following month. This may be related to the time context in which the data were collected (“third wave”), when people’s perception of risk may have decreased significantly due to the high vaccination rate of the population, the reopening of commerce, and the resumption of public events. This hypothesis is supported by the fact that there was no significant effect of the variable belonging to a risk group for Covid-19 and agreement with public figures who advocated maintaining more restrictive measures and the use of vaccination, contrary to what was observed in a previous study in the Brazilian context [21].
It was also observed that the more participants identified with the left, the more they agreed with personalities who publicly advocated more restrictive measures to control the pandemic and the more they supported the use of vaccination as a strategy to cope with the disease. This finding, which supports data from previous studies that also found a significant effect of political identification on vaccination intentions [27], should be highlighted given that vaccination is one of the most important preventive strategies in public health policy.
By December 31, 2023, the percentage of Brazilians who had completed the Covid-19 vaccination cycle (two doses or single dose plus booster) was around 53% of the population, well below countries such as Chile (83%), Singapore (82%) and Iceland (79%), but still comparable to other countries such as New Zealand (56%), Sweden (54%) and Canada (53%) (https://data.who.int/dashboards). The decline in vaccination rates, as one of the consequences of the Covid-19 pandemic, has drawn the attention of health authorities in Brazil and worldwide [44], as it increases the risk of re-emergence of some diseases previously considered eradicated (polio and measles, for example), as well as the emergence of new pandemics.
Taken together, these data point to the need for health promotion policies to take into account gender issues, political party identification, and the socioeconomic level of the population, through the formulation of specific campaigns with messages targeted at audiences in which raising awareness and changing attitudes are more difficult to achieve through other, more comprehensive interventions routinely employed by health authorities [45, 46]. Likewise, health strategies should include social support programs for families in the most vulnerable conditions, since some of them have no choice in following or not following the recommendations of professionals and scientists.
The results of this study also show that susceptibility to accepting fake news negatively predicts the degree to which participants agree with politicians and celebrities who have taken a stance in favor of the use of the vaccine and other measures to restrict human circulation, even after all the efforts of health professionals, researchers and the media to combat misinformation during the first two waves of the pandemic. In this sense, health promotion and prevention strategies should be accompanied by ongoing actions that constitute a digital education program to help people identify truthful content and critically evaluate the information they receive and disseminate [26], not only during a health emergency, but on a routine basis.
In this regard, some authors [25] have compared the strength and speed with which fake news about Covid-19 spread on social media to that of a virus that quickly establishes itself in one vector and then spreads the misinformation to other vectors. Given that the use of the Internet as the sole source of information is becoming more and more common (in the present study, more than 70% of the participants stated that they searched for information about Covid-19 on social networks), health communication actions should be adapted to the formats and dynamics of this medium, seeking to capitalize on the great potential for dissemination and engagement of people on social networks, offering content that contributes to the well-being of the community, especially in situations where ideological differences, beliefs and personal values should be less respected in favor of promoting a common good.
In this sense, the results reinforce the claim that empathy can contribute to the construction of this vision and sense of collectivity, when communication strategies are based on the potential of this variable to mobilize affection, behavior and prosocial attitudes, even when these involve some kind of personal harm [32, 33]. This is exactly what happened during certain periods of the pandemic, when people had to give up the pleasure of moving around freely and meeting friends and family for fear of contracting the disease, but also because they thought that this type of behavior would contribute to worsening the general situation and put others at risk.
The limitations of this study include the size and low variability of the sample, since most of the participants come from the same region of Brazil, creating an imbalance that may limit the generalizability of the results obtained here. Also, given the Internet-based recruitment procedure, this study used a non-probability sample that may not be representative of the general population in Brazil. Future research should address these limitations, ideally by conducting studies with larger, more diverse, and stratified samples.
Another limitation is related to the fact that few participants identified themselves as right-wing or center-right (less than 10%), and this imbalance may have affected the power of the statistical analyses, considering the previously observed effects of political polarization on the attitudes and behaviors of Brazilians during the Covid-19 pandemic [20, 21, 38]. It is also important to consider the possibility that many of the respondents did not know exactly what it meant to be “leftist” or “rightist” and that the measure used in the present study reflects more a preference for politicians, celebrities, or authorities who declared themselves to be somewhere on the left–right political spectrum. Future studies might benefit from an instrument in which participants indicate, through various items, how they think the relationship between the state and the people should be, their priority values, their vision of the world and society, etc., and based on their responses create a measure that indicates their political identification.
Finally, only self-report instruments were used to assess the attitudes and opinions of the participants, which may not necessarily reflect the way they behave during the pandemic period and, furthermore, generated biased responses. For example, only 1.3% of participants reported that they had not been vaccinated, a percentage that differs from data on vaccination coverage of the Brazilian population at the time of data collection [31]. This result may have been influenced by social desirability (e.g., some respondents may have felt uncomfortable saying they were afraid to be vaccinated), an effect that may have affected some analyses.
Despite these limitations, this research contributes to the fields of health and social psychology, as it aims to better understand how psychological and socio-psychological variables may contribute to the planning and implementation of health education strategies aimed at promoting engagement and changing habits and behaviors in the population during health emergencies. The results of this research are consistent with and complement the data obtained from studies conducted during the first two waves of the Covid-19 pandemic, confirming that variables such as political identification, susceptibility to believe fake news, and empathy, as well as gender and socioeconomic status, continued to influence the attitudes and behaviors of the Brazilian population at a time of the pandemic when it seemed that “the worst was over”. Thus, this knowledge could be used to formulate public education and health policies based on scientific evidence.
