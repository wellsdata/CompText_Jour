Keywords:
political science, misinformation, pre-bunk, elections, large language models
We present a scalable artificial intelligence (AI)-assisted framework for rapidly generating 'pre-bunking' articles against election misinformation. Our approach uses a stable template structure that, once finalized through human review, can be quickly adapted to counter emerging false narratives. In a pre-registered two-wave experiment with 4293 United States (US) registered voters, we test this framework against politically charged election misinformation-one of the most challenging domains for misinformation intervention. We find that large language model (LLM)-generated pre-bunking significantly reduced belief in election rumours (effects persisting, though attenuated, one week later) and modestly offset declines in confidence in national election administration, with no evidence of partisan backlash. After finalizing the prompt, articles written with human feedback were no more effective than articles using only AI, indicating that per-rumour human effort can be substantially reduced once the prompt is in place. Within the election-integrity domain, effective misinformation inoculation can therefore be achieved at machine speed without proportional human effort, extending the workflow to other domains (e.g. public health and climate) is a natural direction for future work. To facilitate real-world use, we release an interactive demonstration that automatically drafts pre-bunking articles from trusted facts at electionbot.chat/article.
1. Introduction
Misinformation has become a challenging problem in contemporary societies [1]. Its reach extends across virtually every domain of public concern. Climate action stalls when false narratives about sustainability gain traction [2]. Public health suffers when, as during the COVID-19 pandemic, vaccine misinformation undermines immunization efforts [3]. Perhaps most alarmingly, democratic processes themselves face disruption: from the Brexit referendum [4] to the 2016 United States (US) presidential election [5] to electoral contests in India [6], misinformation has become a recurring threat to informed civic participation.
Election misinformation is a particularly difficult problem and is considered a persistent threat in many democracies, including the United States of America [7]. It can spread quickly and, in the days before an election, can be difficult to counter. It can erode the confidence of voters and stakeholders in the conduct of an election, undermining the legitimacy of an otherwise free and fair election. Election misinformation and concerns about election integrity can threaten the orderly transfer of power from one party to another [8-10]. For example, the attack on the US Capitol on 6 January 2021 was motivated by false claims of election fraud and election manipulation in the 2020 presidential election [11,12]. From encouraging violent protests to justifying hate crimes, from the degradation of civic community to the establishment of terrorism, election rumours have a powerful capacity to destabilize democratic government [13-15].
Addressing this challenge is complicated by the asymmetric nature of the information environment: malicious actors can generate and disseminate numerous, rapidly evolving false narratives relatively cheaply, while defences like fact-checking struggle to keep pace [16]. Generative artificial intelligence (AI) and large language models (LLMs) have further amplified this asymmetry, lowering the cost of producing politically relevant text, audio and video, including deceptive content [17-19]. The same technologies can also be turned towards misinformation defence: recent work shows that AI chatbots can durably reduce some conspiracy beliefs [20], and recent reviews identify using LLMs to counter misinformation as a priority research direction [21]. Psychological inoculation [22], or 'pre-bunking', offers a promising proactive strategy by building cognitive resistance to misinformation prior to exposure [23-26]. However, traditional pre-bunking methods face a critical bottleneck: the need for experts to synthesize persuasive false rumours into attenuated exposures that confer resistance without causing harm. Here, we investigate whether generative AI can help overcome this limitation within US election misinformation, enabling the rapid development of pre-bunking interventions at the speed and volume of modern misinformation.
To overcome this scalability challenge, we propose an AI-assisted framework that maintains a stable core structure while allowing rapid adaptation to new threats. Our system combines a human-refined prompt template with authoritative contextual information (such as official election security guidelines) to generate targeted inoculations against specific false narratives. Human expertise is front-loaded into prompt design: once the prompt is validated, generating a new article for a previously unseen rumour requires no additional per-rumour review. This modular design enables rapid response to emerging misinformation while maintaining consistent quality and effectiveness.We test this using five common myths about the 2024 US election, demonstrating that brief, AI-written arguments can pre-emptively counter misinformed rhetoric about election integrity for rumours previously unseen by the AI, with effects lasting at least a week and without evidence of backlash. Extension to other misinformation domains-for example, public health, climate or shorter and more socially embedded formats (memes, platform posts and short-form videos)-is a natural direction for future work.
The unique temporal dynamics of election cycles compounds the challenges of battling misinformation. Election rumours gain potency precisely when democratic institutions are most vulnerable: in the charged atmosphere leading up to voting, when emotions run high and time for careful deliberation runs short. Traditional debunking strategies, which require extensive fact-checking and expert analysis, cannot match the speed at which false election narratives proliferate across social networks. Although our pre-bunking interventions demonstrate the additional benefit of bolstering participants' confidence in electoral processes, this represents a secondary outcome; our core focus is proactive inoculation against the specific myths that threaten informed democratic participation. Our scalable approach has thus been tested in a potentially challenging setting.
Mitigating election-related misinformation and disinformation has been the subject of an important and growing body of research. Recent work has explored various approaches to do so, including fact-checking and inoculation strategies [27]. However, the inoculation literature has only recently begun to specifically study election misinformation [28]. Traditional methods often lag behind the emergence of new narratives. The rapid spread of many different pieces of misinformation through social media and other channels thus necessitates innovative and scalable solutions. We argue that our scalable approach points to AI-assisted misinformation inoculation within the election-integrity domain: once the prompt is finalized, new election rumours can be pre-bunked at machine speed. The method that we develop below could also be retrained for testing and use on non-election rumours and false narratives, and could be deployed in contexts where rapidly evolving rumours and misinformation are a pressing problem (e.g. public health). Whether it retains its effectiveness when moved across issue domains is an empirical question we encourage others to take up. To illustrate how practitioners might operationalize the election-domain workflow, we provide an interactive demonstration that generates new pre-bunking articles from trusted inputs at electionbot.chat/article.
2. Previous research
Pre-bunking has been shown to be a successful approach to combating misinformation [24,29]. Inspired by inoculation theory in psychology, pre-bunking aims to build cognitive resistance to misinformation by exposing individuals to weakened forms of false claims along with factual counter-arguments before they encounter more persuasive misinformation. Pre-bunking leverages the cognitive processing mechanisms by which individuals develop mental schemas and defences against false but otherwise potentially persuasive claims, thereby establishing attitudinal resistance prior to exposure to manipulative content. It can do so both by pre-empting specific factual misconceptions and by building more general anti-misinformation skills, like exposing disinformation tactics and developing media literacy skills. This method has shown potential in various domains, including climate-change denial, conspiracy theories and vaccine misinformation [25,26,30-32]. Sustained inoculation strategies can produce lasting gains in misinformation discernment [33], and more recent work has begun to apply pre-bunking to election ru-mours specifically [28,34]. However, its practical application at the scale and speed required by modern information ecosystems remains a challenge.
While traditional inoculation theory suggests that pre-bunking is most effective when administered before exposure to misinformation [35], recent research indicates that post-exposure 'therapeutic' inoculation can also be beneficial [36,37]. The success of post-exposure inoculation is particularly important for Republicans and those who endorse other (non-election) conspiracy theories, as these groups are already more likely to have been exposed to (and believe in) election misinformation. The interaction between partisan identity and pre-bunking effectiveness remains underexplored, with theoretical ambiguity about whether Republican identifiers-who have shown heightened susceptibility to certain forms of misinformation-may exhibit differential treatment responses to pre-bunking interventions owing to divergent information ecosystems, elite cuing effects or motivated reasoning processes.
A critical bottleneck limiting the societal impact of traditional pre-bunking is the reliance on time-consuming human expertise to identify emerging narratives, craftspecific weakened counter-arguments, and produce tailored inoculation messages. This manual process inherently struggles to match the pace and sheer volume of misinformation generated online, particularly during dynamic events like election campaigns. Rumours proliferate faster than bespoke interventions can typically be developed and deployed. In an effort to establish a scalable intervention, we introduce a novel element to the pre-bunking approach: the use of LLMs to assist in generating inoculation content. This application of AI offers the potential for rapid, scalable production of pre-bunking materials tailored to emerging misinformation narratives, addressing the core limitation of manual methods. Related work shows that AI dialogue can durably reduce conspiracy beliefs [20], that LLM-authored civic-information tools can inform voters [38] and that pre-emptive source discreditation can counter AI-generated misinformation [39]; recent reviews identify countering misinformation as a priority application for generative AI [21].
The scalability of anti-misinformation interventions is therefore not just an improvement, but potentially a necessary innovation. Recent work on election misinformation suggests that human-written fact checks do not durably increase confidence in election administration [40]. Those hoping to inform the public about the realities of the integrity of elections must currently respond to each false election rumour that arises. The sheer multiplicity of rumours makes a purely reactive, one-by-one debunking strategy insufficient.With their ability to take direction and to copy examples, LLMs are a natural option for quickly and semi-automatically producing effective inoculation doses of misinformation, generated at a speed unachievable by human authors alone.
3. Research design
To test the efficacy of LLM-generated pre-bunking, we conducted a pre-registered, two-wave experimental study prior to the 2024 US general election. Participants were randomly assigned to read a persuasive, human-written article endorsing one of five commonly believed election myths. Before doing so, participants were randomly assigned to either a treatment group, which received an LLM-generated pre-bunking article addressing the myth, or a control group, which received an LLM-generated article about return-to-office policies. All participants were then exposed to a 'full exposure' of misinformation in the form of a persuasive article promoting the assigned election myth. We measured participants' beliefs in election myths, confidence in true election facts and overall trust in election integrity immediately after the intervention and one week later.
Prompt creation is a crucial part of our experiment: if LLM-generated pre-bunking messages are to be scalable, it is important to develop prompts that are able to push against new and emerging disinformation. In the left-hand panel, we describe how we use an iterative process combination of AI and human review to generate a high-quality prompt (which in the figure is 'Prompt·"). The LLM is given a full-exposure article and a prompt asking it to produce an inoculation article. After each generation, the input prompt was edited by the researchers. This process was repeated until the researchers determined no additional edits to the generated inoculation article were required. The right-hand panel shows how we take Prompt·' for each rumour and use it to produce a final high-quality inoculation article, which we use as treatment.
Next, our experimental design is described in figure 2. Participants are randomly assigned to one of the five election rumours. Our design makes it possible to compare the efficacy of pre-bunking articles written by a collaboration between human experts and LLMs, and those written solely by LLMs, by comparing treatment effects for participants given the human review procedure and a wholly automated one.
In this paper, we test two main pre-registered hypotheses.
First, H1: participants exposed to pre-bunking of a specific election-related rumour will report lower confidence in that rumour compared to the control group.
Second, H2: participants exposed to pre-bunking of a specific election-related rumour will report higher confidence that their votes will be accurately counted in the next election compared to the control group. In addition, we report pre-registered analyses of the temporal durability and heterogeneity of these effects.1
Three measures of election confidence are common in the literature, corresponding with the belief that voters' own ballots were correctly counted, that ballots in their locality were correctly counted and that ballots across the nation were correctly counted. In this paper, we focus on confidence in the national election. Prior work has identified large gaps in perceptions of vote counting at the national level, where confidence is lower, and local levels, where confidence is higher [41]. Fraud at the level of the national election is thus more likely to be salient to voters, while also being more directly important to policy makers.
Our findings demonstrate that LLM-generated pre-bunking can effectively reduce belief in specific election-related rumours and increase confidence in accurate election facts, with these effects persisting for at least one week. The intervention appears to be similarly effective across party lines and ideologies, suggesting its potential as a broadly applicable tool for combating election misinformation.
4. Results
Pre-bunking counters the specific pieces of election misinformation included in the full-exposure articles, increases knowledge of true election facts and raises short-run confidence in national-level election administration. 4.1. Immediate persuasiveness of exposure articles
We begin by examining the persuasive force of the misinformation exposure itself. Figures S4 and S9 in the supplementary material plot the pre-versus post-treatment means for the rumour-specific and national election outcomes, with callouts that report the corresponding survey-weighted means, standard deviations and 95% confidence intervals.
Figure 3 shows that placebo respondents move from a pre-exposure mean of 4.89 (s.d. 3.72; 95% CI [4.70, 5.07]) to a post-exposure mean of 5.32 (s.d. 3.45; 95% CI [5.15, 5.49]), an average increase of 0.43 points on the 0-10 rumour scale (s.d. of the change 2.58; 95% CI [0.31, 0.56]). Treated respondents begin at 4.85 (s.d. 3.75; 95% CI [4.67, 5.03]) and end at 4.78 (s.d. 3.56; 95% CI [4.61, 4.95]), yielding a statistically indistinguishable change of -0.07 points (s.d. of the change 2.50; 95% CI [-0.19, 0.05]).
Figure 4 presents the parallel national election outcome. Placebo readers decline from 6.62 (s.d. 3.02; 95% CI [6.47, 6.77]) to 6.25 (s.d. 3.06; 95% CI [6.10, 6.40]), a -0.37 change (s.d. 1.63; 95% CI [-0.45,-0.29]). Treated respondents fall more modestly from 6.69 (s.d. 3.01; 95% CI [6.55, 6.83]) to 6.52 (s.d. 3.02; 95% CI [6.38, 6.67]), a -0.17 shift(s.d. 1.68; 95% CI [-0.25,-0.09]). Figure S9 in the supplementary material traces the same contrasts across rumour assignments and human-assistance variants.
Together, the persuasion plots show that misinformation articles shiftboth rumour beliefs and national election confidence among untreated readers, while the inoculation nearly eliminates those induced belief gains.
These descriptive contrasts set the stage for the regression-based treatment effects presented next.
4.2. Confidence in the truth of false election rumours
Figure 5 translates these descriptive contrasts into survey-weighted regression estimates. The inoculation reduces immediate confidence in the assigned election rumour by 0.55 points (SE 0.07; 95% CI [-0.68,-0.42]) on the 0-10 scale, conditional on baseline rumour belief, and the full set of covariates reported in table S1 in the supplementary material. Table S4 in the supplementary material lists similarly sized reductions when the models are estimated separately for each rumour assignment. One week later, the estimated effect attenuates to -0.17 points (SE 0.07; 95% CI [-0.31,-0.03]) but remains statistically distinguishable from zero, indicating durable-albeit smaller-inoculation benefits.
These regression results support pre-registered hypothesis H1: the LLM-written pre-bunking more than offsets the +0.43 persuasive boost generated by the misinformation articles in the placebo condition, leaving treated respondents below their baseline level of rumour belief.
4.3. Heterogeneous treatment effects by partisanship and ideology
Figure 7 summarizes how the inoculation's impact on rumour belief varies by party and ideology. The baseline treatment effect (evaluated for Democrats and liberals) is a -0.54 point reduction in rumour confidence one week after the exposure (95% CI [-0.81,-0.28]; table 1). The interaction coefficients are uniformly negative and statistically distinguishable from zero for independents (-0.50; 95% CI [-0.95,-0.04]) and Republicans (-0.46; 95% CI [-0.91,-0.00]), and for ideological moderates (-0.66; 95% CI [-1.14,-0.18]). These estimates imply that the total treatment effect is the largest in the groups that entered the study with higher baseline rumour credence: combining the main and interaction terms yields effects of approximately -1.04 among Republicans and -1.10 among moderates. In short, the inoculation is the strongest precisely where misinformation attachment is deepest, contradicting concerns that already-sceptical respondents would be the only beneficiaries.
Figure 6 shows the parallel estimates for national election confidence. The baseline treatment effect is +0.21 (95% CI [0.05, 0.38]), and the party and ideology interactions remain positive but underpowered (table 2); for example, the treatment-by-Republican coefficient is +0.18 (95% CI [-0.13, 0.48]). When we compute the implied total effects, Republicans gain an estimated 0.39 and moderates a similar 0.39 points, so point estimates still align with the 'stronger where needed most' pattern, albeit without conventional significance.
The heterogeneity patterns reflect shifts among both low-and high-belief respondents. Figures S6 and S12 in the supplementary material trace the raw trajectories: strong believers (e.g. Republicans who began at 8-10 on the rumour scale) move sharply downward, while sceptical groups stay anchored near zero. Tables S46 and S42 in the supplementary material show the same logic in binary terms-treatment cuts the share of Republicans scoring above the midpoint by 8-10 percentage points at both the immediate and recontact waves for the assigned rumour.
Turning to backlash concerns, the interaction tables reveal a single worrisome point estimate but no systematic reversals. For rumour belief, every interaction is negative, so we never observe a subgroup in which treatment increases rumour confidence (table 1). On the election-confidence side, table S9 in the supplementary material shows a negative treatment-by-Republican coefficient of -0.21 for county-ballot confidence, and table S13 in the supplementary material records a -0.39 (p < 0.01) interaction for conservatives on own-ballot confidence. Yet, the implied total effects remain positive: adding the main effect of 0.32 (column 1) to the conservative interaction yields -0.07 (s.e. 0.13), which is imprecise but not significantly below zero. Similarly, the county-ballot effect for Republicans nets to approximately 0.11 (0.14). Figures S12 and S11 in the supplementary material show that even when point estimates dip negative, the post-treatment means stay near zero. We therefore do not see reliable evidence of backlash in any subgroup, though the conservative own-ballot interaction remains noisy and merits continued monitoring.
Finally, we examine whether conspiratorial thinking moderates the inoculation's effectiveness. Because conspiracy thinking was pre-registered as an exploratory moderator, we summarize those interactions separately in figure 8. The baseline treatment effect (conspiracy score of zero) mirrors the main pooled estimate, and the interaction slope-interpreted as the change in the treatment effect for a one-point increase in the conspiracy index-has a wide confidence interval that comfortably includes zero. In short, we do not detect evidence that higher levels of conspiratorial beliefs systematically dampen or amplify the inoculation's impact on national ballot confidence. Table S29 in the supplementary material provides the corresponding coefficients, with unweighted specifications reported alongside that table.
4.4. Confidence in election administration
In support of pre-registered hypothesis H2 (that pre-bunking will increase confidence in the administration of the election), we see in figure 5 that treatment increases confidence in the administration of the national election. As we show in figure 4, this effect is driven both by participants in the placebo condition, demonstrating lower confidence in the national election (going from an average of 6.62 to 6.25; a difference of approx. 4 s.d., while treated participants go from an average of 6.69 to 6.52, a smaller decrease of approx. 2 s.d.). These results are replicated in table S1, which shows an average treatment effect of 0.189 (SE: 0.046) on a 10-point scale.
4.5. Party-level heterogeneity
Figure 6 shows that the inoculation boosts national election confidence for every partisan and ideological subgroup, with the largest gains accruing to independents and Republicans (approx. +0.39 each relative to the baseline Democrats) and similarly sized improvements among moderates and conservatives. Figure 7 reveals the same ordering on rumour confidence: the treatment knocks down belief most sharply among independents, Republicans and ideological moderates or conservatives, shrinking those groups' rumour credence by approximately a full point while leaving already-sceptical Democrats and liberals little room to move.
Heterogeneity by conspiratorial thinking is far more muted. As visualized in figure 8, the main effect at the minimum of the conspiracy scale mirrors the pooled estimate, and the interaction slope is close to zero across the observed range, indicating no systematic attenuation among respondents prone to conspiratorial beliefs.
One lingering worry is backlash, whereby counter-attitudinal messages entrench rumour belief or erode institutional trust. Figures 3 and 4, together with figs. S6 and S12 in the supplementary material, show that placebo respondents register the expected increases in rumour confidence and declines in election confidence, whereas treated respondents stay at or slightly below their pre-treatment levels. The resulting post-treatment means remain near zero for the rumour outcomes and positive for national confidence, even among Republicans-the group most predisposed towards the misinformation. Consequently, we find no evidence of systematic backlash, consistent with recent experimental work suggesting that such effects are rare [43].
4.6. Discussion
Our results suggest that AI-assisted pre-bunking can effectively and durably reduce belief in election-related rumours (H1). In this regard, pre-bunking appears to be an effective tool for providing information and for inoculating against particular election rumours. For the harder task of increasing belief in the integrity of national elections, however, while we find that pre-bunking has a strong immediate effect, this effect dissipates within a week (H2). These results align with other recent work targeting election misinformation [40] and might require regular 'boosting' [44].
There is a tension in the literature on pre-bunking as to the optimal method of engagement: pre-bunking that focuses on specific misleading claims versus pre-bunking that focuses on misleading narrative structure [45]. One advantage of our method is that generative AI formulates the intervention by learning the narrative structure of other false claims.
The primary advantage of our approach, however, is simply the scalability. After finalizing the prompt used to produce inoculation articles, we find no evidence that articles written with human feedback are more effective at pre-bunking than articles using only AI. Importantly, this comparison is between AI-only and AI+human review at the article level: the finalized prompt was produced through substantial human iteration, so what these results show is that per-article review can be dropped without sacrificing effectiveness, not that human expertise is eliminated from the workflow. If we can rely on generative AI for effective interventions once the prompt is in place, we can intervene more quickly and at scale within the election-integrity domain. Given that misinformation poses a fundamental threat to democratic governance, evaluating the ability of scalable interventions has never been more pressing. Akey implication of these findings is that these results provide a clear pathway for building pre-bunking arguments at scale, such as via chatbot delivery.
Future work could couple this pre-bunking workflow with automated prompt-optimization frameworks such as DSPy [46], which iteratively tune LLM prompts to new objective functions. By feeding real-time rumour corpora into a prompt-tuning loop, researchers could adapt inoculation messaging to emerging narratives while maintaining guardrails on tone, evidence and factual grounding. Combining those tools with ongoing experimental audits would help balance adaptiveness with reliability, especially when rumours evolve faster than static prompts can capture.
While our experimental design does not make it possible to directly test the effect of human-written versus AI-written articles for pre-bunking, it is possible to test for differences between human-assisted writing and purely AI writing by comparing estimated treatment effects across rumours. Figures S7, S10 and S11 in the supplementary material (see also supplementary material, tables S2 and S4) show no evidence that purely AI-written inoculation articles are less effective than those written with human assistance: treatment interactions with assigned rumour are insignificant, as are interactions comparing human-assisted and purely AI-written inoculations. At the rumour level, the smaller sample size naturally reduces statistical power. Even so, some degree of heterogeneity in treatment effects is apparent across rumours. This may be owing to differences in the inoculation articles that AI produced, the full-exposure articles or even the salience of individual election rumours themselves. It may also be because we are underpowered: each rumour only contains a fifth of the overall sample.
With this caveat, two rumours warrant particular attention, for opposite reasons: 'voter rolls' and 'blue shift'. These rumours, respectively, have the largest and smallest changes in rumour confidence for the placebo group-in other words, they were the most and least persuasive full-exposure articles. On average, the placebo group was a full point (1.01 on a scale from 0 to 10) more confident in the voter roll rumour after reading the article (pre-treatment and post-treatment means for placebo group: (4.78, 5.79), s.d.: (0.20, 0.18)), while for the blue shiftrumour, the placebo group exhibited decreased confidence in the rumour after reading the article (pre-and post-treatment means for the placebo group: (5.21, 5.07), s.d.: (0.20, 0.20)).3 Put differently, the rumour for which our inoculation proved ineffective was exactly the rumour that did not need to be inoculated against because it was unconvincing.
Perhaps the greatest success of our intervention is in mitigating the persuasive effects of articles containing false information. For each rumour, untreated participants were convinced by the full-exposure articles that the election rumours were true. Treated participants, regardless of party, were not: for example, treated voter roll participants were on average only slightly more confident in the rumour (pretreatment and post-treatment means for treatment group: (4.68, 4.73), s.d.: (0.20, 0.19)). Compared with the 1.01 point increase for untreated participants, the mere 0.05 point confidence increase represents a substantial protective effect of our informational inoculation (see supplementary material, fig. S7 or table S4 for more detail). This suggests that an effective strategy for increasing confidence in our robust electoral system is to use the methods proposed in this paper to actively inoculate against election rumours. Rather than targeting individual rumours, an LLM-enabled approach makes it possible to combat election misinformation en masse.
Doing so would require a system that could identify (or be provided with) new election rumours, build targeted inoculations and disseminate them to susceptible people who have yet to encounter the misinformation. By partnering with academics and government agencies, platforms such as social media companies could develop inoculations for vulnerable populations during key moments like the run-up to election day.
4.7. Scope and limitations
Our AI-assisted misinformation inoculation system could in principle be extended to issues other than election rumours and false election narratives.We encourage the testing of this method for other issues, in particular, in contexts where rapidly evolving rumours and misinformation are a pressing problem (e.g. public health). The present evidence, however, speaks only to the election-integrity case: our five rumors all concern US election integrity, our authoritative information source is a single federal agency, the Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency (CISA), and our full-exposure stimuli are adapted from a single partisan outlet. Whether a similarly refined prompt paired with domain-appropriate authoritative information (e.g. Centers for Disease Control and Prevention fact sheets, Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change summaries) produces comparable effects is an empirical question.
Our full-exposure articles were deliberately adapted from a single partisan outlet, chosen both because its 2024 election-integrity coverage was demonstrably persuasive to the target population and because holding the outlet constantly reduces stylistic noise. This strengthens internal validity, but it also scopes what the estimates reflect: effects may look different when misinformation takes shorter, lower-quality or more socially embedded forms (platform posts, memes and short-form videos), and when the source is mixed or ambiguous rather than overtly partisan. Extending the evaluation to such stimuli, and to misinformation encountered in-network rather than as a stand-alone article, is a natural next step for this line of research.
Finally, our outcome measures capture self-reported belief in specific election rumours and self-reported confidence in election administration. These are the central targets of inoculation theory, but they are not a full proxy for downstream behaviour. We do not directly observe whether pre-bunking changes sharing, protest participation, turnout, vote choice or other observable actions. Pairing these self-report measures with behavioural outcomes-for example, experimental sharing paradigms, validated turnout or panel consumption data-is an important complement to the findings reported here.
5. Conclusion
Pre-bunking, whether delivered via articles written with human feedback or only using AI (given a human-refined prompt), appears to be effective at mitigating confidence in false election rumours and increasing confidence in robust national elections. However, the protective effects of pre-bunking appear to be durable only for reducing confidence in election rumours. This may be because factual pre-bunks are sufficient to push back against specific pieces of misinformation (rumours) but not against broader or more ingrained attitudes (scepticism surrounding election results). More research is needed to determine whether other approaches-for example, more intensive or involved (inoculation) treatments-are sufficient to counter scepticism surrounding secure elections, whether the workflow transfers to other misinformation domains and whether effects on self-reported belief translate into behavioural change. To encourage experimentation outside the lab, we make the article-generation workflow available as an interactive prototype at electionbot.chat/article.
6. Survey and experimental design
We conducted a two-wave study prior to the 2024 general election. The first wave was fielded online by YouGov, 7-14 August 2024. YouGov selected subjects from their opt-in panel to be representative of the population of US registered voters and 4293 subjects completed the first wave of the study. The second wave was fielded by YouGov, 21-26 August 2024. Subjects from the first wave were recontacted and given an opportunity to participate in the follow-up study. The recontact rate was 82%, with 3520 subjects completing the second wave of the study. See the supplementary material for a discussion of sample attrition. YouGov provided sample weights that we use for the analyses reported here.
6.1. Survey design
This study uses data from two surveys that were conducted by YouGov.
The first survey was fielded online 7-14 August 2024, and contains the responses from 4293 US registered voters. YouGov selected respondents from their opt-in panel to be representative of the population of US registered voters, and weighted the sample to gender, age, race and education (based on the US Census Bureau's American Community Survey), and to the 2020 Presidential vote, 2022 congressional vote and baseline party identification (the respondent's most recent party identification answer, given prior to 1 November 2022). These weights range from 0.1 to 6.0, have a mean of 1.0 and a standard deviation of 0.6. YouGov estimates the margin of error for the sample to be 1.7%.
The second survey was fielded online 21-26 August 2024, again by YouGov. Respondents from the first survey were recontacted and invited to participate in this second survey, with 3520 completed interviews (an 82% recontact rate). The recontact sample was weighted to adjust to the first national sample using the same features as were part of the first sample's weighting scheme. The recontact sample weights have a mean of 1.0, a standard deviation of 0.6 and range from 0.2 to 5.5. YouGov estimates the margin of error for the recontact survey to be 1.9%.
As we show in the supplementary material, we see no evidence of differential drop-offby treatment effect across survey waves.
6.2. Inoculation article writing process
The two panels of figure 1 describe our procedure for using LLMs to write inoculation articles for different election myths. As shown in the left-hand panel, we use a single myth (concerning general voter fraud) to draftthe prompt we eventually use to produce all inoculation articles using a human review process: given the full-exposure article, a set of baseline facts about election integrity, and an initial prompt, we iteratively produce sample inoculation articles, manually review them and then edit the prompt in order to produce new inoculation articles.We repeat this process until human reviewers (with prior expertise in writing pre-bunk or inoculation articles) are satisfied with the inoculation article. We call the prompt that produced this article Prompt·', and as we show in the right-hand panel, we use it to produce all inoculation articles in the study. Human expertise is therefore front-loaded into prompt design rather than eliminated: once Prompt·' has been produced, subsequent per-rumour articles can be generated without additional human review, but the prompt-refinement step itself requires substantial iteration by trained reviewers.
In all cases of artificially generated text, we used Anthropic's Claude 3.5 Sonnet. As part of the prompt, the LLM was given the full-exposure article and was asked to write a response that could serve as an 'inoculation' for the myth in question. These 'full-exposure articles' were all human-written and taken from the same website (Breitbart). This was done both to minimize heterogeneity owing to different writing styles and to test our intervention against content known to be persuasive to the target population (i.e. a strong rather than a weak exposure). We discuss the corresponding boundaries on external validity in §4.7. Even so, the articles themselves may vary in their ability to persuade different audiences, in addition to potential variation in the salience of the underlying election rumours themselves.
We also supplied the LLM with information taken from the 'Rumor vs. Reality'4 section of the CISA website, which is part of the Department of Homeland Security. Complete details of the prompt can be found in the supplementary material.
6.3. Experimental design
Our experimental design is described in figure 2. The experiment consists of four phases: pre-treatment questions, articles, post-treatment questions and follow-up questions after one week.
All participants completed the same pre-treatment battery of questions, which included demographic information, political affiliation, a series of questions about their beliefs in election myths and facts and their confidence in the integrity of the upcoming election.
Participants were then randomized into one of five treatment arms, each corresponding with one of the election myths in table 3: voter fraud, voter rolls, hacking, blue shiftand voting machines. For each treatment arm, participants were then further randomized into treatment or control. Participants in the treatment condition were shown a single short pre-bunking article related to the treatment rumour, created as described above. Participants in the control condition were shown a neutral article about the effect of remote work on urban planning. For the placebo article, the LLM was given one of the inoculation articles and asked to produce a similar article exploring the effects of remote work on urban planning. Complete details of the prompts and produced articles are contained in the supplementary material.
All participants in each treatment arm were then asked to read an article about their assigned election myth, which we call the full exposure of the myth. These articles were adapted from actual Breitbart articles that advocated for the myth in question, lightly edited to reduce average reading time to two to three minutes.5 Without pre-bunking, these Breitbart messages appear to be persuasive: control participants who only read the full-exposure article had decreased confidence in the national election and increased confidence in the election rumour across specifications. For example, fig. S4 in the supplementary material shows that participants in the placebo condition went from an average rumour-confidence score of 4.89 to 5.32 (pre-treatment versus post-treatment; an increase of approx. 4 s.d.), while participants in the treatment condition went from an average of 4.85 to 4.78 (a decrease of approx. 1 s.d.).
After reading the article, participants were then asked to complete a brief post-treatment battery of questions, which included questions about their beliefs in election myths, and their confidence in the integrity of the 2024 election. Finally, a week after the first wave of the study, participants were asked to complete a second wave of the survey, which included the same battery of election myth and election-confidence questions as the first wave.
We include the full text of the full-exposure articles, the prompts used to generate the inoculation articles and the inoculation articles themselves in the supplementary material.
All analyses were pre-registered unless otherwise noted below (https://doi.org/10.17605/OSF.IO/ S3R95). We did not pre-register comparisons between treatment articles that were written only by the AI and articles written with human assistance. In the manuscript, H2 corresponds to H3 in the preregistration, and the manuscript's H3 corresponds to H4 in the pre-registration. Owing to space constraints, we do not discuss pre-registration H2 (treated participants will report lower confidence in all election rumours, in addition to their assigned rumour) or pre-registration H5 (participants who initially believe more rumours will show smaller treatment effects). While our pre-registration H6 notes that we will test for heterogeneity across a number of factors, including partisanship, we did not specifically pre-register our analyses by political party.
