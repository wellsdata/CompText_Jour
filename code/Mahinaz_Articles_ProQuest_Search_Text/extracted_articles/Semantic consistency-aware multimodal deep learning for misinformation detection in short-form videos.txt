Introduction and background The rapid advancement of digital communication technologies has fundamentally transformed how information is created, shared, and consumed across the globe. Over the latest years, their popularity has risen, with the short-form video being considered one of the primary sources of content distribution, acquiring billions of users across the world (Singhal et al. 2020; Zhou and Zafarani 2020). This has considerably enhanced the speed at which information diffuses but it has also enabled the rapid dissemination of false information in a very short time which is perilous to society, politics and the economy (Shu et al. 2017; Nguyen et al. 2020). Misinformation in short-form videos is especially hard to control, as the content itself is viral and has very little control over it, owing to a lack of sufficient moderation.
Compared to the traditional misinformation on a textual platform, short video misinformation poses unique problems because of its multimodal characteristic. Visual data, audio cues and written materials (captions and subtitles) may be used in two channels (in parallel) to deliver information (Baltrušaitis et al. 2018). Lies are frequently made up with subtextual differences in these modalities instead of blatant lies. As an illustration, images that look real might be accompanied with distorted sound or false graphical overlays, and an illusionary, but credible story may be told (Deng et al. 2026; Yu et al. 2019). Such cross-modal discrepancies are only detected with the help of models that comprehend the complex relationship between the heterogeneous data sources.
In order to overcome this obstacle, scholars have started focusing more on multimodal ways of learning which incorporates visual, audio, and textual elements. Initial attempts were mainly based on unimodal analysis where attention was drawn to either visual or written information, and was not able to detect contextual dependencies (Arevalo et al. 2020; Devlin et al. 2019). More modern works have used multimodal deep learning models which use Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs), Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs), and transformer-based models to extract and combine information between modalities (Vaswani et al. 2017; Liu et al. 2019; Xu et al. 2023; Han et al. 2021). Fusion mechanisms driven by attention have also been used to further improve performance by dynamically assigning weight to the various modalities and thus enhances the representation learning (Xu et al. 2020; Guo et al. 2025).
Although such progress has been made, there are still a number of crucial drawbacks. To begin with, most of the available solutions focus on feature fusion, without directly learning semantic cross-modal links. Consequently, they might not be able to detect inconsistencies, which are critical to detect misinformation (Sahar et al. 2026; Afchar et al. 2018). Second, multimodal models are less interpretable compared to most of the frameworks because most of them lack mechanisms to understand how cross-modal interaction affects the prediction (Li and Lyu 2018; Khan et al. 2026). Third, much of previous research concentrates on long-form videos or non-video multimodal datasets, which fails to capture the nature of short-form videos, including quick scene cuts and less information presentation time and again (Khalid et al. 2021). Also, such models cannot identify hidden contradictions because of the lack of explicit mechanisms that can measure the cross-modal semantic alignment.
These issues inspired this group of research by suggesting a semantic consistency-aware multimodal deep learning model to detect misinformation in short-form videos. The main concept is to go beyond traditional fusion of features through an explicit modeling of the level of agreement or inconsistency among visual, audio and textual displays. Integrating a semantic consistency analysis element with attention-intensive fusion, the framework will replicate fine details of cross-modal inconsistencies, reflecting deceptive content.
The proposed model is distinct from existing multimodal approaches, which typically focus on feature fusion. While current approaches consider the different modalities as complementary sources of information, they do not explicitly model discrepancies between the modalities, which are prevalent in misinformation. The proposed framework overcomes this caveat by utilising a semantic consistency score for classification, which allows it to perceive inconsistencies instead of only confirming the consistency. This enables a more specific way to detect misinformation in short videos. The proposed approach is a mixture of a cross-modality feature extractor and a consistency-based reasoning mechanism. Visual features are extracted using deep convolutional representations; audio features are extracted using recurrent structures that model audio sequences; and text features are extracted using transformer-based semantic embeddings that model text semantics. The feature representations are combined in a manner that highlights most relevant modalities using an attention mechanism. At the same time, a semantic consistency module is used to measure the consistency in between the pairs of modalities, which provides an additional cue of cross-modal consistency.
In order to demonstrate the proposed model's efficiency, several experiments are conducted on various datasets including a cross-platform short video dataset. Assessment of the approach was performed on standard performance measures and the proposed method is compared with unimodal baselines, and traditional multimodal fusion approaches. Qualitative analyses are also presented to provide additional information to quantitative measures to provide an insight into how the model works and interactions between the modalities.
While the technique has achieved a satisfactory performance, there are some limitations. In particular, the current design is more focused on semantic representation matching and does not specifically model time dynamics between video sequences and this may not be adequately reflected against time-varying inconsistencies (Mazhar et al. 2026). In addition, the study has chosen a measure of consistency as cosine similarity and the measure is computationally efficient and hence may not sufficiently represent the complex modal issues of any semantic inconsistencies (Fawcett 2006). These suggestions are guidance for future work, such as the inclusion of time modeling and other representational consistency training. Literature review The fast growth of multimedia content in the social media has also contributed greatly to misinformation detection research. Specifically, multimodal and dynamic content has become a challenge owing to the emergence of short-form video platforms. This subdivision will study the literature related to multimodal detection of misinformation based on fusion schemes, semantic consistency modelling and short video analysis. It also proposes the important drawbacks of solutions in the present, which drives the necessity behind more discoverable and sensible structures.
Overview of multimodal misinformation detection Misinformation detection One of the earliest efforts to deal with misinformation detection has been with textual analysis methods, such as those associated with statistics or deep learning-based natural language processing models (Singhal et al. 2020; Zhou and Zafarani 2020; Shu et al. 2017). Although these methodologies have shown good results in preliminary research, they are comparatively restricted when multimedia content is considered, in which important data is shared among various modalities.
In order to address these constraints, more recent studies have migrated to multimodal learning models incorporating visual, textual and audio support (Nguyen et al. 2020; Baltrušaitis et al. 2018). In image processing, CNNs have been extensively utilized as feature extractors and RNNs and transformers are the most frequently used in audio and texts modeling sequential and contextual information (Deng et al. 2026; Yu et al. 2019; Arevalo et al. 2020; Devlin et al. 2019; Vaswani et al. 2017). Multimodal strategies have shown high performance due to the exploitation of complementary information in terms of modality especially in detecting fake news, classifying video, and verifying multimedia (Vaswani et al. 2017; Liu et al. 2019).
Even with these developments, current approaches tend to be based on implicit fusion of features without the necessary structure of modeling the correspondence between modalities. Because of this they might not learn the presence of subtle inconsistencies in the form of misinformation (Han et al. 2021; Sun et al. 2023).
Multimodal fusion techniques Multimodal fusion is at the heart of the combination of heterogeneous data sources. The currently existing fusion strategies may be divided into early fusion, late fusion and hybrid approaches.
Early fusion algorithms fuse features through input-level combinations, and can therefore learn joint representations; however, they tend to be noise-sensitive and high-dimensionality (Xu et al. 2020). Late fusion methods combine individual modalities prediction, which has flexibility, but there is no cross-modal interaction (Guo et al. 2025). Hybrid approaches strive to mitigate these trade-offs by integrating the two approaches (Rana et al. 2022).
Recent research has made use of attention-based fusion mechanisms to weigh dynamically contributions of modality. Such ways enhance performance by emphasizing on pertinent features at the expense of noise (Afchar et al. 2018; Li and Lyu 2018). As an illustration, multimodal frameworks guided by attention have proved to be effective in activities like video comprehension and misinformation (Khalid et al. 2021).
Nevertheless, the majority of fusion methods focus on merging features as opposed to semantic fusion. They tend to consider modalities as separate sources of information and do not explicitly model the contradiction or the inconsistencies between them. This drawback decreases their usefulness in the case of misinformation that is very complex (Mazhar et al. 2026; Bai et al. 2022).
Semantic consistency and cross-modal alignment To address the weakness of the currently used methods of fusion, recent research has shifted its focus towards semantic consistency modeling, and cross-modal alignment.These approaches aim at quantifying the level of agreement or mismatch of modalities.A variety of works of different nature have proposed consistency-based models, which test the relationships between multimodal embeddings.To illustrate this, the discriminative modalities have been counter-paired with contrastive learning modalities that retain the discriminative modalities (Fawcett 2006; Powers 2020). Even graph-based models develop the cross-modal interaction, but with refined complicated dependencies with structured representations (Zhou and Zafarani 2020).
Also, there are inconsistency-conscious networks, which are able to explicitly represent contradictions among modalities, which facilitates more effective detection of false information (Radford et al. 2021). These processes show that semantic relationship modelling is important to comprehend multimodal information.
Nonetheless, a lot of current methods use preset similarity measures or shallow-alignment algorithms. These approaches would not necessarily be able to capture more semantic contradictions, especially in short video versions in which misinformation is sometimes nuanced and contextualised (Zhou et al. 2020b).
Unlike contrastive learning and CLIP-style approaches that focus on aligning representations across modalities, the proposed framework models semantic consistency as a decision-level signal. Rather than enforcing similarity in a shared embedding space, it evaluates agreement across modalities and integrates this information into the classification process to detect cross-modal discrepancies indicative of misinformation.
Multimodal misinformation detection in short-form videos Recently, short-form video apps have presented novel challenges to the detection of misinformation. In comparison to long-form content, short videos are already characterized by quick shifts, lack of context, and a very condensed flow of information (Liu et al. 2026; Nan et al. 2021).
Very recent research has delved into multimodal models adapted to the analysis of short-form video. These methods combine visual, audio and textual characteristics in a bid to enhance the accuracy of detection (Wu et al. 2023; Figueroa et al. 2022). External signals are used in some works like the user metadata and engagement patterns to increase credibility assessment (Wang et al. 2018).
More recent hacks into transformer architecture and large-scale pre-trained models have also advanced the topic by providing cross-modal contextual reasoning (Jin et al. 2017; Wu et al. 2020). Moreover, explainable AI methods have been presented to enhance interpretability so that models could show case the features that are most relevant, and it may help understand decision-making processes.
Irrespective of these developments, there are a number of constraints. Most of these techniques are based on fixed representations of features and fail to explicitly capture the temporal dynamics which play a critical role in understanding video contents. Moreover, these methods lack much power to identify subtle cross-modal discrepancies since explicit semantic consistency modeling is not explicitly modeled (Wu et al. 2020; Zhou et al. 2020a; OpenAI 2023).

Emerging trends and research gaps Recent innovations of multimodal learning have proposed novel methods including graph neural networks, contrastive learning, and Large Language Models (LLMs) (Touvron et al. 2023; Li et al. 2022). Such solutions are focused on enhancing the learning of representation, reasoning, and scalability.
Graphical approaches represent the connection between modalities and establish structured representations of relationships between modalities, which allow distinguishing additional contextual insights (Garcez and Lamb 2023). In the same vein, Multimodal analysis is also made better by the use of LLC-based structures which use a sense of contextual knowledge and reasoning to improve the analysis (Lewis et al. 2020). Deep learning with symbolic reasoning have also been suggested as hybrid methods to enhance interpretability and robustness (Hamroun and Sauveron 2025; Povedano Álvarez et al. 2023).
Nevertheless, there are still a number of research gaps:
- Lack of explicit semantic consistency modeling across all modalities

- Limited interpretability in multimodal frameworks

- Insufficient incorporation of temporal dynamics in video analysis

- Dependence on simple similarity measures for cross-modal alignment

- Limited generalization across datasets and platforms.



The challenges provide the necessity to have stronger structures that incorporate semantic reasoning, interpretability, and multimodal learning to be able to detect misinformation in short-form videos.
Recent work such as evaluating the efficiency and factual reliability of LoRA-based models for health misinformation detection further highlights the importance of model efficiency and factual robustness in misinformation analysis. There are also recent advances in the area of misinformation detection and multimodal learning. For example, (Li et al. 2024a) investigates misinformation feature detection through unsupervised learning and network modelling approaches, underscoring the role of structural and latent semantic features in detecting misinformation. Moreover, recent advances in multimodal fusion techniques across various applications have shown the benefits of combining diverse data sources in complex decision-making scenarios. Studies like (Li et al. 2025) and (Li et al. 2024b) employ sophisticated fusion schemes and attention-enhanced models to improve detection performance in a multimodal setting, while (Li et al. 2023) emphasizes the fusion of signal decomposition with deep learning for enhanced interpretability.
Additionally, recent methods that harness the power of large language models, like (Li et al. 2025), highlight the increasing importance of contextual awareness and knowledge infusion in predictive tasks. While these approaches are applied in various contexts, they all highlight the significance of multimodal integration, feature alignment and strategies for intelligent fusion. However, existing methods are mainly based on feature-level fusion and do not explicitly incorporate semantic mismatches between different modalities for short-form video misinformation. This motivates our framework.

Despite the progress in multimodal misinformation detection, most existing approaches focus on feature fusion and representation learning, without explicitly modeling semantic inconsistencies across modalities. As a result, these methods may overlook conflicting information between text, visual, and audio signals. This limitation motivates the need for approaches that explicitly capture cross-modal discrepancies, which is the focus of the proposed framework.
Unlike approaches such as EANN and MDFEND, which focus on event-aware or domain-aware feature learning, the proposed framework explicitly models cross-modal semantic consistency as a separate decision signal. Similarly, transformer-based multimodal models emphasize representation learning but do not directly capture contradictions between modalities. In contrast, the proposed approach focuses on identifying inconsistencies across modalities, which are particularly indicative of misinformation.
Proposed methodology This subsection introduces the suggested semantic consistency-aware multimodal model that is used to identify misinformation in short-video content. The most important rationale that lies near the suggested approach is that false information is usually employed as a result of the mismatch within various modalities, including the discrepancy between pictorial scenes, acoustic statements and written explanations. The framework should handle this issue by collectively modeling text, visual and audio data and explicitly assessing the semantic concordance of these data. The state-of-the-art system has four major nodes: (i) modal feature extraction, (ii) multimodal fusion by attention, (iii) semantic consistency modeling and (iv) classification. Overall architecture of the proposed framework

Multimodal feature extraction The structural architecture of the proposed framework is shown in Fig. 1. This model has encoders dedicated to each of the modalities and combines the results with the help of a fusion mechanism. A semantic consistency unit is included to process the explicit evaluation of cross-modal agreement prior to the derivation of the final prediction.
As shown in Fig. 1, textual, visual, and audio inputs are processed in parallel using specialized encoders (transformer-based model for text, convolutional network for visual data, and recurrent network for audio). This parallel design preserves modality-specific characteristics while avoiding premature information mixing. The extracted features are then combined using an attention-based fusion mechanism, followed by semantic consistency evaluation and classification.
The key distinction of the proposed framework lies in treating semantic consistency as an explicit modeling component rather than an implicit outcome of feature fusion. Instead of assuming that modalities reinforce each other, the proposed approach evaluates their agreement and uses this information as a decision factor. This enables the model to identify cross-modal inconsistencies, which are particularly indicative of misinformation in short-form video content.
Multimodal feature extraction To capture the intrinsic characteristics of each modality, the framework employs separate feature extraction mechanisms.
Visual feature representation Given an input video V , a sequence of frames is sampled: 1
F = { f 1 , f 2 , ⋯ , f T }
Equation (1) defines the extraction of representative frames from the video, where T denotes the number of sampled frames.
Each frame is encoded using a convolutional neural network: 2
v t = ϕ v ( f t ; θ v )
Here, ϕ v represents the visual encoder, and θ v denotes its learnable parameters. This step extracts spatial features from each frame.
The overall visual representation is obtained as: 3
v = 1 T ∑ t = 1 T v t
Temporal aggregation is done in Eq. (3) and the final representation is made so that it reflects the entire visual context of the video.
In the current framework, visual features are extracted from sampled frames and aggregated using average pooling. This design focuses on capturing semantic content rather than temporal dynamics. While temporal modeling can provide additional cues for detecting evolving inconsistencies in video sequences, the proposed approach prioritizes cross-modal semantic alignment across modalities. Incorporating explicit temporal modeling is considered as a potential extension of this work.
Audio feature representation The audio signal is modeled as a temporal sequence: 4
h t = ϕ a ( x t ; θ a ) where h t represents the hidden state at time step t , and ϕ a is the audio encoder.
The final audio representation is given by: 5
a = h T
The general temporal information is a part of Eq. (5) that uses the final hidden state of the sequence.
Textual feature representation The textual modality is encoded using a transformer-based model: 6
t = ϕ t ( X ; θ t ) where X represents the input text and ϕ t denotes the encoder. The representation forms contextual and semantic relations in the text.
Attention-based multimodal fusion Once it has been done to get the modality-specific features, the next thing is to combine them into one representation. Rather than just concatenating, a dynamically shifting attention-based fusion mechanism is utilized to place dynamic importance on each modality. 7
Z = { v , a , t }
A set of the modality representations is defined by Eq. (7).
The attention weights are calculated as: 8
α i = exp ( w T z i ) ∑ j exp ( w T z j )
Normalized scores of importance weights are computed in Eq. (8), where wis a is a learnable parameter vector.
It is obtained as: 9
z = ∑ i α i z i
Equation (9) produces a weighted combination of modality features, enabling adaptive multimodal integration.
Semantic consistency modeling One of the key contributions of this work is explicit modeling of semantic consistency between modalities. Although fusion brings in complementary information, it does not necessarily ensure that modalities agree. Thus, a measure of consistency is proposed.
Equation (10) uses cosine similarity to quantify the agreement between two modalities. The correlation between the modality pairs is calculated as: 10
S ij = z i · z j ‖ z i ‖ ‖ z j ‖
Although the semantic consistency is computed using cosine similarity, this choice is motivated by several practical considerations. Cosine similarity is widely used in representation learning for measuring alignment between embedding spaces due to its computational efficiency and scale invariance. It provides a stable and interpretable measure of cross-modal agreement, which is particularly suitable for multimodal data where embeddings may differ in magnitude but preserve directional semantics.
Alternative approaches, such as learnable similarity functions, cross-modal attention alignment, and graph-based interaction modeling, may capture deeper semantic relationships. However, these methods typically introduce higher computational complexity and reduced interpretability. The proposed approach prioritizes a balance between effectiveness, interpretability, and computational efficiency, while leaving more advanced consistency modeling as a direction for future work.
Moreover, the objective of this work is not to model complex semantic reasoning, but to explicitly capture alignment or discrepancy between modalities as a decision signal. In this context, cosine similarity offers a simple yet effective mechanism to quantify agreement across text, visual, and audio representations.
The overall consistency score is defined as: 11
S = 1 3 ( S va + S vt + S at )
In Eq. (11), the similarities of pairs are summed up, giving a universal static of cross-modal congruence. Smaller values show the possibility of inconsistencies which are mostly characterized by misinformation. The textual ( t ), visual ( v ), and audio ( a ) representatio ns are compared using pairwise cosine similarity to compute cross-modal alignment scores. Summary The proposed method combines a modality-specific feature extraction, attention-type fusion, and semantic consistency modeling in one framework. The approach is better at detecting and interpreting relationships between modalities simply because it explicitly relates modalities by exploiting cross-modal associations. The following paragraph explains the experimental set-up that was employed to determine the efficacy of the proposed procedure.
Experimental setup In this section, an experimental design to test the given semantic consistency-aware multimodal framework is presented. The analysis is oriented on the results of the model to identify whether it is effective in the context of identifying misinformation in short-form video in the real settings. In order to guarantee an objective and complete analysis, several sets of data, common methods of preprocessing, clearly defined measure of evaluation, and baseline comparisons are taken into account.
Datasets Experiments are conducted on two multimodal benchmark datasets consisting of short-form videos annotated for misinformation detection. These datasets include textual, visual, and audio modalities, making them suitable for evaluating cross-modal semantic consistency.
The SocialVidMix dataset consists of approximately N short-form videos collected from publicly available social media platforms, such as YouTube Shorts, TikTok, and Instagram Reels etc. The dataset contains both authentic and misleading content, annotated through a combination of manual verification and cross-referencing with established fact-checking sources. Multimodal data includes (i) text from captions and subtitles, (ii) video frames, and (iii) audio represented as features.
The balance of the classes is about 50 percent real and 50 percent misinformation. Preprocessing of data involves sampling of video frames to get visual information, extraction of spectrograms to get audio information and normalization of all textual data to get normal textual data.The dataset is divided into training, validation, and testing sets with the ratio of 80:10:10 to guarantee fair evaluation. The dataset is aimed to represent real-life multimodal misinformation situations, when inconsistencies might occur across modalities. Data preprocessing A variety of preprocessing steps are used to make all modalities consistent and enhance the performance of the models.
- For the visual modality, frames are uniformly sampled from each video and resized to a fixed resolution. Normalization is applied to stabilize training and reduce variance across samples.

- For the audio modality, raw audio signals are converted into time–frequency representations (e.g., spectrograms), enabling the model to capture temporal patterns effectively, and

- For the textual modality , input text is tokenized and encoded using a transformer-compatible tokenizer. Special tokens and padding are applied to maintain uniform input length.

Baseline models While the current study compares the proposed framework with standard baselines, including unimodal models and early/late fusion techniques, more advanced multimodal approaches such as transformer-based and cross-modal learning models (e.g., EANN (Wang et al. 2018), MDFEND (Nan et al. 2021), and CLIP-based frameworks (Radford et al. 2021) have been explored in recent literature. Due to differences in dataset characteristics, implementation complexity, and the lack of consistent benchmarking settings, direct comparison with these methods is not included. However, the proposed framework is conceptually aligned with these approaches in modeling cross-modal relationships, while uniquely emphasizing semantic consistency as a decision-level signal.
Given differences in dataset availability and implementation details, these complex models are not considered in this study and left for future work. However, the proposed framework shares the same goal as these studies in capturing multimodal interactions, especially through explicit semantic consistency, which is a driving motivation of the proposed framework. To evaluate the proposed method, comparisons are made with a number of baseline models that represent varying degrees of modality use:
- Text-only model: Uses transformer-based encoding for textual input

- Visual-only model: Uses convolutional features from video frames

- Audio-only model: Uses sequential modeling of audio signals

- Early fusion model: Combines features at the input level

- Late fusion model: Combines predictions from individual modalities



These baselines provide a clear understanding of the contribution of multimodal fusion and semantic consistency modeling.
Experimental protocol The data is split into 80–10-10 with 80 serving as a training set, 10 as a validation set, and 10 as a test set. The training set is used to train the model and the hyperparameter tuning is performed on the validation set. The test set records final performance to have unbiased performance. To enhance reliability, experiments are repeated several times and mean results are provided. Although mean results are reported, future work will include statistical analysis with standard deviation to further strengthen reliability.

To further analyze the contribution of individual components, the ablation study examines the impact of multimodal integration and semantic consistency modeling. The results demonstrate that removing the semantic consistency component leads to a noticeable decline in performance, confirming its role in capturing cross-modal discrepancies. Additionally, experiments with different modality combinations indicate that the integration of all three modalities (text, visual, and audio) yields the best performance, while partial modality configurations result in reduced accuracy. This highlights the importance of multimodal representation for misinformation detection.
Although the current study focuses on a single consistency formulation, alternative approaches such as learnable similarity functions and attention-based alignment may provide additional improvements. Furthermore, robustness to missing modalities and cross-dataset generalization are important aspects that will be explored in future work.
Functionally, semantic consistency modelling results in better detection of minor discrepancies that can be typical of misinformation in the model. Mismatched or misleading mixtures of modalities that are often found in short-form videos include overblown text assertions and disconnecting or doctored visual content. The proposed method with its explicit measurements of the extent of agreement involving modalities is successful in detecting such inconsistencies.
Crucially, this aspect also increases model interpretability. For instance, when the text describing the video goes against the video, the computed consistency scores drop significantly and this influences the categorisation decision of being classified as misinformation. Conversely, if there is a high agreement between the modalities, then the consistency scores are high and we can be confident about the classification as to the truthfulness of the content. The behavior follows a principled approach of how multimodal interaction effect can be applied to the final prediction.Therefore, modelling multimodal semantic consistency not only improves prediction accuracy but also a principled and interpretable reasoning process to determine multimodal consistency, which is especially applied to the real world such as in misinformation detection.
These findings imply that the semantic consistency modelling provides additional discriminative capability on the conventional feature fusion techniques.More importantly, even a simple consistency model can represent multimodal inconsistencies, which is in line with the practical and understandable design.

Discussion The experimental findings verify that the offered framework significantly exploits the multimodal information in detecting misinformation. Textual, visual, and audio functionalities that are integrated in the model allow the model to concentrate on complementary information whereas the semantic consistency module optimizes robustness in terms of cross-modal discrepancies. Also, it is noticed that unimodal models can work reasonably well, but cannot explain inconsistencies between modalities. Traditional methods of fusion enhance performance, though they do not provide the capability of explicitly testing semantic alignment. The agenda of the proposed solution is to deal with this weakness by integrating a special consistency mechanism.
Nevertheless, there are some restrictions. When one or more modalities are absent or damaged they may reduce the performance of the performance. Furthermore, even very subtle misinformation that can be cross-modally consistent, can be difficult to detect. Its findings also suggest that along with improving performance, semantic consistency modeling also leads to better interpretation of multimodal interaction.
In that way, the offered semantic consistency-sensitive multimodal machine can have higher performance than the baseline approaches. The results highlight the importance of multimodal integration and demonstrate the effectiveness of semantic consistency modeling. The quantitative performance and the visual analysis are combined, which proves the strength and validity of the approach suggested.
It is important to note that the proposed framework does not explicitly model temporal dependencies within video sequences. Therefore, its performance primarily reflects the ability to capture cross-modal semantic alignment rather than temporal inconsistencies.
Conclusion and future work This study has introduced a semantic consistency-aware multimodal deep learning approach for short-form video misinformation. The proposed framework differs from traditional multimodal approaches that tend to focus on feature fusion, by explicitly modeling and incorporating cross-modal semantic consistency into the classification task. This allows the model to identify inconsistencies between textual, visual and audio representations, which are likely to be misleading.
The results show that considering semantic consistency enhances the ability to detect misinformation over unimodal and multimodal baselines. The ablation study also verifies that the consistency term provides valuable discriminative information, even if implemented in a simple manner. These results suggest that leveraging semantic consistency as a decision signal is promising for multimodal misinformation detection. While encouraging, our results have limitations. First, the proposed approach considers only the semantic alignment between representations, but does not explicitly account for temporal contexts in video sequences. Since temporal inconsistencies can also be used to detect misinformation, this is an area worth exploring. Second, the semantic consistency is based on cosine similarity, which, while simple and intuitive, might not be sufficient to model complex cross-modal inconsistencies.
Third, we will explore additional extensions to the proposed framework by leveraging temporal modeling methods, like recurrent networks and video transformers, in order to better detect inconsistencies. In addition, richer approaches of consistency modeling such as learnable similarity measures and graph interactions will be investigated. We will also perform further experiments on a range of datasets and study the robustness of the model under various missing or noisy modalities to enhance its generalisation and robustness.
In summary, this research underscores the need for explicitly considering semantic interactions across modalities, and lays the groundwork for building more reliable and interpretable multimodal misinformation detectors.
