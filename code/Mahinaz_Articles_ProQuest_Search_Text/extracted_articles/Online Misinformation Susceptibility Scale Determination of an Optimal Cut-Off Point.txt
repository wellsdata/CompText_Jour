Abstract
Background: The Online Misinformation Susceptibility Scale (OMISS) is a newly developed instrument that measures levels of online misinformation susceptibility. Aim: To identify an optimal cut-off point for the OMISS in a sample of the general population. Methods: We collected our data during October 2025. We employed the Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) analysis to define an optimal cut-off point for the OMISS. We used the single-item trust in scientists scale, the Trust in Scientists Scale, and the Conspiracy Mentality Questionnaire as external criterions for the ROC analysis. Results: We found that the best cut-off point for the OMISS was 23 using the single-item trust in scientists scale, the Trust in Scientists Scale, and the Conspiracy Mentality Questionnaire as external criterions. Therefore, all external criterions suggested the same cut-off point (23) for the OMISS. Youdens index for the single-item trust in scientists scale, the Trust in Scientists Scale, and the Conspiracy Mentality Questionnaire was 0.35, 0.30, and 0.28, respectively. The AUC for the single-item trust in scientists scale (Figure 1), the Trust in Scientists Scale (Figure 2), and the Conspiracy Mentality Questionnaire (Figure 3) was 0.65, 0.63, and 0.66, respectively. Sensitivity for the single-item trust in scientists scale, the Trust in Scientists Scale, and the Conspiracy Mentality Questionnaire was 0.81, 0.80, and 0.72, respectively. Specificity for the single-item trust in scientists scale, the Trust in Scientists Scale, and the Conspiracy Mentality Questionnaire was 0.54, 0.50, and 0.56, respectively. Conclusion: The best cut-off point for the OMISS was 23. Participants with OMISS score >23 experienced high levels of misinformation susceptibility.
Keywords: Online Misinformation Susceptibility Scale; Receiver Operating Characteristic analysis; cut-off point; misinformation; social media; websites
Introduction
The worldwide circulation of misinformation has had a clearly harmful effect on society. For example, conspiracy theories surrounding COVID-19 vaccines have contributed to greater vaccine hesitancy and lower willingness to get vaccinated (Hotez et al., 2021; Loomba et al., 2021; Roozenbeek et al., 2022). Similarly, false claims about 5G technology have resulted in acts of vandalism against cell towers (Jolley & Paterson, 2020), while misinformation on climate change has weakened public perception of scientific agreement (Maertens et al., 2020; Van Der Linden et al., 2017). Since false and emotionally charged media tend to spread more rapidly and widely than factual and balanced information (Brady et al., 2017; Vosoughi et al., 2018), ensuring the accuracy of information has become a key concern among researchers and policymakers (Lewandowsky et al., 2017).
As a result, there has been a significant increase in research across various fields focused on understanding the mechanisms, effects, and countermeasures related to misinformation (Pennycook & Rand, 2021; Van Bavel et al., 2021; Van Der Linden et al., 2021). Scholars have advanced efforts to develop media and information literacy strategies, including educational games, prompts that encourage accuracy, techniques that introduce hesitation before sharing, and inoculation messages designed to build resistance to false information (Basol et al., 2021; Fazio, 2020; Lewandowsky et al., 2017; Pennycook et al., 2021; Roozenbeek & Van Der Linden, 2019, 2020).
Additionally, many health and wellness influencers and brands frequently use social media to spread misinformation for financial gain (Baker, 2022; Lofft, 2020). In response, public health experts have recently advocated for the social media industry to be recognized as a commercial determinant of health, highlighting the profits platforms earn by allowing misinformation to circulate (Zenone et al., 2022). Sponsored advertising-through platform payments, influencer partnerships, and brand visibility-is widespread across all major platforms, especially in the marketing of health-related products like supplements, weight loss solutions, and fitness services (Denniss et al., 2023; Lofft, 2020). Studies have found that wellness marketing on social media often includes false or misleading claims (Baker, 2022; Denniss & Lindberg, 2025). Because social media remains largely unregulated, influencers and brands can continue profiting from content that violates community standards. Some influencers even build alternative online communities and exploit conspiracy theories, such as antivaccine narratives, to market and sell wellness products (Baker, 2022). These dynamics indicate that many users are regularly exposed to deceptive marketing, increasing their risk of buying ineffective health products or services-potentially resulting in wasted money, little or no health improvement, and, in some cases, harmful health consequences. Recently, a newly developed instrument aim to evaluate levels of online misinformation susceptibility. In particular, the Online Misinformation Susceptibility Scale is developed to measure levels of online misinformation susceptibility (Katsiroumpa et al., 2025). In this context, our aim was to identify an optimal cut-offpoint for the OMISS in a sample of participants in Greece.
Methods
Study design: Study population included 192 participants in Greece. Our study was conducted in October 2025. We employed a convenience sample of participants in Greece. Our participants visited social media or/and websites at least for 30 minutes daily. We used the Online Misinformation Susceptibility Scale (OMISS) (Katsiroumpa et al., 2025) to measure levels of online misinformation susceptibility among our participants. The OMISS includes 9 items such as "Please think about what you do when you see a post or story that interests you on social media or websites. How often do you check the website domain and URL?" and "Please think about what you do when you see a post or story that interests you on social media or websites. How often do you check the publication date of the post?". Answers are on a five-point Likert scale: never (1), rarely (2), sometimes (3), very often (4), always (5). Adding up the responses to the 9 items gives the total score on the OMISS. Total score ranges from 9 to 45. Higher score indicates higher levels of online
misinformation susceptibility (Katsiroumpa et al., 2025).
We used the single-item trust in scientists scale, the Trust in Scientists Scale, and the Conspiracy Mentality Questionnaire as external criterions for the Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) analysis.
We used the single-item trust in scientists scale (Funk et al., 2019) for measuring the participants' level of trust in scientists. Answers are on a five-point Likert scale from 1 (no confidence at all) to 5 (a great deal of confidence). Total score ranges from 1 to 5. Higher score indicates higher levels of trust in scientists. Score <3 indicates low level of trust in scientists.
We used the Trust in Scientists Scale (Cologna et al., 2025) to measure participants' level of trust in scientists. We used the factor "Integrity" that includes three items such as "How honest or dishonest are most scientists?". Answers are on a five-point Likert scale from 1 (very dishonest) to 5 (very honest). Total score ranges from 1 to 5. Higher score indicates higher levels of trust in scientists. Score <3 indicates low level of trust in scientists.
We used the Conspiracy Mentality Questionnaire (Bruder et al, 2013) for measuring conspiracy beliefs of our participants. The Conspiracy Mentality Questionnaire includes five items, such as "I think that many very important things happen in the world, which the public is never informed about". Answers are on an 11-point Likert scale from O (totally disagree) to 10 (totally agree). Total score ranges from 0 to 50. Higher score indicates higher levels of conspiracy beliefs. Score >30 indicates high level of conspiracy beliefs.
Ethical considerations: We applied the guidelines of the Declaration of Helsinki to perform this study (World Medical Association, 2013). Additionally, the study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of Faculty of Nursing, National and Kapodistrian University of Athens (approval number; 55, June 23, 2025). Statistical analysis: We employed the Receiver Operating Characteristic analysis to identify an optimal cut-off point for the OMISS using the single-item trust in scientists scale, the Trust in Scientists Scale, and the Conspiracy Mentality Questionnaire as external criterions. We calculated sensitivity, specificity, and the Youden index.
These measures take values from 0 to 1 with higher values indicating better diagnostic value of the OMISS. The Youden index defines an optimal cut-off point and is calculated as (Sensitivity + Specificity) - 1.
Additionally, we calculated the area under the curve (AUC), 95% confidence interval (CI), and p-value. Values for the AUC between 0.5 and 0.7 indicate low accuracy, values between 0.71 and 0.9 indicate moderate accuracy, and values greater than 0.9 indicate high accuracy (Akobeng, 2007; Fischer et al., 2003; Fluss et al., 2005). After defining the best cut-off point for the OMISS, participants with a score above this value are considered to experience high levels of online misinformation susceptibility.
P-values less than 0.05 were considered as statistically significant. We used the IBM SPSS 28.0 (IBM Corp. Released 2021. IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 28.0. Armonk, NY: IBM Corp) for the analysis.
Results
Study population included 192 participants. Among them, 76.0% (n=146) were females and 24.0% (n=46) were males. Mean age of our sample was 35.7 years, while standard deviation was 14.8 years. We employed ROC analysis to define an optimal cut-off point for the Online Misinformation Susceptibility Scale. We found that the best cut-off point for the OMISS was 23 using the single-item trust in scientists scale, the Trust in Scientists Scale, and the Conspiracy Mentality Questionnaire as external criterions (Table 1).
Therefore, all external criterions suggested the same cut-off point (23) for the OMISS. Youden's index for the single-item trust in scientists scale, the Trust in Scientists Scale, and the Conspiracy Mentality Questionnaire was 0.35, 0.30, and 0.28, respectively.
The AUC for the single-item trust in scientists scale (Figure 1), the Trust in Scientists Scale (Figure 2), and the Conspiracy Mentality Questionnaire (Figure 3) was 0.65, 0.63, and 0.66, respectively. Sensitivity for the single-item trust in scientists scale, the Trust in Scientists Scale, and the Conspiracy Mentality Questionnaire was 0.81, 0.80, and 0.72, respectively. Specificity for the single-item trust in scientists scale, the Trust in Scientists Scale, and the Conspiracy
Mentality Questionnaire was 0.54, 0.50, and 0.56, respectively. Therefore, we considered that an optimal cut-off point for the OMISS was 23. Participants with OMISS score >23 experienced high levels of misinformation susceptibility.
Discussion
As there is currently no established optimal cut-off point for the Online Misinformation Susceptibility Scale (OMISS), we conducted a cross-sectional study to determine one. We utilized the single-item trust in scientists scale, the Trust in Scientists Scale, and the Conspiracy Mentality Questionnaire as external criterions.
Specifically, we applied Receiver Operating Characteristic analysis to pinpoint an ideal cut-off for the OMISS. In this context, we calculated the Youden index, as its peak value indicates the optimal cut-off. We also determined the area under the curve (AUC), identifying the optimal cut-off as the point where the AUC reaches its maximum. Our analysis involved three separate evaluations using the single-item trust in scientists scale, the Trust in Scientists Scale, and the Conspiracy Mentality Questionnaire to ascertain the most effective cut-off for the OMISS.
Ultimately, we identified 23 as the best cut-off point for the OMISS, which aligned with the highest sensitivity and specificity values across the three external criteria. Consequently, participants with an OMISS score of 23 or higher are likely experiencing high levels of online misinformation susceptibility.
Our study, however, had several limitations. Although we employed valid tools as external criteria to determine an optimal cut-off for the OMISS, these tools may not be the ideal gold standard for assessing online misinformation susceptibility. Additionally, while we used three valid tools as extemal criteria, other tools could have been considered. Moreover, since we conducted a cross-sectional study with a convenience sample, our findings may not be applicable to other populations and contexts.
In conclusion, we identified an optimal cut-off for the OMISS with notable predictive power for trust in scientists and conspiracy beliefs.
This cut-off could serve as a quick, reliable, and valid preliminary screening tool to detect participants at high risk of experiencing online misinformation susceptibility. Researchers should extend our study to diverse populations and settings to further validate our findings.
