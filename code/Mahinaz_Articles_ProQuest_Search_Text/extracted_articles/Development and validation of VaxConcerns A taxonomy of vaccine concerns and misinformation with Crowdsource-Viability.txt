1 Background The reluctance of individuals to get vaccinated, despite the availability of vaccines, poses a challenge in the prevention and control of infectious diseases [25] . This phenomenon, termed vaccine hesitancy, is exacerbated by misinformation [4] (i.e., factually incorrect statements regarding vaccines). Loomba et al. [16] found that misinformation induced a greater than 6 % decline in intent to vaccinate against COVID-19 in people who previously said they would “definitely accept” the vaccine. Other reasons for vaccine hesitancy may include vaccine concerns, i.e., innocuous doubts about vaccines (e,g, vaccine safety) or vaccination systems and processes. The identification of misinformation, as well as vaccine concerns more broadly, can inform the development of interventions to support vaccination uptake.
A good place to identify misinformation and concerns regarding vaccines may be on social media platforms, where a large volume of content is shared online every day. During the COVID-19 pandemic, there was a large volume of work published on analyzing and classifying vaccine content on social media platforms. However, there remain three key gaps in identifying vaccine misinformation and concerns online. First, most studies classify tweets into anti-vax or pro-vax categories without identifying granular concerns [7,17,22,26,31] . For instance, Yuan, Schuchard, and Crooks [31] examined the prevalence of bots on Twitter [8] and measured their contribution to spreading or promoting anti-vax content on the platform, but they did not develop a codebook for what claims the bots were spreading. Second, other work to develop taxonomies or categorization schemes for topics surrounding vaccination don’t directly focus on concerns or misinformation claims [10,11,14,15,26,28,31] . For example, Jacobson, Targonski, and Poland [11] examined a taxonomy of common reasoning flaws in the anti-vaccination movement, with labels such as “Wishful thinking and self-serving distortions of reality,” but such labels don’t allow us to understand specific concerns an individual might have about vaccines. Third, recent work is often focused specifically on the COVID-19 vaccines ( [6,18–21,27,39] ), rather than addressing vaccine concerns across many types of vaccines (disease-agnostic). To address these gaps in the literature, there is a need for tools that permit automated classification of granular concerns in a vaccine-agnostic manner and at scale.
Taxonomies are hierarchical structures used for classification of items into distinct ideas and could serve as useful tools for automated classification of data [1,5,32] . Taxonomies can also be helpful in defining common language, providing explicit structure for related concepts, or serve as an intermediary in search engine retrieval. Current vaccine taxonomies include Kata [13] , VaccineLies [29] , and Nuzhath [18] . Kata [13] is a taxonomy proposed in 2010 which features easily separable codes such as “Safety & effectiveness” (could be “Safety” and “Effectiveness” ), while VaccineLies [29] proposes taxonomies for the COVID-19 and HPV vaccines with highly specific codes such as “2.5: The vaccine contains tissue from aborted fetuses” . Nuzhath [18] provides 7 codes in a flat, non-hierarchical structure which has similar codes as Kata [13] . There is a limited description in the scientific literature on how taxonomies are developed or evaluated [26,24] . Nickerson [41] points out that taxonomy development has largely been ad hoc.
Misinformation and vaccine concerns continually evolve, with new information appearing over time. Current taxonomies, however, are not broad enough to withstand such changes, often citing highly specific myths or claims [12,29,38] which may be discarded in favor of new arguments later [37] . For example, a taxonomy called VaccineLies [29] includes a specific claim that “HPV vaccine is causing myalgic encephalomyelitis /chronic fatigue syndrome (ME/CFS)” , which may quickly fall out of favor by the antivax narrative if disproven by new scientific research . Taxonomies of misinformation and concerns must be broad enough to withstand such changes and should allow for future additions seamlessly, while still providing enough detail as to be useful for informing targeted interventions. There has been work showing that providing corrective information regarding specific vaccine misinformation claims may not be an effective approach to promoting immunization [42,43] , suggesting that such messaging does not adequately address remaining concerns among vaccine hesitant individuals. There is a need for exploring new approaches.
In this study, we describe VaxConcerns, a new crowdsource-viable taxonomy that includes a parsimonious presentation of common domains of vaccine misinformation as well as vaccine concerns seen broadly for all vaccines. Our goals were to (1) build VaxConcerns through a process of iterative improvements against key metrics, (2) assess its ease of use with a crowdsource labeling pipeline and validate it against current taxonomies for vaccine misinformation, and (3) conduct a pilot study on application of the VaxConcerns taxonomy to track changes in vaccine concerns pre- and post-emergence of COVID-19.
2 Methods The methods of the study are organized by study goal. Fig. 1 provides a swimlane diagram for all study activities.
2.1 Development of VaxConcerns taxonomy The development of VaxConcerns occurred in 4 steps that were crucial to creating a better taxonomy as shown through the improvement upon the initial taxonomy we created. First, a team of experts in the domains of vaccines and computer science created an initial seed corpus by gathering 170 common vaccine FAQs (Frequently Asked Questions) from 26 websites. The FAQs were collected by exploring trusted websites (see Supplementary Table 1 ) until qualitatively assessing that no new FAQs were being identified. Second, these common vaccine FAQs were sorted into general groups using an inductive coding approach. This list of codes formed the initial version of VaxConcerns taxonomy. Third, that list of codes was then examined to find unclear codes or potential consolidations (e.g., through removal of duplicative codes). For instance, “Dangerous vaccination schedule” and “Multiple doses overwhelm the immune system” were two codes often referring to the same concern that vaccines are delivered in manner that is harmful to health. These concerns were thus consolidated under a single code ( 3.4 Dangerous delivery ). The taxonomy was organized using a hierarchical classification structure, with codes grouped into level 1 and level 2 codes. These levels were distinguished by whether they included a “mechanism” or “rationale”. Level 2 codes described the mechanism or rationale for the anti-vaccine (henceforth “antivax”) stance (e.g., “ How do vaccines pose health risks?” and “ why are vaccine authorities untrustworthy?”) as opposed to level 1, which simply states the higher-level area of concern (“Vaccines pose health risks” and “Vaccine authorities are untrustworthy”).
Finally, we collected a corpus of antivax blog-post articles reflecting vaccine concerns and misinformation (published on dates pre-COVID-19 and during COVID-19) to then iteratively “tune” the taxonomy. These articles were taken from 9 known antivax blog-style websites, identified by Ivy Plus Libraries Confederation [40] . Each iteration included three study team members, who read a small batch of passages of antivax content and discussed which taxonomy codes applied most closely to the claims of the passage. The study team members took four steps during each iteration: (1) they qualitatively noted codes that were poorly defined, (2) computed evaluation metrics for the current taxonomy version, (3) revised definitions, and (4) proposed structural changes to the taxonomy by splitting codes into two new codes, adding a new code altogether, merging codes, or moving codes to a new location in the taxonomy. The metrics used in step 2 were maximum branching factor, mutual exclusion, collective exhaustiveness, and co-occurrence of neighboring codes. These metrics are described in length in Supplementary Section A.
When a given change was ambiguous as to whether it would “improve” the taxonomy or not (i.e., some metrics improved but others got worse, in about the same relative magnitudes), then we opted to not include this proposed change. The iterations saw 8 distinct versions of the taxonomy, with between 1 and 5 changes within each version. Due to the level of detail in each discussion, iterations of the taxonomy occurred incrementally during more than 30 meetings between September 2020 and March 2022. Saturation was qualitatively assessed as revisions became less substantial with time.
2.2 Assessment of ease of use of VaxConcerns with a crowdsource labeling pipeline We developed a crowdsource labeling pipeline for further evaluation of the taxonomy using crowdsource annotators who were unrelated to the study team [2] . Data collection of text to be labeled was done using 9 public and known anti-vax websites [40] using a web-scraping application. Articles were filtered to select only those that discussed the topic of vaccines using a gradient boosted random forest model which achieves a 92.8 % F1 score. This was done using the XGBoost library [30] in Python. The resulting articles were cleaned of extraneous information (removing HTML elements, cleaning URLs, etc.) and then split into smaller paragraphs based on website formatting tags. These resulting paragraphs become the raw data of our pipeline. A uniformly random sample of 200 paragraphs was chosen for annotation by the study team using a random number generator. These paragraphs were randomly assigned as jobs to crowdsource annotators recruited from Amazon Mechanical Turk (AMT). Details on qualifications to participate and training are available in Supplementary Section D. Multiple annotators were assigned to the same paragraphs such that each paragraph was independently annotated by three annotators. Votes were then tallied, and final labels were chosen depending on vote setting: requiring 1, 2, or 3 of 3 votes to record a positive label.
Crowdsource annotators were randomly assigned (using a random number generator) to one of three taxonomies for labeling data: VaxConcerns, Kata et. al [13] , and VaccineLies [29] . No crowdsource annotator was ever assigned to more than one taxonomy to control for any biasing effect due to familiarity with a different taxonomy structure. There are many methods for annotating data, but we used the process described in Stureborg, Dhingra, and Yang [2] . Our labeling pipeline followed a hierarchical-pass multi-label approach where annotators were first shown level 1 codes and then selectively shown codes further down in the taxonomy hierarchy. This process continued as codes filtered out negative labels from additional work and increased efficiency.
Three authors of this paper and three masters-level research assistants labeled paragraphs using the same setup as crowdsource annotators in parallel. These labels were considered gold labels compared to the crowdsource labels. In the development of the gold labels, the authors relabeled paragraphs with any disagreement and provided written rationales. Any passage labeled by the study team members for which there was not unanimous agreement was entered into the second round of annotation. The authors then used the written rationales to decide if an unambiguous gold label could be decided. All labels which could not be resolved were marked using majority vote between the authors but removed from performance evaluations of the crowdsource labeling pipeline. We computed four metrics against these gold labels: accuracy, precision, recall, and F1 score. These metrics are defined by comparisons of True Positives (TP), True Negatives (TN), False Positives (FP) and False Negatives (FN).
As an example, consider a crowdsource worker who has provided an annotation of the passage “HPV vaccines are dangerous since they’ve been shown to cause cervical cancer”. Linking this to our taxonomy, the concern raised is 3.3 Specific side-effects. Assume a crowdsource annotator incorrectly labeled this as 1.2 Poor quality science . In this case, we would record a FP for the code 1.2 and a FN for the code 3.3 . This example would count as a TN for all remaining codes in the taxonomy. For each code, we consider the accuracy, precision, recall, and F1 score. The F1 score is a harmonic mean [36] taken between the precision and recall metrics and serves as a measure of the tradeoff between the two. 70 % precision and 40 % recall would equate to an F1 score of 0.51, which we considered strong for this task [2] . These metrics are further explained in Supplementary Section B.
Note that each code can be considered its own binary annotation task (either the code is “on” or “off” for the passage of interest) and was associated with its own F1 score. Any mention of “mean F1 score” therefore refers to taking the mean of this metric across all the codes of the taxonomy. To compute the F1 score (or any metric) of a designated code in the taxonomy, we considered each binary annotation as a TP, FP, TN, or FN by comparing with the gold labels. Note that we collected several copies for each passage of interest from different annotators (as explained in the next section). In the case where we wanted to evaluate how crowdsource annotators performed individually (noted as “Individual” in Table 1 ), we did not combine these copies in any way. Instead, we simply counted them individually towards the TP/FP/TN/FN counts as if they were independent annotations. We also aggregated these copies before tallying the TP/FP/TN/FN counts, by first converting the 3 copies into a single final binary label using either majority vote (2 of 3 annotations) or unanimous vote (3 of 3 annotations). An example walk-through of this process is available in Supplementary Fig. 3 .
2.3 Validation of VaxConcerns against current taxonomies of vaccine misinformation We sought to compare the feasibility of collecting large amounts of annotations for VaxConcerns versus two published taxonomies (Kata et. al [13] and VaccineLies [29] ).
Kata [13] provided an analysis of 8 known anti-vaccination websites examining the types of arguments used to support vaccine objections. Weinzierl and Harabagiu [29] created a resource (VaccineLies) for Natural Language Processing (NLP) to classify misinformation about COVID-19 and Human papilloma virus (HPV) vaccines, although level 1 of their taxonomy can be reasonably generalized to most vaccines. We chose these taxonomies for evaluation since they were the only hierarchical taxonomies we found in our literature review which provide granular concerns regarding vaccination using interpretable codes which could be modified to generalize to multiple vaccines.
We computed agreement metrics over 200 paragraphs with 3 copies of annotations for each code of the taxonomy. We used Scott’s Pi agreement for evaluation. Scott’s Pi is an agreement metric which normalizes observed agreement between the expected agreement when applying random labels (0.0 Scott’s Pi) and perfect agreement (1.0 Scott’s Pi). Negative values of Scott’s Pi indicate agreement is worse than randomly assigning labels. We only evaluated a portion of Kata [13] and VaccineLies [29] taxonomies to reduce the cost of data labeling and make comparisons feasible across a larger dataset. To do so, we first collected crowdsource annotations on their level 1 categories. Subsequently, we collected annotations for all codes belonging to the two most frequent level 1 categories. We repeated this process for VaxConcerns as well, and we refer to these scores as “top-2” scores. Definitions for Kata and VaccineLies taxonomy elements were derived directly from publications describing those taxonomies [13,29] . VaccineLies [29] does not provide definitions for all taxonomy elements, so study team members randomly sampled examples from their dataset using a random number generator. Definitions were then constructed by joining this text into one or two sentences, shortening/fixing for grammar where relevant. For example, the three statements “Overwhelms the immune system”, “Overrides the immune system”, and “Immune system attacks children's body” were joined to create the definition “Effects such as Overwhelming/Overriding the immune system or the Immune system attacking child’s body”. The sampled examples were double-checked to only contain positive labels for the codes we were writing a definition for, mainly to ensure clear code definitions.
2.4 Pilot study on application of the VaxConcerns taxonomy to track changes in vaccine concerns pre- and post- the emergence of COVID-19 We examined how frequencies of certain vaccine concerns changed across anti-vaccination websites due to the COVID-19 pandemic. We began by separating our corpus of 200 paragraphs into pre-COVID-19 (n = 168) and post-COVID-19 (n = 31) partitions using publication dates. Any article published before March 1st, 2020 was considered pre-COVID-19 and those published on a date after were considered to be post-COVID-19 emergence. Our sample did not contain any missing publication dates. We assessed the frequency with which annotators assigned each of the taxonomy codes to the pre- and post-COVID-19 datasets by measuring the number of positive labels (indicating the presence of a concern) given versus the total number of annotations collected in each category. Paragraphs were not re-annotated; rather we used the annotations from the evaluation of the taxonomy. Note, however, that while multiple copies of each paragraph served to usefully increase the sample size and thereby confidence in our results for the taxonomy evaluation (by directly increasing the number of crowdsource annotators), this is not true for the pilot study. There, we preferred to prioritize higher numbers of sampled articles instead, since the frequency of each code is a measurement of how many articles mention that code. We were ultimately constrained by resources in collecting new annotations for this study.
3 Results
3.1 Development of VaxConcerns taxonomy We present our taxonomy in Fig. 2 . Definitions are written for level 2 codes of the taxonomy.
VaxConcerns is organized into 5 level 1 concerns which people may experience regarding vaccines: 1. Issues with research , 2. Lack of benefits , 3. Health risks , 4. Disregard of individual rights , and 5. Untrustworthy actors . Under each level 1 concern there are specific level 2 concerns such as 1.1 Lacking quantity (of research) or 3.2 Harmful ingredients, totaling 19 such level 2 concerns . Level 2 concerns have definitions of 1–2 sentences as well as succinct shorthand titles of approximately 1–3 words for use in reports. The level 1 (n = 5) and level 2 (n = 19) concerns together yield 24 labels for use in annotation tasks.
3.2 Annotator labeling accuracy Using the crowdsource labeling platform, we collected 14,400 annotations across the 24 codes, getting 3 copies for each of the 200 paragraphs in our sample.
Fig. 3 shows agreement statistics as well as accuracy on a per-code basis for gold versus crowdsourced annotations. Inter-annotator agreement was high, at an average of 87 % observed agreement or 0.50 Scott’s Pi across all codes of the taxonomy. The lowest observed agreement was observed in code 4.2 Right to autonomy , where annotators agreed on the label 58 % of the time. Four of the 19 level 2 codes showed perfect agreement among crowdsource annotators, and 17 of the 21 codes for which there were data had greater than 83 % label agreement among crowdsource annotators. Measurements of agreement had higher variance in performance for low-frequency codes. For example, code 3.4 Dangerous delivery was labeled as positive only 2.4 % of the time at a 1.0 Scott’s Pi agreement statistic, while 5.3 Censorship was labeled as positive 2.1 % of the time at a −0.20 Scott’s Pi agreement statistic.
Table 1 summarizes accuracy, recall, precision, and F1 score for crowdsource annotators without aggregating votes as well as majority vote (2 of 3 votes) and unanimous vote (3 of 3 votes). In a non-aggregated setting (individual) the mean precision of crowdsource annotators across all taxonomy codes was 58 % and recall was 79 %. Changing the vote settings to unanimous vote increased mean precision across taxonomy codes substantially to 82 %. Table 2 shows the performance as compared to Kata and VaccineLies.
3.3 Annotator agreement and comparison to other taxonomies Among the authors of the paper, we measured an agreement of 0.91 Scott’s Pi after resolving disagreement through discussion.
We observed an agreement of 0.50 Scott’s Pi for VaxConcerns using data from the MTurk annotators. While qualitatively there seems to be some possible loose mappings between codes in each taxonomy, our results indicate that agreement of codes varies significantly. We strongly outperformed both Kata [13] and VaccineLies [29] , a > 30 % relative improvement to both taxonomy-wide agreement and to level 1 agreement, and a 18 % improvement over Kata [13] in level 2 agreement.
When focusing on just level 1 codes, the difference was more dramatic: Kata [13] and VaccineLies [29] had level 1 codes with very low agreement while VaxConcerns ensured high agreement on all level 1 codes. Fig. 4 shows boxplots of the distribution of agreement statistics on a taxonomy-code basis. The lowest score among VaxConcerns’ level 1 codes represented substantial agreement (0.50 Scott’s Pi), whereas both Kata [13] and VaccineLies [29] had level 1 codes with agreement below 0.1 Scott’s Pi, which indicates very little agreement beyond a random baseline. Overall, VaxConcerns’ distributions for annotator agreement scored above those of Kata [13] and VaccineLies [29] . Out of all passages annotated as belonging to a Level 1 label, our taxonomy shows a coverage of 80 % with at least one further Level 2 label, indicating high coverage of the parent categories. Kata’s [13] achieves a similar but worse 79.5 % and VaccineLies’ [29] only covers 36.0 % such cases.
3.4 Pilot study: Shift in concerns due to COVID-19 Fig. 5 reports absolute differences in the frequency of positive labels among the pre-COVID-19 data versus the post-COVID-19 data.
Frequencies of concerns increased in general in the post-COVID-19 data, up to a mean of 7.3 % from 5.6 % pre-COVID-19. 1. Issues with research were brought up more post-COVID-19, at 17.5 % frequency vs 12.4 % pre-COVID-19. We saw a stronger focus on 2. Lack of benefits and 5. Untrustworthy actors as well, up to 19.3 % from 11.1 % and 24.6 % from 19.6 %, respectively.
4 Discussion This paper describes the development and evaluation of the VaxConcerns taxonomy. Our findings show that human annotators can use VaxConcerns to annotate anti-vaccine passages with higher agreement (0.91 Scott’s Pi among taxonomy authors) compared to other taxonomies. The agreement observed among crowdsourced workers (0.50 Scott’s Pi), while lower than the agreement between authors, is still considered as substantial agreement. We observed several examples from the data which contained highly ambiguous claims. These claims are hard to link to any particular disease, which is commonplace with debunking vaccine myths. For example, one anti-vaccination passage claimed that “ the CDC has concerns that breast milk interferes with vaccines.” Approaching such passages in a disease-agnostic perspective may be a contributor to why labeling for VaxConcern is easier. Coupled with the up to 96 % labeling accuracy and 81 % precision under unanimous vote setting, this indicates that the data collected through crowdsourcing platforms using VaxConcerns is highly accurate. In future applications where machine learning models may replace crowd source workers, such high data-quality is important both for the training and evaluation of those models. The taxonomy by Kata [13] has been used in recent works investigating online misinformation [3,12] , but we show that annotator agreement on this taxonomy is low (0.38 Scott’s Pi) and it is often used without clearly written definitions. Without agreement about which codes from the taxonomy apply for a particular example, it is impossible to evaluate whether a machine learning model is picking the correct codes.
Our hierarchical taxonomy and annotation pipeline is the first to our knowledge to show crowdsource viability, allowing for building and evaluating machine learning models capable of the type of large-scale data collection which already enables many other research investigations and impactful resources [2,33,34] . We evaluated our taxonomy against existing vaccine taxonomies and showed large increases in inter-annotator agreement with our taxonomy( [13,29] ). Our work is highly reproducible and extendable given our use of 100 % open text data, which eliminates issues of data deletion [35] and access when data from social media platforms may be used. We are not aware of any vaccine taxonomy curated from a publicly open text dataset.
There are many potential uses for VaxConcerns, including helping better understand and measure the landscape of vaccine concerns. We demonstrate the potential utility of our taxonomy through the pilot study of using VaxConcerns to monitor changes in vaccine concerns in online media before and after emergence of COVID-19. The preliminary results indicate that concerns shifted from a focus surrounding “Health risks” before COVID-19 towards “Lack of benefits” from vaccines during COVID-19, potentially indicating a need for adjustments in pro-vaccine messaging during the pandemic. Future work could scrape data over time to allow a longitudinal analysis of shifting anti-vaccine attitudes with higher statistical confidence. This could allow better tracking of concerns by connecting the ever-shifting goalposts of proposed theories between (for example) vaccines and autism that change as prior issues are refuted with more data [37] . Further, AI technologies may make such analyses much more scalable, highlighting the importance of cheap and scalable data collection through crowdsource annotators. Future research could focus on (a) discovering novel emerging concerns regarding vaccines in real-time, (b) finding unaddressed concerns for response by public health organizations, (c) understanding how concerns evolve over time, or (d) measuring differences in concerns across demographics and local regions. Outside of academic or medical uses, the taxonomy could be adopted in education.
4.1 Limitations Social media is one of the most interesting grounds for research of a population’s vaccine concerns given the volume of data and access to real-time opinions. Given the short-form, low-context of our text data, we expect the labeling process to generalize well to social media text. However, our current work does not investigate how the taxonomy generalizes into social media text [8,9] , nor languages other than English. Level 3 of VaxConcerns is designed to offer additional flexibility and possible extensions in the future (see an example in Supplementary Fig. 2 ). This should allow work to investigate tracking specific versions of the same claims, multiple entities related to the same claim, and so on.
Coverage of our taxonomy is difficult to assess without further expert annotations since some passages should appropriately be labeled as not containing vaccine concerns. Without knowing the true ratio of such cases we cannot assess if taxonomy coverage underestimates (due to missing codes) or overestimates (due to annotation error) the true coverage. Therefore we only report Level 2 coverage, or the coverage of already identified passages that belong to a Level 1 code.
While our work offers the potential for scaling data collection in the future, it currently does not have the scale needed to make statistically significant claims in the pilot study. Our investigation collects 14,400 total annotations for this analysis. Due to paragraph sampling, 3-annotator voting, our multi-step annotation process, we are only able to label 200 articles.
While crowdsource annotators provide cheap, human-interpretable labels for our task, they are a population not representative of all stakeholders who will potentially utilize our taxonomy. For instance, we did not evaluate agreement of VaxConcerns among doctors, nurses, or other medical or public health professionals. Studies of such populations may be helpful in showing our taxonomy is easy to teach across many disciplines, not just crowdsource annotators. However, one can reasonably expect that more domain knowledge should lead to better performance rather than worse, and it could be argued that crowdsource workers are more likely to be representative of the world at large than the alternative (small, highly educated research teams). Further, the reason for the difference in annotation performance across the taxonomies is difficult to assess in the absence of more testing.
5 Conclusions We developed a taxonomy of vaccine concerns through a data-guided process of iterative improvements and a pipeline for data collection that shows crowdsource viability. This new taxonomy, called VaxConcerns, organizes topics to be easy to teach: with a tutorial of just 10 simple sentences, crowdsource annotators were able to achieve a Scott’s Pi of 0.50 and labeling precision of 82 %. Our proof-of-concept study on the changes in anti-vaccination content as we entered the COVID-19 pandemic shows potential usefulness of VaxConcerns. Future use cases may include longitudinal studies of the shift in vaccine concerns over time.
