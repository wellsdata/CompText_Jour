Introduction The Merriam-Webster dictionary defines war as, first and foremost, a state of usually open and declared armed hostile conflict between states or nations. With the development of digital media technologies, a growing body of scholarly research has pointed out the danger of reducing contemporary warfare to overtly militant combats among nation-states. Many have come to perceive contemporary digital warfare as an increasingly blurred zone (e.g. Kaplan 2020) where emerging and non-traditional actors like ordinary citizens and non-governmental groups intertwine with the traditional ones, such as the government, political groups and military (Ullah 2017). Scholars problematise the old paradigms that subject mediatised wars to a linear “command and control” mode (Mirrlees 2018), emphasising the participatory features that change both power dynamics and the notion of warfare per se (Boichak 2021; Merrin 2018; Rossiter 2019). Yet, current research on Chinese digital warfare tends to emphasise novel military operations and tech-based economic wars (e.g. Davis 2021; Ma 2021), overlooking ubiquitous information warfare. Scholarly research on disinformation as a form of digital warfare in the Chinese context is not yet prominent. This omission is dangerous, as disinformation campaigns can influence the international political environment in the long run.
This paper explores the nuanced practices of Chinese state-sponsored disinformation campaigns as a form of digital warfare. Disinformation, strategic dissemination of misleading information, is worth exploring as digital warfare for three reasons. First, disinformation campaigns can be used as war strategies, for they are malicious and involve power actors who seek to advance strategic goals (Bennett and Livingston 2018; Marwick and Lewis 2017; Nemr and Gangware 2019). Second, some types of disinformation (such as conspiracy theories and propaganda, see Freelon and Wells 2020) can be implicit and hard to debunk, which echo the traditional Chinese war philosophies that emphasise deception and non-direct confrontation (Macdonald and Neupert 2005; Sun Tzu 1910 1 ). This trait makes disinformation hard to distinguish and combat and potentially more dangerous than traditional weapon systems. Last, the dissemination of disinformation relies on participatory practices, such as liking, sharing and commenting. With the help of global digital networks, disinformation can impact many people without restrictions by the conventional borders of nation-states, adding significant difficulty to its countermeasures. Therefore, this paper asks how we can perceive Chinese state-sponsored disinformation campaigns as a type of digital warfare. Following Charles Tilly’s famous thesis “war makes state” (1985, 170), it further asks how such campaigns can implicate China’s state-building. With these questions, this paper aims to extrapolate the attributes of Chinese state-sponsored disinformation as digital warfare. It does not assume that all Chinese netizens are belligerent, nationalistic or have been impacted by disinformation. However, this paper adopts a normative stance against disinformation as it severely threatens functioning democracies.
Two examples of disinformation campaigns are selected to showcase digital warfare dynamics: on a global social media platform and as a local Chinese news story. The first example refers to a tweet shared by Chinese Foreign Ministry spokesperson Zhao Lijian, which purportedly depicts an Australian soldier slitting the throat of an Afghan child. The second example shows how a high school suicide case was connected to alleged western power infiltration. Analysing these two cases can help us understand the content strategies and distributing channels of disinformation campaigns. They can further reveal how domestic and international disinformation campaigns can mobilise alliances and manufacture enemies across platforms and national boundaries. The examples outline Chinese digital warfare that combines the old military treatise and new technological affordances to prompt disinformation campaigns. This paper attempts to demonstrate that state-sponsored disinformation can be conducted as a mode of nuanced and protracted digital warfare that can “subdue the enemy without fighting”.
I argue that disinformation campaigns stealthily recruit the netizens to combat in the project of state-building. My argument will progress on two grounds. First, disinformation epitomises implicit warfare that is seemingly non-confronting and ambivalent. In Sun Tzu’s Art of War (1910), the height of excellence of warfare is to avoid combat. Avoiding combat is not peace-seeking but an alternative way to attack and triumph (Jullien 2004). This alternative technique echoes Mattingly and Yao’s (2022) observation of how Chinese soft propaganda (i.e. making plausible claims through entertainment media) can insidiously shift public political attitudes and avoid popular pushbacks. Further, disinformation campaigns can facilitate state making beyond arousing xenophobic and nationalist sentiments (c.f., Schneider 2018; Mattingly and Yao 2022). Disinformation campaigns consolidate the boundaries of the Party-state by institutionalising new cyber territories, mobilising participatory warfare and evoking unlikely alliances and enemies. As I will show later, allies and enemies are demarcated less by nationalities but their conformity to the Party line. State-building becomes a more fluid project when foreign countries and actors can be enlisted as allies, while critical media and concerned citizens are portrayed as enemies. In the following sections, I first contextualise disinformation in the current discussions of the Chinese digital war. Then, I propose to understand disinformation as digital warfare rather than adversarial narratives with the help of Sun Tzu’s war concepts. I analyse two cases and discuss how Chinese state-sponsored disinformation can impact the Chinese state-building.
Chinese state-sponsored disinformation as digital warfare Digital war tends to be associated with the fifth generation of warfare (GW). Warfare history has been loosely categorised into five generations (Abbott 2010; Qureshi 2019). Compared to the previous four generations, fifth-generation warfare (5GW) has developed a new focus on information control apart from territorial occupation. Leveraging the affordance of digital and networked technologies, contemporary warfare has become increasingly decentralised. On the one hand, a vast range of salient issues and sentiments can aggregate and create conflicts through digital media and platforms that transgress national boundaries, such as Islamic extremism (Ullah 2017) and the far-right movement (Daou 2019). On the other hand, it is increasingly easy to participate in digital wars. The mediation of contemporary warfare supports a broadened sense of war participation. For example, “sofa warfare” and “sofa troops” (Asmolov 2021; Denisova 2019) capture how internet users can engage in the discursive structure of warfare via minimal effort that happens in their home environment. Civilian crowdsourcing military support (Boichak 2017) is another example that illustrates the logistic actualisation of participatory warfare. Some scholars argue that digital wars enable participation from a distant location (Crawford 2015). Conversely, one can argue that the battlefield has extended to the realm of everyday life, sustained by para-militarised citizen-soldiers from the privacy of their homes (Andrejevic 2007).
Chinese 5GW can be traced to the articulation of “unrestrictive warfare” (Qiao and Wang 1999) that advocated generalised warfare on all fronts. The colonels turned authors believed that China should engage in unconventional, non-direct military confrontations, including political, legal, economic and technological means to overpower superior opponents. In 2003, the Central Military Commission approved the guiding concept for information operations known as the “Three Warfares” ( Sanzhong Zhanfa ), including coordinated psychological operations, overt and covert media manipulation and legal warfare that target audiences abroad (Raska 2015). Current academic researchers predominantly focus on two types of Chinese digital warfare. First, novel military operations can be seen in drone usage and hybrid wars (Burgers and Romaniuk 2017), cyberwarfare and espionage (Davis 2021). Second, technologically powered economic wars, such as artificial intelligence and 5G, can influence international relations, especially between China and the US (Ma 2021). Not enough scholarly attention has been given to theorising information operations like disinformation.
Disinformation is defined as “the purposeful dissemination of false information intended to mislead or harm” (Nemr and Gangware 2019, 4). Disinformation differs from accidentally spreading false messages (misinformation), for it envelops a malicious intent and aims to deceive. Further, disinformation campaigns in authoritarian regimes tend to be state-sponsored and highly coordinated (Bradshaw and Howard 2018). In this way, power actors can use disinformation to advance strategic goals. Disinformation operations increasingly become war agents in regional and international conflicts. Back in 2019, a New York Times article (Myers and Mozur 2019) called out that the Chinese government has been waging a disinformation war against the Hong Kong protesters by branding them as West-backed terrorists and stirring up anti-Western sentiments. The recent Russo-Ukrainian war has witnessed mass circulation of war-related Russian disinformation and subsequent banning of Kremlin-backed media across the European Union and digital platforms like Facebook and Twitter (Scott 2022).
Disinformation can also be used as a grey zone tactic, that is, low-intensity force application in the ambiguous realm between peace and war. Australia’s International Cyber and Critical Technology Engagement Strategy (DFAT 2021) has recognised disinformation as one of the technological vehicles of foreign interference to conduct new means of political and economic coercion. The Russian government has long been known to send tailored messages to different nation-states and socio-political groups to manipulate the adversary’s public opinion and disorganise the adversary’s society. Resorting to a “fluid approach to ideology”, Russian disinformation does not seek to convince or convert but to confuse by creating alternative realities that can be translated into political actions (Pomerantsev and Weiss 2014). Similarly, Taiwan’s latest Quadrennial Defence Review (MND 2021) officially identified information warfare as a new security challenge that coerces adversaries, ignites conflicts and undermines regional stability. More countries and governments have regarded disinformation as a constant strike system that creates confusing alternative realities in favour of the adversaries.
As China’s state-sponsored disinformation emerged as a research topic of global collaboration between scholars, NGOs and think tanks, we need to heed two points about the Chinese context. First, China’s state-sponsored disinformation takes place in a stringently controlled media ecology (e.g. King et al. 2013; Roberts 2018), which shapes the role of mass and social media as well as the popular mentality. Although not all media outlets function as the mouthpieces of the Chinese Communist Party (CCP), nor do all netizens embrace nationalist sentiments, such a media ecology creates a power imbalance that gives the CCP-endorsed messages more advantage (Myers and Mozur 2019). Second, the coordination of Chinese state-sponsored disinformation campaigns has demonstrated high technological capacity on global media platforms. Disinformation campaigns are increasingly automated through collaborations between human and algorithmic actors (Bolsover 2017). International audiences are increasingly targeted by Chinese disinformation on platforms like Facebook and Twitter (although those platforms are not accessible in China without a VPN) (Thomas et al. 2020). The mediation of global platforms increases the difficulty of tracing disinformation to its provenance through anonymised and networked users.
The art of disinformation How do we articulate the nuanced practice of disinformation when it becomes elusive and fluid? Disinformation is conventionally analysed under a political communication framework as adversarial narratives. Following Lasswell’s famous “5W” communication model, Fang (2005) proposed to analyse five war communication components: subject, audience, channel, content and dissemination effect. In a special issue of Political Communication , Freelon and Wells (2020) divided existing disinformation research into two broad categories: studies of disinformation content and of disinformation reception. They called for more research in the areas of disinformation effects and specific subpopulations. However, a conventional communication framework might be considered problematic. First, clearly demarcating the subject from the audience cannot highlight the participatory and decentralised traits of disinformation operations nor address its socio-political context. War is not only the sum of actions and receptions but also a socio-political phenomenon (Von Clausewitz 1982; cited in Davis 2021). Additionally, a communication framework inevitably analyses disinformation as alternative narratives. This framework appears ineffective in countering disinformation as alternative realities that are inherently messy and transgressive, simultaneously organised and organic, human and automated. These limitations propelled me to look beyond the dominant framework of political communication and rethink disinformation as a protracted operation and analyse its form and content, alliances and adversaries.
Sun Tzu’s classical war philosophies can offer insights into understanding digital warfare. The fact that disinformation attacks cognitive realms without resorting to brute force is what Sun Tzu considered as the height of excellence: “the skilful leader subdues the enemy’s troops without any fighting; he captures their cities without laying siege to them; he overthrows their kingdom without lengthy operations in the field” (1910, 39). Three concepts can be further extrapolated to investigate a disinformation campaign: qizheng (奇正) , xushi (虚实) and xingshi (形势).
Since disinformation is often elusive and can hide in plain sight, the first step is determining whether a piece of information belongs to a disinformation war. If so, does it accompany other types of war tactics? Qizheng can be used to distinguish disinformation campaigns as an implicit form of warfare from explicit military operations with brute force. In Chinese, zheng (正) means direct and frontal attacks, while qi (奇) means oblique, unexpected and secretive attacks. Jullien interpreted that “positioning one’s troops in response to the enemy’s position would represent a frontal relationship, whereas dominating the adversary’s troops without taking a position would represent an oblique relationship” (2004, 37). Disinformation campaigns are analogous to oblique warfare that takes place in immaterial realms and territories. Sun Tzu (1910, 48) advocated combining the two approaches, “the direct method may be used for joining battle, but indirect methods will be needed in order to secure victory”. Therefore, qi and zheng are two modes that engender each other and produce constant changes. “Through continuous conversion, the frontal and oblique approaches are equally necessary to permitting the endless renewal of strategic potential” (Jullien 2004). Qizheng reminds us that disinformation campaigns can complement frontal combats like political and diplomatic debates to distract and surprise the enemy, as I will later exemplify.
Xushi describes the propagation of half-truths and half-lies in disinformation campaigns. Xushi is translated as “weak and strong”, while xu (虚)means “false” and shi (实) “real”. According to a former state journalist, He Qinglian, the Chinese news system has long been known to co-opt with the state and skilfully deploy “lies intermingled with a smattering of truth” (2008, 13–14). Rather than fabricating straightforward fake news or “hard propaganda” (Mattingly and Yao 2022) that can arouse widespread popular suspicion, strategic ambivalence can be hard to refute and debunk due to a lack of direct evidence. Half-lies or plausible facts can even trigger more online debates and distort reality in the long run. For Sun Tzu (1910, 54), a “general is skilful in attack whose opponent does not know what to defend; and he is skilful in defence whose opponent does not know what to attack”. Ambiguity disinformation creates constant attacks that leave the enemies wondering “what to defend”, as a total reversal of half-truth needs careful and precise narration.
Xingshi illustrates how disinformation campaigns can generate unlikely alliances and forge animosity. In Chinese, xing (形) means situation and configuration, while shi (势) means position and potential. Combined, xingshi emphasises manipulating the potentials of the strategic landscape as they unfold, which differs from over-relying on predictions and planning (Shrivastava and Persson 2014). By saying, “just as water retains no constant shape, so in warfare there are no constant conditions”, Sun Tzu (1910, 59) praised strategies that can continuously adapt according to the ongoing happenings of the circumstances. Xingshi can alert us how disinformation exploits the affordances and opportunities of global media networks and digital topographies. Temporary allies can be formed through digital third parties that are incentivised purely by commercial interest rather than propelled by patriotic or humanitarian sentiments. One study has found the Taiwanese micro-celebrities promote Chinese disinformation for monetary gains through increased clicks and exposure (e.g. Tseng and Shen 2020). This means that investigating disinformation’s content and dissemination routes can be insufficient to discern and counter disinformation. Instead, understanding the dynamics of the strategic digital landscape can be crucial. Australian Strategic Policy Institute (Zhang et al. 2021) also found that the Chinese government officials and state media increasingly amplify Western fringe media and conspiracist websites and termed these alliances “strange bedfellows”.
In this section, I have argued that Sun Tzu’s classical concepts can bring analytical clarity to the form, content and strategic landscapes of disinformation. As disinformation spreads through decentralised global media networks, it can insidiously attack by purposefully disseminating half-lies and mobilising opportunistic alliances not restricted by the boundaries of nation-states. Two cases of Chinese state-sponsored disinformation campaigns will be analysed in the following sections. The first case concerns a diplomatic tweet, while the second case involves a juvenile suicide incident. These two cases are chosen because they involve coordinated disinformation disseminated through social media platforms and public participation. These cases are also chosen because they are intertwined with diplomacy and politics. Sun Tzu (1910) prioritised non-violent political and diplomatic strategies over military conflicts and fistfights. Combined, the two cases will help us fathom disinformation campaigns' domestic and international impact. Applying Sun Tzu’s concepts, I answer questions, such as who are the major parties involved? Are there different war tactics used other than disinformation? What disinformative messages are involved? Have the enemies responded? If so, were the responses effective? Are new alliances mobilised and formed through the campaigns? Following Charles Tilly’s “war makes state” thesis (1985, 170), I further analyse how such campaigns can implicate China’s state-building.
"How dare a state talk about human rights with blood on its hands?" In November 2020, the Australian Defence Force (ADF) released the findings of the four-year Brereton War Crimes Inquiry, which implicated 25 Australian soldiers in the unlawful killing of 39 civilians and prisoners in the Afghan war between 2005 and 2016 (Doran 2020). The report findings garnered national headlines and attracted criticism of the soldiers’ wrongdoing. The Chief of the Defence Force issued an apology and promised that further actions would be taken. On 22 November 2020, Wuheqilin, a Chinese illustrator, posted on Weibo a computer graphic (CG) Peace Force . In the image, a grinning Australian soldier was seemingly cutting the throat of an Afghan child who held a lamb in his arms. The caption read, “Don’t be afraid, we are coming to bring you peace!” A week later, the image was reposted to Twitter by China’s Foreign Ministry spokesperson Zhao Lijian (Zhao 2020), captioned “Shocked by murder of Afghan civilians & prisoners by Australian soldiers. We strongly condemn such acts, &call [sic] for holding them accountable.” Australian Prime Minister Scott Morrison subsequently demanded an apology from China over the “repugnant” tweet in a press conference. In response, Wuheqilin posted another image titled To Morrison , depicting Morrison covering dead bodies with a corner of an Australian flag and pointing accusingly at the young artist surrounded by western reporters (Global Times 2020).
Analysing this incidence, we can see that  qizheng  played out on two layers. First, Zhao’s tweet can be considered disinformation for it misleadingly depicted a ritualistic murder of a child by a smirking Australian soldier. Zhao posted the realistic computer-generated image without crediting or indicating it as an artistic creation. For at least 24 h, it was widely circulated and perceived as a (doctored) photo. This piece of disinformation later cleverly shifted positions between political satire and malign defamation. Second, this incident was concurrent with a down-spiralling China–Australia relationship due to disputes regarding Xinjiang’s human rights, Hong Kong anti-extradition protests, trade war and the South China Sea conflict (Hurst 2020). A high-ranking Chinese official’s unsourced tweet marked an oblique disinformation war to retaliate against the more direct wars on other fronts. The technique of  qizheng  here first manifested as China’s switching between direct and indirect forms of war dispositions and further played out to disguise disinformation as political satire, which is widely accepted by western liberal democracies.
Qizheng enabled the disinformation operations to reside in a grey zone. A clear temporal mark for the origin of this war is absent. Can we claim that the disinformation war began when a cultural worker, Wuheqilin, had created the image, when Zhao circulated it, or when Morrison escalated it as an international relations issue? Wuheqilin and Zhao can both excuse their behaviours as a personal critique if we still understand war as an action that can only happen between the states. No clear boundaries indicate when a virtual tweet becomes harmful to a nation, contrary to territorial violations or actual casualties.
To understand xushi is to distinguish the half-truth and half-lie entangled in Wuheqilin’s image and Zhao’s tweet. It was true that Australian soldiers conducted war crimes in Afghanistan, which were under investigation by the Australian Defence Force. However, the claim that soldiers cut the throat of two 14-year-old Afghan kids was unfounded (Xiao 2020). 2 The tweet alleged a hideous crime of the Australian special force members while conveniently omitting that the perpetrators were seriously investigated, charged and would be prosecuted by a newly established Office of the Special Investigator.
Countering half-truth half-lie can be achieved through distinguishing lies from truths, clarifying misunderstandings, and responding separately to lies and truths. However, Morrison’s response during the press conference was neither specific nor clarifying. He responded: There are undoubtedly tensions that exist between China and Australia. But this is not how you deal with them… Australia has patiently sought to address the tensions that exist in our relationship in a mature way, in a responsible way… The way to deal with those is by engaging directly in discussion and dialogue between ministers and leaders (Prime Minister of Australia 2020).

China initiated an operation in a detouring and implicit manner, while Australia replied by demanding phalanxes tactics and a Greek facing off. Misunderstanding of China’s tactics led to an ineffective response by Australia, which offered China a second chance to attack. As Chinese Foreign Ministry spokesperson, Hua Chunying, replied "The Australian side has been reacting so strongly to my colleague's tweet. Why is that? Do they think that their merciless killing of Afghan civilians is justified but the condemnation of such ruthless brutality is not? Afghan lives matter!” (Hua 2020). Similarly, Wuheqilin’s To Morrison essentially repeated the half-truth by misleadingly suggesting that Morrison was trying to hide the war crime (Li 2020). An effective response to such disinformation needs to clarify the complex and sensitive nature of the half-truth (i.e. the war crime) while specifying the half-lie that China should apologise for (i.e. the generalised innuendo that falsely accused Australian troops of being war criminals). Responding in this way could have avoided generating further media attention around the tweet and mitigated China’s secondary attack.
Analysing xingshi we will see that the disinformation created alliances on two levels. It succeeded in recruiting Afghanistan as a mythologised ally and mobilising Chinese nationals’ anti-foreign sentiments in an affair that did not officially involve China. The rich symbolism in Peace Force made it a myth in Roland Barthes’ (1972) terms. Barthes famously analysed an image that depicted a uniformed African child giving the French salute by announcing the image created a myth that people of all colours loyally serve the great French nation without discrimination (Barthes 1972, 59). Similarly, in Peace Force , the Australian flag that encircled what appeared to be incomplete puzzles with Afghan flag patterns implied Australian violations in an embattled Afghanistan. The lamb signified innocent sacrifice in many cultures and religions, including Islamic Afghanistan. If Barthes’ image created a myth of an invariably benign coloniser, this image created an invariably brutal and hypercritical West that enjoyed committing horrors in the name of peace against the innocent and the powerless.
Disinformation propagates anti-western ideologies and incites anti-western indignation among the Chinese audience. Some perceived Wuheqilin’s crime scene depiction as a war photograph. Many Chinese netizens showed support under Wuheqilin’s Weibo account and trolled Morrison's Twitter account (Global Times 2020). Chinese netizens ironically commented on non-Chinese media platforms, such as “Wuheqilin was indeed lying: two children were killed when he only painted one. I want to report him!” (NewFOC 2020). A Chinese YouTuber offered a detailed art analysis of Wuheqilin’s two images by calling them Chinese cultural deterrence (Guan Video 2020). Another pointed out that two Australian flags can connect, which meant “the flag in Morrison’s hand was already bathed in blood” (Shanghai Allen 2020). However, it is worth pointing out the transience nature of some of these alliances. Although “Afghan lives matter”, China stood aside when the Taliban resumed power in Afghanistan. As for the indignant Chinese citizens who were appalled by the “Australian atrocity”, they will often end up suppressed while directing their dissatisfaction towards the CCP regime, as shown in the following case.
"Beware of the colour revolution" While the first example took place in the realm of international relations, this second example represented a local incident in Sichuan, China. On 9 May 2021, a woman was informed by Chengdu No. 49 Middle School that her 16-year-old son had died. Denied entrance and access to surveillance footage, she shared her experience on Weibo. This occurrence quickly became a heated discussion generating 1.5 billion views that the state censors could not timely control the topic. Many citizens expressed dissatisfaction online, called for justice and gathered in front of the school to mourn and protest. Singapore-based digital media, Initium , 3 found through on-site interviews that the crowd mainly consisted of the friends and family of the deceased and concerned parents, ordinary citizens and university students (Yang and Zhuo 2021). Simultaneously circulating online were the rumours that the student was murdered by his teacher. In the following days, the school and the police stated that the incident was a suicide and urged the publics to disregard rumours. China’s official media stepped in to subside the rumours by publishing surveillance footage comprising the student’s self-harm and ultimate suicide. The event narrative took a U-turn when Sichuan Public Security Bureau’s cyber police team posted a Weibo message on 11 May, warning the netizens to stay wary of the “attempts at colour revolution”. Multiple online articles subsequently emerged and claimed to find similarities between Chengdu’s “West-backed colour revolution” and the Hong Kong protest, “such as organised protesters mobilised by online rumours and driven by radical emotion making a disturbance and having conflicts with police on the street” (Yang 2021). On 16 May in another post, Sichuan Public Security Bureau’s cyber police team referred to another student’s death and claimed foreign media and institution infiltration.
If we understand the government’s handling of the incident as a public relations (PR) crisis management, the application of qizheng can be seen as using an oblique disinformation campaign to distract public attention from the crisis. The crisis started after media watchdogs, social media commentators and concerned citizens protested against the tardy and opaque investigation of the juvenile death and challenged the government’s credibility. Rather than directly addressing these public opinions, the government built their key response on warning about the danger of the “colour revolution”. The colour revolution is commonly used to describe the non-violent democratising movements in former USSR member states, the Balkan countries and Asia–Pacific regions around the 2000s. However, China defines the colour revolution as an American conspiracy that aims to sponsor other countries to topple their existing governments and, therefore, a national security risk (Bolt and Cross 2018). 4 The Hong Kong protest against the Extradition Bill and the National Security Bill a few months ago was portrayed as a “colour revolution” incited and funded by western powers. Hu Xijin, former chief editor of Global Times, tweeted that he highly suspected that Hong Kong rioters had “infiltrated American states”. The concern of the incident shifted from student safety to national security with newly invented connections between the Hong Kong protest and the disgruntled public outside No. 49 Middle School.
Analysing through the lens of xushi, we can see this campaign consisted of two interweaving half-lies, one organic while the other coordinated. First, upon the child’s untimely demise, rumours about teacher abuse began to circulate online, which accumulated more public attention that demanded a thorough investigation and full police disclosure. In China’s opaque and restricted media environment, the Chinese citizens tend to develop an information completion process, where they engage in the processes of “heuristic learning, reasoning, and peer discussion of clues caught in the context” (Zhu and Wang 2020, 5) to glean extra information and discover hidden messages. Rumours and urban legends can develop from these scenarios, which dissolve trust in the Chinese state (Huang 2017) and cultivate resistant online subculture (Lu 2021). To counter the rumour and restore public trust, the Chinese national press released the surveillance footage of the juvenile suicide. One social media user turned himself into Sichuan police for making up the rumour of the student being murdered (Lin and Yu 2021). Although this action restored the half-truth (i.e. the juvenile suicide), it potentially violated journalist ethics and caused secondary harm to the victim’s family.
The second half-lie was built on the engineered concept of the colour revolution. By defining the colour revolution as an American conspiracy to spread western democracy, this half-lie depicted an alternative reality that reduced the global order to binary confrontations along the lines of the Cold War. It aimed to perpetuate the factual existence of clashes between the US-led western powers and communist powers. The reality can be reduced to “if America wins, China loses” and vice versa. Similarly, if China’s government is under attack, the US must be the driving force. This half-lie further vilifies democracy, i.e. western democracy and justifies China to conduct democracy with Chinese characteristics. Whoever criticises it will be condemned as interfering with China’s internal affairs or being manipulated by foreign powers. By fabricating connections to a colour revolution, the official media invented an external opponent for the whole nation to shadow box with, i.e. foreign power. It further suggested that foreign power had infiltrated ordinary people’s everyday lives and reminded them to increase vigilance. The technique of re-engineering epistemology is repeatedly used in Chinese propaganda against civil protests. For example, Wuheqilin’s other work, Cannon Fodder , caricaturises the Hong Kong protestors as an ignorant child misled by the likes of the USA, Taiwan and liberal intellectuals (Li 2020).
The xingshi of this piece of disinformation transformed as the incident was elevated from a local social affair into the geopolitical realm. The alleged enemies evolved from the online rumour starters to the liberal-minded media, critical citizens and so-called foreign infiltration linked to multiple stakeholders, including the Central Intelligence Agency (CIA), foreign diplomats and their families. The local police disallowed mourning and arrested several people for sending flowers. Newspapers like Beijing News 5 were accused of being unprofessional for reporting that “citizens went to mourn outside the school with white flowers” (Zhao 2021). After 11th May, similar posts started to appear on multiple Chinese social media platforms and questioned the truth-demanding, mourning citizens as plotting a colour revolution to topple the regime for allegedly identifying Cantonese and English speakers amongst the crowd. One post claimed that the white flowers used during the vigil were western-styled carnations, which were later debunked as chrysanthemums traditionally used during Chinese mourning events. Another post speculated that western powers manipulated the demonstrators and traced the foreign forces to the Taiwanese wife of a previously Chengdu-based US consul (Yang 2021). By suppressing some rumours and fuelling others, the official media diverted the public attention from requesting timely and transparent information to a national spy hunt. The liberal-minded and social media have been discredited while the official media’s legitimacy restored, if not strengthened.
Concluding remarks: building the Party-state This article unpacked Chinese disinformation by revisiting China’s traditional war strategies in conjunction with its leapfrogging technological capacity. For Sun Tzu (1910, 37), “the highest form of generalship is to balk the enemy’s plans. The next best is to prevent the junction of the enemy’s forces. The next in order is to attack the enemy’s army in the field; and the worst policy of all is to besiege walled cities”. To balk the enemy’s plan is not to defend but preventatively counter-attack by anticipating the enemy’s plan and delivering the attack first. The two cases analysed above have shown that Chinese state-sponsored disinformation campaigns increasingly perpetuate a sense of conflict between a brutal, corrupt and hypercritical West and a Chinese state endangered of being toppled by liberal democracies and their proxies. This alarming messaging does not only rehearse the cold war rhetoric but can also lead to strengthening the authoritarian Party-state.
In the article War Making and State Making as Organised Crime (1985), Charles Tilly proposed that the formation of the modern nation-state resulted from war making. The citizens pay tax in exchange for being protected from external threats. The "state-makers" extract resources from the people to fund wars. In this way, the citizens and the nation-states are bonded through fraud and forced to continue a state of war. Disinformation triggers similar dynamism: the citizens pledge loyalty to the Party-state and subscribe to the alternative reality to be sheltered from real or fabricated foreign enemies and their proxies. The empowered Party-state, in turn, wages more disinformation wars, manufactures more alternative realities and recruits more citizen-soldiers to maintain the disinformation war machine. All these processes take place without seemingly resorting to violence or physical fighting. Nonetheless, this mechanism equally relies on fraud and force and leads to a consolidation of the Party-state.
Chinese disinformation consolidates the Party-state on three fronts. First, disinformation strengthens the Chinese government’s control of cyberspace, defined as a new territory “as important as land, sea, sky and space” in China’s 2016 National Cyberspace Security Strategy (NIIO 2016). With a stringent and highly censored media ecology, systematic state-sponsored disinformation campaigns further create confusion and dissuade digital activism.
Second, disinformation can program the citizens to engage in what I call people’s war 2.0. Asmolov (2019) used “participatory propaganda” to describe how digital propaganda can shape individual activities and manipulate relationships by forcing a uniform perspective upon the citizens. Similarly, the Chinese state-sponsored disinformation campaigns train the Chinese citizens to interpret global events only through the lens of a binary conflict between China and other western liberal democracies. It further concretises the perception of such conflict by presenting partial facts that only make sense when interpreted through the lens of a perpetual conflict. In this way, disinformation programs a pathway between the people’s cognitive realms and the fabricated alternative reality, persuades the audience to internalise the conflict and trains the audience to behave accordingly by supporting perceived friends and severing ties with perceived enemies. Such a people’s war 2.0 differs from a Maoist mobilisation of the rural populations to revolt against established institutions. Case two shows that disinformation mobilises the Chinese populations against the critical and liberal dissidents to reinforce established institutions.
Third, disinformation warfare increasingly reshuffles the belligerents of the war by evoking unlikely alliances and enemies. It may be tempting to interpret Chinese state-sponsored disinformation as both the propellor and the result of the rising Chinese nationalist and xenophobic sentiments (e.g. Schneider 2018; Mattingly and Yao 2022). However, such a view offers a limited explanation of China’s unlikely alliances (i.e. western fringe media outlets sympathetic to the narrative positionings of authoritarian regimes) and CCP’s notional enemy coalition (i.e. the curious categorical mingle of non-political critics, liberal citizens with political dissidents, foreign journalists and diplomats). Chinese state-sponsored disinformation campaigns are increasingly and visibly backed up by anti-liberal and pro-authoritarian ideologies rather than nationalism. It can be inferred that the belligerents of disinformation warfare are less marked by their nationalities (being Chinese or not), but more by whether they toe the Party line and embrace the Party ideology. In this way, disinformation campaigns strengthen the authoritarian Party-state and add to the shrivel of western liberal and democratic attitudes.
To counter the Chinese state-sponsored disinformation operation, Taiwan’s Quadrennial Defence Review (Ministry of National Defence 2021) suggested the following: enhancing Taiwan’s countermeasure toolkits like big data systems to predict and prevent cognitive warfare, availing diversified media to win national support, appealing to the international community and improving regional communication. Taiwan’s counter-strategies paid attention to utilising technological tools and engaging domestic and international stakeholders through communication and collaboration. Based on the findings and discussions of this paper, two aspects can be considered further. First, the significance of Chinese citizens should not be ignored, especially those who are critical and liberal minded. Better understanding and engaging heterogeneous Chinese citizens in constructive global conversations should become a long-term endeavour of interest-relevant liberal democratic governments. Additionally, the role of arts and cultural forms of storytelling need more ethical discussion and governmental guidance. Contemporary digital technologies allow artists to make powerful and realistic images, memes and motion images, which can be used as techniques to both spread disinformation and counter disinformation. Yet, how to distinguish the artistic licence of free expression from disinformation should become another relevant discussion.
In conclusion, this paper has examined Chinese state-sponsored disinformation as a form of digital warfare using Sun Tzu’s classic concepts. Analysing two examples has shown that Chinese state-sponsored disinformation campaigns can stealthily recruit netizens to combat in state-making projects that consolidate the authoritarian Party-state by institutionalising new cyber territories, mobilising participatory warfare and evoking unlikely alliances and enemies. Disinformation, facilitated by digital and participatory technologies, transforms the architecture, logistics, temporality and operation of contemporary warfare and broadens the notion of warfare beyond armed conflicts between nation-states. Echoing the feature of 4GW that witnesses non-state actors’ decentralised participation, this paper further highlights the alliances and enemy formation in 5GW between state actors and non-state actors that may not observe national borders. This means that we need to be cautious when new technological practices and participatory powers coalesce into statecraft. Further attention can be paid to participatory means to counter disinformation, such as Chinese fact-checking groups.
