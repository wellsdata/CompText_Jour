1. Introduction The challenge of forming true and justified beliefs has long occupied philosophers, both in classical and contemporary traditions (Conee & Feldman, 2004, p. 199) [1]. In democratic societies, the circulation of accurate information is not merely a practical necessity but an epistemic good: it enables citizens to deliberate, to hold institutions accountable, and to make informed decisions. When this informational environment is compromised, the epistemic standing of public discourse is weakened, and the possibility of collective knowledge is endangered. This is the concern at the heart of the contemporary debate over fake news.
In recent philosophical literature, fake news has been described as “news that lacks truth and truthfulness” (Jaster & Lanius, 2021, p. 20) [2]. That is, fake news is information that fails both in truth value and in truthfulness: it is not only false but also produced with the intention to deceive. On this view, fake news is not simply an error in reporting but a deliberate subversion of the testimonial practices on which much of our knowledge depends. This makes fake news a deeply epistemic problem because it undermines the trustworthiness of our sources, and it also makes it an ethical problem insofar as it runs contrary to intellectual virtues such as honesty and the sincere pursuit of truth, and because it erodes the trust that should exist between individuals and the institutions on which they depend for reliable information.
Several recent contributions have refined our understanding of this dual epistemic–ethical challenge. Harris (2022) [3] examines the rise of “real fakes” in online contexts and how they interfere with the warrant condition for knowledge. Anderau (2023) [4] introduces the notion of “epistemic flooding” to describe the way information overabundance compounds the difficulty of discerning truth from falsehood in digital spaces. Fritts (2022) [5] frames the fake-news environment as a noxious market that undermines epistemic fairness and agency. Cassam (2019) [6] offers a broader theoretical lens, arguing that epistemic vices such as closed-mindedness and gullibility are central to understanding the appeal and persistence of fake news. More comprehensively, Bernecker, Flowerree, and Grundmann (2021) [7] bring together a range of perspectives on how misinformation challenges our testimonial practices and what this reveals about the norms of epistemic responsibility. Collectively, these works show that addressing fake news requires more than technical detection methods; it demands a normative framework for epistemic responsibility.
While these studies have sharpened the diagnosis of fake news and its harms, comparatively less attention has been paid to integrating non-Western philosophical resources into the normative discussion. This paper argues that the epistemology of al-Ghazali, particularly his distinction between taqlid (uncritical acceptance of authority) and ijtihad (independent reasoning), offers a distinctive and philosophically robust framework for cultivating epistemic responsibility in the digital era.
A further clarification is necessary regarding the scope of the term “fake news.” In this paper, fake news refers primarily to disinformation that presents itself in the format of news while lacking both truth and truthfulness, often with the intention to deceive. This differs from misinformation, which may be false but not intentionally deceptive, and from broader strategies of epistemic destabilization whose aim is not always to secure belief in a specific false proposition, but to generate confusion, polarization, or distrust toward established sources. The Ghazalian framework developed here applies most directly to cases of disinformation, though its normative guidance extends to adjacent phenomena whenever testimonial practices are corrupted or manipulated.
The core thesis is that al-Ghazali’s framework, interpreted in light of contemporary analytic epistemology, yields a tiered and competence-sensitive model of epistemic responsibility. This model distinguishes between cases in which individuals are obliged to verify information for themselves and cases in which they may justifiably rely on authoritative testimony. To capture this adaptation in the digital age, the paper introduces two terms: digital ijtihad , referring to the responsible and competent verification of online information, and justified digital taqlid , referring to the legitimate reliance on credible digital authorities when independent verification is impractical.
This paper is not intended as a comprehensive historical study of al-Ghazali’s interpretive disputes, nor as a complete decision procedure for ranking authorities in every contemporary context. Rather, it offers a constructive retrieval of a normatively structured epistemic model and examines how its underlying logic can illuminate the problem of belief formation under digital conditions. The aim is therefore both historical and philosophical, drawing on al-Ghazali to articulate a principled account of epistemic responsibility suited to digital environments saturated with competing testimony.
A clarification is needed regarding justification. In many real cases, an agent may be rational and epistemically responsible in relying on an authority even though the resulting belief later turns out false. The aim of the present argument is not to guarantee truth in every case, but to articulate norms for responsible belief formation under conditions of dependence on testimony. Read in this way, al-Ghazali’s distinction between taqlid and ijtihad primarily concerns whether an agent is blameworthy or conscientious in adopting beliefs on the basis of testimony, given their competence and the epistemic situation in which they act.
This approach not only illuminates an underexplored connection between medieval Islamic epistemology and present-day epistemic challenges but also offers practical normative guidance for navigating the complex testimonial environment of the internet. Section 2 clarifies the epistemic and ethical dimensions of the fake news problem. Section 3 reconstructs al-Ghazali’s taqlid – ijtihad framework and shows its normative force. Section 4 adapts this framework to the digital sphere by developing the concepts of digital ijtihad and justified digital taqlid while also addressing key objections concerning this framework. Section 5 concludes by drawing out the implications for epistemic responsibility and suggesting directions for further research.
2. Fake News as an Epistemic and Ethical Challenge 2.1. The Epistemic Challenge Philosophers generally agree that belief is inherently truth-directed: to believe is to regard a proposition as true, and epistemic success occurs when beliefs actually align with reality (Williams, 1973; Wedgwood, 2002) [8,9]. Fake news frustrates this aim by introducing falsehoods into testimonial networks. Consider the case of fabricated health information circulated during the COVID-19 pandemic. A false claim, such as “5G towers spread the virus,” was widely shared online. For those who accepted this claim, the epistemic harm was not only that they came to hold a false belief, but that their capacity to track truth through testimonial practices was compromised. When sources of information are flooded with deliberate falsehoods, individuals lose their orientation toward truth; the epistemic environment itself becomes polluted. As Anderau (2023) [4] puts it, this is not simply a case of error, but of epistemic flooding, where the sheer volume of unreliable testimony overwhelms the individual’s ability to filter what deserves credence.
The result is that the normal epistemic practice of testimonial reliance, which is a cornerstone of human knowledge, becomes less reliable. Testimony, usually a mechanism for amplifying truth across social networks, becomes a channel for amplifying falsehood. This corruption of testimonial exchange illustrates why fake news must be considered a structural epistemic problem rather than an incidental one.
2.2. The Ethical Challenge The ethical challenge of fake news arises from the fact that it is not an innocent mistake but an intentional act of deception. As Bok (1978) [10] argues in her analysis of lying, deception is wrong because it manipulates others’ beliefs without their consent, depriving them of the opportunity to make informed choices. Similarly, fake news operates precisely in this manipulative way; it treats audiences not as rational agents capable of judgment, but as instruments to be steered toward political, commercial, or ideological ends.
An illustrative case is the 2016 U.S. presidential election, during which false reports such as “Pope Francis endorses candidate X” circulated widely. Even if some recipients quickly recognized the implausibility, others accepted and acted upon the claim. The harm here is not only epistemic (the adoption of false beliefs), but ethical. Such manipulation disrespects the autonomy of individuals, reducing them to passive recipients of propaganda. Moreover, the long-term effect is corrosive. As trust in shared institutions (journalism, science, governance) erodes, the social fabric that depends on mutual recognition of truthfulness begins to unravel. Harris (2022) [3] highlights this dynamic when he notes that fake news produces “epistemic cynicism,” where individuals no longer know whom to trust and retreat into echo chambers.
From an ethical standpoint, then, fake news undermines two key goods: the virtue of honesty in communication and the fragile but essential trust between individuals and institutions. Once this trust collapses, the possibility of collective deliberation and shared knowledge is jeopardized. The examples above demonstrate that fake news must be understood in its dual nature. Epistemically, fake news obstructs the aim of belief, and ethically, it manipulates and deceives, corroding trust.
With that said, these dimensions make clear that a purely technical or descriptive response, such as algorithmic detection or fact-checking, is insufficient. What is required is a normative framework that tells us how individuals ought to navigate testimonial environments responsibly and how they can balance independent judgment with justified reliance on trustworthy authorities. This need is especially visible in educational settings, where teaching itself is a paradigmatic form of testimonial exchange and where habits of trust and critical inquiry are first formed. It is precisely here that al-Ghazali’s epistemological framework, with its nuanced distinction between taqlid and ijtihad , provides an original and philosophically robust resource.
3. The Ghazalian Epistemic Framework: Taqlid and Ijtihad 3.1. Taqlid as Reliance on Authority At its most basic level, taqlid “means uncritically accepting authority and is usually used as a term for criticism” (Alhayyani, 2024, p. 112) [11]. In this sense, it refers to accepting a belief based on the testimony of another without offering independent proof. In his work, al-Ghazali observes that the majority of people cannot access demonstrative reasoning in every domain and thus inevitably rely on authorities. To demand otherwise would paralyze human knowledge. Griffel (2005) describes the necessity of taqlid according to al-Ghazali:
Emulating other people’s thoughts is considered a grave mistake for those capable of independent reasoning. There should be no doubt that, in the case of the awamm , i.e., the ordinary people, taqlid is not only tolerated but welcomed since an acquaintance with independent thinking would run the risk of having this group of people fall into unbelief. A scholar or someone who considers himself a mutakallim [theologian] must, however, accept the religious imperative to reason independently (pp. 280–281) [12].
Griffel’s observation highlights two critical aspects of taqlid in al-Ghazali’s epistemology. The first is its necessity for the vast majority of people, since it would be practically unbearable to engage in independent reasoning across every domain of life. Human beings live within a dense web of practices, such as legal, medical, political, theological, and no single individual can be expected to master them all. To insist on independent reasoning in every aspect would not only paralyze daily decision-making but also risk widespread epistemic failure, as individuals would be forced to deliberate in areas where they lack the required competence.
Taqlid , therefore, is not a sign of intellectual weakness but a recognition of epistemic interdependence. For example, a patient who relies on the medical advice of a qualified physician is not being gullible but is acknowledging the limits of their own expertise. This reliance is both rational and necessary, and without it, the pursuit of truth would collapse under the weight of impractical expectations.
The second point concerns the relativity of being among the ʿawamm (common people). Al-Ghazali’s insight, as illustrated by Griffel, is that “commonness” is not an absolute condition but one relative to the subject matter. Al-Ghazali clarifies this point in his important work Iljam al-ʿAwam ʿan ʿIlm al-Kalam (Restraint of the Common People from Theology):
Therefore, silence about certain questions is obligatory for the common people, since engaging with them may lead to what they cannot bear and to matters for which they are not qualified. If an ignorant person asks, an answer would only increase his ignorance … The goldsmith is unable to explain the subtleties of his craft to the carpenter, for even if the carpenter is skilled in his own trade, he is incapable of grasping the intricacies of goldsmithing (2017, p. 63) [13]. 1
According to this, an individual may be an accomplished physicist, yet when confronted with the complexities of economics or Islamic jurisprudence, they occupy the position of the ʿawamm and are thus warranted in practicing taqlid . Conversely, in their own field of specialization, they may bear the responsibility of ijtihad . This relativity underscores al-Ghazali’s nuanced approach: one’s epistemic status shifts across domains, and taqlid becomes legitimate wherever competence is absent. Such a perspective guards against both elitism and naivety, since it prevents the expert in one domain from overestimating their abilities in another while also affirming the rationality of reliance on authority.
The preceding analysis allows us to distill the underlying structure of this model. The force of this framework lies in more than the general advice to trust experts. It introduces three structurally significant claims. First, epistemic status is domain-relative, so competence in one field does not license authority in another. Second, competence generates obligation, since those capable of ijtihad are responsible for exercising it rather than deferring indiscriminately. Third, reliance on authority is normatively constrained by discernible markers of integrity and reliability, rather than by mere social status or inherited allegiance. These features give the Ghazalian model a principled structure that goes beyond simple appeals to humility or deference.
With that said, these two dimensions of taqlid reveal its central role in al-Ghazali’s epistemology. It is neither an abdication of responsibility nor a blind submission, but a carefully bounded practice. It is justified when independence is impractical or when one stands as ʿawamm in relation to a given field, and replaced by ijtihad only where genuine competence is present. This framework reflects al-Ghazali’s broader concern for epistemic humility and responsibility. It shows that taqlid is not merely tolerated but necessary for the functioning of epistemic communities, while ijtihad remains the ideal for those capable of fulfilling its demanding conditions.
Yet, al-Ghazali’s endorsement of taqlid is never unconditional. Griffel (2005, p. 278) [12] has shown that in works such as the “Incoherence of the Philosophers”, al-Ghazali critiques the philosophers precisely for encouraging an elitist taqlid , where non-experts defer to them without justification. For al-Ghazali, the problem is not deference itself but unjustified deference. This anticipates a distinction that Peter Adamson (2022) [14] has labelled “justified taqlid ”: reliance on authority that is epistemically responsible because one has grounds for believing the authority is reliable. For instance, when a patient trusts a licensed physician rather than a random blog post, that act of reliance is a form of justified taqlid . By contrast, believing a rumor circulated on social media without source evaluation exemplifies unjustified taqlid , the very mechanism by which fake news spreads.
3.2. Ijtihad as Competent Independent Reasoning If taqlid is necessary for most, ijtihad represents the ideal for the few who are trained and capable. Al-Ghazali reserves this category for those who possess mastery of the relevant sciences, intellectual discipline, and moral integrity. As Alhayyani (2024, p. 115) [11] notes, al-Ghazali emphasizes that only those with “epistemic competence” should engage in ijtihad , and for them, failing to do so would amount to epistemic negligence. In other words, if one has the skills to investigate and evaluate evidence, one has an ethical and epistemic duty to exercise them.
In his “Deliverance from Error”, al-Ghazali describes himself as someone who managed to break free from the constraints of imitation ( taqlid ):
From the very beginning of my youth and the prime of my life, my habit and concern was always to thirst for grasping the realities of things. It was an instinct and disposition that God had planted within my nature, not something of my own choice or contrivance, until the bond of imitation [ taqlid ] was loosened from me and the inherited beliefs collapsed before me (2019, p. 29) [15]. 2
Consider a contemporary analogy: during the COVID-19 pandemic, epidemiologists and virologists evaluating vaccine data were engaged in epistemic ijtihad . Their training enabled them to assess the quality of trials and data. But when laypeople attempted to substitute their own judgments for expertise, often relying on conspiracy-laden websites, the result was error and confusion. Al-Ghazali’s model thus highlights the importance of competence; independent reasoning is valuable, but only when exercised by the properly equipped.
What is striking in al-Ghazali’s framework is its moderation. On the one hand, he rejects absolute skepticism, which demands proof for every belief and collapses under its own weight. On the other hand, he criticizes blind credulity, which accepts any authority without scrutiny. His balance lies in distinguishing when taqlid is justified and when ijtihad is required. As Adamson (2022) [14] underscores, this balance respects human epistemic limitations while upholding our responsibility to truth.
In practice, this dual framework allows us to evaluate belief formation in the age of fake news. Consider first the domain of public health. During the COVID-19 pandemic, social media platforms circulated viral claims that drinking herbal teas or eating garlic could cure the disease. An uncritical share of such claims amounts to unjustified taqlid , since it relies on unreliable sources without scrutiny. By contrast, trusting the testimony of reputable medical authorities, such as peer-reviewed research or public health organizations, constitutes justified taqlid , where reliance is grounded in demonstrable reliability. For epidemiologists and medical researchers, however, the obligation extends further; experts are required to exercise ijtihad , independently assessing data from clinical trials and weighing evidence before reaching conclusions.
Similarly, a viral post may claim that a government is about to devalue its currency, leading people to panic-sell their savings or assets. Here again, uncritical acceptance and reaction would illustrate unjustified taqlid : belief based on hearsay or rumor. On the other hand, checking the claim against official central bank reports or established financial news outlets is an instance of justified taqlid , where the agent trusts authorities that have proven reliability. For economists and financial analysts with expertise in monetary policy, the duty is to engage in ijtihad , carefully evaluating economic indicators, market data, and official statements before making or endorsing such a claim.
This point becomes clearer when we situate the distinction within al-Ghazali’s broader intellectual practice, where questions of authority were embedded within a structured methodological framework. His engagement with rival theological schools and with the philosophers was not a mere appeal to hierarchy, but involved criteria of interpretive coherence, evidential accountability, and intellectual discipline. The appeal to taqlid and ijtihad therefore functioned within a structured epistemic practice rather than as an unexamined or purely hierarchical endorsement of deference.
Thus, al-Ghazali’s distinction between taqlid and ijtihad provides a rich epistemic framework that anticipates modern concerns. It recognizes the inevitability of reliance on others while insisting that such reliance must be normatively constrained. It also affirms the duty of independent reasoning, but only for those competent to bear it. This dual vision prevents both gullibility and arrogance, charting a middle path that we will later adapt into the concepts of digital ijtihad and justified digital taqlid as tools for resisting the epistemic and ethical challenges of fake news.
4. Adapting the Ghazalian Framework to the Digital Context 4.1. Justified Digital Taqlid In today’s digital landscape, individuals are flooded with information at a pace that renders independent verification practically impossible. The inevitability of testimonial dependence is heightened online, where users must constantly decide which sources merit trust. This constant evaluative demand does not imply that agents are epistemically passive.
It is also important to avoid oversimplifying those who reject mainstream sources. Some conspiracy-oriented communities and online influencers exhibit significant epistemic effort: they compare sources, scrutinize institutional claims, and understand themselves as engaged in independent inquiry. Research on epistemic vigilance suggests that human agents are not naturally passive recipients of testimony but are disposed to monitor credibility cues and detect deception (Mercier, 2020) [16]. The problem, therefore, is not the aspiration to independent judgment as such, but whether the methods employed are truth-conducive and proportionate to one’s epistemic competence, which is precisely the normative condition that distinguishes responsible ijtihad from its distorted forms. In this respect, the Ghazalian distinction between ijtihad and taqlid does not condemn independence, but regulates it by connecting it to genuine epistemic qualification.
What distinguishes the digital environment from earlier testimonial contexts is the scale, speed, and algorithmic amplification of information, features that transform the structure of testimonial exchange itself. Testimony online circulates anonymously, rapidly, and often detached from stable institutional accountability. Moreover, digital platforms reward attention rather than accuracy, creating structural incentives for sensationalism and distortion. These features intensify both the risks of unjustified taqlid and the temptation toward ill-informed forms of digital ijtihad . It is this structural transformation of testimonial exchange that justifies adapting al-Ghazali’s framework specifically to digital conditions.
Epistemic duties are therefore environment-sensitive. In contexts where institutions are generally reliable and accountability mechanisms function effectively, default reliance on established authorities may be rational and responsible. In degraded environments marked by polarization, anonymity, and distorted incentives, however, the standards for justified reliance must be more carefully calibrated. The Ghazalian framework accommodates this variability because it does not prescribe blind trust, but rather proportionate reliance relative to competence and context. Even justified taqlid involves an exercise of judgment, since trusting an authority presupposes an assessment, however limited, of one’s own epistemic position and the likely reliability of the source (Zagzebski, 2012) [17].
A solution to this problem can be found in al-Ghazali’s epistemic framework:
If you are acquainted with medicine or jurisprudence, you can recognize jurists and physicians by observing their states and listening to their words. Even if you have not directly observed them, you are not incapable of knowing that al-Shafiʿi was a jurist or that Galen was a physician—knowing this truly, not merely by imitation of others. Rather, you may learn something of jurisprudence and medicine and study their books and treatises and thus acquire certain knowledge of their status (2019, p. 76) [15]. 3
According to al-Ghazali, one can know with certainty that al-Shafiʿi was a jurist or that Galen was a physician, not by mere blind imitation, but by learning something of jurisprudence or medicine and examining their writings. Such familiarity yields a warranted judgment about their authority, even if one cannot independently master their fields. This perspective parallels what I call “justified digital taqlid ”; reliance on digital testimony that meets rational constraints such as reliability, transparency, and accountability.
Unlike blind or unjustified taqlid , this reliance is normatively constrained by evidence of expertise and integrity. For instance, when confronted with health-related claims on social media, turning to peer-reviewed journals or official health authorities mirrors al-Ghazali’s suggestion to study the works of jurists or physicians. In both cases, reliance is not naive but grounded in epistemic authority demonstrably aligned with the pursuit of truth.
This Ghazalian insight shows that justified digital taqlid is not a passive surrender but an active form of discernment. It requires the agent to recognize, through available evidence, which sources are worthy of trust. Recent work in the epistemology of fake news supports this line of thought. As Bernecker, Flowerree, and Grundmann (2021) [7] argue in their comprehensive volume, understanding misinformation requires examining how testimonial practices can be both corrupted and normatively constrained. Their analysis emphasizes that the central epistemic challenge lies not merely in detecting individual falsehoods but in clarifying the norms that guide responsible reliance on testimony. In this respect, justified digital taqlid both extends and operationalizes al-Ghazali’s framework, offering a principled way to distinguish legitimate epistemic reliance from gullibility in the digital age.
4.2. Digital Ijtihad By contrast, digital ijtihad captures the responsibility borne by those with the requisite competence to directly scrutinize information. Not everyone is equally situated to evaluate epidemiological data, financial statistics, or scientific reports. Yet, for those who possess the training, access, and skills, the duty of independent reasoning remains. Digital ijtihad thus requires experts to engage actively with evidence rather than relying passively on secondary reports.
Take the example of viral claims about cryptocurrency regulations. For most users, reliance on reputable financial outlets constitutes justified digital taqlid . But for economists or legal scholars specializing in financial regulation, the epistemic duty is greater. They must exercise digital ijtihad by analyzing official legislative documents, cross-checking economic data, and interpreting the broader implications before forming or disseminating beliefs. This form of intellectual labor ensures that epistemic competence is exercised where it is available, and not abdicated in favor of convenient reliance.
Together, digital ijtihad and justified digital taqlid constitute a dual strategy for resisting the epistemic and ethical harms of fake news. The former prevents arrogance by recognizing the limits of individual competence, while the latter guards against gullibility by constraining testimonial reliance. In combination, they cultivate epistemic responsibility and humility, virtues that are indispensable in digital societies marked by information overload and manipulative communication practices.
This adaptation not only connects al-Ghazali’s epistemology with the modern problem of misinformation but also shows that Islamic philosophy can contribute to ongoing debates in contemporary epistemology. As Coady (1992) [18] famously argued, testimonial reliance is a central yet under-theorized aspect of epistemology. Since Coady’s pioneering work, social epistemology has developed substantially. Contemporary discussions have examined the conditions under which testimonial knowledge is transmitted, the responsibilities of speakers and hearers, and the ways in which power, credibility, and trust shape epistemic practices.
Work by authors such as Jennifer Lackey (2008) [19] and Miranda Fricker (2007) [20] has refined our understanding of testimonial responsibility and credibility, particularly in socially unequal contexts. While the present paper does not aim to survey these debates, the Ghazalian framework proposed here is compatible with this broader shift toward normatively structured accounts of epistemic trust. By extending al-Ghazali’s categories into the digital sphere, we gain a conceptual toolkit that aligns with the concerns of recent scholarship on misinformation (Rini, 2017; Levy, 2021) [21,22] while providing a distinctively normative framework grounded in intellectual virtues.
4.3. Challenges and Objections No philosophical framework can remain immune to criticism, and the proposal of digital ijtihad and justified digital taqlid is no exception. To clarify its scope and refine its applicability, I consider here three concerns, two epistemic and one ethical, that may be raised against it.
A first concern relates to the ambiguity of the boundary between justified and unjustified digital taqlid . In practice, individuals often disagree about which sources deserve trust, and what one group considers reliable another may reject as biased or corrupt. This was especially visible during the COVID-19 pandemic, when some regarded public health agencies such as the WHO or CDC as trustworthy authorities, while others dismissed them as politically compromised. If no clear criteria distinguish legitimate reliance from gullibility, then justified taqlid risks collapsing into mere preference that varies with ideological commitments. This ambiguity raises an epistemic challenge, suggesting that the framework requires standards of reliability that go beyond subjective trust.
Yet, this objection can be answered by pointing to criteria that are intersubjectively accessible, such as consistency in correcting errors, transparency in reasoning, accountability to evidence, and a demonstrated track record of truth-conducive practice. Although the line may never be perfectly sharp, it need not be arbitrary. What matters is that reliance be constrained by identifiable markers of epistemic integrity.
A further clarification concerns the range of doxastic responses available to agents. The framework proposed here should not be understood as restricting individuals to simple acceptance or rejection of claims. In many digital contexts, the most responsible response is suspension of judgment while seeking further evidence or consulting more reliable testimony. Such suspension is not epistemic indifference but a form of conscientious restraint. Even when reliance on authority is justified, belief remains revisable and responsive to new evidence or defeaters. In this respect, the Ghazalian emphasis on disciplined inquiry and epistemic humility is compatible with treating many online beliefs as provisional rather than fixed.
A second concern presses on the sufficiency of taqlid in contexts where the stakes are exceptionally high. Even if justified reliance on authority is permissible in ordinary matters, why should it be enough when lives or collective security are at risk? Should we not demand that everyone seek additional evidence in such cases? The objection highlights an epistemic worry about the demandingness of responsibility. If fake news can lead to harmful consequences, then mere reliance might seem negligent.
Yet, here too al-Ghazali’s framework offers a way forward. He acknowledged that reliance on authority is often necessary and that independent reasoning becomes obligatory for those capable of it. Applied to the digital sphere, this suggests that epistemic duties are context-sensitive. The higher the stakes, the greater the responsibility to verify. While an ordinary person may justifiably trust established authorities on everyday matters, more effort is required in cases of grave consequence, such as confirming life-and-death instructions through multiple independent and credible channels. Thus, the objection does not undermine justified taqlid . It clarifies that reliance must be scaled according to both competence and context.
A third concern concerns a deeper ethical objection arising from the apparent elitism involved in distinguishing between those capable of ijtihad and those confined to taqlid . Such a division might seem to endorse epistemic hierarchies that relegate most people to the role of passive followers, leaving authority only to a select few. This risks clashing with democratic ideals of equality and participation in public reasoning. Moreover, as Alhayyani (2023) [23] has argued in his critique of Ibn Tufayl’s epistemology, elitist frameworks risk marginalizing the majority by assuming that only a natural intellectual elite can attain truth, while others must remain dependent. This is not only ethically problematic but also epistemically short sighted, since expertise in one domain does not imply inferiority in all others.
Yet, the Ghazalian model need not be read in this elitist fashion. Its point is not to deny the rational agency of ordinary people but to acknowledge the unavoidable division of epistemic labor. Even experts rely on others outside their field. A physician entrusts engineering knowledge to those who design airplanes, just as an engineer relies on physicians in medical contexts. This division does not diminish dignity but reflects epistemic humility, the recognition that no one can master every domain. Moreover, justified taqlid is not blind obedience. It requires active discernment in choosing whom to trust, a responsibility that falls on all individuals. Understood in this way, the framework does not endorse elitism but instead balances autonomy with responsibility, providing each person with a role suited to their capacities.
The preceding discussion also has implications beyond digital media consumption, extending to formative contexts in which testimonial exchange is central. A final practical implication concerns epistemic formation. If digital environments intensify both dependence on testimony and exposure to deception, then the cultivation of responsible belief formation becomes an educational priority. This is especially true for children and adolescents whose habits of trust and inquiry are still developing. A Ghazalian approach would recommend fostering epistemic humility alongside skills of discernment, including learning when independent verification is required, when reliance on qualified authority is appropriate, and how to recognize markers of reliability such as transparency, accountability, and responsiveness to correction. Such formation aims to resist both gullibility and cynical distrust while remaining consistent across domains of authority, including one’s own inherited traditions. In this way, digital ijtihad and justified digital taqlid can be understood not only as theoretical distinctions but as intellectual virtues to be cultivated in practice.
5. Conclusions This paper sets out to explore whether al-Ghazali’s distinction between taqlid (reliance on authority) and ijtihad (independent reasoning) can provide a normative response to the contemporary problem of fake news. It was argued that fake news represents a dual challenge. Epistemically, it frustrates the aim of belief by corrupting testimonial practices and polluting the informational environment. Ethically, it manipulates audiences, corrodes intellectual virtues such as honesty, and undermines the trust necessary for collective life. Against this background, al-Ghazali’s epistemology offers a distinctive and philosophically fruitful model for responsible belief formation.
Reconstructing al-Ghazali’s framework shows that taqlid is not mere blind imitation but a recognition of epistemic interdependence. Taqlid is justified when reliance is placed on credible authority, while ijtihad remains obligatory for those with the competence to reason independently. When extended into the digital sphere, this framework yields two categories—justified digital taqlid and digital ijtihad —that together offer a dual strategy for resisting misinformation. The first guards against gullibility by constraining testimonial reliance through transparent markers of credibility; the second guards against arrogance by requiring experts to exercise their epistemic competence.
This paper also considered objections concerning the ambiguity of justified reliance, the demandingness of epistemic duties in high-stakes contexts, and the risk of elitism in distinguishing between experts and laypersons. It was suggested that these concerns, far from undermining the framework, serve to refine it. Reliability must be grounded in intersubjective criteria; epistemic duties must scale with context and competence, and the division of epistemic labor must be understood as an expression of humility rather than exclusion.
These findings show that al-Ghazali’s epistemology not only illuminates the enduring challenge of how to balance trust and independence in belief formation but also contributes to contemporary debates on misinformation by offering a normative framework grounded in epistemic responsibility. Future work could expand this project in several directions: empirically, by examining how individuals actually practice forms of digital ijtihad and taqlid ; theoretically, by connecting the framework to broader discussions in virtue epistemology and social epistemology; and comparatively, by exploring how other non-Western traditions, such as the Nyāya account of testimony, might enrich our understanding of epistemic responsibility in the digital age. In these ways, the dialogue between medieval Islamic thought and contemporary epistemology can continue to generate insights for navigating the challenges of our informational environment.
