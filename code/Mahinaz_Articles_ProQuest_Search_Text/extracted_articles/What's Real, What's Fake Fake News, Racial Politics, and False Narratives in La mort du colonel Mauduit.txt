DELIBERATE FALSE NARRATIVES have shaped public perception of political events long before the invention of deep fakes and social media. 1 Today we associate new technologies with the rise of 'fake news', convincing ourselves it is a modern phenomenon. Yet for centuries, publications such as libelles have adeptly mixed truth and fiction to exploit readers' fears and biases. During the Ancien Régime and the Age of Revolutions misinformation campaigns abound, but one case in Saint-Domingue stands out: the assassination of the French Colonel Mauduit by his own troops. This shocking execution was a direct result of a tangled web of misinformation circulated throughout the colony on the eve of the Haitian Revolution. This article examines the many politically motivated narratives surrounding the 1791 assassination of Colonel Mauduit, which culminated in a theatrical interpretation of the event: La mort du colonel Mauduit ou les anarchistes au Portau-Prince . 2 As if the assassination was not theatrical enough, this one-act play transformed the incident into a drama featuring a grief-stricken slave who defends his master (the colonel) against the faux-décret spread by corrupt soldiers. The twist: the slave did not actually exist. 3 To understand the choice to feature a fictional slave, we must sift through the layers of 'fake news' that circulated in real life and those represented in the play, which serves as a microcosm of the racially charged political narratives circulating throughout the Atlantic World. By untwisting these interwoven accounts of the revolutionary/counterrevolutionary, white/free people of color, slaveholder/abolitionist (or sympathizers), we can better understand the evolving construction of French and colonial identities during the Age of Revolutions.
Before unweaving this tapestry, let us examine the historical backdrop where the drama, real and imagined, played out. The murder of Colonel Mauduit and its aftermath are prime examples of how complex racial politics intertwined with enlightenment and revolutionary rhetoric on the cusp of the Haitian Revolution. From the outset of the French Revolution, colonists set out to claim their right to representation in the Assemblée Nationale by lobbying for their causes in France. 4 Tensions mounted between wealthy white colons or settlers, the less affluent petits blancs , free people of color, and eventually slaves as each group tried to maintain or increase their power and autonomy. 5 Aside from maintaining slavery, debates in the colonies and metropole centered on whether free people of color should receive the same representation as white colonists in the Assemblée Nationale (Garrigus 229–63). Many white colons were staunchly opposed to giving concessions to free people of color, fearing a slippery slope effect would lead to the abolition of slavery.
These disagreements between colonial factions set the scene for the assassination of Thomas Antoine Mauduit du Plessis on March 3, 1791, by his own troops, the regiment of Port-au-Prince. 6 Previously lauded for his efforts in the American War of Independence, after his arrival in Saint-Domingue the Colonel soon fell victim to the racial politics on the island. The story begins in early 1790, when white colonists, inspired by the Assemblée Nationale's March 8 decree, elected their own assemblies in the three provinces of Saint-Domingue. 7 On May 28, 1790, the most radical group, the Assemblée Générale of Saint Marc, created their own constitution granting them authority to govern the colony and even trade with other nations, a bold move that compelled then Governor Comte de Peynier to intervene. 8 Under the orders of Peynier, Mauduit's regiment set out to dissolve the Comité de Port-au-Prince on July 29, 1790. 9 Accounts indicate Mauduit tried to minimize violence during this incident, and we can argue he was simply following orders since France was still a monarchy (Mauduit 3, Leichman 197–98). Regardless, the Assemblée Générale would not forget this affront, quickly labeling Mauduit as a counter revolutionary. In response, eighty-five of its members illegally commandeered a military vessel, the Léopard , and set sail for France to bring their case before the Assemblée Nationale. The accusations that Mauduit was a monarchist only grew after the Assemblée Nationale chastised and dissolved the Assemblée Générale on October 12, 1790. 10 Adding insult to injury for the Léopardins , this decree lauded Peynier and Maudit as heroes who thwarted insurrection against France ( Journal des débats et décrets 468: 12). Despite the ruling, Peynier resigned his post amid the scandal, and the General Louis-Philibert-François Rouxel de Blanchelande replaced him.
A change in governor did not settle the unrest in the colony; instead, the debate surrounding representation for free people of color reached a point of crisis in late 1790. Shortly after the October 12 décret , a leader amongst the free people of color, Vincent Ogé, returned to Saint-Domingue to advocate for equal rights for his people. 11 Following purported actions of insurrection, Ogé and many of his followers were executed in February 1791. In March, ships from Normandy and Artois arrived to reinforce the governor; however, what was meant to bolster French authority and stabilize the colony would instead incite Mauduit's assassination. The French regiments had previously encountered the Léopard while anchored at Brest and were ostensibly influenced by the Saint-Marc Assembly members prior to arriving in Port-au-Prince. Predisposed to sympathize with the white 'patriots' of Saint-Domingue, the sailors were swayed by food and drink to believe local agitators when they falsely accused Mauduit of hiding information from the Assemblée Nationale. Agitators' claims that a new decree had in fact reversed the October 12 ruling turned the soldiers against Colonel Mauduit. Governor Blanchelande recounted that the French sailors repeated these false narratives to Mauduit's Port-au-Prince regiment and shamed them for being complicit with Mauduit, inciting them to assassinate their own colonel.
The so-called patriots, primarily composed of petits blancs , used Mauduit as a pawn in their fight against free people of color and the French establishment. The 'patriots' wove a web of tales about Mauduit taking advantage of troops from France who were less familiar with the politics of Saint-Domingue. 12 But how was this possible? The complexity of the colonial situation was further aggravated by the distance between the metropole and the colonies and the explosion of politically motivated press and communication networks during the revolutionary period. 13 Geggus has demonstrated that "false rumors of various sorts had flourished since the start of the revolution, favored by slow transatlantic communications, illiteracy, and wishful thinking." 14 Recent work by Lauren Derby on fugitive speech's ability to reveal "alternate understandings" of colonial history posits that we also should not discount "general rumors" that abounded along with printed news. 15 Though harder to verify historically, the power of suggestion through orally circulated narratives holds weight in a society with limited literacy.
To these tangled historical narratives we add the fictional one-act play La mort du colonel Mauduit where the author brings slaves from the shadows to the forefront of the conversation, further complicating the memory of Mauduit. 16 La mort du colonel Mauduit exemplifies what Logan Connors calls the national-military phenomenon of revolutionary France, which featured "totalizing performances of wartime concerns and military incursions both inside and outside the theaters of Revolutionary France." 17 At this time the theater in France was a "space in which French subjects (then citizens) could (re)imagine and (re)live armed conflict and military concerns as well as theorize new relationships among soldiers and between military populations" (Connors 2). This (re)staging of Mauduit's death imagines the scene that is focused more on national French concerns than the squabbles of Saint-Domingue's factions, once again rewriting Mauduit's story.
What's real, what's fake?
The corpus for this study includes letters, published accounts, testimonies, and historical accounts of the Haitian and French Revolutions that circulated after the events of July 1790. Rhetoric excoriating Mauduit appeared repeatedly in journals like the Affiches américaines where he is termed inexécrable Mauduit and Peynier is dubbed a traitor. 18 In one lengthy tirade published on September 11, 1790, the Assemblée Générale accused Mauduit of numerous offenses, including liaisons with enemies of the French Revolution, incendiary writings, plots of counter-revolution, and even engaging in orgies with the soldiers under his command to distract them from his exploits ( Affiches américaines 482). Calling Mauduit a traitor and counterrevolutionary ironically masked the Saint-Marc Assembly's own actions that pushed for selfgovernance and even separation from France.
After the October 12 decree, the diatribe against Mauduit intensified. In Thomas Millet's lengthy response to the Assemblée Nationale, he openly sought vengeance and punishment of their 'oppressor' Mauduit in particular. 19 Millet positioned Peynier and Mauduit as the oppressors of the Assemblée Générale, conflating the French colonial government with the Ancien Régime (Millet 4). The text singles out Mauduit's arrival as the turning point of cooperation between the Assemblée Générale and the government's representatives (Millet 17). 20 His tirade coopted the revolutionary rhetoric of oppression and patriotism, all the while fighting to maintain the system of slavery at odds with the French revolution's ideals of liberty and equality (Millet 18).
Millet placed white colonists in opposition to Mauduit to assert a unique identity for colons of Saint-Domingue. Invoking Rousseau and Socrates, Millet declared the colonies should represent themselves because laws must be appropriate to Saint-Domingue specifically (Millet 5, 92–93). He repeatedly cited the assembly's ignorance of Saint-Domingue, accusing them of setting "une classe de Colons contre l'autre," even though the delegation from Saint-Marc had already set themselves apart from other groups in Saint-Domingue (Millet 18–20, 78). Millet affirmed that the colonies were separate and distinct from France, asserting his identity as a colonist separate from metropolitan France, but he maintained that this act was not one of independence from France. He evoked a familial bond:
A ces expressions franches et loyautés, a cette effusion, de nos cœurs, a ces elans du patriotisme et de l'orgueil d'être françois, vous avez pu méconnoitre nos principes […] vous avez privé l'assemblée nationale de la consequante conviction que nous etions bons patriotes, et, mère sensible et compatissante elle a eu la douleur de ne voir en nous que des coupables.
(Millet 53)
Here France is cast as a compassionate mother and Saint-Domingue as a proud, well-meaning, but misunderstood child. In positioning Saint-Domingue as a child, he foretold a potential future separation when the colony would come of age and claim its independent, if familial, identity.
Unsurprisingly, Millet's conception of colonial identity excluded free people of color. He accused Peynier and Mauduit of arming people of color and seducing the soldiers to their cause with anti-constitutional sentiments (Millet 36, 42). This letter, coupled with other examples, points to this supposed alliance with the free people of color as the real root of the hatred of Mauduit. Other accounts corroborate widespread rumors that Peynier and Maudit "furent généralement accuses d'être les protecteurs avoués des hommes de couleur," but once Peynier resigned as governor the hatred seems to have been transferred solely to Mauduit (Lacroix 59). The cries of counterrevolution were a strategy employed by the white planters to garner sympathy from France and distract from their true motive: maintaining racial divides in the colony and gaining autonomy (if not independence) from the metropole.
Though the accusations of the Assemblée Générale were frequent and widespread, some wealthy white planters exalted Mauduit before his death. In January 1791, Mauduit was crowned a guest of honor at the theater where speeches commended him for purging the colony of the "monster of Saint-Marc" and subduing rebellious people of color. 21 This dramatic display of support is not only a prime example of the intertwined nature of the military-theatrical complex envisioned by Connors, but also ironically foreshadows the spectacle of Mauduit's death and Marsollier's subsequent dramatisation of it. 22 Furthermore, on February 16, 1791, the same publication featured two original poems praising Mauduit ( Gazette de Saint-Domingue 177–78). Just days before his death, a March 2, 1791, article described how Mauduit and his regiment received "une vive reconnaissance […] une glorieuse recompense" in Port-au-Prince ( Gazette de Saint-Domingue 232). The juxtaposition of these effusive praises of Mauduit and the Assemblée Générale's accusations illustrates a divided Saint-Domingue where various factions employed the press to advocate for their political ideologies. Some tried to forge a new colonial identity in a grasp for power, whereas others (especially the wealthy colons) clung to their historic identity to maintain the status quo. Nonetheless, the narratives disseminated by the Assemblée Générale achieved their intended effect with the assassination of Mauduit. But death is not the end of Mauduit's tale. As news of the murder spread to other Caribbean colonies and France, different constituencies continued to manipulate the memory of Mauduit to serve their own agendas.
Fake news turns deadly
In firsthand accounts following Mauduit's assassination, Governor Blanchelande and soldiers positioned Mauduit as a heroic, self-sacrificing servant of France. 23 The Colonel's loyal soldiers Germain and Gallezot gave an eyewitness account to the National Assembly in June 1791 describing Mauduit as an ideal commander. 24 The soldiers asserted that he performed his duties to put people of color in their place with little violence or bloodshed (Germain 4). Far from being a sympathizer to people of color, Mauduit was called a restorer of peace and a valiant hero who would rather die than shirk his duty (Germain 16). Germain and Gallezot blamed the Assemblée Générale for spreading "fausses nouvelles [sous] un masque de l'hypocrisie" that led to Mauduit's murder (7). The soldiers identified white 'patriots' as the instigators of the attack, while soldiers both from France and the Port-au-Prince regiment were depicted as previously loyal soldiers who were seduced by lies to become "vils assassins" (Germain 13). The Port-au-Prince regiment's tie to French identity was so strong that they could not believe the French soldiers would lie, but the soldiers from France had fallen prey to the agitators' lies themselves (Germain 14–15, 18). The local 'patriots' leveraged fears of counterrevolution to inspire the French soldiers to insurrection, an eerie foreshadowing of the terror that would soon come to France.
Careful to separate the perpetrators in Port-au-Prince from other colonists, Germain and Gallezot recalled being well received as fugitives elsewhere in the colony when they fled Port-au-Prince (Germain 27). They suggested that news of Mauduit's murder was met with indignation and horror by "toute la colonie" (Germain 27–28). Fellow soldier Tousard's eulogy also separated the 'patriots' from other colonists, calling on the people to reunite as one against the dangers of these factions by recognizing those in power (the Assemblée Nationale) for the sake of peace. 25 This appeal to restore order and unity in Saint-Domingue is a refrain featured again in Marsollier's play (Tousard 12).
This image of Mauduit as a fallen hero was far from universally accepted outside Port-au-Prince. In a letter dated April 26, 1791, Jean-Pierre Antoine de Béhague, Governor of Martinique, complained that news of Mauduit's death led to unrest amongst certain white planters, writing, "Les perturbateurs s'en réjouirent et ne craignirent pas de dire, que 'ce n'étoit pas des exemples à négliger.' Aussi ces gens-là redoublèrent d'audace pour insulter les gens de couleur et leur arracher la cocarde." 26 The power of rumor is evident as the white inhabitants of Martinique also considered Mauduit an ally to the free people of color and used this narrative to justify their attacks. Mauduit is an "example," a warning that those who threaten the power and identity of the white 'patriots' will be met with violence. When other letters from the colonies were shared with the National Assembly on April 25, 1791, the French representatives were reportedly shocked at the accounts of joy in the colony over the Colonel's death, exposing again the assembly's misunderstanding of colonial politics ( Journal des débats et décrets 699:8).
Charles Tarbé's December 1791 Rapport sur les troubles à Saint-Domingue and his correspondence with Jacques Pierre Brissot (founder of the Société des Amis de Noirs ) directly accused Mauduit of plotting with free people of color. 27 He wrote:
on voit les chefs des hommes de couleur accoles aux chefs de l'aristocratie coloniale, peut on raisonnablement douter que les hommes de couleur soient influences par le parti aristocratique de la colonie, qu'ils soient égares par la suggestion des ennemis du bien public? […] les hommes de couleur sont coalises avec le parti ennemi de la Constitution, qui abuse de leur manque de lumières; pour les entrainer dans les mesures violentes et atroces.
(Tarbé 7)
What better way to reduce the influence of people of color than to accuse them of participation in a counterrevolutionary plot? Rumors of Mauduit as a monarchist and friend of free people of color were purposefully intertwined to destroy both 'enemies' at once. With the turbulence of the increasing slave insurrections of August 1791, the white 'patriots' continued efforts to exploit the incident revealing that the attacks were never really about Mauduit, but instead targeted the free people of color he supposedly supported.
Staged anarchy: La mort du Colonel Mauduit ou les anarchistes
With this backdrop, it is not surprising that the tale of Mauduit became the subject of a dramatic play just one year later. Written in 1792 and published in 1798, the one-act play by Benoît-Joseph Marsollier des Vivetières gives voice to a previously silent group of stakeholders—the slaves themselves. Too controversial for the time, the play was never produced, as acknowledged by the playwright in his avant-propos . While Marsollier claimed his work was based on the words of Mauduit's loyal soldier M. Germain, the slave in the play seems to be a figment of the author's imagination. There is no record of Mauduit owning a slave and no mention of a slave in any eyewitness accounts of the assassination. This play is not the only posthumous instance of Mauduit having been represented with a slave, but as Leichman has established, the other account published in 1817 cannot be considered the source material for Marsollier's play. 28 A vague reference in Tousard's eulogy about a servant retrieving the broken body of Mauduit could be the source of this rumor; but given that the author was not present at the assassination, and the vagueness of the reference to a "domestique," it is by no means definitive evidence of Mauduit owning a slave (Tousard 3). Furthermore, an analysis of the enslaved character's implausible actions and dialogue establishes the character's role as fictional. Marsollier also transformed the villains from white 'patriots' into the anarchists Miller and Roger, shifting the focus of the play from the division in Saint-Domingue to a more empirical view of France (Marsollier 5). Though the drama acknowledged the realities of slavery, the playwright subsumed colonial concerns under French concerns by using a slave character as a mouthpiece against the increasingly violent chaos of the French Revolution, rather than as a true voice for abolition.
The aptly named Domingue is depicted as a loyal slave who views his master Mauduit as a paternal figure in keeping with the trope of the 'bon nègre' that emerged during this period. 29 His opening line, written in a broken French, is "Oui, Maitre … le jour qu'il a acheté moi … père depuis; et moi resté son esclave, par mes services … ami, par mes sentimens" (Marsollier 6). Mauduit is not just a master, but a father and a friend whom Domingue willingly serves. This depiction of a familial master/slave relationship is similar to 'bon nègre' depictions in other works (such as the play Adonis ou le bon nègre or the wildly popular Paul et Virginie ). 30 Domingue's words parrot the platitudes used to defend slavery while repeating the praises published in defense of Mauduit. The familial relationship between Domingue and his master is extended to the military 'family' as well when the Colonel refers to the soldiers as "mes enfants" (Marsollier 28). The character Domingue mixes ostensibly 'truthful' commentary on Mauduit's character with the fallacy that slavery can function as a benevolent familial institution. The narratives about Mauduit become intertwined with the ethnocentric rhetoric meant to soften the realities of slavery, further complicating the understanding of the colonial reality.
Yet Marsollier could not ignore the question of abolition when he cast a slave in his drama. As Sylvie Chalaye illustrates in Du Noir au nègre: L'image du Noir au théâtre , giving a voice to the black man was problematic for play-wrights of the period because "créer un personnage de nègre au théâtre, ce serait lui donner la parole et reconnaître sa liberté, ce serait donc l'affranchir de fait, en lui donnant les moyens de se défendre seul." 31 Building upon the trope of the 'noble savage,' the 'bon nègre' effectively defends his white master from criticism instead of being a mouthpiece for reform. Domingue's voice echoes the prejudicial verbiage common among French abolitionists who recognized the humanity of men of color but still espoused an ethnocentric view of humanity according to which less evolved blacks needed white men to guide them before they could self-govern (Cohen 13, 82). 32 The broken nature of Domingue's speech bolsters the impression that slaves need 'civilizing' before they could be free. Domingue quickly moves from denouncing whites for considering black people "comme espèce différente, pas vouloir qu'hommes de couleur soient jamais frères d'hommes blancs … Jamais … pas juste, ça" to disparaging his fellow slaves for crimes against whites such as "habitations brûlées, villes détruite, blancs égorgés" (Marsollier 6–7). He cites ignorance, laziness, and ferocity as reasons for their actions, even saying he would kill such slaves to protect "good whites" (Marsollier 7). Domingue's characterization of his fellow slaves as criminals is a direct reaction to the slave uprisings in Saint-Domingue. When slave insurrections began in earnest in 1791 many abolitionists condemned their violent actions, losing sympathy for the slaves' plight when whites were killed (Hoffman 116). 33 To end his monologue, Domingue seems to advocate for a slow emancipation or even continued servitude when he philosophizes, "Liberté, pas être pain pour tout le monde" (Marsollier 7). 34 Domingue's metaphor suggests that the enslaved are not yet ready for liberty. He even rejects advancement for himself if that means he is no longer grateful, tender, and docile (that is, the ideal slave) (Marsollier 8). 35 If he sacrifices this tenderness or docility to obtain his freedom through violence (like his fellow slaves), then Domingue is no better than the "barbares" who slayed Mauduit (Marsollier 32). In the play both whites and blacks are potentially criminal, but the white assassins are absolved of some culpability because they were tricked into the assassination. The rebellious black slaves are painted as naturally ferocious and ignorant, which means that violence against the masters who enslaved and brutalized them is not justified.
Domingue's self-sacrifice upon his master's grave further complicates the character's role. Up to this point, Mauduit has clearly been cast as the tragic hero who must sacrifice himself for the greater good. When Domingue defends his master and then ends his own life, is he, too, a noble hero? Domingue's life is destined to remain intertwined with his master's even in death, yet Domingue is not the archetypal tragic hero. Far from being a flawed noble, Domingue is a humble slave, lacking the hubris of a traditional tragic character. Whereas Mauduit's death calms the crowd, Domingue's death does not provide the same sort of resolution; instead, it solidifies his status as 'bon nègre'. Motioning to his master's corpse in the final scene, Domingue declares, "Le voilà! Si noir était cruel, ferait voir à vous avec quelle barbarie … dirait à vous: blancs! blancs! Voilà votre ouvrage!" (Marsollier 37). Domingue calls out the whites for their barbaric acts, but he cannot condemn their actions without invoking the insurrectionist black slaves who are also blameworthy. Whites may be barbaric, but the slaves are cruel too. There is no acknowledgement that the white slavers' initial act of enslaving Africans was barbaric and responsible for the violence of revolting slaves.
Even in death Domingue exalts Mauduit as a benevolent slaveholder worthy of his fidelity. Domingue's final words are "et vous voir si bon Mauduit était digne d'avoir serviteur fidèle, et si noir mérite d'être ami de blanc" (Marsollier 37). The loyalty of Domingue is juxtaposed against the 'criminal' slave rebels and murderous soldiers. Still, this juxtaposition does not truly highlight the worth of slaves or call for abolition, rather it suggests that humane slaveholders will engender faithful slaves. The character M. Germain affirms this, saying, "pauvre Domingue, quelle fidélité! … un esclave!" (Marsollier 37). Domingue is the quintessential 'bon nègre', standing out amongst the 'criminal' insurrectionists as an exception. Eva Bellot described his role as "l'apôtre de la modération, moins soumis que philosophe, moins pathétique que sublime." 36 With Domingue as the voice of moderation, the loop of false narratives is completed. The playwright perpetuates the falsehoods that slavery is not entirely evil, and that order is more important than liberty, seductive untruths that recognize the humanity of black figures while still subjugating them to white authority.
The play positions the future of the colony, and France, as more important than the moral dilemma of slavery. Early on, Domingue states that soldiers must keep fighting and he must keep serving for the good of the patrie because "Points parens, point patrie" (Marsollier 6). Without the system of slavery there is no Saint-Domingue, without order there is no France. The Colonel also declares, "malgré les mers qui nous séparent, faisons voir qu'il ne doit plus y avoir d'intervalle entre la France et les Colonies, quand il s'agit d'établir le règne de la justice et les droits de l'humanité" (Marsollier 10). Mauduit's character describes an idealized unified French empire under attack on either side of the ocean. The final paragraph of the play reinforces this message when the character M. Germain suggests that they must unite for "le bonheur de la France, la sûreté de tous, le règne enfin des moeurs et de la vertu" (Marsollier 37). A reign of virtue that apparently does not include an immediate abolition of slavery. 37
Rather than being fuel for abolition, the assassination of Mauduit is used to demonstrate what can happen when order is disrupted. From the first scene, the French soldiers debate the balance of pushing for progress versus maintaining order (Marsollier 8). After Mauduit's murder, M. Germain declares, "voyez où conduisent les méfiances, les calomnies, l'insubordination … Soldats, il est des erreurs qui peuvent coûter la gloire d'une nation" (Marsollier 35–36). Germain's warning echoes those warnings given by white planters in Martinique citing Mauduit's assassination as an example (Béhague). Germain's fears for the lost glory of France call to mind the white patriots' anxiety for their way of life. The petits blancs painted Mauduit as counterrevolutionary because they feared his association with free people of color would disrupt the social order. Both planters and play continue to subject the black man so as to maintain order, even at the cost of true liberty and equality amongst men. Marsollier may be sympathetic to Domingue's plight, but the risk of immediate emancipation is too dangerous to order to be the primary concern.
Throughout La mort du colonel Mauduit the free people of color who appeared so prominently in the narratives thus far are markedly absent. While "mulâtres" are listed amongst the cast, no speaking characters come from this influential ethnic group. Free people of color are not documented at the scene of his execution, but their near absence in the play is symptomatic of Marsollier's and France's lack of understanding of colonial society. The claim that Mauduit colluded with free people of color is missing entirely from the play. When Marsollier replaced the white 'patriots' with anarchists, he transformed the narrative from a debate on Saint-Domingue's colonial identity to a concern for France's future. The petits blancs assassinated Mauduit in part to assert their own identity as independent from French interference, but in La mort du Colonel Mauduit the memory of Mauduit is used to subsume Saint-Domingue under France's identity once again.
While the play's representation of Domingue is the most poignant and elaborate depiction connecting Mauduit with slavery, it is not the only one. An oval portrait by miniaturist Claude Bornet produced around 1791 also depicts Mauduit above a smaller engraving of a slave (Figure 1). Beneath Mauduit's portrait are several inscriptions. One reads "L'amitié aux mânes du cher

Thomas Mauduit Colonel du Régiment du Port-au-Prince dans l'Isle de St Domingue," while another at the bottom of the page says, "Ses Soldats qui l'aimoient comme leur Pere et leur ami l'assassinerent trompés par des Factieux, le 4 Mars 1791" (Bornet). These inscriptions recall the remembrances of Colonel Mauduit by his comrades, but the second engraving that accompanies Mauduit's portrait reminds us of La mort du Colonel Mauduit . Amidst the inscriptions is a vignette of a slave posing beside a fresh grave with a shovel lying across the mound. Beneath this slave miniature we find the inscription "Esclave par attachement, ma liberté fut mon Malheur." Without clear provenance for this image, there does not appear to be a real-life connection between the portraitist and Marsollier. But one cannot help but notice how the inscription echoes Domingue's sentiments of attachment to Mauduit. Just as Domingue said "Liberté, pas être pain pour tout le monde," the slave in the engraving declares liberty to be his source of grief, especially if it comes at the expense of Mauduit (Marsollier 7). This anti-abolitionist stance is further complicated by the appearance of the figure itself who bears features of a white man, albeit with curly locks, perhaps intended to represent the free people of color rather than an African slave. Regardless, the marked lack of African features in this depiction of the slave once again masks the plight of the slaves of Saint-Domingue. Even as this portrait, likely reproduced as political propaganda, links Mauduit to slaves, it also effaces them.
The discrepancies between the historical record, the public discourse surrounding Mauduit, and the fictionalization of La mort du Colonel Mauduit illustrate how convoluted the messages of the French Revolution became when transposed to the colonial space. The ideals of the French Revolution were at odds with white colonists' identity that was rooted in opposition to the Other(s): enslaved blacks, free people of color, and metropolitan France. Even when a white Colonel is assassinated by his own white troops, his memory remains mired in the racial politics of Saint-Domingue because there is no Saint-Domingue without slavery. Each faction, wealthy white colons, petits blancs , free people of color, and revolutionaries on both sides of the Atlantic effectively rewrote the narrative of Mauduit's life and death for their own interests. The internet was not needed to spread 'fake news,' the printing press and word of mouth sufficed because the authors knew their audience. Using familiar rhetoric and a grain of truth, agitators capitalized on the fears of the public. In a tumultuous period without reliable means of fact-checking, we can understand how easily people could be influenced by such narratives. Yet over 200 years later, Wikipedia entries about Mauduit still repeat the rumor that provoked his murder as fact. 38 Perhaps it is finally time to learn from history and not only revise Mauduit's story but reexamine our own relationship with political narratives of past and present.
