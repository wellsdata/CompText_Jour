1. Introduction Social network sites have achieved impressive levels of popularity and have opened doors to fascinating possibilities that no one could have imagined, changing the process of disseminating, producing, and consuming news. Consequently, they are used in various applications as an inherent part of many systems. The dynamic nature of the features offered in the social media space has changed over time to make the transition to these platforms desirable even for highly recognized news outlets and agencies.
Social media has grown significantly in the recent past, especially because it is real-time, transparent, and interactive, and has become one of the most important platforms through which the public accesses news [1,2]. The low entry barriers provided by social media accounts also play a part in the emergence and proliferation of false news across online platforms. This issue became especially damaging during the COVID-19 pandemic [3], when many self-publishing sites and users spread fake news to drive clicks. Such content was blindly reproduced, as well as reposted and retweeted, thus increasing its circulation, and was detrimental to its audience, while destabilizing the authority and relevance of the mainstream media. Furthermore, it has also brought new threats to economic, political, and other areas [4]. There are specific news stories for which the task of distinguishing between fact and fiction becomes almost impossible, and even if this can be discerned, it requires a lot of time and resources. Consequently, the study of identifying fake news on social media platforms has emerged as crucial in the present world [5].
There has been a direct correlation between increases in the use of social networking sites and the risks associated with fake news and disinformation. Fake news refers to the flow of fabricated stories that are based on actual news content in terms of their format and visuals but that vary in purpose and approach, aiming to deceive their audience [6]. Fake news is similarly becoming rife on social media platforms, blogs, online magazines, forums, and newspapers, meaning that people cannot easily distinguish between reliable and fake news. This makes social media a breeding ground for anyone who wishes to generate, alter, and spread fake news thanks to the freedom to share information. For instance, Facebook [7] stated that hostile actors had created below 0.01% of the total of the civic content available on the platform.
Fake news has been accused of inciting political conflict and factionalism, as well as influencing areas such as vaccination, dietary choices, and financial markets. A study conducted by researchers from Ohio State University [8] proposes that false news could have been an influencing factor that led to the reduction in Hillary Clinton’s popularity on election day. Researchers claim that approximately 4% of Barack Obama’s supporters in 2012 did not vote for Clinton in 2016 for that reason. Another incident entailed fake news involving Tesla that involved it purchasing a lithium mining firm whose share rose by nearly 250%. Furthermore, another study [9] identified that 24.8% of the COVID-19-related tweets posted contained unreliable information and were often negative. A total of 17.4% of them are erroneous or misleading in some way. The effects of fake news on its readers are significant, especially in influencing the flow of public opinion, either positively or negatively. Consequently, it is critical to build effective analytical tools capable of analyzing content on social media platforms to prevent negative impacts on the social, economic, and political realms.
The design of the Deep Neural Network (DNN) proposed incorporates the use of Bidirectional Encoder Representations from Transformers (BERT) and Bidirectional Long Short-Term Memory (BiLSTM) with Gated Recurrent Unit (GRU) architecture to capture both temporal and spatial features. Furthermore, the approach called Term Frequency-Inverse Document Frequency ( TF - IDF ) is used to capture global textual characteristics. These extracted features are then combined in the Fast Learning Network (FLNet), as proposed by [10]. The FLNet is considered to be very well known for its architecture, which works in parallel forward neural networks. The combination of BERT, BiLSTM-GRU, and TF - IDF facilitates comprehensive extraction of both local and global features, capturing context, sequence relationships, and global word importance. By leveraging BERT and BiLSTM-GRU together, the model gains the ability to understand both the deep contextual meaning (from BERT) and sequential temporal patterns (from BiLSTM-GRU). Meanwhile, TF - IDF adds simplicity and efficiency to the feature set, making the overall system both robust and computationally efficient. The use of the FLNet enables fast convergence and an improved classification performance by integrating various feature representations into a parallel learning framework. This model enhances the power of regression and provides better generality, robustness, and faster convergence, making it an accurate data-analyzing model. This strategy allows for the extraction of the maximum number of features while retaining all the important information, making it particularly effective in countering fake news. The effectiveness of the proposed approach is assessed by performing experiments on three publicly available news datasets and comparing our findings with those obtained using traditional machine learning (ML) and Deep Learning (DL) methods.

2. Literature Review With a surge of fake news and misinformation on social media, researchers have increased their attempts to identify solutions. Several algorithms have been developed to distinguish fake news, and they can be classified into two major categories: traditional and sophisticated algorithms based on DL. Some of the ML algorithms that have been implemented previously and are commonly used to detect fake news include the MNB, LR, LSVM, DT, and XGBoost. Features for fake news detection have been extracted using n-gram analysis, accompanied by the TF - IDF method. Six ML models were used in one study, with an LSVM having the highest accuracy of 92% on the ISOT dataset. However, the ability to apply this approach to other datasets seems questionable at the moment [11]. In the same vein, in another study [12], five different ML models were compared using the same embedding technique, where the LSVM and XGBoost gave better performance. The TF weighting method and a Document Term Matrix were used for feature extraction and to investigate 23 supervised models for the detection of fake news, but the DT was the most prominent among these [13]. Furthermore, the same authors [14] also investigated the applicability of a gray wolf optimizer and salp swarm optimization to real news classification instead of conventional ML techniques for fake news detection. However, these approaches may need large datasets of labeled data and intricate model structures for high performance. Equally, in addition to this, they may find it difficult to locate and counter new emerging trends in fake news. The phenomenon of image analysis has been the subject of substantial research in previous studies since it has been recognized as an efficient method for enhancing the representation capabilities of machine learning in multiple domains, like object recognition [15], the detection of human movement [16], target detection [17], spoof detection [18] and person identification [19].
DL has risen as a popular subject in the domains of ML and artificial intelligence because of its strong data-learning capacities. Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) have also achieved tremendous success in recent times [19,20,21]. Recently, researchers have employed CNNs for human action recognition [22,23], document classification [6], blockchain security [7], and superhero classification [8]. Popular techniques such as CNNs and LSTM are being used more frequently. A hybrid model of a CNN and LSTM has been suggested. CNNs performed feature extraction, which was followed by using LSTM to capture the long-term dependencies. GloVe’s pre-trained word embeddings were used to encode words into vectors, resulting in the model outperforming seven conventional ML methods [24].
One hybrid model combined the DeepCnnLstm and DeepCnnBilstm models and had the maximum accuracy in detecting false news on the FA-KES dataset [25]. A system for detecting fake news is discussed in [26], which involves vote classifiers, feature extraction, and feature selection. This system outperforms other methods in its classification precision for the ISOT dataset, distinguishing between fake and real news. Several studies have established that DL models are more effective than probabilistic models. In [27], the authors propose an approach to identifying fake news on Facebook with the help of DL approaches. Analyzing their method, account behavior is assessed by employing features associated with Facebook accounts, as well as news content characteristics.
In response to the fake news problem, [28] presents novel solutions depending on ML and DL. OPCNN-FAKE is a novel variant of CNN that demonstrates superior performance on several datasets and outperforms the existing models in the detection of fake news.
More recently, the transformer model emerged as a critical innovation in DL in the context of Natural Language Processing (NLP). A comparative investigation was conducted to detect fake news using five distinct transformers: XLNet, BERT, RoBERTa, DistilBERT, and ALBERT models. The authors then tried out different hyperparameters, with a similar performance between the transformer models [29]. Another model utilized a transformer model with RoBERTa and BART capabilities to distinguish between various types of articles. The BART and RoBERTa embeddings were sorted using two different LSTM CNN-based branches and then integrated and fed through another LSTM and CNN to acquire the final output [30].
FakeBERT was introduced as a solution to address the problem of ambiguity in NLP. Due to its incorporation of BERT and DL techniques, this model presents a high accuracy of 98.90%, which is considered a better performance than that of previous models [31]. Another state-of-the-art model was described in [32], where a modern strategy for positioning and hyperparameter optimization called HyproBert was designed to meet the challenge of detecting fake news. The experiments that were conducted on the ISOT and FA-KES datasets show that the proposed model performs better than other baseline and state-of-the-art methods.
However, in the more recent past, Large Language Models (LLMs) such as GPT and BERT have been used for fake news detection because they capture contextualized language usage patterns. RumorLLM is one of the models built upon this tradition, which are models fine-tuned to identify rumor-writing styles and contents. It must be noted that the underlined fine-tuning used in RumorLLM extends prior work that established the significance of domain-specific training for LLMs [33]. Some existing studies have portrayed the effectiveness of solely using models such as ChatGPT and Google Gemini in misinformation detection. Thus, in the process of verifying facts, both developed models demonstrated efficiency when interacting with the LIAR benchmark dataset by providing high levels of accuracy and F1-score. Such conclusions echo those of previous studies of automated fact-checking systems [34].

3.1. Data Preprocessing A sequence of preprocessing phases is implemented on the textual data, during which they are normalized, transformed, and preprocessed into a format that is appropriate for feature extraction and the subsequent process. In the context of fake news detection, removing redundant elements and reducing noise can enhance the model’s ability to focus on key terms that carry semantic weight.
- Stop word elimination: In NLP and text mining, there are some word terms that have a high usage across languages, which are commonly termed “stop words.” In the English language, these are the words “a”, “the”, “is”, and “are”. These words are normally removed, as they add no significant amount of useful information to text. Fortunately, the NLTK library offers a huge list of stop words as per the requirements. First, the text is preprocessed through stop word removal, and stop words present in the text are deleted. This is applied because stop words are much more common and do not contribute much in terms of meaning to a given text. Some well-known conjunctions include “and”, “or”, “but”, etc.. In NLP, preprocessing is very important since handling these less informative but very frequent words will largely take time and resources.

- Clean-up: Commas as used in natural language provide a necessary grammatical framework to sentences. However, a symbol such as a comma does not significantly contribute to the overall comprehension of the text that has been provided. This process also entails removing links in posts from social media accounts. This is usually carried out through the application of regular expressions.

- Tokenization: The text is first cleaned and subsequently divided into various tokens or terms, resulting in a tokenized version of the content. This phase is crucial for the facilitation of subsequent processing and additional analysis.

- Lemmatization or stemming: At this point, either lemmatization or stemming is performed on the text as a preprocessing step. The WordNet Lemmatizer in the NLTK package is used for lemmatization, and the NLTK’s Snowball Stemmer uses the Porter2 stemming algorithm for stemming.

- Lowercase conversion: In this process, all words are converted into lowercase in order to reduce variability due to capitalization.

- Substitution: Some textual components are changed into their corresponding words or basic forms. This process comprises unfolding of contractions, like converting “I’ll” into “I will”; translating emojis into the words they represent; and replacing multiple instances of the same symbol or character with one instance if this is possible, like transforming “happppppy” into “happy”.

3.2. Vector Representation TF - IDF is a statistical method that gives consideration to the normalized amount of feature items in document analysis while taking the IDF of the feature item in a document selection into consideration at the same time [35]. This is used to calculate the importance of every term in a document by comparing it with other documents; hence, it is useful for tasks that involve text classification and information retrieval [36]. Due to the fact that TF- IDF boosts terms that are more informative and reduces the significance of those terms that provide less valuable information, the application of this method allows us to enhance the model and distinguish between different types of content, including fake news and real information. TF - IDF , being the simplest but most common method for weight calculation, appropriately estimates the relevance of selected words in the total text. 3.4. Proposed BERT Figure 4 provides an overview of the BERT model that has been proposed. We chose BERT because of its high effectiveness in NLP tasks and its ability, which prove better than those of many standard techniques according to its abilities in attention and contextual understanding [40]. Below is a clear explanation of the BERT model, with its different parts, the role each plays, and its importance.
The Input Layer: The data are preprocessed before being processed in the BERT input layer, which consists of the title and body of the news article tokenization. They are then encoded using a dictionary, which assigns a distinct number identifier to each token. To achieve this, the vector input is padded, guaranteeing equal sizes of the inputs. The words in the document N are ultimately converted into tokens t and subsequently mapped to an index using the dictionary D , where N is represented as N ∈ D 1 × t . For tokenization, the DistilBERT Tokenizer is used due to its potential to provide more extensive character-level tokenization.
The Word Embedding Layer: The word embedding layer is one of the features used in word representations and is used to train the word representations on huge quantities of real-world information. It notes that words that have similar meanings in the dictionary have similar vectors. DistilBERT, a simplified variant of BERT, embeddings are employed. DistilBERT is trained to generate approximately 97% of the performance of the BERT model by estimating the posterior distribution injected into the BERT model. While DistilBERT is a model with over 110 million parameters, it is much more compact compared to BERT because it only uses 6 layers of transformers. In contrast, its training and prediction times are significantly shorter, which makes DistilBERT a better fit for our proposed model.
The Convolutional Layer: The architecture of CNNs is composed of convolutional layers. This ensures that the output of the neurons is properly controlled and corresponds to the scalar product of its weight with the local receptive field of the input and enables the connection of the neurons with the region near the input. In the proposed BERT model, the convolutional layer is employed to obtain the spatial features of the embedding layer. We use a one-dimensional convolution layer with a filter size of 128. To obtain high-level spatial features, the local features obtained from the previous step are passed through the MaxPooling function. The ReLU activation function is incorporated and helps to boost the performance. Filter weight W , window size x , bias b , and i represent the index feature sequence.
The Attention Layer: A fixed-size vector is used to store the contextual data and representations that are derived from the convolutional layer. However, when it comes to large input sequences, not all the required information is preserved in the vector, which thereby hampers the model’s understanding and results in its lower efficiency. The ability of attention mechanisms to assign dynamically and adaptively relevant weights to the most important segments of the input is acknowledged. Also, self-attention (SA) helps in the interaction of all inputs at once to give an output for each and every input, which is shown in Figure 5. SA is a reliable algorithm for the identification of self-awareness, as well as the dependency within input sequences. It incorporates the whole environment of the vector representations and then replaces the words by considering the same weights of their position.
The proposed BERT model uses self-attention (SA) to improve the contextual information and suppress important features to uncover the relationships within a news dataset. 3.6. The Classification Phase In the classification stage of our proposed methodology, we employed a neural network architecture called the FLNet [10], as illustrated in Figure 6. The FLNet has a unique characteristic in how its weights are initialized, as well as the weighted connections.
Initialization of the weight: The weights and biases of the FLNet’s hidden layer are initialized randomly. This is a common practice in neural networks, as it aids in training by adding variability to the initialization of weights to prevent the model from being trapped in local minima. The network can initiate a search of the weight space and identify superior solutions by randomly initializing the weights.
Weighted connections: The input and output layers, as well as the hidden and output nodes, of the FLNet are connected through weighted connections. Within the architecture of the multi-layer and multi-node network, these weighted connections regulate the flow of information. These connection weights are then determined by least squares methods—calculations that are used to determine the line or curve that fits a provided set of data best. Essentially, the FLNet aims to improve the learning rates of the network and the connectivity of the nodes in the network by directly connecting the input nodes to both the hidden layer and the output nodes. This decreases the number of layers between the input and output, which may enable the network to collect significant features directly and provide exact predictions.

4. Experiment In this section, the results of the proposed model on three typical benchmark datasets are presented in detail and further supported by a comprehensive statistical analysis.
4.1. Description of the Dataset The ISOT dataset [11] comprises 45,000 items, with an approximate distribution of both fake and legitimate news. The authentic news stories were sourced from the Reuters website, while the counterfeit ones were sourced from other untrustworthy websites scrutinized by PolitiFact. The information comprises the title, complete text, article date, and subject. The subjects predominantly pertain to politics and global news, covering the period from 2016 to 2017.
A curated list of 1001 websites provides millions of news stories for the Fake News Corpus (FNC), a benchmarking dataset ( https://github.com/several27/FakeNewsCorpus , accessed on 10 June 2024). Due to its original lack of reliable websites, The New York Times and Webz.io (English-NewsArticles) were added to balance the data. The HTML files and tags were preprocessed to establish the metadata on the author information, full text, and title and other metadata. The corpus tags articles as fake news, conspiracy theories, political, and credible. For this study, 1,000,000 articles were randomly selected from the database, including equal amounts of fake news and reliable content.
The FA-KES dataset [41] is incorporated specifically to target news stories pertaining to the Syrian war. The collection comprises 804 articles, each comprising the full text, location, news sources, publication date, and headline. The class labels are defined as 0 and 1 to represent fake and real news, respectively. The dataset is evenly distributed, with 53% as true and 47% labeled as fraudulent. The total number of real and fake news is shown in Table 1. The train, test, and validation split corresponding to each class is given in Table 2.
4.2. Experimental and Hyperparameter Settings The study utilized three datasets—ISOT, FNC, and FA-KES—that were partitioned into independent training, testing, and validation sets. While 20% of the cases from all three datasets went into the testing set and 10% were set aside for validation, 70% of the cases overall from all three datasets went into the training set.
The experiments were performed using an Intel i9-10850K processor, along with a Nvidia TITAN Xp GPU. As for the implementation of the model, Python was used. The softmax activation function, Adam as the optimizer, and a dropout rate of 0.1 were implemented in the proposed model, which was run over 100 epochs with a batch size of 16. The model was trained using the categorical cross-entropy loss function, which is an ideal function for multi-class classification. Further, the early stopping technique was used so that training was stopped at 20 epochs if no improvement in the validation accuracy was observed. This makes the results generalize better by avoiding overfitting and checking that the chosen hyperparameters give the most accurate results within the training set at the same time. For evaluation, we adopted four commonly used metrics: accuracy, precision, recall, and F1-score. The experimental results are replicated a minimum of three times under uniform settings to calculate the average outcomes, which are presented in the subsequent section.
4.3. Results on the ISOT Dataset We evaluated the proposed technique in relation to various ML and DL models utilizing TF - IDF’s same word embedding strategy. The proposed model is conclusively superior to all the other models, encompassing an accuracy of 99.94%, along with a recall of 99.66%, a precision of 98.82%, and an F1-score of 99.22%. Among the other models, DistilBERT and BiLSTM are also quite effective, with DistilBERT having an accuracy level of 94.76% and BiLSTM achieving 92.67%. Thus, these findings corroborate the effectiveness of the proposed model, exemplifying increased accuracy alongside an evenly matched set of recall, precision, and F1-score values. This shows that the proposed model gives the ability not only to identify instances that relate to the given search terms (recall) but also to ensure that the instances identified are accurate (precision), thus providing an excellent classification ability on the whole. The KNN model has a slightly better performance, with an F1-score of 67.18% and an accuracy of 68.19%. Sophisticated models comprising LSTM and the GRU bring additional performance improvements, with LSTM attaining 71.57% and 70.71% in terms of accuracy and F1-score. BERT-base and DistilBERT, using transformer structures, show substantial progress, while DistilBERT leads, with an accuracy and F1-score of 86.78% and 83.57%, outperforming BERT-Base, which achieves an accuracy of 81.44% and an F1-score of 80.75%. The BiLSTM model shows an even higher performance, with an accuracy of 96.89% and an F1-score of 92.29%, surpassing all previous models. Notably, the proposed model yields the highest accuracy of 99.32%, a recall of 99.99%, and an F1-score of 98.84%, setting a new benchmark in terms of performance.
4.5. Results on the FA-KES Dataset Table 5 depicts the performance of the different models on the FA-KES dataset: As for the SVM and KNN, these classifiers have a lower performance and accuracies of 59.08% and 56.21%, respectively. The LSTM and BiLSTM models show a higher performance, and BiLSTM reached an accuracy of 72.84%. Transformer-based models, DistilBERT in particular, achieved up to 76.42% accuracy. The proposed model performs exceptionally well, achieving an accuracy as high as 96.67%. This positive accuracy of 96.67% and F1-score of 92.55%, far ahead of the values of all other models, indicate a significant performance improvement.
4.6. McNemar’s Statistical Test for Model Performance Comparison One of the most popular metrics for measuring the performance of models and their selection in studies is accuracy. To compare the accuracy of the models and determine whether there were significant differences between the models, we used a correction of McNemar’s test. Table 1, Table 2 and Table 3 reveal that the proposed model has the highest accuracy compared to any other model used in the study. To compare the proposed model with other models, we calculated the χ ² statistic and p -values and put them in each table. In our study, we fixed the significance levels at α = 0.01, while the confidence level was set at 99%. As per the configured α and the calculated p -values, we made the decision of whether to accept or reject the null hypothesis. If p < α, the null hypothesis was rejected, indicating a large disparity in the performance of the models. On the other hand, when the p -value was equal to or greater than α ( p -value ≥ α), this meant that the null hypothesis could be accepted, which can be interpreted as meaning that the performance of the models is not significantly different.
All of the models in Table 3, such as the SVM, KNN, LSTM, BiLSTM, the GRU, and even the BERT-Base model, have p -values less than the 0.01 level, meaning that their performance is rather divergent from the proposed model at the 0.01 significance level and thus they have a significantly different performance. On the other hand, one can observe that DistilBERT achieved a lower level of significance, with a p -value larger than the 0.01 level, indicating the closest proximity to the proposed model and still registering a statistically significant difference. In Table 4, it can be noted that only BiLSTM ranks potentially statistically significantly higher than a p -value of 0.01, thus implying that it was somewhat comparable to the proposed model’s performance in terms of accuracy on this dataset, although the other models showed poor performance on this dataset as well. Table 5 provides the chi-square test results, with p -values less than 0.01 for the comparison of all models with the model proposed, indicating that the differences in performance are statistically significant at the 99% confidence level on the FA-KES dataset.
4.7. Comparison of Computational Complexity Our methodology, characterized by 11.4 billion parameters, demonstrates impressive sequence processing capabilities, vital for complex tasks like NLP. This extensive parameterization allows the model to discern intricate patterns and relationships in data, making it adept at applications such as sentiment analysis and text classification. However, this complexity comes at the cost of substantial computational resource demands, resulting in high operational costs and prolonged training times, particularly with large datasets.
Scalability presents another challenge, as the model requires effective parallelization to mitigate these issues, often necessitating high-performance hardware for efficient training and inference. In contrast, the distributed SVM approach discussed in [42] achieves fast convergence while maintaining a minimal optimality gap. This method is particularly advantageous in decentralized multi-agent environments, enabling agents to collaboratively reach an optimal classifier with a reduced communication overhead.
Nevertheless, the distributed SVM’s reliance on agent communication can introduce latency, especially in larger networks. Each methodology has its advantages: our hybrid model excels at feature extraction from sequential data, offering deep contextual understanding but at a significant resource cost. Conversely, the distributed SVM provides efficient scalability and speed, making it suitable for tasks requiring fast and effective classification in decentralized settings. Ultimately, the choice between these methodologies should depend on the specific demands of the application, weighing up the importance of computational efficiency and scalability against the need for advanced feature extraction and model performance on complex datasets.
4.8. Comparison with State-of-the-Art Methods Table 6 presents a comparison of the model performance across different datasets, highlighting the accuracy and F1-score metrics. On the ISOT dataset, previous models achieve high accuracy, with values ranging from 92.32% to 99.00% and F1-scores ranging between 91.85% and 99.00%. The proposed model stands out, with an accuracy and an F1-score of 99.94% and 99.22%, respectively, exceeding all other models. For the FNC dataset, the existing models show varying levels of performance, with accuracies ranging from 92.50% to 96.00%, but only partial F1-score information is available. The proposed model demonstrates superior performance on the FNC dataset, with an F1-score of 98.84% and an accuracy of 99.32%. On the FA-KES dataset, the previous models exhibit a lower performance, with accuracies from 60.00% to 68.00% and F1-scores between 59.00% and 74.00%. The proposed model again excels, achieving an accuracy of 96.67% and an F1-score of 92.55%. Overall, the proposed model consistently delivers exceptional results across all datasets, setting a new standard in its accuracy and F1-score. Figure 7 visually illustrates these findings.
5. Conclusions In this work, utilizing three distinct datasets, we aimed to evaluate a DNN model for the detection of false news. The research works we introduced have mostly addressed the use of DNNs and TF - IDF for feature extraction, the possibility of working on samples of various sizes, and the application of the FLNet in the classification stage. The idea of applying DNNs to feature extraction is based on the fact that BERT can learn spatial features while BiLSTM-GRU can learn temporal features, with both incorporated in parallel in the proposed approach. The results of our approach illustrated its high levels of accuracy for both datasets compared to other similar approaches. Nevertheless, this is restricted only to datasets in the English language. As for future studies, they should involve non-English datasets to define a more representative system and the investigation of assessments of multilinear content created by users.
