Headnote Abstract
Correcting misinformation is a complex task, influenced by various psychological, social, and technical factors. Most research evaluation methods for identifying effective correction approaches tend to rely on either crowdsourc-ing, questionnaires, lab-based simulations, or hypothetical scenarios. However, the translation of these methods and findings into real-world settings, where individuals willingly and freely disseminate misinformation, remains largely unexplored. Consequently, we lack a comprehensive understanding of how individuals who share misinformation in natural online environments would respond to corrective interventions. In this study, we explore the effectiveness of corrective messaging on 3898 users who shared misinformation on Twitter/X over 2 years. We designed and deployed a bot to automatically identify individuals who share misinformation and subsequently alert them to related fact-checks in various message formats. Our analysis shows that only a small minority of users react positively to the corrective messages, with most users either ignoring them or reacting negatively. Nevertheless, we also found that more active users were proportionally more likely to react positively to corrections and we observed that different message tones made particular user groups more likely to react to the bot.
INTRODUCTION
In today's fast-paced information age, misinformation poses a significant hurdle to the quest for scientific knowledge and clarity. Misinformation is contaminating our decision-making in various critical areas, from public health to politics, eroding trust and hindering informed choices. Throughout the Covid-19 pandemic, studies demonstrated the rapid proliferation of misinformation, along with the resilience of certain false claims against fact-checking efforts (Burel, Farrell, and Alani 2021).
People tend to have some degree of resistance to corrections (Lewandowsky et al. 2012). This resistance to misinformation correction is often driven by many factors, including social, cognitive, affective, legal, educational, and technical (Ecker et al. 2022).
Many studies emerged to assess the effectiveness of various intervention methods for correcting misinformation (e.g., Velez, Porter, and Wood 2023; Boukes and Hameleers 2023; Bode, Vraga, and Tully 2020). However, most of the interventions investigated were not put into practice and evaluated within a real scenario in a genuine social media environment. Instead, they were tested in highly controlled settings or simulated environments (Gwiazdz-ihski et al. 2023). As a consequence, the applicability and real-world effectiveness of these interventions may be limited or uncertain.
In this paper, we investigate the effects of providing corrective information to Twitter/X users who are involved in spreading misinformation. We focus our work on social media as people are shifting away from accessing news on traditional news outlets (Flintham et al. 2018). The aim is to reach a better understanding of how social media users react when exposed to such corrections in a real-world setting. To run this experiment, we created a Twitter/X bot that searches the platform for misinforming posts, using a database of known misinformation claims and their corresponding fact-checks. When the bot detects a misinforming post, it automatically sends a reply message to alert the sender about the related fact-check and its verdict on the shared content. The replies posted by the bot are formed using different templates to enable us to study the potential impact of different tonal narratives on users' reactions.
In this article, we describe our work where we analyze 3898 corrective messages sent by the bot to unique users for nearly 2 years (May 2021 to June 2023) to understand how misinformation spreaders react to automatic online misinformation corrections. Out of the 3898 corrective messages sent, only 443 messages showed a detectable kind of reaction to the bot's intervention, and 246 of them even replied back.
We were able to detect and collect data for six types of reactions to our corrective messages; reposting (i.e., retweeting) or liking the bot's reply, replying back, following or blocking the bot, and deleting the misinforming post. Some of these actions can be considered as positive reactions, such as liking the bot's corrective post or deleting the misinforming post, whereas some are negative reactions, such as blocking the bot or sending back an offensive reply. As expected, only a small minority of users reacted positively, and some triggered a mixture of positive and negative reactions, such as liking the correction but also blocking the bot. In this paper, we analyze these interactions to understand the impact of our intervention on the recipients who deliberately and publicly shared misinforming posts.
Our findings demonstrate the additional complexity of correcting misinformation in real-world environments and scenarios, identify several user properties that appear to correlate with general user reactions as well as positive, negative, or mixed reactions, and highlight some of the issues of concern or dissatisfaction that were relayed back by the recipients of our corrections. The contributions of this paper can be summarized as follows:
(1) Analyze the real-world reactions of 3898 unique Twitter/X users who shared misinformation after receiving automated corrections;
(2) Show that out of the 11.4% of the users who reacted to the bot, 12.2, 78.3, and 9.5% showed positive, negative, or mixed reactions respectively;
(3) annotate 246 replies to our interventions, identifying 15 (6.1%) of these replies as positive, 195 (79.3%) as negative, and 36 (14.6%) as neutral;
(4) Discover that, in general, users' reactions are not strongly influenced by the tone of corrective messages of the bot; except for users that are more active on Twitter/X (i.e., users that, relatively, are more popular and produce a lot of content);
(5) Highlight that more active users are proportionally more likely to react positively to corrections compared to less active users.
RELATED WORK
Interventions for addressing negative online human behaviors tend to focus on three strands (Kozyreva, Lewandowsky, and Hertwig 2020; Kozyreva et al. 2022); refuting beliefs, boosting competence, and nudging behaviors. Refuting belief (technocognition) involves altering how users interact and consume content online, including UX changes and source assessment. Boosting competence is focused on enhancing media literacy and fact-checking. Nudging is concerned with influencing people's behavior through warnings and corrections.
Various nudging techniques have been studied to identify the most efficient means of delivering corrective information to individuals who are inclined to spread misinformation. Most of such studies were performed in lab or crowdsourcing environments (Gwiazdziriski et al. 2023). Field-based studies (ecological methods) on correcting misinformation are performed in real-world settings and use people who are unaware that they are in an experiment (Bhuiyan et al. 2018). We review work related to these two forms of misinformation correction studies in the following sections.
Lab misinformation correction studies
Most misinformation correction studies involved some type of controlled lab experiments (Fridkin, Kenney, and Wintersieck 2015; Wood and Porter 2019; Ecker et al. 2020; Piccolo et al. 2021; Wang 2022; Huang and Wang 2022; Velez, Porter, and Wood 2023; Boukes and Hameleers 2023). One advantage of lab experiments is that complex variables and scenarios can be investigated. However, such experiments are likely to suffer from self-selection bias (Heckman 1990) and be limited in the number of participants. Another challenge encountered in field experiments is that individuals who have been corrected may actively counterargue to challenge the corrective information. Such defensive reactions can weaken the effectiveness of the corrections (Huang and Wang 2022). In the context of misinformation correction, this means that indirect measures of misinformation correction effectiveness need to be designed.
Many lab studies focused on testing different communication formats for reducing belief in misinformation. Velez, Porter, and Wood (2023) explored the influence of corrections in curbing the spread of false information in marginalized communities. They found that different formats of corrections have a notably greater effect on positively altering beliefs compared to the influence of misinformation in negatively shaping beliefs.
Research by Fridkin, Kenney, and Wintersieck (2015) showed individuals with a different sensibility to a type of misinformation (i.e., negative advertisements) are likely to react differently to corrective fact-checks and that fact-check communications that use a negative tone are more impactful. Ecker et al. (2020) compared "false-tag" retractions (i.e., repetition of the misinformation claim and adding a false tag) and short-format refutations (i.e., full textual explanations of the misinformation). They found that short refutations are more effective than retractions. Another study by Swire-Thompson et al. (2021) investigated the order in which climate change myths are presented alongside corrections (i.e., myth-first, fact-first, fact-only, or myth-only). They found that corrections tend to be effective independently regardless of their format.
The study in Huang and Wang (2022) compares the effects of two correction formats, narrative and non-narrative, when applied through different correction mechanisms: algorithmic (i.e., sent by an algorithm) and social (i.e., sent by a friend). Participants were presented with Facebook posts and corresponding corrections in different combinations of formats and mechanisms. The authors concluded that for algorithmic corrections, the non-narrative format had a more significant influence on behavioral intentions. This impact was even more pronounced in social corrections, where the non-narrative format was considered both more credible and more effective. Their findings support our choice to utilize non-narrative algorithmic corrections in this study.
Other lab studies found that correction tones have little to no impact. A recent work by Boukes and Hameleers (2023) investigating satirical fact-checks confirmed previous research (Hameleers and van der Meer 2020; Nyhan et al. 2020; Wood and Porter 2019) and showed that different formats of fact-checks do not influence the impact of corrective information. Bode, Vraga, and Tully (2020),in their study, observed that the tone (uncivil, affirming, or neutral) applied to corrections on deceptive memes in a simulated Twitter feed did not affect the corrections' effectiveness. The studies above on the impact of tone show that the outcome vary according to various contexts and parameters. This supports our approach to further testing various tones when correcting individuals in a public setting, where they have naturally shared misinformation.
An initial limited user study by Piccolo et al. (2021) on the best tone to use for correcting Twitter/X users showed that individuals preferred factual and alerting corrective messages as they were identified as not being aggressive and confrontational whereas suggestive and empathetic messages were judged more negatively as being judging, aggressive, and condescending. The findings were limited and not tested on real misinformation sharers. In our work, we decided to reuse the same or very similar templates to identify if such observations translate to our live experiment.
Studies also showed that, naturally, individuals are more likely to accept fact-checks in private environments compared to public ones. Their research confirmed that individuals tend to be less receptive to fact-checks when they are corrected publicly (Wang 2022; Wood and Porter 2019). The experiments presented in this paper are performed in the wild on a public platform, and hence negative reception is to be expected. The aim is to repeat part of such lab tests in the wild and to measure and compare the performance of our corrective interventions in natural environments.
Overall, existing lab experiment research suggests that the way fact-checks are communicated can impact how they are perceived by misinformation spreaders. This observation serves as a motivation for employing different message tones in the research presented in this paper.
Field misinformation correction studies
Unlike lab-based studies, field-based experiments (Opgen-haffen 2022; Zhang et al. 2022; Whitehead et al. 2023) are naturally more complex to run, and their outcomes are more difficult to measure. They can also be influenced by more uncontrollable variables and unpredictable factors that could impact the outcome of the correction methods (Lee and Bissell 2023; Swire, Ecker, and Lewandowsky 2017). Very few studies have looked directly at communicating explicit correction to users sharing misinformation in natural environments, or to understand what type of individuals are more likely to accept fact-checks.
One of the few approaches that looked directly at correcting users through humorous imagery on social media was proposed by Opgenhaffen (2022). In this research, the author argues that direct fact-checking works. However,the study was limited to very few interactions. His work is complemented by research on the use of satire by Boukes and Hameleers (2023).
The utilization of humor and the incorporation of warnings concerning exposure to misinformation have been identified as promising approaches, as outlined in Whitehead et al. (2023). However, Zhang et al. (2022) noted that issuing warnings about misinformation in a stern or demanding tone may negatively impact the effectiveness of corrective measures. While analyzing online COVID-19 misinformation corrections on MicroBlogs based on theories on psychology and communication, Zhang et al. (2022) discovered that the corrections that are short, concise, persuasive, rich in graphics, in a gentle tone, and published by influential users are more effective.
Mosleh et al. (2021) studied the reaction of 2000 users being corrected on Twitter. They found that being corrected decreases the quality and language toxicity of the users' subsequent posts. Although their study used a bot, their approach voluntarily hid that fact to study the impact of social corrections. This approach differs from our work as we are not trying to hide the fact that a bot is used when correcting individuals.
In a similar vein, Tay et al. (2022) also reported that corrections decrease users' references to misinformation and increase the positive sentiment expressed in social media posts. Another recent study by He, Ahamad, and Kumar (2023) presented a method for generating corrective responses based on historical misinforming posts on social media. Although the author showed that automatic approaches could be used for generating quality responses by bots, their effectiveness was not evaluated in the field.
Our approach differs from prior experiments as we investigate the influence of corrective interventions with diverse tones on user reactions. We evaluate whether users respond, and if so, whether their reactions are positive, negative, or mixed. We transparently inform users that corrections come from fact-checkers and are delivered by a bot. Additionally, we scrutinize these responses to identify any significant correlations with different user characteristics, aiming to uncover patterns between specific traits and corresponding reactions.
CORRECTING MISINFORMATION WITH A TWITTER BOT
While the prevalence of bots on social media has been on the rise, serving both positive and malicious purposes, limited research has explored their potential to correct individuals who disseminate misinformation. Utilizing a bot to address misinformation spreaders offers several advantages compared to prior research, as it enables systematic corrections on a large scale with minimal supervision required.
To study the impact of automated corrections on individuals who shared misinformation, we created a Twitter/X bot that automatically identifies misinforming posts and replies with corrective information. We then monitor and analyze the reactions generated towards our interventions to better understand the effect of automatic corrections in a live environment. Our method for identifying fact-checks and misinforming posts follows a similar approach to our previous research on understanding the co-spread of misinformation and fact-checks on social media (Burel et al. 2020; Burel, Farrell, and Alani 2021) and our work on the Fact-checking Observatory (Burel and Alani 2023).
Our automated misinformation correction process and experiment can be broken down into four distinct steps:
(1) Retrieve information from a database containing misinformation URLs (e.g., web page, social media post) and their associated fact-checks;
(2) Search Twitter/X for posts containing any of the misinforming URLs in our dataset;
(3) Form and post an automated reply to misinforming posts, with a link to corresponding fact-checks;
(4) Collect reactions of the target users; that is, the user profile accounts that were corrected by the bot.
We collect the data generated from the experiment process above using the Twitter/X API and then proceed to analyze it in search of user reactions with various parameters related to the bot's intervention and the characteristics of the target user. We describe each step in more detail in the following section.
Step 1: Retrieving misinformation and fact-check data
We collect misinforming URLs and their credibility assessments from ClaimReviews.1 ClaimReview offers a standardized method for presenting and publishing online fact-checks, ensuring their accessibility for applications. ClaimReview tags can be found embedded into the HTML of fact-check pages, as in the snippet below (Figure 1). From these semantic annotations, we collect the claim content, URL, fact-checker's name, and credibility assessment outcomes (e.g., true, false, mixed). Since each fact-checker may use a different type of label for their assessment and more than one fact-checker may review the same claim, we normalize the ClaimReview assessments by combining the observed assessment into a credibility rating that we use for identifying misinforming claims (Mensio et al. 2023) (each claim is labeled as one of the following: not credible, uncertain (mixed), mostly credible, credible or not verifiable). To ensure high data quality, we exclusively gather ClaimReviews from organizations that are vetted and registered by the International Fact-checking Network2 (IFCN).
The database currently contains more than 140k fact-checks from 70 different fact-checking agencies based on 32 different countries.
Step 2: Searching for misinforming posts
The second step is focused on identifying the tweets that contain misinformation (i.e., posts that share URLs labeled as not credible by fact-checkers). These tweets exist in the public domain and we safely assume that they were shared by a Twitter/X user in a natural setting; that is, a nonlaboratory setting. Our misinformation correction bot continuously searches Twitter/X for the appearance in any language of any of the misinformation URLs in the dataset created during Step 1. Once the bot finds such a post, it will progress into Step 3 of the process. In accordance with Twitter's best practices for bots, we have implemented a set of restrictions to govern the behavior of our bot. Within this framework, our bot is programmed to conduct searches for up to 60 distinct URLs every 15 min and retrieves a maximum of 100 individual posts for each URL it searches for. To ensure compatibility with Twitter/X's query character constraints, our bot curates URLs that fall within the prescribed 512-character threshold. Additionally, it exclusively targets original tweets, thereby excluding responses to retweets.
Step 3: Forming and posting a corrective reply
Once a misinforming tweet is found, the bot automatically creates and posts a reply to that tweet in English, using the fact-check information in our dataset. One hypothesis we aimed to examine is whether varying corrective tones can elicit different reactions from target users. The base templates we chose are listed in Table 1, which are designed around different message tones (e.g., use of informal or sympathetic language). These templates are based on marginally modified versions of the ones proposed by Piccolo et al. (2021). To form a reply, the bot randomly selects one of these templates, and substitutes <FACT-CHECK-URL> token with the fact-checking URL that corresponds to the shared misinforming URL, and the <VERDICT> token with the fact-checker rating of the shared misinforming URL. Each reply ends with: "I'm a research bot fighting misinformation spread. Plz follow me & DM any feedback." The final step performed by the bot is to post the corrective message as a reply to the misinforming post.
Step 4: Collecting and categorizing user reactions
To understand the effectiveness of the bot in correcting misinformation, we collect a set of user reactions and categorize them into positive, negative, or neutral reactions.
Table 2 highlights the individual reactions we collect and their polarity categorization. In order to categorize the various textual replies we obtained from the target users, we manually annotate each message as either negative, neutral, or positive. The annotation methodology is discussed in more detail in "User Replies Data" section.
The overall reaction of the target user is drawn from the mixture of their individual reactions. We consider a target user to have reacted positively if all the user's reactions are positive (e.g., user liked and reposted the bot's post). The user's overall reaction is deemed negative when all reactions taken by the user are negative (e.g., sending a negative reply and blocking the bot). When the user takes a mix of positive, negative, and neutral actions (e.g., liking the bot's reply but also blocking the bot) or the user only responds with a neutral reply, we consider this to be a mixed reaction.
In addition to these reactions, we also collect for each target user their number of followers, the number of accounts they are following, and the total number of messages they have posted.
AUTOMATED MISINFORMATION CORRECTIONS DATABASE
In this section, we describe the user reaction data we collected from the bot's automated corrections. This data forms the basis for our analysis, which aims to reach a better understanding of the impact of using our Twitter/X bot to post replies to misinforming social media posts.
Between March 25, 2021 and June 27, 2023, the bot posted 5345 corrective tweets targeting 3898 distinct users. As part of our analysis, we only focus on the first message sent by the bot to the distinct 3898 users in order to reduce the risk of bias towards the users that have been corrected multiple times. As a result, we concentrate our analysis on 3898 messages representing the first messages that targeted each individual. Within these messages, only 11.36% of the targeted users (443 users) elicited one of the reactions listed in Table 2.
User replies data
Within the 3898 first messages sent by the bot, 246 users wrote a message back to the bot in the form of replies to the bot's corrective message. Replies can be regarded as positive, negative, or neutral, depending on the content of these replies. To determine which category every one of the 246 replies belongs to, three annotators labeled each reply independently as positive, negative, or neutral. Fleiss'
Kappa interannotator agreement measure was 0.92, showing a high degree of overall agreement. Only 10 out of the total of 246 did not receive full agreement from the three annotators. Those were subjected to a discussion between the annotators to reach a forced agreement between the annotators (Braun 2023) following the approach recommended by Oortwijn, Ossenkoppele, and Betti (2021) and a final label was agreed upon for each one. User reaction patterns data
Out of the 64 possible reaction combinations (e.g., no reaction to the bot (P01), blocking the bot only (P33), blocking the bot and deleting (P49), etc.), only 17 distinct reaction patterns were found in our data when not considering the replies polarity (we observed 22 patterns when including the replies polarity). All the patterns are listed in Table 4, along with the number of times each pattern occurred in the target-user reaction data we were able to collect. The top pattern is No Reaction, which formed the vast majority of cases. Although there are more distinctive positive patterns than negative ones, the total number of times negative patterns appeared is much higher than the positive or mixed ones. As explained in "Step 2: Searching for misinforming posts" section, the bot randomly selects one of our seven reply templates to form the automated corrective response. ANALYSIS OF THE IMPACT OF CORRECTING MISINFORMATION SHARERS
In this section, we apply various analyses to the data described in the previous section that we collected from our experiment. The aim of the analysis of the reactions generated by our interventions is to gain a deeper understanding of the impact of automatic corrections in a real-time environment.
Our analysis is split into the following two main parts:
(1) Reaction versus no reaction: Compare two groups of target users; those who reacted to the bot in some manner and those who did not elicit any reaction.
(2) Positive versus negative versus mixed reactions: Analyze the cases where the target users reacted in a positive, negative, or mixed manner.
In each part, we conduct a range of statistical analyses to assess the potential influence of various factors on people's responses to the corrective messages from the bot. The factors we analyze are:
(1) User popularity: number of people following the person targeted by the corrective bot.
(2) Followed accounts: number of people followed by the target user. Post count: number of posts created by the target user.
(3) Account age: number of days since the creation of the target user account on Twitter.
(4) Reply template: the template used by the bot when correcting a target user (see Table 1).
Reaction versus no reaction
The first part of our analysis focuses on understanding whether individuals who share misinformation react to the bot when they are targeted by corrective messages. In this analysis, our primary focus is fixed on the binary presence of reactions, regardless of whether they are positive, mixed, or negative in nature. Overall, out of the 3898 unique users targeted by the bot, only 443 explicitly reacted to the bot (11.36%). This shows that most users ignore the corrective messages sent by the bot and do not react explicitly to the bot posts.
To analyze the relationship between user attributes and the bot messages, we categorize each user attribute's values based on their quantiles. This categorization divides each user group into low, medium, and high categories, with each category containing an equal portion of users based on the attribute's distribution in our dataset. Using this technique, we obtain the following intervals for each user attribute:
(1) User popularity (number of followers): low: < 186, medium: [186,774), and high: > 774.
(2) Number of followed accounts: low: < 266, medium: [266,991), and high:> 991.
(3) Post count (number of posts created by the user): low: < 4007, medium: [4007,16,512) and high: > 16,512.
(4) Account age (number of days since the creation of the account): low: < 508, medium: [508,2648), and high: > 2648.
Next, we compare the two user groups, those who reacted and those who did not, in relation to the five factors mentioned above. Our goal is to identify any significant correlations that may provide insights into the issues influencing users' likelihood to react or not react.
Replies and binary reaction stance
In this section, we focus our analysis on the previously defined user groups and low, medium, and high categories to understand how they react to the bot's corrections. We define the user group-specific null hypothesis as follows: Distinct user categories do not display different binary reactions to the bot's corrective messages. The user categories are determined by the analyzed user variable, such as user popularity levels or seniority on Twitter/X. Figure 2 shows the distribution of binary reactions for each analyzed user variable.
If significant relationships are identified, we perform post hoc analyses to identify where these relationships differ (e.g., comparison across different popularity levels). Since multiple analyses are performed when performing the post hoc analyses, the p-values are adjusted using Bonferroni correction.
User popularity and binary reaction stance: As mentioned above, user popularity is reflected here by the number of people who follow the target user on Twitter/X. We use Fisher's Exact Test and Chi-square analysis to determine the general relation between user popularity and reacting or not reacting at all to the bot's correction interventions.
We observe a significant correlation between user popularity and user's reaction/no-reaction stance (Table 6), indicating that target users with different levels of popularity take distinct binary reaction stances towards the bot.
To further explore and understand the differences between specific feature groups (Figure 2), we use
Bonferroni-adjusted Chi-square and Fisher's Exact Post hoc Analysis Tests. The post hoc tests results in Table 7 show that significant differences exist across the high/low and low/medium user popularity levels. The chi-square residuals (noted r) indicate that relatively highly popular users (more than 774 followers) are more likely to elicit a reaction to the bot (r = 3.65, 43% of the users that reacted) compared with users with lower popularity (<186 followers, r = -AAA, 21%).
Followed accounts and binary reaction stance:
Here we analyze the correlation of the number of users followed by an individual with their reaction or no reaction stance. The aim is to see if those that follow a high, medium, or low number of other accounts are more or less likely to react to the bot's correction posts. Similarly to the previous analysis, we also see a significant correlation between the number of followed accounts and the user's reaction/no-reaction stance (Table 6). This indicates that target users with different levels of followed accounts have distinct binary reaction stances towards the bot.
In this case, we only observe a significant difference between the high (> 991) and low (< 266) levels (Table 6). Looking at the residual, we see that high values (r = 2.66, 41%) lead to more reactions than low values (r = -2.78, 26%). In other words, users targeted with corrections are more likely to take action if they are following a relatively high number of people on Twitter/X. Those who follow a relatively low number of accounts tend to ignore the bot's correction posts completely.
Post counts and binary reaction stance:
As shown in Table 6, there are significant differences in how the levels of users posting activities react to the bot. We see significant differences for all three user posting activity levels (Table 7).
The residuals for the post counts and binary reaction show strong residuals between the users that post a lot (high number of posts, r = 4.23, 45%) and the users that publish fewer posts (low number of posts, r = -4.34,21%) indicating that users that react to the bot are very unlikely to be relatively low post producers.
Account age and binary reaction stance:
Similarly to the above, significance is found when applying Chi-square and Fisher Tests to account-age and reaction/no-reaction stance (Table 6). The post hoc analysis results for the user's account age on the platform (Table 7) show significant differences between high and low as well as low and medium user seniority levels in the platform. We also observe that users with a moderate account age on the platform (users that have been registered on Twitter/X for more than 508 days up to 2648 days) are more likely to react to the bot (r = 2.35, 40%) compared to users that have been registered less than 508 days (r = -3.77, 23%). One plausible explanation is that users with lower activity levels visit the platform less frequently, which may result in them paying less attention to ongoing activities, including the bot's corrective replies.
Reply templates and binary reaction stance
As previously discussed in "Step 3: Forming and posting a corrective reply" section, we employed a series of templates to craft the corrective responses sent to target users. In this section, we assess the influence of utilizing various templates on users' reactions or lack thereof to the bot's corrections. The objective is to determine whether certain reply styles we employed may have encouraged more users to respond. Therefore, the hypothesis we are testing here is that user reactions to the bot are influenced by the different message templates employed by the bot. The overall reactions to the various bot templates are highlighted in Figure 3.
The Fisher's Exact Test comparing the bot templates to overall user reactions yielded a nonsignificant p-value of 0.6115, indicating that there is no significant association between the bot templates and the overall response (reaction or no reaction) of the users targeted by the bot. A similar outcome is observed when conducting a Chi-square Test with simulated p-values (x2(N = 3898) = 4.411, p = 0.617, p > 0.05). We use simulated p-values for the Chi-square Test due to the low distribution of some of the expected values. This suggests that the hypothesis mentioned above is invalid.
Next, we expand this analysis by taking into account the four user features we tested earlier; user popularity, followed accounts, post count, and account-age. The goal is to test the hypothesis that the reactions of certain groups of users to the bot are influenced by the different message templates employed by the bot. To examine this hypothesis, we employ the Cochran-Mantel-Haenszel (CMH) test to assess how specific message templates influence the binary reaction stances of our different user groups. The reactions to the various bot templates for each user category are highlighted in Figure 4.
The results presented in Table 8 reveal a significant association between users' binary responses (reaction or no reaction) to our corrections and the different user categories when considering various bot templates. This outcome indicates that there are disparities in how different user categories respond to the various types of bot messages.
To gain deeper insights into how user behavior may differ based on the bot templates, we conduct Bonferroni-adjusted Post hoc Chi-square and Fisher's Exact Test with simulated p-values tests to pinpoint which template types may elicit a distinct reaction or no reaction outcome for each user subtype (Table 9).
We notice a significant result (p < 0.05) in cases where Alerting and Factual messages are directed at users with varying levels of popularity. We also identify significant correlations between the use of Alerting and Factual message templates and various post-count groups (p < 0.05),with an even greater significance when employing the Friendly template (p < 0.001).
While an initial significant association was detected using the CMH Test (Table 8) concerning different reply templates and user groups based on the number of accounts they follow or the age of their account on the platform, no significant results were found during the post hoc analyses. Therefore, we are unable to confidently determine which bot corrective message template contributes to the observed cumulative statistical significance revealed by the CMH Test.
Reply templates and user post-counts:
Since we observed significant correlations between the use of Alerting, Factual, and Friendly templates for the various post-count groups (Table 9), we perform additional post hoc tests for these templates and user categories (Table 10). We only observe significant results for the low and high post-count groups for the Alerting, Factual, and Friendly templates.
Upon examining the residuals and the distribution of reactions, it becomes apparent that Friendly messages primarily influence users who generate a relatively high volume of posts (r = 3.06, 53%). We see that users with medium to high numbers of posts represent 81% of the users reacting to the Factual messages whereas the same users represent 80% of the users reacting to the Alerting messages. This shows that users with medium-high post counts are more likely to react to corrective messages using the Friendly, Factual, or Alerting writing styles.
Reply templates and user popularity:
As we observed significant correlations between the use of Alerting and Factual templates for the various popularity groups (Table 9), we focus our post hoc tests on these templates and user groups (Table 10). We only observe significant results for the low and high popularity groups for the Alerting and Factual templates.
Highly popular users (more than 774 followers) are most likely to react to the Alerting messages compared to less popular users (r = 2.29, 50%). Users with a medium or higher level of follower counts represent 78% of the reacting users. When looking at the response to the Factual messages, we do not observe a significant difference between the distribution of moderately and highly popular users that react to the bot messages even though they represent 80% of the reactions. However, we see that very few users with <186 followers are likely to be among the reacting users (r = -2.34, 20%).
These results suggest that users with moderate to high popularity are more likely to react to corrective messages when they are written in an Alerting or Factual styles.
In summary, significant differences are evident when examining users' reactions to the bot. Specifically, users with high or medium levels of popularity, a substantial number of followed accounts, a significant number of posts, or users with a low to moderate account age on Twitter/X are more inclined to react to the bot compared to others. In the subsequent section, our focus shifts to users who have generated a reaction, aiming to comprehend the factors that may be influencing the polarity of their response, whether it is positive, negative, or mixed.
Positive, mixed, and negative reactions analysis
In the previous section, we focused on the binary reaction status of targeted users. In this second part of our analysis, we turn our attention to the subset of 443 users who triggered one of the reactions listed in Table 2. Each reaction falls into one of three categories:posi&temppound;ive, negative, or mixed (Figure 5).
We conduct an analysis similar to the one described in the previous section to gain a better understanding of the potential factors influencing the various levels of user reactions. In other words, we explore the potential impact of user popularity, followed accounts, post count, account age, on eliciting positive, negative, or mixed reactions. In the following section, we test the hypothesis that the polarity of user reaction is dependent on the factors in our analysis. We reuse the same low, medium, and high categorical buckets that were defined in "Reaction versus no reaction" section. We decide to not analyze the impact of the different templates on various levels of reactions due to insufficient data.
We consider reaction polarities as ordinal values since there is a natural order between the user reactions (negative < mixed < positive). Therefore, we use the Kruskal-Wallis H Nonparametric Statistical Test to determine if a particular user polarity level is more likely to generate a positive, mixed, or negative user reaction.
Replies and polar reaction stance
In general, we observe that the majority of users who reacted to the bot exhibited negative reactions, accounting for 78.3% (n = 347), followed by positive reactions, which constituted 12.2% (n = 54). Users reacting with a mix of positive, negative, and neutral reactions represented 9.5% (n = 42). In the following sections, we perform Kruskal-Wallis H Tests (Table 11) and where appropriate, Post hoc Bonferroni-adjusted Dunn Tests (Table 12) in order to better understand how polar reaction stances vary between different user groups.
User popularity and polar reaction stance:
When we analyzed the binary reactions, we observed that users with high or medium levels of popularity were significantly more likely to elicit a reaction ("Reaction versus no reaction" section). However, the Kruskal-Wallis H Tests in Table 11 show no significant correlation between their reactions and the polarity of those reactions. In other words, although popular users are more likely to react to the bot, we are unable to reliably anticipate whether their reactions are going to be positive, negative, or mixed.
Followed accounts and polar reaction stance:
The high-level overview in Figure 5 shows that more users who follow a high number of accounts react positively to bot messages. Specifically, they exhibit more positive reactions compared to users with a moderate number of followed accounts and users following a low number of accounts. This implies that individuals who follow a greater number of information sources are more receptive to the bot messages.
Test results in Table 11 show a significant difference for users that have different levels of followed accounts (j2(JV = 443) = 11.683, df = 2, p = 0.002904, p < 0.01). The Bonferroni-adjusted Post hoc Dunn Test results, as illustrated in Table 12 for the different levels of followed accounts, reveal significant differences. Specifically, the users who follow fewer than 266 accounts and those who follow a high number of accounts (> 991) exhibit significant differences, as do users who follow a moderate number of accounts (266,991) and a high number of accounts. These findings indicate that users with a high number of followed accounts are notably more likely to positively respond to the bot messages compared to the other groups.
Post counts and polar reaction stance:
The Kruskal-Wallis H Tests presented in Table 11 indicate a statistically significant difference among users with varying numbers of posts (J2(JV = 443) = 6.2595, df = 2, p = 0.04373, p < 0.05). This suggests that how people react to the bot's interventions is influenced by how many times they post on the platform.
Figure 5 shows that users with a high number of posts are comparatively more inclined to respond positively to the bot, showing a 16% increase compared to users with a moderate number of posts and an 8% increase compared to users with a low number of posts. Using the Bonferroni-adjusted Post hoc Dunn Tests (Table 12), we learn that the significant difference is only found when comparing low (<4007 posts) and high (> 16,512) content producers (p = 0.0371, p < 0.05). This result demonstrates that highly active users are more likely to be receptive to the bot messages in comparison to low content producers.
Account age and polar reaction stance: We learned from our binary reaction tests in "Reaction versus no reaction" section that users with low-to-moderate account age are more likely to react to our bot's corrective replies. When performing the Kruskal-Wallis H Tests (Table 11), no significant correlation was found between their reactions and the polarity of those reactions. To put it differently, while the age of a user's Twitter/X account can assist in predicting whether a user will react or not react, it does not provide a reliable indicator of whether those reactions will be positive, negative, or mixed.
DISCUSSION AND FUTURE WORK
In this paper, we introduced an experiment on using a Twitter/X bot to directly correct users sharing debunked information by posting them corrective information and analyzing their responses to the bot. Our experiment of using automated misinformation correction interventions outside lab environments demonstrated the complexity involved in real-world implementations. In this section, we explore constraints, insights from our analyses, and future directions.
Correction tone
Our experiment showed that the different templates we used to form the corrective posts did not significantly affect how users reacted overall. This observation supports previous research on the general effectiveness of message tones for correcting misinformation spreaders (Boukes and Hameleers 2023; Bode, Vraga, and Tully 2020). One reason is that, in hindsight, all templates shared several parts in common and the differences between the templates might not have been pronounced enough. They all (1) identified the correction to be issued by a bot, (2) gave a strong and explicit verdict of the claim (e.g., false, misleading), and (3) attributed the correction source to a fact-checker. Although the replies received by the bot from target users show that these factors were the basis for criticism from many users (fact, bot, check, checkers were the most mentioned words in the negative replies), further analysis is necessary for understanding if particular criticisms are associated with specific templates.
Common reaction patterns
As highlighted in Table 4, most users did not explicitly react to the bot (P01). We also observe that most popular patterns consist of only one reaction. The most popular positive reaction pattern was deletion (P17) followed by liking the bot message (P05) or liking and replying positively to the bot (P07+). Although deletion is a positive reaction, it may not be always a consequence of the bot message (see measuring user reaction subsection). However, previous research on rumor cascades has also shown that users who receive corrective messages are likely to delete their content as a result of the correction (Friggeri et al. 2014).
Negative reactions were almost equally split between blocking the bot (P33) and negatively replying to the bot (P03-). This shows that users preferred to either engage with the bot directly or avoid future interventions from the bot.
The mixed reactions were dominated by neutral replies (P03). This shows that most mixed reactions are currently biased towards neutral reactions and future work should consider subdividing the mixed reaction category to reflect it.
Personalization
Our analyses identified specific user characteristics that exhibit significant correlations with users' responses to automated misinformation corrections. We found a significant correlation between how users react (reaction/no-reaction) and the number of people who follow them when using alerting or factual language. Users with varying amounts of historical posts also highlighted significant correlations when looking at the alerting, factual, and friendly templates. Even though it appears that the choice of the most active users (i.e., users with a high post count or high popularity) to react or not react to the bot was affected by the message tones we provided, it is unknown how the templates affect their decision to respond positively or negatively to the corrections. Further research is required to enhance the appeal of messages to less active users and increase their potential to elicit positive reactions from responsive users.
The observation that alerting, factual, and friendly templates were more likely to engage users with the bot corrections correlates with the result in Piccolo et al. (2021) where such templates were preferred over the other templates. However, in our analysis, this association is only observed for the most active users in our data.
Users' motivations for sharing false claims vary (Buchanan 2020), and their affiliations with topics or sources can impact their willingness to accept corrections (Lewandowsky et al. 2012). Furthermore, different types of misinformation spread differently on Twitter/X, and this spread is also influenced by the emergence of fact-checks (Burel, Farrell, and Alani 2021). In future work, our goal is to consider the type of misinformation and users'historical sharing of misinformation in general, as well as from specific sources, to further refine our analysis. Other approaches include using visual corrective messages to attract more attention, and generative Large Languages Models (LLMs) to create messages that fit the personality of the targeted user; for example, tuning the message to their personality traits. Making the corrective messages more concise and graphical is of particular relevance for future research as it may make users more receptive to the bot corrections (Zhang et al. 2022). These approaches may help with dealing with less active users on the platform and increase the likelihood of positive reactions to the bot. Language is another personalization factor. The corrective replies posted by the bot used were always in English, regardless of the language used in the misinforming tweet and/or the corresponding fact-check. A small number of target users highlighted the language mismatch issue (see Table 3). This factor could have potentially influenced whether and how people responded to the bot's corrections. In future work, we will tune the language to the one used by the target user.
Restraining the bot
We imposed several restrictions on the bot to adhere to the principles of good bot behavior, following best practices on Twitter/X. This entails restricting the bot's search capacity to 60 misinforming URLs within a 15-min time frame, specifically targeting tweets posted no more than 7 days earlier, and refraining from responding to retweets. A drawback of these constraints is that they limit the number of misinformation corrections the bot can post, thereby reducing the amount of data we can gather.
Measuring user reactions
In Table 2, we listed the type of reactions we capture with the Twitter/X API, which forms the basis for our analysis. We followed a heuristics-based approach to determine the polarity of each reaction. However, although we believe our assumption is safe, we acknowledge that it is possible for other factors to influence that target user's reaction, besides or instead of our intervention. For example, it is theoretically possible that users deleted their misinforming posts without paying attention to our corrective replies, or deleted them after receiving warnings from their followers or even from the platform itself. Future experiments might explore the time delay between the corrective post and the deletion of the original post as an additional factor, even though it will not provide a foolproof solution. In future analysis, we hope to add additional features, such as the geographic location of target users (Berinsky et al. 2023), and their frequency of past interactions with reliable as well as unreliable information (Mensio et al. 2023; Gordon et al. 2023).
Misinformation sometimes has a continued influence effect (Chan et al. 2017), where people continue to be influenced by the false claim even after they receive and accept a correction. Therefore, one extension to our experiment would be to compare the target users' interaction with misinformation before and after receiving our corrective replies. This would help measure and understand the long-term impact of the automated corrective intervention and identify if the users who did not explicitly react to the bot (89% of the bot messages) were affected by the corrective messages.
We also encountered instances where the target user shared a misinforming post while simultaneously indicating the claim to be untrue. While sharing a false claim could potentially reinforce it, regardless of accompanying comments, these instances underscore the necessity for additional processing of misinforming posts. This would ensure that automatic corrective replies are more attuned to the intent or stance of the target user who shared them, which in turn is more likely to elicit a positive reaction.
Minding the audience
The bot's messages are not limited to the person it targets. The bot's corrective tweets are shared in public, as replies to a publicly shared misinforming tweet. In the experiment we described in this paper, the bot received 304 replies from the target users, and an additional 167 replies from the audience. The audience also contributed 11 retweets (vs. only seven by target users) and 128 likes (vs. 33 by targets). These numbers emphasize the need to study the reactions elicited by the users who were not directly targeted by the corrective tweets, to better understand the impact of indirect corrective interventions on the audience.
Ethical issues
Correcting users in public bears an ethical dilemma. On the one hand, people are likely to resent being publicly corrected and hence such correction could backfire by strengthening their initial belief (Nyhan and Reifler 2010). On the other hand, posts on Twitter/X are likely to be ready by a very large audience, and hence it is important that corrective information is placed where the false claim was placed. Furthermore, leaving potentially harmful misinformation uncorrected raises more profound ethical concerns. CONCLUSION In this paper, we present the findings from an innovative real-world experiment in which we employed a bot to correct misinformation posts that were publicly and freely shared in a natural environment on social media. Our study findings offer valuable insights into the design of social media bots for the correction of misinformation within real-world contexts by providing directions about the way corrective messages need to be shaped and personalized to be more impactful. The experiment ran for a 2-year period and consisted of sending corrections to 3898 unique users on Twitter/X. This is one of the first large experiments that studied misinformation corrections outside the lab environment. We identified the user categories that are more or less likely to react to such automated corrections. We found that users with high popularity, users who post a lot, and those who follow many other users are more likely to elicit a reaction to the bot than other user categories. Additionally, we observed that users who follow many accounts or engage in frequent posting are proportionally more likely to respond positively to these corrective measures. Another important finding was that some particular message tones could influence some user groups to react (e.g., users that post the most on Twitter/X reacted more to Alerting, Factual, and Friendly messages).
