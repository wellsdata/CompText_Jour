Introduction The dissemination of fake news currently poses a critical threat to public trust in democratic institutions and societal stability 1,2 . Misinformation campaigns manipulate public opinion, influencing election outcomes and even creating conditions for violent conflicts. Turkey faces unique challenges in this regard, given its wide social media use and deep political polarization that exacerbate the problem of combating fake news. Research reveals that Turkish-language misinformation proliferates through platforms like Twitter and Facebook, encompassing political, health-related, and national security topics 3,4 . However, most automatic detection systems rely on English-language training datasets to identify misinformation while neglecting non-English content - specifically Turkish, with its morphologically complex structure 5,6 .
Current fake news detection technologies range from traditional machine learning algorithms (SVM, Naïve Bayes) to advanced deep learning approaches (LSTMs, transformers) 7, 8–9 . BERT achieves state-of-the-art performance but requires substantial computational capabilities and underperforms under conditions of insufficient pre-training data for languages outside its main scope. Furthermore, existing models exhibit limited capacity for domain generalization (e.g., politics to health), which affects their real-world applicability 10,11 . The potential of attention-based mechanisms to assess word importance appears promising for Turkish fake news detection; however, their application in this area remains insufficiently investigated.
The integration of attention mechanisms could enhance Turkish fake news detection by identifying subtle cues, such as emotionally charged phrasing and campaign-driven narratives. The model emphasizes scalability, enabling potential integration with social media moderation tools and fact-checking platforms. By bridging global NLP advancements with local language requirements, this study lays the groundwork for countering misinformation in Turkish and numerous other underserved languages.
It is important to clarify the nature of our contribution. While the components of our architecture (FastText, LSTM, Attention) are individually well-established, their specific integration and optimization for the challenges of Turkish fake news detection represent the novel aspect of this work. Turkish is a low-resource, morphologically complex language, and the focus was not on inventing a new core architecture, but on designing a highly effective and efficient pipeline tailored to this specific problem. This pipeline achieves superior performance compared to both traditional models and more complex modern baselines, as detailed in the results.
Literature review The global proliferation of fake news has necessitated strong detection systems to address this process’s substantial societal concerns 12 . Early approaches to fake news detection primarily employed traditional machine learning (ML) techniques, particularly Support Vector Machines (SVM) and Naïve Bayes classifiers. These models relied on hand-crafted features such as word frequency data, n-gram sequences, syntactic patterns, and sentiment markers to distinguish false information from authentic content. The function of TF-IDF vectors allowed SVM to achieve moderate accuracy during English-language fake news classification. However, these methods fail to adequately capture contextual complexities and often require complicated feature engineering, which hinders their practical applicability across different languages or domains 13 .
The advent of deep learning has revolutionized text recognition technology through Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) and Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) networks. CNN filters effectively detect local textual patterns, while LSTMs excel in sequence analysis, making them particularly suitable for evaluating the temporal structure of news articles. LSTM-based text processing demonstrated superior accuracy compared to standard ML techniques by capturing sequences and identifying distant word connections. Nevertheless, deep learning models remain limited in their ability to understand contextual connections within texts - especially for morphologically complex languages like Turkish 14,15 .
Transformers such as BERT (Bidirectional Encoder Representations from Transformers) and RoBERTa advance natural language processing through pre-trained language models that process contextual information from both directions. The effectiveness of fake news detection has considerably improved due to the application of domain-specific dataset training to these models. However, their reliance on large-scale pre-training data and substantial computational demands render BERT and RoBERTa models impractical for Turkish and other low-resource languages. Additionally, their black-box characteristic nature reduces interpretability, hindering researchers’ ability to identify specific linguistic markers of misinformation 16,17 .
Self-attention has emerged as the fundamental technical building block among all transformer models. This mechanism provides a dynamic system for evaluating word significance, enabling the models to more effectively capture broader contextual relationships and long-range dependencies. In BERT models, individual attention heads specialize in detecting specific syntactic or semantic features, including subject-verb agreement and negation patterns. Transformers achieve excellence in understanding tasks that require sophisticated analysis because their self-attention abilities excel at fake news detection 18,19 . Transformers excel at complex understanding tasks, particularly fake news detection, due to these sophisticated self-attention capabilities.
In the context of fake news detection, the application of attention mechanisms has extended to Graph Neural Networks (GNNs) and Graph Attention Networks (GATs). In GATs, news articles are structured as graph nodes, with relationships between elements represented through edges. The model assigns attention weights to consolidate information between neighboring nodes, effectively tracing misinformation across connected networks. However, GAT implementations face challenges due to their requirement for structured data and complicated calculations, limiting their real-time deployment potential 20, 21–22 . Both traditional and deep learning models benefit from attention mechanisms through improved feature recognition and enhanced interpretability capabilities. Attention weights provide users with linguistic markers, for instance, phrases like “secretly planning” and “urgent action” that frequently appear in fake news content. This explainable model particularly benefits fact-checking processes by enabling fact-checkers and policymakers to understand the rationale behind misinformation classifications.
Turkish society is particularly vulnerable to disinformation due to its active social media presence and polarized media environment. Numerous studies confirm that fake news with political motivation proliferates during both election periods and crises 23,24 . Research by Teyit.org demonstrated how fabricated stories about refugee policies and health hazards spread rapidly through Turkish Twitter. The current situation in Turkey underscores the urgent need for automated, scalable detection solutions, as fact-checking and keyword detection remain the primary methods for identifying disinformation 25,26 .
The detection of false news presents greater complexity when analyzing non-English content due to unique linguistic and contextual factors. Natural language processing (NLP) models encounter particular processing challenges with Turkish, given the language’s agglutinative morphology and complex syntactic structure. Specialized language tools become essential for tasks like word stemming and stop-word removal, as general-purpose NLP pipelines demonstrate poor performance with Turkish texts. Turkish-specific detection models are necessary due to culturally-specific references and local phrases that lack direct equivalents in English datasets 27,28 . The scarcity of adequately labeled datasets creates barriers to the development of effective Turkish fake news detection systems. Researchers working on Turkish fake news detection typically rely on unstructured datasets since existing public English-based resources lack sufficient coverage and relevance 29, 30–31 .
The literature review identifies limitations in current approaches while highlighting how attention mechanisms can solve these problems. By integrating global and regional perspectives, the present study aimed to advance the development of improved fake news detection systems for Turkish and other lower-resource languages, offering both scalable and efficient solutions. The main objective of this research was to develop a resource-efficient and interpretable attention-based neural network specifically for Turkish fake news detection. This addresses the language’s morphological complexity and the resource constraints typical of non-English scenarios. The study pursued four specific goals: (1) to devise a novel architecture that integrates FastText embeddings with LSTM layers and attention mechanisms to learn Turkish-specific linguistic patterns in misinformation; (2) to compile and annotate a balanced and diverse set of 10,000 Turkish news articles with rigorous validation; (3) to evaluate the model’s cross-domain generalization following a one-stage adaptation to different topics without retraining, and (4) to optimize computational efficiency for practical deployment, ensuring the model outperforms both traditional and transformer-based models.
Methodology Dataset collection We compiled a representative dataset comprising 10,000 Turkish news articles (5,000 authentic and 5,000 fabricated) sourced from diverse channels. This diverse collection spans January 2020 through December 2023, capturing significant historical events, including elections, economic crises, and the COVID-19 pandemic. Data was acquired from three primary sources:
- Verified News Outlets . Articles were selected from Bianet and T24 due to their partnerships with fact-checking organizations and rigorous editorial policies.

- Social Media . The dataset incorporates 3,000 Twitter posts collected via API and 3,000 Facebook posts sourced from fact-checking communities.


A team of native speakers conducted manual annotation, with verification performed using automated tools (Google Fact Check Explorer and Hoaxy) to ensure labeling accuracy. Google Fact Check Explorer (web-based tool) and the Hoaxy misinformation tracking platform (Version 2.1) were used to support automated verification during dataset validation. All discrepancies, which accounted for less than 5% of the data, were resolved by consensus (Table 1).

All preprocessing operations were performed using the Zemberek NLP Toolkit (Version 0.17.1, https://github.com/ahmetaa/zemberek-nlp), which provides tokenization, stop-word removal, and lemmatization specifically optimized for the Turkish language.
Tokenization The raw text was segmented into discrete tokens (words or subwords) using Zemberek, a specialized NLP library for Turkish language processing. For example, the sentence “Bu haber doğru değil” (“This news is not true”) was decomposed into the tokens [Bu; haber; doğru; değil] . The text was divided into meaningful units for further processing.
Stop-word removal Frequently occurring Turkish stop-words (e.g., ve (and), ama (but), ise (if)) were filtered out to eliminate noise and reduce dimensionality. This filtration process concentrated the model’s attention on content-bearing words that were more likely to indicate fake or real news.
Stemming/lemmatization Words were reduced to their root forms using Zemberek’s lemmatizer. For instance, geliyorum (“I am coming”) was lemmatized to gel (“come”). This process normalized morphological variations, allowing them to be treated as a single feature and enhancing consistency.
Feature extraction The text was converted into numerical representations using two methods:

- TF-IDF (weighs words based on their importance within individual documents relative to the entire corpus);

- Word Embeddings (utilizes pre-trained FastText embeddings for Turkish to capture semantic relationships between words).



The embeddings were generated using the FastText Library (Version 0.9.2).

Baseline models for comparison To establish a performance benchmark, we evaluated our attention-based neural network against four distinct baseline models representing diverse approaches to fake news detection. The baseline implementation incorporated a linear Support Vector Machine (SVM) and TF-IDF features, as well as a Random Forest comprising 100 trees (max_depth = 10). For deep learning comparisons, we included a standard LSTM architecture with 64 units and the fine-tuned Turkish BERT model (dbmdz/bert-base-turkish), representing the latest Turkish NLP model. All models used matching training-test splits (80%−20%) and shared the same preprocessing pipeline and evaluation metrics to ensure objective/standardized comparison. The chosen baseline models enabled performance evaluation across various architectural levels, ranging from traditional feature-based foundations to advanced neural solutions. While SVM and Random Forest exemplify traditional machine learning operations using human-created features, LSTM and BERT represent modern deep learning standards. This comprehensive evaluation framework enabled the precise measurement of our attention mechanism’s effectiveness in Turkish-language fake news detection tasks. To further strengthen our evaluation and address potential concerns about architectural novelty, we implemented a Bidirectional LSTM with Attention (BiLSTM-Attention) as an additional strong baseline. This provided a more direct comparison to our unidirectional LSTM approach.

To ensure reliability and prevent overfitting, we employed 5-fold cross-validation. This technique randomly splits the dataset into five equal parts. The protocol included five iterations, with four folds allocated for training purposes and one for validation, rotating until each fold served as the validation set exactly once. Final assessment scores were computed as average performance metrics for each fold, with accuracy, precision, recall, and F1-score calculated for each partitioned subset. This approach validated the model’s consistency.
In addition to these core metrics, we report the Area Under the Receiver Operating Characteristic Curve (ROC-AUC) and the Area Under the Precision-Recall Curve (PR-AUC) to provide a more comprehensive evaluation of the model’s ranking and classification capabilities across different threshold settings. Given the balanced nature of our dataset, we focus on the macro-averaged versions of these metrics to ensure equal importance is given to both classes.
Performance metric confidence intervals were calculated to guarantee reliability in real-world environments. To statistically validate the performance differences between models, we employed McNemar’s test on the predictions of our proposed model and the Turkish BERT baseline. A p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Discussion The rapid proliferation of fake news, particularly in non-English contexts such as Turkish, necessitates robust and efficient detection mechanisms. This study presents an attention-based neural model for Turkish fake news detection that solves existing problems in computational speed, cross-domain generalization, and linguistic specificity. The model outperforms traditional machine learning algorithms (SVM and Random Forest) that rely on manually engineered TF-IDF and n-gram features. While these conventional methods maintain low memory requirements, they prove inadequate for capturing Turkish semantic and morphological complexities. SVM only reached 82% accuracy and failed to analyze sequence connections and semantic patterns in texts. In contrast, our model combines FastText embeddings with attention mechanisms to dynamically evaluate linguistic feature importance, achieving 92% accuracy. Alghamdi et al. 7 reported similar observations regarding deep learning models’ capability to detect elusive patterns within misinformation.
Our model not only matches Turkish BERT in terms of accuracy but also demonstrates 40% faster training and substantially reduced computational demands. Intensive preprocessing requirements and 4.2 GB GPU memory consumption make BERT impractical for time-sensitive and resource-constrained applications. Our architecture features a single-layer LSTM structure integrated with selected attention mechanisms, achieving outstanding efficiency and optimal results. The proposed approach meets a critical requirement pointed out in Hoy and Koulouri 10 , who emphasized the balance between system complexity and operational usability.
The interpretability of our attention mechanism represents a key advancement over BERT’s “black-box” nature. By explicitly highlighting discriminative features (e.g., kesinlikle [absolutely], acil [urgent]), the model provides fact-checkers with actionable insights. This aligns with the arguments of Kukkar and Kaur 8 , who supported interpretable models as a means to foster trust in automated detection systems. Additionally, as quantified in the Results section (Cross-Domain Generalization Analysis), our model demonstrates robust cross-domain performance. It maintains high accuracy (often above 0.85) when trained and tested on different topics. This represents a considerable improvement over domain-specific approaches and is a direct response to the research gap identified by Yu et al. 11
The effectiveness of our model in processing Turkish texts suggests promising applications for other morphologically rich, low-resource languages. The present study complements and extends recent advancements in languages such as Arabic 15 , Hindi, and Bangla 5 . For instance, the approach in research 5 combines multiple deep learning models for Bangla tasks, achieving high accuracy but potentially at a greater computational cost. Similarly, research on Arabic 15 highlights the critical need for tailored solutions in low-resource contexts. Our model achieves a comparable performance level (92% accuracy) while emphasizing the crucial balance between performance, interpretability, and computational efficiency. This balance is highly significant for practical deployment in resource-constrained settings. Three key implications emerge from our findings:
- Linguistic Adaptability . The combined FastText and attention architecture proves particularly suitable for morphologically rich languages like Arabic and Finnish, supporting the findings of Kazi et al. 15 on addressing NLP gaps for low-resource languages.

- Policy and Deployment . The model’s speed (40% faster than BERT) enables real-time content moderation, overcoming a challenge noted by Malik et al. 21 in deploying GNNs for fake news detection.

- Cross-Cultural Patterns . While Turkish misinformation tactics (e.g., urgency markers like acil ) mirror global trends, they require localized analysis, as emphasized by Alghamdi et al. 7 through their semantic deep learning approach.


Although our model exhibits efficiency and interpretability, Hoy and Koulouri 10 caution that generalizability depends on continuous dataset updates. We plan to address this limitation in future developments (e.g., by incorporating regional dialects). Collectively, these studies underscore the need for scalable, language-specific solutions to combat misinformation globally. Despite these advances, further research is needed to address the model’s operational challenges:
- Text-Only Limitation. The current model analyzes only text and is therefore unable to detect fake news that uses images or videos. Future models should integrate textual and visual analysis to address this limitation.

- Multimodal Misinformation. The model failed to detect approximately 13% of false news stories containing multimedia content. Subsequent studies should incorporate vision-language models, including CLIP, to evaluate text-visual consistency, building upon multi-domain and multi-modal fusion approaches such as those in research 11 .

- Satire and Opinion Detection. The model frequently misclassified satirical (62%) and opinion-based pieces (23%) as false news due to their overlapping linguistic patterns. Implementing dedicated satire detection systems or sentiment analysis layers, as explored in interpretable ensemble models 8 , could mitigate this by recognizing exaggerated absurdities and personal pronoun usage.

- Dynamic Misinformation Trends . The model requires further testing to examine its ability to detect modern threats such as AI-generated texts. Future research should assess its resilience against artificial media using methods that include GPT-4 detection approaches.

- Dataset Expansion . Our dataset, which includes 10,000 articles, provides a robust foundation. However, the inclusion of regional dialects and underrepresented topics, specifically environmental misinformation, would improve generalizability. Collaboration with fact-checking organizations could facilitate labeled data collection through crowdsourcing.

- Real-World Testing . Deploying the model on active social media platforms would validate its real-world effectiveness. The system can use an A/B testing methodology to demonstrate performance comparable to that of human fact-checkers in terms of accuracy and processing speed.


Furthermore, while our evaluation was comprehensive on benchmark datasets, it did not explicitly include adversarial examples or highly nuanced disinformation campaigns engineered to evade detection. In future research, the model should be stress-tested against such real-world, adversarial threats to improve its practical resilience.
The study underscores the critical importance of combating misinformation in modern society. This need is particularly acute in Turkey, where high political polarization and low public trust in the media prevail. Any deployed system must be designed with a focus on transparency to avoid exacerbating public skepticism. The interpretability features integrated into our attention mechanism help users comprehend the rationale behind the detection strategies used in the process of content classification.
The model provides a globally applicable framework for democratic misinformation detection. It enables resource-constrained regions to develop localized solutions, reducing their reliance on systems dominated by English-language technologies. Moreover, the model aligns with United Nations Sustainable Development Goal 16 (Peace, Justice, and Strong Institutions), which identifies misinformation as a growing threat to social cohesion.
Conclusion This study successfully developed an effective and interpretable attention-based neural network for fake news detection in Turkish, addressing an important gap in non-English misinformation research. The proposed model integrates FastText embeddings, LSTM layers, and attention mechanisms to capture nuanced Turkish linguistic patterns while achieving computational efficiency and cross-domain portability. A rigorously curated dataset of 10,000 balanced Turkish news articles was used, verified through manual annotation and fact-checking software.
A preprocessing pipeline utilizing Zemberek (for tokenization, stop-word removal, and stemming) was employed, followed by feature extraction using TF-IDF and FastText embeddings. The model incorporates an attention layer to dynamically weight salient features, achieving 92% accuracy with a precision of 0.91, and a recall of 0.93. Comparative analysis showed that the proposed architecture outperformed baseline models, including SVM (82% accuracy), Random Forest (85%), LSTM (88%), and Turkish BERT (90%). Notably, the model trained 40 times faster than BERT, underscoring its feasibility for real world implementation.
Despite the positive results, this study has several limitations. The model was less effective at processing satirical material (62% of false positives) and advanced fake news that imitated legitimate sources (68% of false negatives). Furthermore, the method was inefficient for multimedia-based misinformation, highlighting the necessity of multimodal extensions. Although the dataset provides a sound foundation, it could be improved by incorporating regional dialects and emerging misinformation patterns.
Future research should prioritize multimodal integration and the development of advanced satire detection systems. Expanding the dataset to include underrepresented topics and evaluating the model in real-life social media environments would further enhance its practical applicability. If these challenges are addressed, the proposed model can serve as a scalable solution to fake news detection in Turkish and other low-resource languages, thereby contributing to the global effort to combat disinformation.
