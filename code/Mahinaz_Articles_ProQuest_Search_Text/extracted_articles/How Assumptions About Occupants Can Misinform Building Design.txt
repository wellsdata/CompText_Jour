This article demonstrates how different assumptions about occupants during building performance simulation (BPS)-aided design can lead to different design decisions. This is accomplished by evaluating the sensitivity of several building design parameters against the occupant-related assumptions made by the energy modeler, architect, and mechanical engineer. The model described in this article is that of a real office building in Toronto, Canada. By altering occupant-related assumptions (such as the number of people in the building, the plug-in equipment power density [EPD], and the lighting power density [LPD]), this study shows the sensitivity of the ranking of design alternatives as well as energy-savings potential fluctuations when different assumptions are applied.
This article is part of a larger study, whereby the project design practitioners were interviewed on occupantrelated assumptions made during the design process of the case study building. The original model developed by the energy modeler was obtained and recreated using EnergyPlus.
The building design process is a complex, multidisciplinary, and multi-objective process that involves several design disciplines such as architects, energy modelers, and engineers.1 These practitioners have different views, approaches, motivations and liabilities, and tools to achieve their design objectives. BPS has become an important tool for designers to support decisions.2 During BPS-aided design, each team member uses particular assumptions, typically sourced from building codes and standards or their past experience, to support decisions regarding the building size (floor area), envelope performance, heating, ventilation and air conditioning (HVAC) equipment sizing, energy performance evaluation, and to plan for safety and egress.3 Mechanical engineers typically use modeling to perform heating and cooling load calculation for the purpose of sizing HVAC equipment, while energy modelers conduct modeling to demonstrate that the proposed design meets the code specified performance on an annual basis. Architects may use energy modeling to guide selection of appropriate early design strategies in relation to geometry and materials. Given the various modeling objectives, associated risks and liabilities, the assumptions made by each design discipline are typically not the same. This discrepancy in assumptions can lead to unsubstantiated and oftentimes conflicting design decisions. This discrepancy can be due several factors such as the lack of effective communication amongst the design disciplines, the difference of tasks and duties among stakeholders, and the degree of BPS literacy of the design stakeholders. In other words, it is not expected that all design stakeholders will perform modeling at same level of detail. However, they should have similar assumptions to start with. This article discusses a simulation-based study that was conducted to demonstrate the impact that different occupant-related assumptions have regarding the energy-savings potential of key design decisions.
Methods
Using EnergyPlus v 9.0.1, a real office building located in Toronto, Canada (ASHRAE Climate Zone 6A) was modeled. A typical floor of the building was selected to represent the building, as shown in Figure 1. The building has a floor area of 1,728 m2 (18,600 ft2) per story and has been modeled with five thermal zones: four perimeter zones and one core zone, all representative of an open-plan offices. The south façade has a gross windowto-wall ratio of 85%, while the east, west, and north façades have a WWR of 41%, 41% and 12%, respectively. The building is ventilated, cooled, and heated using a rooftop package unit (RTU) with variable air volume (VAV) terminal devices and hydronic radiant baseboard heaters. Hot water for space heating is supplied by condensing boilers with a thermal seasonal efficiency of 90%. The building also has an energy recovery ventilator (ERV) with 85% sensible recovery serving the RTU.
Table 1 and Figure 2 show LPD, EPD, occupant density, and equivalent internal gains assumed by the architect, the energy modeler, and the mechanical engineer. These values were obtained during interviews with the project stakeholders. These values shown for each discipline have different sources: designer experience and code of practice (e.g., mechanical engineer assumptions), the project selected fixtures and equipment (e.g., LPD by modeler), or the BPS software defaults (e.g., the architect assumptions). Codes or standards used to arrive at these assumptions were not provided at the time of the interviews. ANSI/ASHRAE/IES Standard 90.1-2019 office building schedules of occupancy and operation were used.
Several design parameters were explored in the investigation. The parameters included increasing the R-value of walls and roof, upgrading windows' thermal properties (e.g., SHGC, U-factor), decreasing window to wall ratio (WWR) on the south façade, increasing the efficiency of cooling, heating, and energy recovery systems, improving the variable speed pump efficiency and deploying demand-controlled ventilation (DCV). The design parameters were used to generate design alternatives for each assumption scenario made by a design discipline (i.e., architect, modeler).
Additionally, the mechanical engineer assumptions were used to obtain the maximum heating and cooling loads and compare them to the corresponding values obtained from the results based on the architect and the modeler assumptions (see Table 2). The mechanical engineer typically uses the assumption for sizing the HVAC equipment and not to optimize energy use of the design.
Findings
Overall, the results of the simulations indicate the significance of the impact of using different occupantrelated assumptions. Figure 3 and Figure 4 reveal numerous changes to the design alternatives' ranking and differences in savings potential that occurred from different occupant-related assumptions. For example, the results show that DCV becomes more influential in terms of energy savings and ranked higher as an important design parameter when a higher occupant density (more people) is modeled (the architect assumptions). In contrast, increasing the R-value of the building roof is much more effective in saving energy and becomes more influential as a design parameter (moved up in ranking) when lower occupant density, LPD and EPD were used (modeler assumptions). Some design parameters demonstrated robustness to changes in occupantrelated assumptions in terms of energy savings (e.g., cooling systems COP). That is, they are similarly effective or ineffective at saving energy, regardless of occupantrelated assumptions. Other parameters show robustness to occupant-related assumptions in terms of ranking (e.g., window with U = 1.467 W/m2-K (0.26 Btu/h-ft2-°F) & SHGC = 0.245).
Additionally, Table 2 shows that disagreement in occupant-related assumptions leads to different maximum heating and cooling loads. Peak loads are typically used to size equipment (e.g., HVAC), so over/underestimation of occupant-driven thermal loads can lead to over/ undersized equipment, which affects the initial and operational costs of the building.
Concluding Remarks
The status quo of modeling occupants during building design does not fully recognize the actual implications of occupant-related assumptions, which have proven to be critical.5 Through analysis of the results, one can see that using different values for common occupant-related assumptions may affect the selection of design parameters and increases or decreases the energy savings from design parameters by a factor of two or more.
The difference in occupant-related assumptions used by design disciplines can be considered as a major shortcoming of current design practices. The simulation results demonstrate the impact of these differences on the outcomes of the design process. While mechanical engineers and energy modelers are cognizant of liabilities associated with their assumptions (which leads them to be conservative with the assumed values), a lack of formal and efficient information exchange mechanism amongst the design disciplines could be contributing to the discrepancies in assumed values. Furthermore, the designer comprehension of the implications of occupant-related model inputs on the outcomes of the design process can also lead to such disagreement in assumptions as well.
Addressing some of these sources of discrepancy might be out of the hand of design team members. For instance, timing of different disciplines' involvement in the design process is largely determined by the owner and based on the requirements and regulations of local and municipal authorities. However, adopting an efficient information exchange mechanism among different design disciplines could assist in standardizing the assumptions used to represent occupants during different design stages. The authors recommend a formal reporting method to ensure agreement about occupant-related assumptions among practitioners. Finally, enhancing the awareness of the implications of occupant-related assumptions and inputs on the outcomes of the BPS-aided design process is critical. Starting at the early design phase, organizing design meetings routinely can promote information and knowledge exchange among the different design disciplines. Future research should consider examining commonly used design parameters in terms of their robustness to changes in occupant-related assumptions to introduce a set of occupant-proof design parameters.
