ABSTRACT
Given a rapidly evolving disinformation environment, many initiatives are currently being developed to counter the harmful impacts of disinformation. The aim of this study is to explore how an SMS intervention, as a hitherto untested modality for raising awareness of disinformation with promising potential and wide reach, could boost media literacy among adults. We probe the effects on a national sample of adult Swedish respondents (N = 2536). An initial survey is followed by a five-week text messaging intervention and concluded with a second survey. Respondents' objective ability to identify false information, and their subjective perceptions of attitude and behavioral change are analyzed. Our findings suggest that knowledge on disinformation can be enhanced, and that self-reported verification of news can be increased. Participants' test scores on discerning true and false news headlines could also improve. Our results lay the groundwork for future research aimed at determining causal effects.
Keywords: disinformation, media literacy, intervention, SMS, Sweden.
INTRODUCTION
Democracy needs a well-informed electorate to function. However, today, intentionally false and misleading information, including news information, is propagating lies as truths while discrediting authentic information and its sources. The impact of the current rapidly evolving disinformation landscape on citizens can be overwhelming (Watts et al., 2021). The negative consequences for democratic discourse and for citizens' relationship with elected leaders may ultimately put the democratic system at risk. Yet, as concerns about the harmful and disruptive effects of disinformation mount, the question is what could be done to counter it.
There is an emerging realization that citizens need to be well equipped to handle the flows of both true and false information. Citizens" ability to discriminate between real and false news, here referred to as media literacy, forms part of civic literacy (Livingstone, 2004). Key elements of media literacy are the ability to analyze and evaluate messages in a variety of forms (Christ & Potter, 1998; Potter, 2020). Media literacy is also about understanding public communication and information, and how social media increasingly plays into that messaging (Chambers, 2021). In democratic societies, the education system is often seen as the main provider of media literacy. Proactive media literacy education to counter disinformation has, for example, been undertaken in Finland where children are taught from an early age (Bjola & Papadakis, 2021).
However, for the adult population above age 18 - including those who have left the schooling system - how could media literacy be boosted?
The Swedish population has been subject to mounting disinformation pressures in recent years with changes in the Swedish security context after the Russian war against Ukraine, culminating in Sweden's NATO accession in 2024. Topics subject to disinformation have been marked by continuity, such as migration (Giandomenico & Linderstal, 2023) and climate change (Oscarsson et al., 2021). Other topics have emerged in connection with real-world events, such as the Covid-19 pandemic (Colde et al., 2020), or elections (LaForge, 2020).
While interventions to boost awareness of disinformation is a rapidly growing field of study, most focus on children and youth in education (Dumitru et al., 2022). Few interventions target adults out of formal education (Moore & Hancock, 2022) or adult populations at larger scale (Nekmat, 2020).
We explore the use of SMS as a new potential modality for media literacy interventions, promising for its wide reach, scalability, low cost, and positive track record in yielding awareness and behavioral change in other domains (Fjeldsoe et al, 2010; HernándezAgramonte et al., 2024). The objective of our paper is thus to take a first step to empirically explore if media literacy among adults could be boosted through an SMS intervention. We pose the research question: How can knowledge and ability to assess disinformation be increased through an awareness intervention via SMS?
We explore the effects of an SMS intervention on a national sample of Swedish respondents aged 18 and above (N = 2536). A survey is followed by a mobile text messaging intervention over a five-week period and ended with a second survey for the same respondents. The messaging content is based on an existing campaign "Stop. Think. Check" by the Norwegian Media Council (2022). Effects of the intervention are examined in terms of respondents" post survey (objective) knowledge and capability to identify false information and their (subjective) perceptions of attitude and behavioral change.
Our study fills two main gaps. First, we explore mobile text messaging as a promising new modality that, to our knowledge, has not been applied before in countering disinformation efforts. This study thus sets the foundation for future research aimed at establishing causal effects. Second, we focus on the broader adult population, including adults out of formal education as a largely overlooked constituency, thus offering empirical findings from a larger and more diverse sample than most other disinformation interventions.
Interventions to counter disinformation
Disinformation is here defined as false information with the intent to harm (Tandoc et al., 2020). Some characteristics of disinformation is that it is false but may still fall below the threshold of blatant lies (Allcott & Gentzkow, 2017; Lazer et al., 2018). Disinformation can be a mix of authentic and slightly distorted information. Disinformation is often charged with negative emotions, to make ordinary people unknowingly diffuse it on social media (Chuai & Zhao, 2022; Horner et al., 2021).
People need knowledge and skills to navigate the overwhelming flows of true and false information for the democratic system to work as intended. Effects of the current information and disinformation flows may be overwhelming on individuals, especially those who are less resilient to disinformation. Previous studies associate certain sociodemographic features with greater receptivity to disinformation, such as low education (Humprecht et al., 2021), higher age (Brashier & Schacter, 2020), and extremist ideology (RodriguezPérez & Canel, 2023). Disinformation threatens the idea of a well-informed electorate that can hold leaders to account. What do we know about different types of countering disinformation interventions and their effectiveness?
Interventions to counter disinformation can be placed into two broader categories: Nudging or technocognition - and boosting interventions. Gwiazdzinski et al. (2023) describe nudging in terms of, for example, rigging social media platform architectures to alter people's behavior through warnings, corrections, or empathy nudges. Technocognition is similarly centered on the ability of the information architecture to introduce friction in the sharing of offensive material to incentivize individuals to deliberate on content before sharing. Boosting 15 about providing people with a latent ability to identity disinformation through interventions that foster cognitive and motivational competencies (Gwiazdzinski et al., 2023).
Boosting interventions are often framed as 'literacy enhancing," and among these - media literacy interventions are gaining traction. Media literacy enhancing interventions are aimed at strengthening disinformation awareness and knowledge. Some are framed as increasing general awareness to false news (Apuke et al., 2023). Others focus on a combination of awareness, knowledge and skills (Tamboer et al., 2024; Moore & Hancock, 2022), or provide more elaborate tools for determining the authenticity of website content (Dame Adjin-Tettey, 2022).
The most common targets for media literacy interventions on disinformation are students and educators in educational settings (Dumitru et al. 2022). Many studies focus on youth and children (Geers et al., 2020; Kudrnäè, 2020; Tamboer, et al. 2024). Others address teachers as target group (Phippen et al., 2021). However, that students and educators are overrepresented, suggests poor reliability of prevailing interventions according to Dumitru et al. (2022). Few interventions are aimed at the broader adult population.
The modalities of media literacy interventions range from online modules to offline teaching, lectures, and workshops. Offline trainings span from hour-long presentations to semester long programs with take home assignments (Apuke et al., 2023; Dame Adjin-Tettey, 2022). Online interventions may encompass selfdirected interactive modules (Moore & Hancock, 2022; Tamboer et al., 2024).
While the field is under intense development, there are no conclusive results on what types or modalities of interventions are the most effective (Eisemann & Pimmer, 2020). Some studies show positive impact in terms of improved accuracy in discerning fake news (Moore & Hancock, 2022; Soetekouw, 2024). Other studies find no significant results (Badrinathan, 2021; Eisemann & Pimmer, 2020; Tamboer et al., 2024). Negative unintended effects are also observed, through a reduction in the perceived accuracy of both true and false news (Guess et al., 2020; Modirrousta-Galian & Higham, 2023). Some studies focus on subjective effects such as attitudes to news verification or reputational concerns (Apuke et al, 2023; Hameleers, 2022; Kudrnác, 2020). Other studies favor objective results in terms of a demonstrated ability to separate between true and false information (Moore & Hancock, 2022).
Despite its positive impact in other fields, SMS interventions have so far not been used as a modality to boost disinformation awareness. Its potential lies in its wide reach, scalability, and relatively low cost. Studies have found considerable effects of SMS interventions in the areas of health (Evans et al., 2022; Fjeldsoe et al., 2010; Suffoletto, 2016; Patrick et al., 2009), early childhood development (Hernández-A gramonte et al., 2024), education (Castleman & Page, 2015), and recycling (Buil-Cosiales et al., 2014). SMS have also been usefully applied to enhance financial literacy (Abebe et al., 2018), saving behavior (Rodríguez and Saavedra 2019), and political participation (ShaulCohen & Lev-On 2020), including voting (Dale & Strauss 2009). Evidence suggests that people are more affected by concrete and specific SMS instructions of "how and what" rather than by abstract messages around "why" (Kim et al., 2024).
This study addresses two knowledge gaps. First, we pioneer SMS as a modality to counter disinformation by launching a media literacy intervention using text messaging. Although its positive effects have been shown in other fields of study, SMS have not previously been applied in the disinformation realm. Second, while most media literacy interventions target youth and students, we focus on the broader adult population, including those outside formal education, thus offering a larger and more diverse sample (N = 2536).
Hypothesis formulation
We want to explore possible objective and subjective effects of an SMS intervention. To investigate improvements in the objective capability to identify disinformation, we pose two 'objective outcomes' hypotheses, the first about conceptual knowledge and the second about applying that knowledge:
Hypothesis 1: A person who has received the SMS intervention will state more correct factual knowledge answers about factors used to identify disinformation.
Hypothesis 2: A person who has received the SMS intervention will improve their results on news test questions.
Our two 'subjective outcomes' hypotheses examine if a person who has received the SMS intervention reports a more critical and self-reflective attitude and behavioral change:
Hypothesis 3: A person who has received the SMS intervention will to a larger extent double check news sources deemed to be upsetting'.
Hypothesis 4: A person who has received the SMS intervention will demonstrate stronger belief in their own ability to identify false news.
RESEARCH DESIGN
Data collection procedure
We explore how an SMS intervention can affect participant = objective capability to identify disinformation, and subjective attitudes and selfreported behavior. In this three-step repeated measures study, participant awareness and abilities are measured before and after the intervention. Respondents are asked to answer an initial survey to create baseline data, including their ability to assess true and false news headlines. In a second step, respondents are invited to receive an SMS intervention based on established messaging content from a previous communication campaign on disinformation. If respondents consent to receive the SMS, they proceed to the third final step where they are asked to fill out a follow-up survey also including a news headline-test.
Surveys and SMS intervention
Survey 1. The first survey contains questions about news and trust in information sources. Questions cover for example preferred news sources, trust in specific news media, online sharing behavior, and emotional reactions to news. Survey 1 does not mention the term disinformation. The final part consists of ten news headlines, five true and five false. The headlines cover current news topics, such as the Gaza war, climate change, the Covid-19 pandemic, and immigration. The false headlines are retrieved from the fact-checking websites Snopes.com and Faktisk.no. The real headlines are retrieved from international and Swedish news media. (See Appendix, table Al).
SMS-intervention. The SMS -intervention is based on an existing campaign 'Stop. Think. Check.' (originally "Stopp. Tenk. Sjekk.") developed by the Norwegian Media Authority (2022). Ten text messages, containing basic information on factors that characterize disinformation and some guidance for how to identify disinformation, are sent out. The text messages are dispatched twice a week in a five-week period. (See Appendix, table A2).
Survey 2. A second survey follows the SMS intervention. The difference from survey 1 is that questions about trust in news agencies and authorities are not asked, and that questions explicitly addressing disinformation are added. Respondents are asked if they consider themselves able to identify disinformation and how often they encounter disinformation, to capture possible subjective effects. To detect possible objective effects, respondents are asked about what factors they look for to identify disinformation, and they are, again, called to assess the authenticity of ten news headlines.
Participants
The respondents consent to participate in the surveys and to receive the SMS intervention. They can withdraw at any moment during the process. The study is approved by the Swedish Ethical Review Authority.
Since a three-step process is used to collect the data, a large initial sample size is needed (Guo et al., 2013). The data collection 1s administered by Norstat with a pre-existing survey panel with over 115,000 members in Sweden (Norstat, n.d.). The Swedish panel comes as close as possible to a gross national representation of gender, age and place of residence. The majority of the panel members are recruited by telephone and others from a variety of online and offline sources. For this study, the sampling is made on all Swedish panel members of 18 years and above. An algorithm is used to automatically perform the gross selection. The net sample is so precise 1n relation to the true population distribution that the weighting factors deviate to a modest extent from 1. The groups are set up according to the size of the total sample - the more respondents, the smaller the age range or geography.
Our study has a national sample of 2536 Swedish respondents, 18 to 91 years of age, who answer both surveys and receive the SMS. (See Appendix, table A3). Our 2536-person sample reflects the following demographic features: 50% women (national level 50%)!, 30% youth of 18-35 years (national level 29%)? 54% middle aged of 36-69 years (national level 52%), and 16% elderly of 70+ years (national level 19%), 46% had more than 1 year of tertiary education (i.e. university or equivalent; national level 33%)3, 21% lived in rural areas (national level 16%)". In terms of ideology, 17% report definitely left leaning and 20% definite right leaning political orientation . The mean age of the participants is 48 years.
Given the three-step process involved, SMS as an untested modality in countering disinformation efforts, and considerable uncertainty regarding both the recruitment- and dropout rate, participants are not formally divided into a control and treatment group for concerns of getting statistically insignificant results. In a multistep study like this one, participants are expected to drop out, and the potential impacts thereof are discussed below. However, of the 2536 participants that respond to both surveys and consent to receive the SMS intervention, 236 report that they do not receive any messages, and 158 say that they either do not read all or some of the SMS, do not know, or prefer not to answer. The 236 respondents who report that they do not receive any text messages thus form a "no SMS" natural control group.
The natural control group is not necessarily random, but expectedly more random than those who receive but choose not to read the SMS. Comparing those who receive the text messages (2300) with those who do not receive the messages (236), allows us to isolate the intervention effect. We control for demographics and other relevant characteristics between the groups. Furthermore, we compare the initial test scores and subjective ability for the two groups.
Variables and descriptive statistics
The variables used in our analysis can be grouped into three categories: Demographic- and nondemographic independent variables, and dependent variables. For full technical explanations, see Appendix table A4.
Demographic independent variables. The demographic independent variables are gender, age group, education, place of residence, and political ideology.
Non-demographic independent variables. The nondemographic independent variables consist of participants stating that they did not receive any SMS (9%), high trust (23%), low trust (11%), no time spent on social media (7%), and 2 hours or more daily spent (26%), never shared social media content (52%), daily sharing (2%), and the feeling 'upset' by recent news in both surveys (42%).
Dependent variables. The four dependent variables correspond to the four hypotheses:
Factual knowledge on disinformation. This dependent variable is the number of correct knowledge answers about the factors used to identify information, reflecting the content of the SMS intervention. The maximum score is 4.
News test results. To measure the ability to successfully discriminate between false and true news headlines, we record the "distance from correct answer". For a false news heading, responding 1 - ie. "very unreliable" - yields a score of 100, while responding 7 - Le. "very credible" - yields a score of 0, and vice versa for true statements. The aggregate score is calculated as the mean of the individual scores, meaning that the maximum score for all questions is 100.5 An aggregate score of 0 represents maximum deviation from correct response for all questions." An aggregate score is calculated for the test in survey 1 and 2 respectively, which allows for comparison. (The headlines are shown in Appendix A, table A2). In the regressions we exclude respondents who only answer 5 or less test questions. The overall mean response in survey 1 is lower for false news headings (2.93) than for true ones (4.62). This means that respondents overall consider false statements to be more unreliable than the true ones. The false statements are considered even more unreliable in survey 2 (mean of 2.38) than in survey 1. The means for the true statements are almost identical (4.62 vs 4.61).
News source verification. This third explanatory variable reflects the reported propensity to double check the authenticity of news deemed to be emotionally "upsetting".
Belief in own ability to identify false news. The fourth dependent variable is reported belief in one's own subjective ability to identify false news, going from 1 to 7.
RESULTS
To test the hypotheses, we perform Ordinary Least Squares (OLS) regressions. We initially compare participants who receive the SMS intervention with those who did not. Thereafter we do a similar comparison while controlling for structural and individual characteristics to account for possible differences. Where possible, we compare the two groups before and after the intervention, to account for other potential changes external to the study. To account for heteroskedasticity we use robust standard errors in all models.
Exploring objective effects
Hypothesis 1: A person who has received the SMS intervention will state more correct factual knowledge answers about factors used to identify disinformation. For hypothesis 1 we use the dependent variable, Factual knowledge on disinformation. The results show that respondents who receive the SMS correctly identity, on average, 2.64 of the factors used to discern information (Table 1, model A). Those who do not receive the SMS identify on average 0.48 factors less, a statistically significant difference. The results hold and the magnitudes are similar when controlling for demographic and non-demographic factors (Table 1, model B). Our results thus support hypothesis 1.
Hypothesis 2: A person who has received the SMS intervention will improve their results on news test questions. To test hypothesis 2, we use the aggregate test scores from test 1 and 2 and the difference in test scores (test score 2 - test score 1) as dependent variables. There is no statistically significant difference in initial test scores and subjective ability between the groups (Table 1, models C and D). Respondents who receive the SMS score higher in test 2 than test 1, with a significant difference of 4.43 (Table 1, model G). This difference approximately represents being one step closer to correct in 3 questions, i.e. responding 7 instead of 6 in true statements, for a respondent that answered all questions. However, the test score difference is not significantly different from respondents who do not receive the SMS. Also, when controlling for structural and individual characteristics (Table 1 Model H) there is no statistically significant difference between those who receive the SMS and those who do not. Thus, while the test scores are higher in test 2 than test 1, the difference is not higher for those that received the SMS and there is therefore no support for hypothesis 2.
Exploring subjective effects
Hypothesis 3: A person who has received the SMS intervention will to a larger extent double check news sources deemed to be 'upsetting' before sharing on social media. To test hypothesis 3, we focus on the survey 2 respondents who have recently read news that make them upset. Of these 1572 respondents, 61% state that they try to find out whether the news is true or false. In Table 2, model A and B, the dependent variable is binary and equal to 1 if the respondent tries to find out whether the news is true or false. The negative and significant coefficient for those who do not receive the SMS suggests that those respondents are less likely than those who receive the SMS to verify whether the upsetting news is true or false, supporting hypothesis 3.
Hypothesis 4: A person who has taken part in the SMS intervention will demonstrate stronger belief in their own ability to identify false news. To test hypothesis 4, we use the reported subjective ability to identify false news (ranging from 1 to 7) in survey 1 and 2 and the difference (survey 2 - survey 1) as dependent variables. Table 2 model G shows that for respondents that receive SMS the subjective ability is higher in test 2 than test 1, with a significant difference of 0.3. However, the difference in subjective ability is not significantly different for respondents who do not receive the SMS. Nor when controlling for demographic and non-demographic characteristics is there any statistically significant difference between those who receive the SMS and those who do not (Table 2 model H). Thus, while the subjective ability is higher in test 2 than test 1, the difference is not higher for those that receive the SMS, resulting in no support for hypothesis 4. This instead suggests a general effect for all, both those who receive the SMS and those who do not.
Subjective and objective effects by category of respondent
When analyzing objective and subjective outcomes for different categories of respondents, interesting insights emerge (see Tables 1 and 2).
Among those is, first, that being young, i.e. between 18-35 years, predicts a lower test score but higher subjective ability. In other words, for youth there seems to be a mismatch between the objective capability and self-perceived ability to discriminate between false and real news. In contrast, being elderly, i.e. 70 years or older, 15 associated with higher test scores in surveys 1 and 2 but also a lower subjective ability to identify false news. While the elderly have lower confidence in their own abilities, they demonstrate higher objective skills.
Second, people reporting daily sharing on social media have lower test scores. Never sharing posts predicts higher test scores. Sharing behavior is also expectedly linked to the time spent on social media. Our results confirm that participants spending two or more hours per day have lower predicted scores in the second test. Conversely, spending no time on social media predicts higher score in the first test and lower subjective, i.e. self-assessed, ability in the second survey. Third, reporting low trust predicts lower scores in the first test, while having high trust predicts higher test scores and higher subjective ability in the second survey. Fourth, self-reported ideological leaning to "definite" right or left is associated with higher subjective ability. However, definitely leaning to the left politically predicts markedly higher scores in both tests. Definitely leaning to the right predicts a lower score in the first test.
Sensitivity analysis
While we use the general OLS method for all regressions for interpretation and comparability, we also employ the more specific logit and ordered logit models for dependent variables that are binary or ordinal. These models yield similar results in terms of statistical significance and the signs of coefficients and do not change our main findings and conclusions.
DISCUSSION
The SMS intervention is followed by two clear positive effects. The first is enhanced objective factual knowledge or awareness on how to detect disinformation. The second is an increase in selfreported efforts to verify news deemed to be upsetting. The reported effort at verification is encouraging for its possibility to curb misinformation, i.e. the unintentional sharing of false information.
Moreover, we see an increase in overall test scores in survey 2 compared to survey 1. As the improvement in tests scores is approximating the ability to detect false news, this might indicate that critical thinking skills have been enhanced. However, there is no significant difference in test score performance between participants who receive the SMS intervention and those who do not. This raises questions about the underlying reason for the overall test score increase. One possibility is that some test questions are simpler in the second test. The low number of test questions, ten, could make results sensitive to the difficulty level of individual questions.
Another possibility is that of a real general improvement among both participants who receive the SMS and those who do not. This could point to the relevance of the participant engagement component in survey 1. Step two, the SMS intervention, consists of only one-way messaging alerts. In contrast, survey 1 requires active participant engagement by answering questions and assessing news authenticity. Previous research has found unilateral media literacy interventions, such as lectures or one-way messaging, to yield more insignificant results (Badrinathan, 2021). However, when interactive feedback is added, results improve (Ali & Qazi, 2023). Some previous successful SMS studies also allow participants to respond (Fjeldsoe etal., 2010; Patrick et al, 2009). That active engagement may be important, resonates with recent trends towards "gamification" (Eisemann & Pimmer, 2020), and gaming elements in media literacy interventions (Lu et al., 2024).
Our results also highlight the poor performance of youth and avid social media users. There seems to be a generational gap in objective capabilities where youth perform worse than older respondents. Youth and keen social media users seem to be over-confident in their abilities. We may witness an adverse selection effect whereby individuals with weaker judgment are more active on social media and unintentionally spread false information. Alternatively, social media exposure to disinformation over time may weaken judgment. Such an interpretation resonates with previous studies suggesting a relationship between online exposure to false information and subsequent subconscious change (Bastick, 2021; Pennycook et al, 2018; Rapp & Salovich, 2018). Here, the elderly, aged 70 or more, outperform the other age categories on the tests. It is possible that the elderly are lesser users of social media compared to youth (Ohlsson, 2023) and greater consumers of public service (SOM-institutet, 2023). In Sweden, public service generally has high trust among the population although news consumption patterns have been changing (Medieakademin, 2024; SOMinstitutet, 2022).
Our findings on the relationship between trust and test performance go to the core of the challenge with disinformation. People who report high trust score better on both tests. Distrusting respondents score lower in test 1 but improve their results in test 2. A person who is overly distrusting fails to identify authentic news. Media literacy requires citizens to be able to discriminate between real and false news. This means being able to identify authentic news, not only to dismiss false information.
The differences in participants" ability to answer test questions linked to ideology, education and gender, concur with previous research. Respondents with a "definite left" leaning ideology score significantly better in both tests, and respondents self-characterizing as "definite right" perform below in the first test. While the effects of definite left and right are largely consistent with earlier literature (Baptista et al., 2021; Calvillo et al., 2021; Rodrigues-Pérez & Canel, 2023), they are pronounced here. University education predicts higher test scores and higher subjective ability, consistent with earlier findings (Baptista et al., 2021; Humprecht et al., 2023). The ambiguous effect of gender on test scores is unclear, also consistent with previous results (Almenar et al., 2021; Humprecht et al., 2023).
CONCLUSION
How can media literacy be boosted through a disinformation awareness intervention via SMS? In this study, we explore text messaging as a new and untested modality to increase awareness of disinformation among adults. The potential of the SMS modality lies in its wide reach, low cost, and positive results in other fields such as health and education. On a national sample of adult Swedish respondents (N = 2536) we conduct a three-step study consisting of an initial survey, followed by a fiveweek SMS intervention, concluded by a second survey. We analyze hypotheses on objective and subjective ability to identify disinformation using OLS regression analysis.
Our findings suggest that factual knowledge on disinformation can be enhanced, and that self-reported verification of news can be increased with an SMS intervention. Participants' test scores in identifying true and false news headlines can also improve. However, as the SMS intervention as such does not seem to be the key factor, the question of (inter)active participant engagement emerges as relevant.
This study makes the first pioneering use of mobile text messaging to foster awareness of disinformation in wider adult populations. Its importance lies in the reach also to adults outside formal education. This group often constitutes the majority of the population, is vital for the functioning of democracy, and is generally harder to reach for the purpose of media literacy interventions. While our findings confirm the potential of the SMS modality, there is room for further development.
Future research on SMS interventions may want to experiment more systematically with interactive participant engagement. Future interventions would also benefit from a combined focus on disinformation and information awareness, in line with traditional media literacy teaching. This links back to the difference between nudging or techcognition interventions on the one hand, largely focused on false content, and boosting interventions, on the other hand, aiming to equip people with a broader capability to discern both false and authentic information. Citizens' capability to separate false from real is key. In democratic societies, citizens also need to be able to trust information from certain sources.
The poor performance of youth and frequent social media users in separating false from real news is concerning and resonates with previous research on the subconscious impact of online exposure to false information (Bastick 2021; Pennycook et al., 2018; Rapp & Salovich 2018). False information currently permeating the overall online environment can take the form of videos, texts, images, or audio clips conveying humor, satire, conspiracies, hoaxes, and rumors (Aimeur et al. 2023). A demanding set of cognitive, emotional and technical knowledge and skills are needed to navigate this online environment. The challenges may be further compounded by the deliberate use of disinformation as a tool of hybrid warfare to destabilize societies (Hoogensen Gjorv et al. 2022). Media literacy, as part of civic literacy can, therefore, be important for the resilience of democratic societies against malign interference (Lilja et al. 2024).
The variable capabilities and vulnerabilities of different constituencies call for tailored and adaptive media literacy education strategies. The formal education of children and youth needs strengthening. However, given the evolving nature of disinformation, linear approaches premised on schooling at a young age are insufficient. Out-of-school adults are a largely neglected target group that contains several sub-groups. Interventions need to be tailored to different constituencies through online and offline modalities in ways that are engaging.
We acknowledge some limitations of our results. First, this study makes no claim to causality. Still, it represents an initial step in exploring the use of mobile text messaging to inform future research designed to establish causal effects. Second, the participant dropout during the three-step process could have a possible impact on the generalizability of our findings if drop-out participants have different characteristics or survey results compared to those who remain. While the demographic characteristics of our final sample is similar to those of the Swedish population, our results should be interpreted with this caveat in mind. Third, our findings are specific to Sweden with its history of public service media and relatively centralized governance. This said, findings might apply to other European democracies where public service has historically played a central role in news provision the way it has in Sweden. This study forms part of a broader project on resilience to disinformation in Sweden and Norway. Future SMS interventions could thus be extended to Norway as well as to other Nordic and European countries.
