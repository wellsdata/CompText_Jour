In September, 2016, in Reno, NV, USA, a woman died from a multidrug-resistant Klebsiella pneumoniae infection acquired in India. Apparently, the strain that she was infected with was resistant to 26 antibiotics and carried the mcr-1 gene conferring resistance to colistin, as well as other resistance mechanisms. 1 Cases of multidrug resistance are becoming less startling, even in the USA, where most cases are imported. Worldwide, the awareness and fear of these potentially untreatable infections is understandably high.
The recognition of antimicrobial resistance has risen to a global level, most notably at the UN General Assembly meeting in September, 2016, during which attending nations agreed on a five-part plan to combat antimicrobial resistance, two parts of which were focused on awareness and understanding of antimicrobial resistance through local surveillance and research programmes. The discussions at this meeting were undoubtedly spurred on by the May, 2016, antimicrobial resistance report chaired by Jim O'Neil, 2 commissioned by the UK Prime Minister and funded by the Wellcome Trust and UK Government, which projected some staggering numbers with respect to the impact of antimicrobial resistance within the next 30 years, including 50 million deaths each year worldwide by 2050 and a financial burden in the trillions of dollars, accounting for large proportions of gross domestic product.
In 2013, the US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) published a list of 18 resistant bacteria that presented various levels of threat on the basis of their epidemiology, extent of resistance, and ability to be treated. They classified the 18 species as either urgent, serious, or concerning. The urgent group consisted of Clostridium difficile, carbapenem-resistant Enterobacteriaceae, and drug-resistant Neisseria gonorrhoeae. Serious challenges included multidrug-resistant Acinetobacter baumannii, drug-resistant campylobacter, extended spectrum β-lactamase-producing Enterobactericeae, drug-resistant Streptococcus pneumoniae, and others. The CDC estimated the impact of these various species in terms of incidence of infection and infection-related mortality per year in the USA. 3
Unsurprisingly, the O'Neill report and the CDC data prompted action to be taken by the UN General Assembly and committees convened by the US President, and funds to fight antimicrobial resistance were made available by European Union bodies and some other public funding initiatives such as the Biomedical Advanced Research and Development Authority in the USA. However, two reports 4,5 have since been published discussing the validity of these data: one by Reuters news agency 4 regarding the CDC data, and the other by de Kraker and colleagues 5 on the O'Neil antimicrobial resistance report. These two articles 4,5 were highly critical of the methods used by the CDC and in the O'Neill report, both strongly suggesting the results were misleading or, at worst, wrong.
Reuters published a series of cases of patients with a range of antibiotic-resistant infections, each illustrating the clinical and, in some cases, financial challenges of antimicrobial resistance. They reported the weaknesses in data collection and assimilation by the CDC, highlighting the low numbers of state health departments providing data and number of counties in each of the states providing data. The reporters did point out that the systems in place were flawed in some cases-eg, with respect to how deaths were assigned to infections, or the original reason given for hospital admission. Clearly no system is perfect, but Reuters proceeded to discredit the data presented by the CDC. Moreover, the CDC website had examples of death certificates showing several causes of death, none of which described the infection or the incriminating resistant pathogen that led to the patient's demise.
De Kraker and colleagues 5 approached the O'Neill antimicrobial resistance report in a less sensational manner, but nevertheless called into question the integrity of the estimates of the impact of antimicrobial resistance over the coming decades. Notably, De Kraker and colleagues stated, “we contend that unreliable global estimates like those provided in the AMR [antimicrobial resistance] Review potentially undermine, rather than support, the fight against a post antibiotic era”. De Kraker and colleagues use methods such as attributable mortality, which is somewhat controversial, and indeed they admit that such assessments can be affected by a range of factors such as timing of appropriate therapy (whatever that may be), the resistance profile of the pathogen or pathogens, and host-related and pathogen-related factors. The lack of scientific peer review is a criticism of the O'Neill report. However, researchers who have had manuscripts peer reviewed can attest to the mixture of responses that can be received, and reconciling these can be a challenge.
Accurate data is required to establish the size and scope of the increasing challenge of antimicrobial resistance. The UN General Assembly put the topic in the limelight, albeit briefly, and financial support for more accurate and detailed surveillance is vital to understand the scale of the problem and to estimate the progress made, if any, in the fight to combat antimicrobial resistance. Surveillance programmes are needed in both developed and developing countries to accurately assess the scale and evolution of the problem.
We are in an era in which misinformation, post-truths, fake news, and lies are increasing across many media platforms. We have to hope that the genuine efforts of well intentioned experts to bring the enormity of this escalating problem to a large majority of less informed people are understood to be estimates rather than promises.
