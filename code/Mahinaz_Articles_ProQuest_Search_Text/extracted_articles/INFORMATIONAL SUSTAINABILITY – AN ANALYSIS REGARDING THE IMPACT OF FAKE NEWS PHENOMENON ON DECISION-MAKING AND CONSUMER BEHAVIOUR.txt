Abstract
In the context of accelerated digitalisation and the expansion of social media platforms, the phenomenon of disinformation has become a major challenge for the economic and organisational environment. This paper aims to develop a multidimensional framework for assessing how the fake news phenomenon alters the perception of value and trust in the market/organisations, thereby undermining the foundations of informational sustainability. The study analyses the impact of fake news on the perception of organisations, consumer behaviour, decision-making rationality, and sustainability. Based on a sample of 1,064 respondents, selected through a non-probability sampling procedure and structured to reflect the main socio-demographic characteristics of the population under study, statistical methods such as hypothesis testing, correlations, regression and the Population Attributable Fraction (PAF) indicator are used to determine the proportion of effects attributable to exposure to fake news. The results highlight a significant and multidimensional impact of misinformation on consumer trust, consumption behaviour, decision-making rationality, and orientation toward sustainability. These findings suggest the need to strengthen institutional and organisational mechanisms for combating misinformation, as a prerequisite for developing an environment characterised by informational sustainability. The study uses data collected between 19-22 December 2025 through the CAWI method. Exposure to fake news was assessed based on respondents' perceptions of the influence of false information on trust, consumption behaviour, decision-making rationality, and sustainability orientation. The study primarily considered the influence of exposure to fake news on two target groups, namely organisations and consumers, but also the existence of distortions in the information flow as a result of information asymmetry and also the role of governance in regulating the phenomenon analysed, with the aim of creating a framework that generates an information sustainability system. The results are relevant for organisations, decision-makers, researchers and consumers, providing resources for public policies and organisational strategies aimed at strengthening trust through truthful information.
Keywords: fake news, Population Attributable Fraction (PAF), decision-making and consumption behaviour, sustainability, information asymmetry, organisations.

Introduction
The rapid spread of false or distorted information (fake news) influences consumer perceptions damages the reputation of organisations and can lead to irrational economic decisions. Despite the increased interest in this topic, most existing studies are predominantly descriptive, without quantifying the real impact of disinformation on economic behaviour. This paper aims to address this gap by using the Population Attributable Fraction (PAFFN) indicator, adapted from the field of health and risk analysis, to estimate the proportion of economic and organisational behaviours associated with exposure to fake news. The study aims to analyse the relationships between exposure to disinformation, cognitive distortions, consumer behaviour and implications for sustainability. A false information tends to generate, cumulatively, new forms of disinformation, amplifying the initial effects and contributing to the formation of distorted information ecosystems.
This phenomenon is closely related to information asymmetry, as actors who generate or distribute fake news have an informational advantage over those who receive it. This asymmetry is no longer just the result of natural differences in access to information but becomes an active mechanism through which perceptions and economic decisions are strategically influenced. Thus, exposure to fake news heighten existing informational imbalances, reducing the ability of consumers and organisations to correctly evaluate available information and make decisions. Integrating the concept of information asymmetry into the proposed analysis allows for a deeper understanding of how disinformation affects the functioning of markets and organisations. In this sense, the PAFFN indicator provides an innovative framework for quantifying the impact of these informational imbalances, highlighting the proportion of economic and organisational effects that can be directly attributed to exposure to false information. Through this approach, the paper contributes to the expansion of the specialised literature, bringing to the forefront the intersection between economics, decision-making behaviour and the study of disinformation.
Thus, the authors aim to identify the contribution of the parties involved, approaching the problem from several perspectives: 1. societal (governmental) point of view - by analysing the behaviour of the categories involved; 2. consumer perspective - regarding the influence of fake news on consumer behaviour; 3. business organisations' perspective - materialised in highlighting the possible effects of information asymmetry on them. Thus, the authors try to answer the following research questions: "How does false information influence organisations and consumers?"; "What types of distortions can occur in the context of the dissemination of fake news?"; "What responsibilities do stakeholders have in identifying and resolving the effects of the phenomenon studied, in order to create an information sustainability system?".
The article is structured as follows: (1) presentation of the scientific framework regarding fake news, information asymmetry and sustainability; (2) description of the research methodology; (3) analysis and interpretation of the results. The authors have developed a series of conclusions highlighting the contributions and implications of the scientific approach.
Although the literature documents the effects of fake news on individual perceptions and behaviours, there are few attempts to quantify the proportion of economic and organisational effects attributable to exposure to misinformation. In this context, the study proposes adapting the Population Attributable Fraction indicator to assess the systemic impact of fake news on consumer behaviour and their relationship with organisations.
1. Review of the scientific literature
Starting from the definition given by Allcott & Gentzkow (2017) for "fake news" - as news articles that are intentionally and verifiably false and that can mislead the public, in the case of this paper, "false information" about a good and service is that piece of information that is deliberately distorted, incomplete or fabricated, with the aim of influencing consumers' perception and altering their decision-making behaviour in the market. Thus, this information can target essential characteristics of the good or of the service such as quality, price, provenance, safety or associated benefits and is disseminated through formal or informal channels (deceptive advertising, fake reviews, viral content on social networks), contributing to the creation of an erroneous image about its real value. In this context, fake news does not represent just simple informational errors but can also constitute strategic tools that can be used to generate unfair competitive advantages or to manipulate consumers (Akerlof, 1970; Varian, 2014). When in an economic exchange one of the parties has more information about a good or a service than the other party, then the information is considered asymmetric or disseminated in an asymmetrical manner, a phenomenon known as information asymmetry (Akerlof, 1970; Barbaroux, 2014).
The information asymmetry generated by fake news can manifest itself on several levels, as follows:
* Between content producers (media platforms, anonymous creators or interested actors) and the general public, in situations where information is intentionally distorted or taken out of context, and the receivers do not have the necessary tools to verify its truthfulness (Allcott & Gentzkow, 2017).
* Between digital platforms and users, in the context of algorithms that filter and personalise content, creating information bubbles that limit access to alternative perspectives and favour the propagation of erroneous information (Wardle & Derakhshan, 2017).
* Between public institutions and citizens, when official communication is insufficient, ambiguous, or delayed, allowing false information to fill information gaps (Wardle & Derakhshan, 2017).
* Between experts and non-experts, especially in technical or scientific fields, where the complexity of information facilitates the spread of misinterpretations or pseudo-explanations (Flynn et al., 2017).
* Between users, within social networks, where the uncritical redistribution of information contributes to the amplification of information imbalances (Del Vicario et al., 2016).
These forms of manifestation do not operate independently, but rather reinforce one another. Thus, the information asymmetry generated by fake news amplifies the difficulties of information assessment, reduces the capacity for critical verification, and fosters the emergence of suboptimal economic decisions. From this perspective, information asymmetry represents one of the explanatory mechanisms underlying the hypotheses formulated in the present research.
The specialised literature highlights multiple effects of fake news on the economic environment. At the organisational level, disinformation can generate reputational crises, affecting the trust of stakeholders and the market value of companies. Regarding consumer behaviour, exposure to false information is associated with impulsive decisions, emotional reactions and diminished economic rationality. From the perspective of market mechanisms, the presence of false information accentuates the information asymmetry between sellers and buyers, reducing the latter's ability to make rational and efficient choices. As a result, phenomena such as adverse selection, decreased trust in the market and even the withdrawal of quality products in favour of those promoted through misleading information may occur. (Stiglitz, 2000).
Also, in the context of sustainability, fake news can negatively influence perceptions of ecological products, contributing to their rejection or to the distortion of choice criteria. Integrating perspectives from behavioural economics and communication studies allows for a deeper understanding of the mechanisms through which disinformation influences economic decisions. Therefore, in this paper, false information is conceptualised as a disruptive factor in the efficient functioning of markets, having direct effects on consumer behaviour, competition and resource allocation, especially in the current digital context, in which the speed and scale of information dissemination significantly amplify their impact (Pennycook & Rand, 2019; Vosoughi et al., 2018).
2. Research methodology
To answer the research questions, the contextual and strategic interactions between the actors involved in the generation, spread and regulation of fake news were taken into account, in order to measure the intensity of the influence of the studied phenomenon on the categories considered. In addition, the authors assumed that the relationships considered are also under the influence of distortions generated by other elements, such as cognitive and emotional distortions or asymmetry distortions.
The study used a sample of 1,064 respondents, selected through a non-probability sampling procedure and structured to replicate the main socio-demographic characteristics of the voting-age population in Romania (age, development region, etc.). This research adopts a quantitative approach, based on a representative sample of 1,064 respondents, with explanatory elements, having as its main objective to estimate the impact of exposure to false information on economic and organisational behaviours, by using the Population Attributable Fraction indicator (PAF). This indicator, calculated to estimate the proportion of a synthetic health indicator, once integrated as part of a risk assessment, can overcome some limitations historically associated with the causal attribution of health indicators (Fernandez et al., 2025; Plass et al., 2022). Some authors (Counil, 2021) have noted that the Population Attributable Fraction (PAF) can be used as a tool in the decision-making process.
The methodological design integrates concepts from economics and behavioural sciences, to capture the relationships between disinformation, information asymmetry and the decisions of economic actors. The conceptual model starts from the relationship between exposure to fake news and economic behaviour, mediated by cognitive and emotional factors. Studies show that constant exposure to fake news can generate social media fatigue (decreased interest in social networks, as a result of information overload with exaggerated, distorted or even false content), causing users to reduce their use of digital platforms (Dinu et al., 2024). Thus, exposure to misinformation leads to the emergence of cognitive distortions, such as anxiety and distrust, which subsequently influence consumer's behaviour and perception about organisations. The calculation principles of PAF can also be transferred to behavioural economics, provided that explicit assumptions are established to build a realistic scenario (Plass et al., 2022).
Given this context, the following main hypotheses are formulated:
* H1. Exposure to fake news positively influences irrational consumption behaviour.
* H2. Exposure to fake news negatively influences sustainable consumption behaviour.
* H3. Exposure to fake news increases the level of distrust in organisations.

The following mediation hypotheses were also formulated:
* H4. The effect of fake news on irrational consumption is mediated by cognitive distortions.
* H5. The effect of fake news on sustainability is mediated by the level of distrust.
In addition, the authors believe that a significant proportion of dysfunctional effects can be attributed to exposure to fake news, based on the PAF indicator (Levin, 1953).
* H6. Exposure to fake news contributes significantly to the emergence of irrational consumption (PAFFN > 0, FN = Fake News).
* H7. A significant proportion of unsustainable behaviours is caused by fake news.
Statistical and econometric data processing was performed in Microsoft Excel (Microsoft 365), and in IBM SPSS Statistics (v. 26/27).
The proposed study uses a quantitative, cross-sectional design, with the objective of assessing the impact of exposure to disinformation on economic and organisational behaviour. Hypotheses H1-H3 have been tested through simple and multiple linear regression (OLS) models, in which the dependent variables were: consumer behaviour (Q2), sustainable behaviours (Q4) and the level of trust in organisations (Q1), and the main independent variable was "exposure/perception of fake news".

To test hypotheses H6 and H7, the research integrates methods from behavioural economics and risk analysis, using the Population Attributable Fraction indicator to estimate the proportion of dysfunctional effects attributable to exposure to fake news (PAFFN). The PAF indicator is mainly used in health and risk analysis fields to estimate the proportion of cases of a disease or event that could be prevented if a certain risk factor would be eliminated. In the context of analysing the phenomenon of disinformation, the PAFFN indicator can be reinterpreted to answer the question: "What proportion of dysfunctional economic behaviours (e.g., irrational consumption, loss of trust in organisations, or rejection of sustainable products) could be avoided at the level of the entire population if exposure to fake news would be completely eliminated?".
The research is based on a national study in Romania, conducted by Cult Market Research in December 2025, using the online CAWI method. The sample was selected using a nonprobabilistic method, ensuring diversity according to relevant socio-demographic characteristics, with an error margin of ±3% and a confidence level of 95%. The impact of fake news on companies was assessed based on the respondents' perception, through the question: "To what extent do you think fake news about companies influences: Q1) Consumer trust; Q2) Consumers' ability to make rational decisions; Q3) Consumer behaviour; Q4) Consumers' ability to make sustainability-oriented decisions". These four dimensions, considered as risk factors, were analysed differently according to the socio-demographic characteristics of the respondents, namely: gender, age, level of education, occupation, area of residence and level of net income. In order to cumulatively capture this impact, the authors determined the PAFFN, indicator, to estimate the proportion of dysfunctional effects associated to exposure to fake news.

3. Results and discussion
The analysis of the sample structure (Table no. 1) reveals a balanced socio-demographic profile relevant for investigating the perception of the fake news phenomenon in the economic environment, given a validated volume of 1,064 respondents.
The profile of the respondents indicates a heterogeneous and balanced sample, respecting the structure of the total population, suitable for investigating the phenomenon of disinformation at the national level from a multidimensional perspective. The socio-demographic diversity allows for the exploration of the relationships between individual characteristics and vulnerability to fake news.
3.1. The systemic impact of disinformation on economic and social behaviour
The values obtained (Figure no. 2) indicate that respondents perceive the impact of fake news on companies as moderate to high, exceeding the neutral level of the scale. The highest level of influence is recorded in the case of consumer trust (3.33), closely followed by the ability to make rational decisions (3.32). This result indicates that respondents perceive the influence of fake news as being more pronounced on consumer trust and the ability to make rational decisions than on the other dimensions analysed.
Regarding consumer behaviour (3.26), the average value indicates a relevant impact, but slightly lower than the previous dimensions, suggesting that the effects of misinformation on concrete purchasing actions are mediated by other factors. The lowest level of influence is observed in the case of sustainability-oriented decisions (3.15), which suggests that respondents perceive a lower impact of fake news on sustainability-oriented decisions compared to the other dimensions analysed.
According to the correlation matrix (Table no. 2), the strongest association is observed between consumer trust and sustainability-oriented decisions (r = 0.51), indicating a moderate to high correlation. This result highlights the existence of a moderate association between consumer trust and sustainability orientation. Given the cross-sectional design of the research, the observed relationship does not allow for causal conclusions.
The data in the graphical representations (Figures no. 3-8) highlight the fact that the perceived impact of fake news on consumer behaviour and their relationship with organisations is differentiated depending on the socio-demographic profile, but maintains a relatively high level in all analysed categories.
By age, the influence of fake news is more pronounced among adults (35-64 years old), where T2B values consistently exceed 50%, while young people (18-34 years old) show a lower level of perception of the impact. Education level is the strongest differentiating variable, with respondents with higher education reporting significantly higher impact values (up to 78% for trust), which indicates that respondents with higher education report higher levels of the perceived impact of fake news.
From a gender perspective, the differences are moderate, but male respondents consistently perceive a slightly higher impact compared to female respondents. Depending on the area of residence, urban respondents perceive a higher impact of fake news compared to those in rural areas, a difference that could be associated with particularities of information consumption in the two environments, an aspect that requires further investigation.
In terms of income, there is a trend of increasing perception of impact in the middleand high-income categories, especially for the dimensions related to trust and rationality, which indicates a greater sensitivity to information risks. From employment status perspective, the impact is perceived more intensely among economically active people (employees and entrepreneurs), compared to inactive categories, which can be explained by a more direct involvement in consumption processes and in the relationship with organisations.
3.2. Proportion of effects attributable to exposure to fake news - indicator PAFFN
The methodological steps and application of the calculation algorithm to estimate the proportion of effects associated to exposure to fake news are presented in Tables no. 3-5.
The RR values are not direct estimates from the data collected in the study, but indicative ranges derived from the literature. They were used exclusively to construct an exploratory scenario regarding the potential impact of fake news. To assess the robustness of the estimates, a sensitivity analysis was performed based on the minimum and maximum values of the RR ranges identified in the relevant literature.
The estimates obtained by applying the PAFFN indicator suggest the existence of a potential impact of exposure to fake news on the main dimensions analysed. The estimated values of PAF, located between 3.05% and 21.0% at the individual level of the indicators, indicate that a significant proportion of the analysed dysfunctions can be attributed to exposure to disinformation. In particular, the most pronounced effects are observed in the case of consumer behaviour and consumer trust, where the maximum values exceed 16-20%, suggesting a high vulnerability of these dimensions to the influence of false information.
In the case of rational decision-making capacity, PAFFN values (6.23%-14.2%) confirm that misinformation affects consumers' cognitive mechanisms, reducing their capacity for critical evaluation and favouring the emergence of decision-making biases. Regarding sustainabilityoriented behaviour, the attributable impact is lower (3.05%-11.2%), suggesting that this type of behaviour is less dependent on immediate informational influences and more anchored in structural and value factors.

The aggregate PAFFN indicator should be interpreted as an exploratory synthetic estimate of the potential impact of disinformation. It is not a direct measure of the actual economic effects, but an approximation built on the basis of perceived prevalence and RR ranges taken from the literature. Also, this aggregate value highlights the non-linear and interdependent nature of the effects of disinformation, confirming that: a false piece of information does not produce an isolated effect, but progressively triggers new chains of disinformation and behavioural distortions. Thus, the results suggest that the effects of fake news can manifest cumulatively, through relationships between trust, rationality of decisions, and consumption and sustainability behaviours.
The integration of the correlation analysis (Table no. 2) with the PAFFN (Table no. 5) highlights that the effects of fake news are not uniform, but manifest themselves differently across dimensions. The relatively high correlation between consumer trust and sustainabilityoriented decisions (r = 0.51), corroborated with significant PAFFN values for trust (9.1-21%), indicates that: trust functions as a mechanism for transmitting the effects of fake news to other behavioural dimensions. Although PAFFN for buyer consumption behaviour is also high (16.4%), correlations with the other variables are relatively low (r ≈ 0.022-0.091), showing that fake news influences consumer behaviour directly. But this effect is less dependent on other dimensions (trust, rationality, sustainability).
At the same time, although rational decisions show very weak correlations with the other variables (r < 0.03), the PAFFN values (6.23-14.2%) indicate a significant attributable impact. This apparent contradiction suggests that: fake news affects rationality directly and individually, but the effect is not necessarily correlated with perceptions of other dimensions. Rationality thus functions as an independent dimension, but vulnerable to disinformation.
Consumer trust appears as a central node, through which the effects of disinformation propagate towards sustainable behaviours, while consumer behaviour and rationality of decisions are influenced rather directly. This structure suggests the existence of complex mechanisms for the propagation of disinformation, characterised by direct and indirect effects, which reinforces the hypothesis that fake news generates progressive chains of behavioural distortions at the population level. These results indicate the existence of differentiated relationships between the analysed dimensions, which can be investigated in future research.
3.3. Hypothesis testing
The results support most of the hypotheses formulated, indicating the existence of significant relationships between exposure to fake news and the analysed variables (Figure no. 9).
The results in Figure no. 9 are compatible with the hypotheses formulated regarding the relationships between the analysed variables. Thus, exposure to fake news is associated with higher levels of irrational consumption (β = 0.2094, p < 0.001) and a reduction in sustainable practices (β = -0.181, p < 0.01). At the same time, it contributes to the amplification of distrust in organisations (β = 0.366, p < 0.001), highlighting an important effect on institutional trust capital. Mediation analysis suggests that cognitive distortions are associated with the amplification of the relationship between exposure to fake news and the decisionmaking behaviours analysed (β = 0.205; p < 0.001). The estimated indirect effect on sustainability-oriented behaviours is positive (β = 0.036), but does not reach the conventional threshold of statistical significance (p = 0.077). Regarding the PAFFN indicator, the estimates obtained suggest that approximately 14.1% of irrational consumption behaviours and 11.2% of anti-sustainable behaviours could be associated with exposure to fake news within the analysed exploratory scenario.
The methodological approach used allowed not only to test direct relationships between variables, but also to identify indirect mechanisms and quantify the attributable impact of disinformation at the population level. This combination of methods allows for the analysis of the phenomenon from complementary perspectives and provides results that can form the basis for future research.
Conclusions
The paper contributes to the existing academic literature by proposing a conceptual model based on the relationship between exposure to fake news and economic behaviour, with the aim of creating the necessary framework for achieving informational sustainability, identifying the actors involved and highlighting the relationships between them. The contribution of the paper can also be appreciated from the perspective of extending the application of a specific epidemiology method in the analysis of economic and organisational phenomena, by capitalising on the results of a national research.
By quantifying the impact of fake news, the study provides a useful tool for underpinning public policies and organisational strategies to combat disinformation. The results may have significant implications for reputation management, media education and the promotion of sustainable consumption, highlighting the need to develop effective mechanisms to reduce the negative effects of disinformation.
The aggregate value of the ... indicator has revealed that the effects of disinformation cannot be understood in linear or isolated terms, but must be interpreted as part of a complex system of informational and behavioural interdependencies. In this framework, a false information does not act as a singular event, but as a nucleus generating cascading effects, triggering cumulative processes of distortion that progressively propagate in the structure of individual and collective decisions and perceptions. As a result, disinformation acquires the status of an emerging phenomenon, characterised by self-reproduction and systemic amplification, in which each erroneous informational act contributes to the reconfiguration of social reality. From this perspective, combating fake news is not just a punctual intervention on the informational content, but a structural necessity, oriented towards restoring the balance and sustainability of the information ecosystem. The results suggest that the perceived effects of fake news can be associated with multiple dimensions of the relationship between consumers and organisations, highlighting the need for further research on the mechanisms through which disinformation can influence economic and social behaviours. The level of education appears as the main differentiating factor, with respondents with higher education reporting the highest values of influence, especially on trust and consumer behaviour. The perception of impact is also more pronounced among economically active people, those from urban areas and those with medium and high incomes. These results are consistent with the specialised literature, which emphasises the importance of the level of education and cognitive capacity in the way individuals evaluate information and become less vulnerable to disinformation (Pennycook & Rand, 2019). At the same time, the identified effects on trust and consumer behaviour confirm previous conclusions regarding the impact of fake news on economic perceptions and decisions (Allcott & Gentzkow, 2017).
The data suggest an association between education level, exposure to information, and the perception of the impact of fake news on the consumer-organisation relationship. At the same time, the results are associated with lower levels of trust and the perception of less rational consumer behaviours, confirming the research hypotheses. Thus, the perceived effects of fake news can be associated with multiple dimensions of the relationship between consumers and organisations, highlighting the need for further research on the mechanisms through which disinformation can influence economic and social behaviours.
The research has several limitations. First, the cross-sectional design does not allow the establishment of causal relationships. Second, the results are based on self-reported perceptions and may be influenced by cognitive biases. Third, the use of a non-probability sample and RR values taken from the literature requires a cautious interpretation of the PAFFN estimates.
The main contribution of the study consists in the exploratory adaptation of the Population Attributable Fraction indicator to the analysis of the fake news phenomenon and its economic and organisational implications. The study does not propose a definitive validation of the PAFFN indicator, but an analytical framework that can be developed and tested in future research.
