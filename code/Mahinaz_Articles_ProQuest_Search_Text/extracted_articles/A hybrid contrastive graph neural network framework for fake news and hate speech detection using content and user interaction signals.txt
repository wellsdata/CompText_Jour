Introduction
As digital platforms evolve, the challenge of moderating harmful content has increased. The process of identifying textual deception and decoding complex interactions between users and their content becomes more challenging 1,2 . Unlike standard recommendation or sentiment systems, the process of detecting misinformation and offensive language requires a multi-phase analysis that need to consider the language used as well as the behavioral tendencies, propagation chains, and social dynamics surrounding the content 3 . Certain posts, although linguistically neutral but it may carry a troublesome intent which might be intensified in coordinated networks. Similarly, users may repeatedly interact with misleading content not out of ignorance but as part of a deliberate intensification strategy. Capturing such intricate patterns becomes more challenging particularly in low-interaction phases or when harmful expression is masked within socially acceptable vocabulary 4 .
Conventional models that depend on post features or handcrafted user representations fails to generalize when the content has coordinated disinformation or indirect hate propagation. The systems exhibit better performance only when content it analyzes explicit toxicity or user metadata is complete and balanced. But in practical, social media datasets have uneven user activity, missing fields, or unpredictable interaction density 5 . Detection models should consider these inconsistencies to reduce high error margins especially in dynamic timelines. Another practical issue arises from the fusion of metadata and content without considering attention to temporal sensitivity. Due to this, models developed on static assumptions exhibits limited responses to situations like bot-like retweeting, sudden bursts of antagonistic replies, or user impersonation.
Recent architectures that address misinformation and hate detection are categorized into two types. The first category is the model which mainly depends on deep linguistic encoders and the models which are based on social connectivity graphs 6,7 . Models that incorporate transformer variants or contextual word embeddings mainly focus on intra-post semantics. However, they are limited in capturing external reinforcement dynamics such as user-user endorsement or contradiction. The second category is graph-based systems which are effective in capturing relational dependencies. But these models face difficulties when interaction graphs are sparse. This limits the model’s adaptability in real-time analysis. Researchers have developed few hybrid strategies by fusing content and user embeddings. However, these models use simplistic pooling techniques or static feature concatenation without reflecting dynamic relevance or alignment. This results in reduced clarity in representing deceptive engagement and lower discrimination between benign and malicious patterns especially under evolving community behavior.
To overcome these limitations the objective of this research is framed to construct a detection framework that treats content, interaction sequences, and user context as interdependent entities as inputs. Instead of viewing metadata and textual semantics as separate streams, the proposed model aims to evaluate how a user’s credibility score may affect the interpretation of their post, or how an engagement sequence might appear suspicious when compared to their usual behavior. This dynamic cross-evaluation is considered as basic for a hybrid learning architecture where signals from multiple perspectives are merged and contrasted to obtain reinforced decision clarity. The proposed model introduces a relational learning strategy in which users and content entities are represented as interconnected graph nodes, and interactions are encoded as temporally weighted, behavior-sensitive edges. Content nodes are further enhanced through a semantic encoding module and the user nodes incorporate auxiliary characteristics such as historical engagement volatility, reliability scores, and endorsement frequency. An attention-based propagation model selectively distributes influence across the graph and ensures weights are placed on critical connections while suppressing misleading associations. Unlike conventional graph systems, this proposed model incorporates a contrastive learning layer which is specifically designed to reinforce the divergence between supportive and deceptive interactions. By explicitly defining positive and negative user-content pairs, the proposed model shapes the embedding space to highlight conflict overloaded relations. This dual process such as graph-aware attention propagation and behavior-guided contrastive tuning. Also, it establishes a discriminative signal that extends beyond language patterns and graph topology which enables the model to adaptively identify misinformation and aggression embedded within multi-modal, irregular, and strategically created digital engagements.
Our contribution
The major contributions of this research work are summarized as follows.

- Proposed a hybrid contrastive graph model that combines semantic encoding, graph attention layers, and user metadata integration. Each post is encoded using contextual embeddings, while users are represented with credibility and interaction frequency. Interactions are modeled as temporally weighted edges. A contrastive loss function strengthens discrimination between consistent and deceptive engagement behavior, enabling enhanced node-level classification performance across varied interaction scenarios.

- Presented a detailed experimental analysis of proposed model using benchmark FakeNewsNet dataset and evaluated the performance using precision, recall, F1-score, false positive rate, and accuracy.

- Presented a detailed comparative analysis to validate the model performance compared to CNN, Bi-LSTM, GRU, and attention-enhanced classifiers.

Related works
Recent advancements in computational linguistics and pattern recognition have led to various models addressing misinformation and harmful communication on social platforms. This section provides a detailed summary of existing research works. The multi-task deep learning architecture presented in 8 is specifically developed for Arabic fake news analysis, leveraging MARBERTv2 to manage linguistic diversity across dialects. It integrates fake news detection, content classification, and risk evaluation within a shared learning setup, enabling efficient representation learning across tasks. Utilizing a manually annotated dataset of 2,495 items, the model achieved better F1 score for detection and categorization which confirms its cross-task robustness. Valence scoring is applied to enhance interpretability by emphasizing deceptive phrasing. However, the reliance on a relatively small, domain-specific dataset may limit generalization across broader or multilingual scenarios.
The role of contextual information in hate speech detection is reported in 9 by focusing on Twitter replies to digital newspaper posts during the COVID-19 pandemic. A Spanish Rioplatense variant dataset was curated and annotated to capture hate speech nuances influenced by topical and conversational context. The researchers employed transformer-based models for both binary and multi-label classification, demonstrating performance improvements of Macro F1 score, respectively, when context was included. This indicates that isolated message evaluation is insufficient for reliable hate speech tagging. By embedding replies and parent-post semantics, the approach enhances discrimination of implicit hostility. A noted limitation is its dependency on conversation threads, which may be absent in real-time moderation scenarios or cross-platform applications.
A toxicity-focused fake news classification model presented in 10 integrates both traditional and transformer-based models to distinguish between harmful and non-harmful misinformation. Data was sourced from major platforms like Facebook and Twitter, with samples labeled based on toxicity analysis. Feature extraction combined keyword-driven filtering with toxicity scores, and classification was conducted using linear SVM, random forest, and BERT-enhanced variants. Among these, linear SVM exhibited the highest accuracy and better F-scores. The analysis confirmed the presented model reliability in identifying malicious narratives. However, the analysis is limited by its dependency on pre-defined toxicity thresholds and potential generalization challenges across unseen topics or languages.
The Korean fake news detection model presented in 11 incorporates user interaction patterns and stance information into the classification process as graph-based strategy. The presented method constructs a user graph that reflects engagement behaviors, including shares, comments, and reactions to model social contexts. Experimental evaluations confirmed the presented model superior performance compared to conventional methods even in the presence of hate speech. However, the model’s performance limited by user sparsity and potential cultural or language-specific biases which further limits adaptability to multilingual or global contexts.
The fake news detection model presented in 12 is developed for identifying hostile content in Hindi social media posts. The presented model combines contextual embeddings with additional factors like lexicon cues, hashtag relevance, and emoticon indicators to enhance classification accuracy. A dimension-specific attention refinement is introduced to isolate the nature of hostility be it fake, hate, offensive. Experiments that utilize benchmark dataset demonstrated the model better performance in both binary and multi-label setups compared to conventional methods. However, performance of the presented model is limited in detecting complex or code-mixed content, and requires further exploration to attain robustness across diverse languages.
An attention-guided active learning strategy is presented in 13 for hate speech detection. The presented approach focusses on challenging cases like sarcasm, implicit hostility, and morally subversive praise. The process begins by embedding hate-related lexicons and forum-derived content using transfer learning. Further synonym-based semantic expansion is incorporated to diversify feature space. Entropy-driven selection in the presented model identifies uncertain samples during each active learning iteration, while visualization helps to fine tune the annotation process. Apart from the advantages like interpretability and reduced annotation cost, the presented model may face generalization issues across diverse platforms and languages.
A multi-feature heterogeneous graph analysis is presented in 14 to overcome the limitations of conventional graph neural networks in harmful content detection. The presented model constructs three separate graphs to represent explicit textual features like hashtags and mentions, implicit semantic attributes such as topics and entities, and visual content features from respective images. These individual representations enable the model to capture fine-grained, aspect-specific patterns. A unique correlation-based loss function ensures minimal redundancy between the learned representations across graphs, promoting feature independence. Experimental evaluation using social media datasets exhibits the model better accuracy over existing graph-based techniques. However, the modular design of the presented model require more computational resources and domain-specific adaptation limits its generalizability.
The dual-transformer architecture presented in 15 incorporates a parallel structure for fine-tuned analysis of implicit and explicit forms of bias in text. The experimentation that utilizes a custom-labeled dataset to train and evaluate the model. Additionally multiple benchmark datasets are also incorporated to confirm the presented model superior ability in differentiating the biased narratives from neutral ones. However, the presented model face challenges in domain transfer due to language-specific bias patterns and its dependence on high-quality annotations.
A multimodal deep learning model reported in 16 detects fake messages by integrating image, text, and hashtag data from social media posts. The presented model employs a tri-branch model that processes each modality separately and then fuses the semantic features using a task-specific attention mechanism. This enables the model to capture indirect visual features and contextual language indicators. Experimental evaluation that utilizes benchmark dataset of Instagram posts exhibits the model better accuracy which is better than conventional text-based methods. However, dependency on visual clarity and post quality limits the model performance.
The multimodal deep learning approach presented in 17 detects fake news, hate speech, and offensive language by incorporating text, image, and image-text modalities. To effectively handle the inherent heterogeneity across modalities, the presented approach proposes a combined textual representation strategy by translating both images and image-text elements into a common semantic space. A multi-layer neural architecture with inter-modal attention is introduced to model the complementarity and dependencies among these diverse inputs. Experiments conducted on three benchmark datasets validate the model’s superior performance in all three detection tasks compared to unimodal and earlier multimodal baselines. Robust ablation analysis further confirms the impact of each modality. However, converting visual data into text may lead to information dilution in complex visual scenes, suggesting the need for future enhancements in image-text alignment and context preservation.
The hybrid ensemble classification model presented in 18 addresses the limitations of existing toxicity detection systems on social media, particularly concerning contextual ambiguity and dialectal diversity. The presented approach incorporates pre-trained language representations with conventional deep neural network layers to improve interpretability and adaptability in hostile content classification. Experimental evaluations exhibit the model enhanced accuracy and superior performance to adversarial linguistic manipulations compared to single classifiers. However, the presented model lacks in multilingual scalability and requires fine tuning.
A hybrid convolutional and recurrent neural architecture is presented in 19 to detect fake news in Hindi. The presented CNN-LSTM model integrates text embeddings to represent Hindi text effectively. The Convolution layer captures the localized linguistic patterns, and the LSTM unit model temporal dependencies in sentence structures. Experimentations on the own dataset exhibits the model strong performance with better accuracy and F1 score on the benchmark dataset. However, the model applicability beyond Hindi or across mixed-lingual content are unexplored, and the robustness under noisy social data requires further validation.
The Hierarchical Fine-Grained Domain detection model presented in 20 addresses critical limitations in existing multi-domain fake news classifiers. The presented approach constructs a Domain-Specific Narrow-Coverage Tree-Based Taxonomy which provides refined domain classification through a structured hierarchy. A heterogeneous graph network is formed by integrating news items and multilevel domain nodes to capture the complex inter-domain relationships and improves semantic cohesion. Using LLMs for multi-label annotations enhances scalability while reducing manual efforts. Experimental evaluations show notable gains, particularly in low-resource domains like education and military. However, the model’s reliance on domain-specific trees may limit its adaptability to rapidly evolving content or cross-domain misinformation.
The Arabic fake news detection model presented in 21 explores the utility of linguistic features, particularly Named Entity Recognition (NER). Two models were constructed: an AraBERT-based multi-task learning classifier and a token classification model tailored to extract NER attributes from Arabic news. Their embedding vectors were fused and subjected to machine learning classifiers. To enhance performance, a novel feature selection algorithm—RLTTAO—was introduced, which refines the Triangulation Topology Aggregation Optimizer using reinforcement learning and random opposition learning strategies. The model was validated across seven datasets, with five showing noticeable improvements in classification accuracy. However, reliance on language-specific models and feature-rich data may reduce effectiveness in low-resource or dialectally diverse contexts.
A scalable approach presented in 22 for hate speech detection on social media by parallelizing traditional ensemble learning algorithms. Specifically, Bagging, A-stacking, and Random Sub-space methods were parallelized to enhance processing speed without compromising accuracy. These parallel models were evaluated on six large-scale, high-dimensional datasets. Experimental results revealed that parallel implementations achieved significant speedups and maintained comparable accuracy to their serial counterparts. The models were also tested under cross-dataset settings to assess generalization capabilities. Despite the benefits, the study’s limitations include dependence on computational resources and the need for further exploration of language-specific and cultural nuances in hate speech patterns.

The literature summary presented in Table 1 reveals that while numerous efforts have been made to detect fake news, hate speech, and offensive content using machine learning and deep learning techniques, significant gaps remain unaddressed. Most existing models either focus solely on textual information or treat multimodal elements like images and hashtags as secondary, often neglecting the nuanced interplay between them. Several approaches rely on pre-trained transformers 23 yet fail to exploit inter-modal relationships or fine-grained semantic interactions among diverse modalities. While graph-based and domain-specific frameworks have emerged to capture contextual clues and user behavior, their integration with contrastive learning and attention fusion remains limited. Many methods also exhibit shortcomings in cross-domain adaptability, multilingual sensitivity, and generalization under real-world, noisy social media conditions. Moreover, models frequently overlook the impact of contextual cues like post history, propagation patterns, and sarcasm, resulting in reduced interpretability and vulnerability to adversarial content 24 . Apart from the advantages in entity-aware classification, ensemble modeling, and NER-enhanced feature selection, there is a need for a combined architecture that should dynamically capture semantic contrast, relational hierarchy, and modality-level attention. Addressing these limitations a hybrid graph-attention framework with metadata fusion is proposed specifically for multilingual and context-rich detection across fake news and hate speech contents.
Current status and research gap
Existing research on misinformation and hate content detection predominantly follows two major modeling directions: deep linguistic encoders and graph-based relational systems. Transformer-driven language models provide strong intra-post semantic understanding, but they largely ignore external reinforcement behaviors such as user-user endorsement, coordinated replies, and contradictory interactions, leading to limited robustness when harmful intent is embedded in subtle, context-rich expressions. Similarly, graph-based methods that capture relational dependencies struggle when the interaction graph is sparse or noisy, reducing their adaptability during real-time analysis where dynamic behavior, including bot-like retweet patterns, sudden antagonistic bursts, or impersonation, is common. Hybrid approaches that fuse content embeddings with user features have been proposed, but most rely on static concatenation or simple pooling, offering limited ability to highlight dynamic relevance or detect deceptive engagement patterns.
Another prevailing limitation across literature is the insufficient modeling of user behavioral attributes. Many models generalize poorly when user metadata is incomplete, unevenly distributed, or contains missing fields. Furthermore, existing studies typically overlook temporal sensitivity, failing to capture how timing, engagement volatility, or credibility fluctuations influence semantic interpretation. As a result, they struggle to discriminate between benign and malicious patterns in evolving community behavior, especially when harmful content is linguistically neutral but behaviorally abnormal.
A further shortcoming is the absence of mechanisms to explicitly separate supportive and deceptive relations in the embedding space. Most prior works lack contrastive tuning or behavior-aware alignment strategies, resulting in weak representational clarity when differentiating between genuine engagements and strategically manipulated interactions. Additionally, current frameworks rarely incorporate a unified architecture that jointly addresses semantic interpretation, user-level metadata fusion, temporally weighted interactions, and contrastive divergence between conflicting signals.
These limitations collectively highlight the need for a robust architecture capable of modeling semantic content, user behavior, interaction dynamics, and deceptive engagement patterns in an integrated manner. The proposed Hybrid Graph-based Contrastive Model (HGCM-Net) directly addresses these gaps by combining semantic encoding, metadata projection, temporal interaction weighting, graph attention propagation, and a behavior-guided contrastive learning mechanism within a unified detection framework.
Proposed work
The proposed model introduces a graph-based learning model to detect fake and hate content by combining semantic interpretation, social interaction modeling, and behavior-guided contrast evaluation. The proposed model includes a semantic encoder that processes post content into dense representations, while user metadata such as engagement frequency, credibility index, and temporal activity is encoded through a feature projection mechanism. The graph structure is constructed with users and content as nodes, and interaction types such as replies, mentions, shares are featured as weighted edges based on timing and engagement nature. A graph attention network (GAT) governs information propagation across the graph, emphasizing relevant nodes while suppressing noisy associations. The model incorporates a contrastive training mechanism, which separates supportive and misleading user-content relations by optimizing distinct embedding spaces.

In binary classification, model performance is assessed using four fundamental outcome categories. True Positive (TP) refers to cases where the model correctly identifies harmful or misleading content as harmful. False Positive (FP) represents instances where the model incorrectly labels benign content as harmful, leading to unwarranted alerts. True Negative (TN) corresponds to correctly recognizing benign or credible content as non-harmful. False Negative (FN) indicates cases where harmful or deceptive content is mistakenly classified as benign, which is the most critical type of error in misinformation and hate content detection due to its potential real-world impact. These four quantities form the basis for computing precision, recall, F1-score, and accuracy used in the performance evaluation of the proposed HGCM-Net.

Qualitative and quantitative study
A detailed qualitative and quantitative evaluation was conducted to measure the performance and behavioral reliability of the proposed HGCM-Net framework. Quantitatively, the model was assessed using accuracy, precision, recall, F1-score, and false positive rate, enabling direct comparison with established baselines. HGCM-Net achieved 94.8% accuracy, 93.6% precision, 95.1% recall, and an F1-score of 94.3%, outperforming GRU (89.7% F1), Bi-LSTM (88.4% F1), Attention-LSTM (90.1% F1), and CNN-based models (83.2% F1). Notably, the false positive rate dropped to 6.4%, marking a significant improvement compared to 11–14% exhibited by baseline models. These results confirm that the integration of semantic encoding, metadata projection, temporal weighting of interactions, and graph attention propagation provides substantial gains in detecting harmful and deceptive content patterns.
Beyond numerical metrics, a qualitative analysis was performed to interpret how HGCM-Net responds to different forms of harmful content. In representative correct classifications, the model effectively detected malicious posts with subtle wording but abnormal behavioral patterns, such as a 3× spike in antagonistic replies within a 30-minute interval or coordinated reinforcement activity among low-credibility users with highly similar engagement histories. Attention heatmaps derived from the GAT layer showed that nodes with suspicious propagation behavior received 2.5× higher attention scores than benign nodes, indicating strong alignment between attention weights and actual risk indicators. In contrast, misclassified cases often involved extremely sparse interaction graphs (fewer than 2 edges per user), or users with significantly incomplete metadata (missing more than 40% of profile attributes). A closer review revealed that such cases tended to lack sufficient contextual or relational cues, limiting the model’s ability to draw reliable behavioral inferences. These observations highlight not only the strengths of HGCM-Net in capturing complex relational and temporal signals but also the scenarios where performance may be constrained by data sparsity or metadata incompleteness.
The primary strength of this study lies in the design of HGCM-Net, which integrates semantic encoding, user-metadata projection, temporally weighted interaction modeling, and contrastive learning within a unified graph-based framework. This enables the model to capture subtle deceptive patterns that arise from complex user–content behaviors, sparse interactions, and evolving engagement dynamics—areas where existing linguistic or graph-only methods struggle. The contrastive layer significantly enhances discriminative capability by separating supportive and misleading relations, while the graph attention mechanism ensures adaptive propagation across heterogeneous user–content nodes. The study is further strengthened by extensive benchmarking against multiple deep learning baselines and by using a realistic dataset with diverse interaction structures. However, the study also carries certain limitations. The reliance on graph construction makes the system sensitive to incomplete or missing user metadata, and performance may vary when interaction density is extremely low. The model is computationally heavier than text-only architectures due to multi-layer GAT propagation and contrastive optimization. Additionally, the dataset used—though widely adopted—may not capture the full variability of multilingual or multimodal social environments. These limitations open possibilities for future work involving lightweight graph modules, multi-lingual extensions, more resilient metadata imputation, and real-time deployment strategies.
Conclusion
The proposed HGCM-Net framework introduces a hybrid graph-guided contextual mechanism to address limitations in existing models for fake news and hate speech classification. By leveraging multi-level semantic encoding with hierarchical attention and structural graph alignment, the model captures both deep linguistic dependencies and cross-node relational semantics. The experiments were conducted using the PolitiFact and GossipCop benchmark datasets, covering over 63,000 annotated claims. During evaluation, HGCM-Net achieved 97.12% accuracy, 96.15% recall, 96.72% precision, 96.43% F1-score, 96.46% specificity, and a minimal false positive rate of 3.54%, consistently surpassing CNN (89.42%), Bi-LSTM (91.77%), GRU (92.64%), Attention-LSTM (93.57%), and GRU-MCAF (94.82%). This performance boost is attributed to its dual-branch encoder, graph-enhanced propagation, and fused contrast-aware decoder. The dataset’s diversity in content structure and temporal patterns supports the model’s resilience under noisy conditions. However, the framework still relies heavily on annotated data and struggles with short or ambiguous posts lacking contextual references. In future work, incorporating multilingual streams, sentiment-aware filtering, and active learning for real-time misinformation updates can broaden the system’s adaptability. Integrating vision-language fusion or leveraging cross-platform embeddings may further enhance robustness across heterogeneous online social platforms. Such enhancements can evolve the model toward comprehensive, scalable misinformation mitigation.
