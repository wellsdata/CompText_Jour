Introduction
The rapid development of social media has notably increased the speed of information dissemination and streamlined interactions among people. However, it has also become a breeding ground for fake news [1], negatively impacting political, economic and social stability, particularly during major events such as elections [2] and public health crises [3]. Fake news is defined as intentionally fabricated information that has been verified as false [4]. In many cases, it is difficult for ordinary people to identify fake news. Hence, there’s a pressing requirement for methods that can automatically evaluate the authenticity of news to identify misinformation and thus reduce or avert the hazards linked to its dissemination.
At the same time, multimodal news that incorporates images or videos can provide a more engaging and enhanced reading experience [5], and its content is more susceptible to malicious forgery (such as misunderstood or falsified images) [6], making it increasingly impractical to rely solely on a single model to handle various types of fake news. Current multimodal fake news primarily manifests in two forms: (1) Unimodal forgery: Where either text or image is maliciously altered individually, such as fabricated text (inventing events that never occurred) or manipulated images (forging visual evidence using techniques like Photoshop or Deepfake); (2) Cross-modal inconsistency: Where text and image exhibit semantic or logical contradictions, such as irrelevant image-text pairing (combining real images with false text) or logical conflict (text descriptions conveying information opposite to the image content). As shown in Fig. 1, example (a) has little evidence in the text to suggest that this is fake news, but the image is clearly forged. In example (b), the image appears normal, but the textual content indicates that it is fake news. In example (c), the textual modality expresses the idea that selling dog meat is illegal, but merely showing images of a sleeping dog and a baby does not illustrate ”dog meat” and ”illegal” actions. From this, we can draw a conclusion that multimodal fake news detection (MFND) not only needs to focus on multimodal content but also needs to pay attention to inter-modality inconsistencies. It is worth noting that the rise of AI-generated content (AIGC) (e.g., deepfake text/images) has introduced new challenges to fake news detection. However, at the current stage, user-generated content (UGC)-based misinformation remains dominant, characterized by a larger base and more diverse dissemination pathways, resulting in greater harm. Given the quantitative dominance of UGC misinformation, we focus on the latter to deepen the understanding of traditional misinformation propagation mechanisms.
Existing research on MFND is primarily divided into two categories. The first category mainly focuses on extracting and integrating news text and image information for fake news detection (FND). For instance, Zhou et al. [7] initially extract textual and visual features from news, then construct aligned attention interactions to study the relationships between cross-modal extracted features. However, the model only fuses embeddings through simple feature concatenation, failing to fully leverage multimodal information. Wu et al. [8] have developed a model known as MCAN to detect fake news. This model enhances the accuracy of identifying fake news by examining the relationships between spatial and frequency domains of images and its accompanying textual content. With the rise of pre-trained models like BERT [9], XLNet [10] and ViT [11], researchers can now significantly enhance the extraction of text and image features simply by fine-tuning these models, without the need for additional data. As a result, many researchers use these models to extract features and then combine them through direct concatenation. For example, Singhal et al. [12] used BERT combined with contextual information for textual feature extraction and VGG-19 [41] for image feature extraction, with feature concatenation employed for FND. The second category mainly focuses on detecting inconsistencies between text and images. Choi et al. [13] were the first to propose using Contrastive Language-Image Pretraining (CLIP) to capture the degree of semantic inconsistency in multimodal relationships, providing a new perspective to address online fake news and misinformation. Jiang et al. [14] introduced a similarity-aware fusion method, using cosine similarity generated by CLIP to adjust the strength of fusing multimodal features. Compared to the first category of methods, which focus on mining intra-modal knowledge within news articles, the second category of methods places more emphasis on mining inter-modal inconsistency information. However, these methods still have the following limitations.
Firstly, with the advancement of technology, recent fake news is often meticulously crafted. To fully discern the authenticity of news, it is not sufficient to rely solely on parts of the news information. For instance, Qi et al. [15] were the first to utilize both the frequency and spatial domains of news images for detection, which can accurately identify fake news as shown in Figure 1 (a), but cannot accurately identify text-image inconsistency fake news like the example (c). At the same time, Zaeem et al. [16] observed a statistically significant relationship between negative sentiment and fake news. Consequently, researchers began to use sentiment knowledge to assist in FND. Zhang et al. [17] started to extract emotional features from news text and news comments for FND. However, these methods typically use limited, single-source information for detection, lacking comprehensive data integration. Secondly, irrelevant noise present in multi-source information can severely affect the performance of FND. Most existing methods integrate embeddings through simple operations(e.g., concatenation), which may include redundant features, resulting in wasted computational resources and potentially ineffective, noisy cross-modal embeddings [18].
To address the aforementioned limitations, our main focus is on designing a more effective fusion method to enhance inter-modality information complementarity, while achieving more accurate and interpretable detection results through fully leveraging multi-source multi-dimensional cross-modal clues for fine-grained feature extraction and cross-modal semantic association learning. Specifically, we propose a multi-dimensional feature collaborative fusion (MFCF) model for determining the authenticity of multimodal news, which first constructs two branch networks to learn news content knowledge, including image frequency and spatial domain features, as well as text semantics and sentiment features. A cross-modal co-attention fusion (CCF) module is also designed, which effectively eliminates noise in multi-source data through dynamic and context-aware feature fusion strategies, thereby extracting key information for FND. Furthermore, we have designed a CLIP-based cross-modal consistency learning branch to enhance the model’s ability to detect consistency between text and images. The main contributions of this paper are summarized as follows: - We propose a novel MFND scheme, multi-dimensional feature collaborative fusion, which fully leverages various cross-modal clues to construct a multi-dimensional fine-grained feature representation system, effectively integrating multi-source news information.

- A novel cross-modal co-attention fusion module is proposed, achieving dynamic and context-aware fine-grained feature fusion, optimizing the model’s processing of multimodal data.

- Comprehensive experiments were conducted on three widely used datasets-Weibo, Twitter and GossipCop. The experimental results show that compared with current mainstream fake news detection methods, MFCF has achieved the best results in terms of accuracy.

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows: Section 2 systematically reviews multimodal fake news detection methods and the application of CLIP in fake news detection, with a focus on analyzing the limitations of existing work in extracting and fusing various cross-modal clues. Section 3 details the MFCF framework, including multi-dimensional feature extraction, cross-modal feature fusion, CLIP-based consistency learning and multi-feature fusion strategies. Section 4 evaluates model performance through comparative experiments, ablation studies and visualizations, while discussing the contributions of each module as well as the model’s limitations and future work. Section 5 summarizes the advantages of the proposed method.
Related work
Multimodal fake news detection
Intuitively, any form of falsification in a single modality can lead to discordance among modalities. Thus, the similarity between text and other modalities serves as an effective clue for assessing the credibility of news articles. Based on this, Zhou et al. [7] developed a MFND framework called SAFE, which assesses the relevance between news text and visual content using an advanced cosine similarity metric. However, as shown in Table 1, it only fuses embeddings through simple feature concatenation, failing to fully utilize multimodal information. Similarly, Xue et al. [19] developed a multimodal consistency neural network (MCNN) that utilizes a similarity assessment component to evaluate the consistency across various data modalities. Additionally, it employs an error level analysis (ELA) technique to detect the physical characteristics of the visual content, which helps identify potential incongruities between the text and images. Additionally, Xiong et al. [20] controlled the fusion strength of multimodal features based on the semantic consistency between text and images, thereby reducing noise in the fused features. However, the relationship between cross-modal consistency and the authenticity of news remains an unresolved issue. Nevertheless, the above works are all based on the static correlation assumption, overlooking detection blind spots in unimodal forgery scenarios: when only text/images are manipulated, cross-modal consistency may maintain a pseudo-high value, failing to clarify how cross-modal consistency relates to news authenticity.
On the other hand, Chen et al. [21] have crafted a framework known as CAFE, which stands for cross-modal ambiguity learning, for the dynamic integration of both single-modal and multimodal features. Within this framework, when there is a higher degree of ambiguity, the emphasis is on single-modal features; conversely, when the degree of ambiguity is lower, the multimodal features become more significant. However, CAFE severely penalizes single-modal features in cases of high consistency, which may be questionable in practical scenarios. Similarly, LIIMR [22] identifies and suppresses information from weaker modalities and extracts relevant information from stronger modalities for each sample. Furthermore, Ying et al. [23] separate information in single-modal and multimodal features through single-view prediction and cross-modal consistency learning, followed by adaptive re-weighting and guidance to achieve improved detection performance. However, in Ying et al. [23], the use of spatial and frequency domain image features for FND is conducted independently, which contrasts with the finding in Qi et al. [15] that the spatial domain plays a critical role in FND while the frequency domain is supplementary. Additionally, the model is highly dependent on text data, leading to a significant performance drop when text data is missing. In contrast, our model employs a multi-dimensional feature collaborative fusion approach, integrating image pixel and frequency domain features with text semantic and emotional features. This approach significantly enhances detection accuracy and robustness, maintaining high performance even when text information is incomplete or missing. Table 1. CLIP in fake news detection
In 2021, Radford et al. [24] introduced the CLIP model, a dual-tower architecture trained on large-scale noisy datasets, which employs contrastive loss to align visual and textual representations, thereby learning the matching relationship between text-image pairs. Due to its exceptional performance, CLIP has started to be applied in the field of FND. Zhou et al. [25] proposed the FND-CLIP model, which utilizes ResNet [26] and BERT [9]-based encoders along with two paired CLIP encoders to extract feature representations from both images and text. Hua et al. [27] developed a BERT-based reverse translation text and whole-image MFND model through contrastive learning and data augmentation. Inspired by the work of Zhou and Hua, we have incorporated a cross-modal consistency learning auxiliary task to address the synchronization issue in multimodal data. This involves using a CLIP-based encoder to learn the semantic correlations between text and images in mixed datasets, optimized with cosine embedding loss [42].
Proposed method
This paper presents a MFCF network for MFND, as illustrated in Fig. 2. The proposed method consists of four stages: multi-dimensional feature extraction, cross-modal feature fusion, CLIP-based consistency learning and multi-feature fusion. The first two stages are further divided into image and text branches. [See PDF for image]
Fig. 2 Architecture of MFCF. The image branch extracts image tampering and semantic feature representations from the frequency and spatial domains, respectively, while the text branch obtains corresponding feature representations from semantic and sentiment perspectives. Cross-modal collaborative fusion, single-modal prediction, and CLIP-based consistency learning are combined to guide the attention mechanism-based multi-feature fusion prediction


Specifically, the multimodal news input is represented as N = [ I , T ] ∈ D , where I , T and D denote the image, text and dataset, respectively. Initially, features are extracted by using four separate modules from the image’s frequency and spatial domains, as well as from the textual content and sentiment. Subsequently, single-modal fake news is identified through a cross-modal collaborative attention fusion mechanism. Finally, an integrated prediction of multi-features is achieved by employing an attention-weighted matrix. CLIP is employed for cross-modal consistency learning tasks, while computing text-image semantic similarity to guide the strength of text-image semantic fusion. The model produces an output set Y = { 0 , 1 } , with Y = 0 signifying that the news is fake and Y = 1 indicating that it is true. Further sections will delve into the specifics of the individual modules and the training processes.
Multi-dimensional feature extraction
Image branche
Frequency domain feature extraction : Research findings indicate that fake news images often undergo malicious editing or multiple recompression during dissemination [19]. Unlike methods directly converting images to frequency domain via discrete cosine transform (DCT) [15] or discrete Fourier transform (DFT) [28], we employ ELA [29] to highlight tampering traces and recompression artifacts, effectively identifying digitally manipulated images. As shown in Fig. 3, under the same color contrast conditions, the contrast of the player sections in (d) is significantly higher than that of the surrounding environment, indicating more processing in those regions. Additionally, images subjected to recompression and their originals exhibit different features after ELA transformation, as evidenced in (b), (e) and (f). [See PDF for image]

During the sentiment feature extraction phase, we employ the mBART-50 [32] to convert Chinese Weibo text into English before applying SenticNet for sentiment analysis. We first construct a sentiment knowledge graph for the text T , then apply a graph embedding method to obtain the final sentiment features.
To construct the sentiment knowledge graph, the original text is first preprocessed to retain only words containing letters and digits. The graph is denoted as G = ( V , E , H ) , where V and E represent the sets of sentiment nodes and edges, and H is the feature set for each node. V 1 represents words from the original text, and V 2 represents external sentiment knowledge from SenticNet, V 1 ∪ V 2 = V . Each node v i in the graph has an associated vocabulary embedding vector h i , extracted by BERT [9].
The adjacency matrix A is an n × n matrix, where n denotes the total number of nodes. It is assumed that an edge e ij exists between the lexical node v i in the original news text and the node v j within SenticNet. The weight w ij of this edge is proportional to the frequency of co-occurrence of the two words. Each collaborative attention layer contains two parallel collaborative attention blocks, enabling interactive learning between different modalities. Fusion of text and image features

The processing of image feature fusion and text feature fusion is similar to that of text-image semantic feature fusion. Considering the dominant role of spatial domain image semantic information in FND [15], we use CCF module to obtain image fusion features h M E ↔ I that are enhanced with spatial domain information. Similarly, in the text branch, we lead with textual semantic information and use the CCF module to obtain text fusion features h S A ↔ T that are enhanced with textual sentiment knowledge.
CLIP-based cross-modal consistency learning
Cross-modal consistency learning
Real news exhibits consistency between text and image content, while fake news may have mismatched text and image content. To address this, we have designed an auxiliary task for cross-modal consistency learning. First, we define the semantic relevance of text-image pairs from the same real news as positive and mark it as y ′ = 1 , and y ′ = 0 otherwise. Next, we randomly select pairs of text and images, comprising both positive and negative examples, from the original dataset D to construct a synthetic dataset D ′ . This is done to facilitate supplementary learning for cross-modal consistency. Additionally, when synthesizing negative samples, we ensure a 1:1 ratio between negative and positive samples to balance the training data.
CLIP is capable of discerning semantic connections that extend beyond mere emotional cues or noise patterns. Consequently, to effectively integrate image and text features, we deploy CLIP to independently harvest semantic features from both visual and textual data. Input N ′ = [ I ′ , T ′ ] ∈ D ′ . Extract text features h t and image features h i through CLIP, then input them into MLPs and a modality-shared layer to learn shared semantic embeddings h t ′ and h v ′ . Next, the combined embeddings are input into an average pooling layer, succeeded by a fully connected layer for binary classification. The entire module is trained with a cosine embedding loss with margin d, together with positive and negative pairs, as specified in Eq. (16): 16
L c = 1 - cos ( h t ′ , h v ′ ) , y ′ = 1 max ( 0 , cos ( h t ′ , h v ′ ) - d ) , y ′ = 0 Where cos ( ) denotes the normalized cosine similarity, with a threshold value of d set to 0.2. The objective is to enhance the cosine similarity between text and image pairs while minimizing the similarity between mismatched pairs, until the specified margin is reached.
Cross-modal consistency learning is designed to implicitly constrain the alignment between textual and visual representations, thereby optimizing the multimodal representation space to reduce feature conflicts and enhance model robustness.

Classifier
The image fusion feature h M E ↔ I , the text fusion feature h T ↔ S A and the multi-feature fusion feature h Fusion are used to detect fake news respectively, and the binary cross-entropy loss is applied. This results in classification losses L Image , L Text and L Fusion . The binary cross-entropy loss is shown below. 21
L BCE ( x , x ^ ) = x log ( x ^ ) + ( 1 - x ) log ( 1 - x ^ ) Here, x represent the ground truth for the samples, with 0 indicating a prediction of fake news and 1 indicating a prediction of real news, while x ^ represent the predicted scores for the samples.

L = L Fusion + α · ( ( L Image + L Text ) / 2 ) + β · L c In which, α and β are learnable hyperparameters.
Experiments
Datasets
The Weibo [33], Twitter and GossipCop datasets were employed for training and testing. Statistics of the processed datasets are summarized in Table 2. The Weibo dataset includes verified real news from sources such as Xinhua News Agency, as well as fake news from Weibo’s official debunking platform. The training set contains 3,749 real news items and 3,783 fake news items, with a test set of 1,996 news items. The Twitter dataset for the Verifying Multimedia Use task at MediaEval [34] contains posts with text, videos and social context. Therefore, we filtered out tweets containing videos and non-English tweets. The filtered training set contains 4,031 real news items and 5,130 fake news items, while the test set contains 1,406 tweets. And the GossipCop dataset is a multimodal news dataset sourced from the entertainment domain within the FakeNewsNet [35]. This dataset includes 10,010 training news items (7,974 real and 2,036 fake news items), with a test set of 2,285 real and 545 fake news items. We trained the MFCF model five times on each dataset with different initial weights and finally reported the average results and standard deviations. Table 2. Experimental dataset statistics

Experiment settings
All modules were implemented using PyTorch and executed on a single NVIDIA GeForce RTX 3090 GPU with 24GB of memory. For the image branch, the input size was set to 224 × 224 pixels. The visual tampering feature extraction module utilized a ResNet50 model, while the visual semantic extraction module employed the “mae-pretrain-vit-base” model. In the text branch, the maximum text length was set to 197, with Chinese data processed using the “bert-base-chinese” model and English data processed using the “bert-base-uncased” model. Both the MAE and BERT hidden layers were sized at 768 and were kept frozen during training. For the consistency learning module, semantic features from text and images in the synthesized dataset were extracted using CLIP (ViT-B-32) model’s text and image encoders. The two MLPs each consisted of one hidden layer, one BatchNorm1D layer and a ReLU activation function, the margin d for the cosine embedding loss was tuned within the range of 0 ∼ 1 at intervals of 0.1 and was ultimately set to 0.2. The model was trained for 50 epochs. We selected the optimal parameter configuration based on model performance, with a batch size of 24, using the Adam optimizer with default parameters to optimize the objective function, learning rate is 1 × 10 - 4 and Cosine Annealing Warm Restarts was employed as the learning rate scheduler. The MFCF model is designed to be compatible with unimodal news data, implemented as follows: (1) When the input lacks an image, a zero matrix is used as substitution; (2) When the input lacks text, ”No text provided” is used as a placeholder. Additionally, the hyperparameters are set to α = 2 , β = 0.5 , and L = 2 .
Baselines

- VQA [36] entails crafting natural language replies to queries about images. We adapted it by swapping the multi-class classifier for a binary one, transforming it into a binary classification problem.

- MVAE [6] uses a multimodal VAE, with text processed by BiLSTM and images by VGG-19. The features are combined, encoded, and used for reconstruction and fake news detection.

- SAFE [7] employs VGG-19 for images and Text-CNN for text to extract features, measuring their similarity via cosine similarity, and combines these for fake news classification.

- SpotFake [12] leverages BERT for contextual text features and VGG-19 for image features, merging them for classification in fake news detection.

- SpotFake+ [37], unlike SpotFake, uses XLNet and VGG to extract features from text and images.

- HMCAN [38] introduces a hierarchical multimodal contextual attention network that enhances multimodal comprehension through complementary data, with BERT capturing text’s hierarchical semantics.

- CAFE [21] quantifies ambiguity between text and images using KL divergence. It adaptively aggregates cross-modal and unimodal features based on learned fuzzy scores for fake news detection.

- BMR [23] adaptively reweights and guides the predictions of unimodal and multimodal for fake news detection.

- FND-CLIP [25] employs two unimodal encoders and two pairwise CLIP encoders to extract deep representations from news content, and utilizes a modal attention module to aggregate features.

- FSRU [39] extracts spectral features from news images and text, performing multimodal rumor detection in the frequency domain.

The results in Table 3 reveal that MFCF demonstrates superior performance in fake news detection across all three real-world datasets, achieving the highest accuracies of 93.1%, 93.5% and 90.1%, respectively. In comparison, other models show relatively weaker performance. A detailed analysis is as follows:(1) Some models, such as VQA, MVAE, SpotFake and SpotFake+, although attempting to integrate multimodal information, are not efficient in feature extraction, merely connecting text and visual representations in a simplistic manner. This leads to insufficient cross-modal interaction and fusion, thereby affecting detection performance. The experimental results of HMCAN prove the effectiveness of the Transformer-based multimodal context attention fusion network, but it neglects to provide coarse-grained information that offers global semantics; (2) SAFE learns the similarity between text and vision but may identify relevant real posts as fake news due to ignoring ambiguity. CAFE defines and utilizes cross-modal ambiguity to alleviate inconsistencies between different modalities, but prioritizing cross-modal consistency does not have benefits, and consistency only plays an auxiliary role in fake news detection. In contrast, BMR employs a multi-perspective representation weighted guidance scheme and uses cross-modal consistency learning to implicitly guide the refinement of multimodal representations, achieving better experimental results. However, it is highly dependent on textual data, and its performance significantly declines when textual data is missing. (3) FND-CLIP leverages the pre-trained CLIP to effectively capture cross-modal correlations, addressing the inconsistency in semantic spaces of unimodal features inherent in traditional methods. However, when image-text relevance is low, the weights of fused features are suppressed, causing the model to over-rely on unimodal features and consequently impairing detection performance. (4) FSRU effectively explores and integrates multimodal features within the frequency domain, bridging the spatial and frequency domains with Fourier transformation, but its effectiveness is somewhat diminished when applied to the Weibo and GossipCop datasets. (5) The GossipCop dataset exhibits a significant data imbalance, with the ratio of real news to fake news exceeding 3:1. Moreover, we observed that nearly 50% of the images in this dataset are close-up shots of celebrities’ faces, which are difficult to distinguish even for human observers. As a result, MFCF primarily relies on textual features for decision-making in GossipCop, leading to relatively limited improvement.

Contribution of different feature dimensions : To evaluate the impact of varying feature aspects on the MFCF model’s effectiveness, we contrasted the outcomes from MFCF models stripped of certain elements-ME, V, T, SA, V & T and ME & SA-with those of the full MFCF model. The findings reveal that the MFCF’s efficacy is substantially reduced in the absence of either the visual or textual components, highlighting the essential role of these elements in achieving the model’s optimal outcome. In contrast, the removal of image frequency domain features and text sentiment features results in only a slight performance decrease, indicating that these features play a supportive role in the FND task. When both image spatial-domain and text semantic features are absent, MFCF’s detection accuracy decreases by 14.2%, 15.4%, and 6.0% respectively; whereas when both image frequency-domain and text sentiment features are missing, the accuracy drops by 2.1%, 2.8% and 3.3%, respectively. Through in-depth analysis, we discovered asymmetric interdependencies among image spatial features, frequency features, text sentiment features, and semantic features. These four components provide complementary evidence from content, technical, and linguistic dimensions, covering more comprehensive detection scenarios.
Effectiveness of each component : We compared MFCF with variants lacking CL, CCF and multi-feature fusion, as shown in Table 5. (1) The CLIP model aids in identifying semantic inconsistencies between text and images. The complete MFCF model outperforms the variant without CLIP, demonstrating the effectiveness of incorporating cross-modal consistency learning as an auxiliary task. (2) The CCF component is engineered to strengthen the profound interplay and amalgamation of diverse modalities. Its efficacy is demonstrated by the heightened efficacy of the comprehensive model in contrast to its counterpart devoid of the CCF element, underscoring the advantages this module confers for the amalgamation of features. (3) The complete MFCF model significantly surpasses the variant without multi-feature fusion, indicating that attention-based multi-feature weighted fusion dynamically adjusts the weights of different features. This adjustment helps emphasize key features. The removal of this module likely increases interference from noisy features, adversely affecting detection performance.

Limitations and future work
This study has several limitations. First, the MFCF framework primarily focuses on accuracy metrics without considering model lightweighting. Second, detecting certain types of misinformation (e.g., semantically misleading combinations of authentic images and text) requires external knowledge bases or fact-checking mechanisms, which exceeds the technical boundaries of multimodal content analysis. We propose exploring the integration of our model with knowledge-enhanced modules and fact-checking components to address such complex scenarios. Third, further investigation is needed into MFND methods incorporating diverse modalities (e.g., news entities and social networks).
The rise of AI-generated content (AIGC) is spawning novel forms of deepfake disinformation. Future work will explore integrating MFCF with generated content detectors. Additionally, incorporating the semantic reasoning capabilities of multimodal large language models (MLLMs) could address the prevalent ”black box” decision-making dilemma in fake news detection while performing detection tasks. Finally, to mitigate semantic distortion risks and emotional intensity attenuation in cross-lingual sentiment feature extraction, we plan to construct a Sino-English cross-modal sentiment mapping matrix. This will involve screening concept sets with high cross-lingual emotional consistency from SenticNet through semantic space mapping, followed by culture-specific calibration using Chinese sentiment lexicon.
Conclusion
To address the limitations of existing multimodal detection methods, we propose a multi-dimensional feature collaborative fusion model. Initially, image frequency domain and spatial domain features, along with textual semantic and sentiment features, are extracted separately. These features are then deeply integrated through a cross-modal coordinated attention fusion module to predict fake news within individual modalities. Following this, a multi-feature fusion prediction is performed using the combined features and similarity-aware text-image semantic fusion features. Additionally, we introduce a CLIP-based cross-modal consistency learning auxiliary task. The final fake news detection is completed by weighting the aforementioned prediction scores. We conducted extensive experiments on three renowned MFND datasets to evaluate the performance of MFCF against various multimodal methods, and the outcomes indicate that MFCF outperforms in multiple aspects.
