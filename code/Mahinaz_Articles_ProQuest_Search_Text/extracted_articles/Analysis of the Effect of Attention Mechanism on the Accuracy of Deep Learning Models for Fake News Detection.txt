1. Introduction In today’s age of digital communication, the dissemination of fake news accelerates faster than in the past, resulting in a severe societal problem. The rapid spread of disinformation poses a serious threat to society and to democracy. An important role in the fight against disinformation is played by its early detection and the regulation of its dissemination. Machine learning and artificial intelligence are thus increasingly gaining an important role in the fight against this phenomenon, as they offer tools for the early detection of fake news. Every year, machine learning and artificial intelligence methods are constantly improving, opening up new opportunities to effectively detect and combat fake news. Recent machine learning methods include deep learning and the attention mechanism, which are the focus points of this article.
This paper investigates the effectiveness of attention mechanisms in the task of fake news detection. The research is focused on comparative experiments by incorporating attention layers into several widely used deep learning models, including CNN, LSTM, BiLSTM, and GRU. The performance of these models—with and without attention—is evaluated on two benchmark datasets: LIAR and WELFake. Those results are finally compared with the results of transformer based on multi-head attention. The main contributions of the work are as follows: Verifying the hypothesis that the integration of attention mechanisms can improve the accuracy of fake news detection models;
Basic detection models training (CNN, LSTM, BiLSTM, GRU) on pre-processed data, optimizing the model parameters to achieve acceptable performance;
Extension of basic models by integration of the attention mechanism layers and consequently training and testing extended models the same data as basic models;
Comparison of test results of basic models, extended models, and transformer;
Conclusion, that an attention mechanism, especially multi-head attention, can enhance fake news detection models’ effectiveness when applied to high-quality, well-balanced datasets.

Fake News Phenomena
Fake news is defined as information that mimics the media’s presentation in its formal form but does not replicate their editorial processes or intentions and does not attempt to offer objective and truthful information. Fake news is classified as a sub-category of disinformation, which means that it provides false or misleading information about the state of the world. Its danger lies in the fact that it seriously damages the credibility of the traditional news media.
It is important to point out that there are views that suggest avoiding the use of the term ‘fake news’ due to its political abuse. Nevertheless, a better approach is to retain the term, both for its value as a stand-alone scientific concept and for its political relevance, which points to an important issue.
In addition to false information, fake news may also include satirical articles, parodies and other forms of humor. These forms may be aimed at entertainment and not at influencing public opinion. However, even when entertaining, these forms of entertainment can be misleading and can confuse people if they are not clearly labeled as satire or parody.
Fake news poses multiple threats that can impact society and individuals [1]: Influencing public opinion: Fake news can influence people’s opinions and attitudes on a wide variety of topics, including politics, health, environmental issues, and more. This can undermine democratic processes and polarize society.
Undermining trust in the media: The spread of fake news can undermine public trust in traditional media and journalists, which can have a negative impact on information and transparency.
Creating conflict and hatred: Fake news can spread hatred, discrimination, and conflict between different groups of people based on false information.
Economic consequences: Fake news can have economic consequences, for example, affecting stock prices, business performance, and consumer behavior.
Health risks: In the case of health topics, the spread of fake news can influence people’s decisions about necessary treatment, with potentially serious consequences.
Misuse of personal data: Fake news can be used to obtain personal data and misuse it for manipulation or fraud.

The two main heuristics that can influence the assessment of the veracity of information [2] are as follows: Familiarity heuristics: If something is said or written often, it may seem credible, regardless of the actual truth of the content.
Source credibility heuristic: This heuristic focuses on the evaluation of information based on the assessment of a source as reliable or unreliable. If information is presented through a credible source (e.g., a reputable media outlet), people have a propensity to regard it as true without further investigation.

These heuristics can be easily manipulated or misused. People can be influenced by frequent repetition of false information or by credible-looking sources that may not be reliable. In this way, reliance on heuristics can lead to errors in the assessment of the veracity of news and to the eventual spread of misinformation.
In the study [3], the main channels in which fake news can spread were the following: Negligence and automatic sharing: The discrepancy between what people believe and what they share on social media is largely due to negligence, not the intention to share misinformation. This suggests that individuals may not critically assess the accuracy of the information they encounter before sharing it, leading to the inadvertent dissemination of false or misleading content.
Impact of social media algorithms: Social media often use algorithms that prioritize content based on indicators such as likes, shares, and comments. This can create a feedback loop in which sensational or misleading content is spread more intensely.
Impact on public opinion: When misinformation is spread on social media, it can influence public opinion and attitudes, with potentially negative consequences such as influencing voters.
Potential for rapid dissemination: Social media provides a platform for the rapid dissemination of information. When false or misleading content is widely disseminated, it can have a large impact before corrections or fact-checking are implemented.

Research indicates that people are more likely to use the media for entertainment rather than for truth-seeking and prefer information that confirms their existing views. Research on fluency/ease of remembering information in politics shows that people tend to remember information according to the emotions it arouses in them, forgetting the context in which they encountered it. They are also more inclined to accept familiar information as true.
2. Related Works Detecting fake news is a challenging task because it often requires recognizing subtle differences in language and a style within a recognized context. Deep learning and the attention mechanism provide a powerful tool to model and understand these subtle differences. Their combination enables the development of sophisticated and accurate fake news detection models. The attention mechanism is a key building block of many successful machine learning models. Its ability to focus on relevant parts of the input data is extremely valuable in text document analysis. In the context of fake news detection, the attention mechanism can help identify characteristic patterns that reveal information manipulation. Deep learning and the attention mechanism are relatively new techniques that are gaining increasing popularity in the field of natural language processing. Their application in fake news detection could provide new insights on how to effectively limit the spread of fake news in online environments [4,5,6].
Many approaches to fake news detection focus on fact-checking and source credibility. These methods use available data and information to verify the veracity of claims contained in fake news. To this end, there are organizations that specialize in investigating and debunking fake news, such as FactCheck.org, Snopes.com, and others. The evolution of the fake news detection process is an ongoing process and requires constant updating and adaptation. Current technologies such as artificial intelligence and machine learning are providing new approaches and tools to detect fake news. At the same time, however, it is important to recognize that fake news detection is a complex problem, and it is not easy to achieve absolute accuracy. In any case, fake news detection is important in a digital age were misinformation spreads rapidly. The use of these methods, but also the collaboration between experts, researchers, and society, could be an effective tool in combating the spread of fake news and preserving information integrity [2,7].
In the study [8], a complete framework is presented that uncovers Twitter conspiracies. The authors defined an initial set of metrics that allow semi-automatic labeling of fake news. These metrics are based on popularity, activity, and basic textual analysis. The work uses Feature Vector Extraction to extract relevant features, alongside Classifier Conspiracy (a deep learning model for classification based on contextual metrics) and Framework Architecture with two main modules for data processing and content evaluation. In addition, the paper also describes the use of deep learning in text similarity analysis on Twitter and in conversations on BORJIS. It uses conversational sequences to exploit more complex semantic properties of text. The algorithm achieved an accuracy of 98.5%.
In [9], the FakeBERT (Fake Bidirectional Encoder Representations from Transformers) model was used for the detection of fake news and its effectiveness for the problem was demonstrated. Their model combines BERT (Bidirectional Encoder Representations from Transformers) with three parallel 1d-CNN blocks that use different convolutional layers using more filters to improve the learning. The model uses a pre-trained word embedding model BERT based on bidirectional transform coder. The results of the test show that FakeBERT achieved better results, with 98.90% accuracy. Its accuracy is higher than the results of models in related works with a real fake news dataset, which contains thousands of fake and real news from the 2016 US presidential election. The models were evaluated with various metrics such as accuracy, FPR, FNR, and cross-entropy.
In the study [10], they used a neural network model integrating the BERT language model and external sources to represent fake news content and source credibility. The integrated ECOL model achieved the highest achieved accuracy of 97.99% and F1-rate of 98.13, indicating its effectiveness in detecting fake news. The framework combines three parts: the content of the posts, their similarity to verified fake news, and the credibility of the source URLs. This combination allows a more accurate distinction between fake and real news.
The study [11] deals with fake news classification and performs a comparison between unimodal and multimodal detection based on deep learning techniques from related works. The multimodal approach outperforms unimodal ones that focus on textual data. In this paper, different models have been used for fake news detection including CNN, BiLSTM, BERT, and multimodal approaches. The multimodal approach achieved the best results, with an accuracy of 87% and a micro average F1-rate of 72%. The BERT model achieved an accuracy of 78% and a micro average F1-rate of 74%, which is better than the other unimodal approaches. Moreover, using dynamic word vector, GloVe outperforms random initialization in CNN and BiLSTM architectures. A multimodal approach was used in [12] as well. It proposed a fake news detection model utilizing the correlations between different modalities. The method first uses BERT and BLIP-2 encoders to extract text and image features. Then, it employs the attention mechanism to process the input text and image sequences, enhancing the important aspects of the bimodal features.
In [13], a fake news detection system is presented that uses two approaches: classical machine learning and deep learning. In the deep learning framework, a model called OPCNN-FAKE was proposed and achieved the best performance. The model was optimized using hyperparameter optimization technique. TF-ID feature extraction and n-gram representations were used for classic machine learning and word embedding for deep learning. The OPCNN-FAKE model was compared with RNN, LSTM, and six classical machine learning methods: decision trees, linear regression, KNN, random forest, SVM, and Naive Bayes. This comparison experiment was performed on four different datasets containing fake news. In the testing, the OPCNN-FAKE model achieved 97.84% accuracy, 97.86% precision, 97.84% recall, and 97.89% F1-rate.
In [5], a convolutional network with long short-term memory and an attention mechanism was proposed for automatic fake news detection using the LIAR dataset, which contained authentic and original short statements from different speakers in different contexts. In the first step, the LIAR dataset was pre-processed. In the next step, word vectors were generated for the input data using pre-trained word embeddings such as GloVe. Finally, an AC-BiLSTM model was proposed that uses these word vectors to predict fake news across multiple classes. The model can capture the local, global, and temporal meaning of a text using C-BiLSTM and uses an attention mechanism to process longer input sequences. It is further reported that the presented hybrid model achieved an accuracy of 35.1% and a micro F1-rate of 39%, showing the difficulty of obtaining good results with training on the used LIAR dataset.
In [14], experiments have been performed with transformer-based and sequential models. Experiments were performed on a corpus of texts about COVID-19, and the learned models were fine-tuned using transformers. It was found that pre-training a model on a large general dataset and then retraining the model for the specific task achieves the best results in accuracy. The transformer-based models outperform the baseline models in terms of accuracy by up to 3–4%. The maximum accuracy achieved was 98.41% when the BERT language model was pre-trained, compared to the result of the baseline model, which achieved an accuracy of only 93.32%. This research demonstrated the importance of pre-training in achieving high performance in deep learning.
In [6], fake news detection models based on deep neural networks were successfully implemented for binary classification; specifically, an attention mechanism was combined with CNN, LSTM, and BiLSTM. The models were trained and tested on the LIAR dataset. The performance graphs during training clearly show that the performance of the trained neural network does not improve with the increasing number of training epochs. In this work, the CNN and LSTM models achieved the highest success rate of 56% and 57% accuracy using the attention mechanism. BiLSTM with the attention mechanism achieved a higher F1-rate of 93%.
The trend of employing LLMs (large language models) for fake news generation and detection is well represented and analyzed in [15]. It is a curated collection of many papers on misinformation research including decision making and explaining in the field of fake news.
An analysis of the state-of-the-art in the field of fake news detection using deep learning with the attention mechanism reveals various gaps that need to be addressed. One of the identified gaps is the lack of datasets of sufficient size and quality to train and evaluate complex fake news detection models. Existing datasets are often limited to specific domains or types of news and therefore need to be extended and made to account for the diversity of fake news. Another identified challenge is the need to improve the interpretability and explainability of deep fake news detection models. Although these models achieve high success rates, their decision making processes are often considered a “black box”, meaning that it is difficult to understand how they arrive at their output. Improving the interpretability of the models would enable confidence and understanding of their decisions, which is important for the credibility of detection systems.
The analysis of the current state of knowledge identified the scope for the design and development of new approaches and models using deep learning and attention mechanisms for fake news detection. One possibility is to further explore neural network architectures that better model the complex relationships between elements in text. For example, a combination of CNN and RNN could provide a powerful tool for analyzing word and phrase sequences. As an example, in [16], different structures based on BERT, CNN, BiGRU, and attention are investigated using the FakeNewsNet dataset. BERT is used to obtain deep contextualized representations, CNN is used to capture local features, and BiGRU is used to process in-formation from both left-to-right and right-to-left directions. The attention mechanism is applied to the output of BiGRU or CNN.
In addition, the effect of different variants of the attention mechanism, such as self-attention or multi-head attention, on the detection of fake news should be investigated. These mechanisms could help models to focus on key information and relationships in the text input and increase their ability to detect manipulative and misinformation elements.
Different approaches and strategies also need to be considered. In contrast to classification-based machine learning, in [17], the authors focused on analyzing fake news for discovering the exact elements. They combined natural language processing and latent semantic analysis (LSA) using singular value decomposition techniques to analyze fake news. In the study, they identified several concepts extracted from LSA that are representative of political fake news during the election.
It is also possible to consider such approaches as the combination of automated detection with human control, balancing the speed and accuracy of detection systems, and considering the content, context, source, and spread of fake news in the online environment [18]. In addition, it is also important to analyze the consequences of fake news detection and its impact on society. For example, how detection can contribute to fighting misinformation and the protection of the public interest.
3. Deep Learning of Neural Networks Based on the current state on fake news detection problem using neural networks, following methods were selected for detection models training: LSTM, BiLSTM, GRU, and CNN. We briefly describe the selected methods in Section 3.1 and Section 3.2. Although large language models are the most popular now, we have deliberately focused on less complex architectures to achieve our main objective.
Deep learning is characterized by using multi-layered neural networks to extract and transform data to achieve successful solutions to tasks such as text, image, and speech analysis, and many more. Deep learning models are based on neural networks that are designed to be able to automatically learn representations of input data with different levels of abstraction and hierarchy. These networks are typically very deep, which means that they contain many layers.
3.1. Convolutional Neural Network (CNN) CNN is a special type of neural network that has been developed to efficiently process structured data, especially images. The basic principle of CNN is convolution, where a small filter, also called a kernel, passes through the input data and performs a convolution operation in convolutional layers. This procedure allows the network to detect patterns and structures in images. The idea was further adapted for the NLP (Natural Language Processing) tasks. With text, the 1D convolution is used. In 1D convolution, the filter moves only in one direction, while going through the entire input text.
CNNs are a class of neural networks which have left their mark in computer vision, but thanks to their capabilities, they have also been used in natural language processing [19]. Starting with the convolutional layer followed by either pooling layer, and continuing with their repetition, the network ends with the last fully connected layer. The complexity of CNN grows with each additional layer, thus being able to identify larger portions of the text data for natural language processing tasks solving [20].
3.2. Reccurent Neural Network (RNN) RNN is a type of neural network capable of handling sequential data and remembering previous states of input. RNN is derived from differential equations and implements the backward numerical integration method. The way RNN works is that at each step a new network state is calculated based on the current input and the previous state. This new state is then used as the input for the next network step. RNN, while a powerful tool, also has several drawbacks. These networks tend to lose information about very long dependencies in sequences (long sentences). The problems relate to gradients vanishing during training. This problem interferes with the stability and reliability of RNN training [21].
Long short-term memory (LSTM) is a very successful type of RNN designed to solve the traditional RNN problems with vanishing gradient. The main benefit of LSTM is the ability to store information and work efficiently with long sequences of input. The LSTM network achieves this capability by using special structures called “gates” (Forget, Input, and Output) that allow the network to decide which information to remember and which to forget. However, despite these advantages, LSTM has disadvantages such as higher computational complexity compared to standard RNN and the need for more data resources for training. Another disadvantage is the greater susceptibility to overtraining and so losing the ability to generalize to new patterns [21]. Bidirectional long short-term memory (BiLSTM) can capture information from both directions (start-to-end and end-to-start). This means that BiLSTM has two sets of hidden states. This ability to capture information from both directions allows BiLSTM to better capture context and long-term dependencies in sequences, which can lead to better results for tasks such as detecting fake news in texts [22].
The Gated Recurrent Unit (GRU) is a simplified LSTM that has two gates: an update gate and a forgetting gate. The updating gate controls how much of the previous memory is to be retained and how much information from new input is to be added, while the forgetting gate determines how much past information is to be forgotten. Compared to LSTM, GRU contains fewer parameters, which can lead to faster learning and less risk of overlearning, and it also has lower computational complexity, making it more suitable for applications where faster training times are required or limited computational resources are available [23].
3.3. Attention Mechanism (AM) AM is a method of deep learning that helps a model to focus on parts of the input data which are important for solving tasks. It is inspired by the cognitive function of human attention, which allows humans to quickly select desired information from text [24]. The attention mechanism works by first computing an attention distribution on the input data and then computing a context vector based on this distribution. The attention distribution is generated by encoding the input data as keys in relation to a representation of the vector, called a query, related to the solved problem. The neural network then computes the correlation between the queries and the keys in the form of an energy score function—its value represents the importance of the keys with relation to the queries in processing the next output. The context vector is then computed based on this distribution to represent a weighted sum of the key vectors, with the weights determined by the attention distribution. Thus, the attention mechanism works in two steps: Calculating the attention distribution . First, the network encodes the features of the input data as K -keys. The key can be represented in different shapes depending on given tasks and specific neural network architecture. In addition, a representation matrix, Q -query, is created for the solved task. The neural network then computes a score function to obtain an energy score, which represents the relation between queries’ importance and keys. The energy score is used in the generation of the next output.
Context vector calculation . Based on the known attention distribution, a context vector is computed which represents the weighted sum of the key vectors, where the weights are determined by the attention distribution. This allows the neural network to focus on the relevant parts of the input and ignore irrelevant ones [25].

The attention score value is calculated as the scalar multiplication of the query and the key vectors. The obtained value is further square rooted by the cardinality of the key vectors and passed through the SoftMax function. Thus, the attention weights are obtained and used for the weighted sum of the value vectors calculation. The d k is the dimension of key vectors. Additive attention is more expressive and allows the model to learn complex interactions, while multiplicative attention is computationally efficient and widely used in sequence modeling. 3.4. Transformer First proposed by Google in 2017 [27], the transformer architecture has redefined standards in the field of NLP by introducing the attention mechanism. The introduction of transformer architecture is considered as a watershed moment for the development of LLMs. With the use of parallel computing accompanied by processing of sequences, the introduction of transformers led to significant improvements in training and processing times. This led to the dawn of large language models (LLM), notable examples being GPT or BERT, which can learn complex language representations. These models contain billions of parameters. The transformer architecture comprises an encoder and decoder. The advantage of transformers lies in their ability to process input sequences in parallel, increasing and speeding up the process of training and inference.
The main innovations of the transformer architecture are positional encoding and self-attention. Positional encoding is used to provide positional information to the model through including position-dependent markings to embeddings of words, allowing the model to incorporate the order of words in the input sequence. The self-attention, or attention, mechanism is a technique which enables the model to focus on specific parts of input sequence during the creation of output sequence. There are multiple types of attention mechanisms which are used in transformer models, such as scaled dot-product attention, multi-head attention, self-attention, encoder–decoder attention, and masked self-attention. Scaled dot-product attention (SDPA) is an important part of the AM of the transformer. It includes three main components: queries ( Q ), keys ( K ), and values ( V ). The attention score value is calculated as the scalar multiplication of the query and the key vectors. The obtained value is further square rooted by the cardinality of the key vectors. This score is then passed through a SoftMax function to obtain attention weights, which are used to compute a weighted sum of the value vectors.
The purpose of self-attention, also called intra-attention, is to enable a model to consider various positions of the same sequence when generating the word representation. It is present in both the encoder and decoder layers. Self-attention is a necessary component in enabling the model to find important dependencies and relationships in the long input sequence. Encoder–decoder attention (EDA), also called cross-attention, is used in the decoder layers of the transformer. It enables the decoder to find the important parts of the input sequence during creation each word for the output sequence. This ensures that the decoder has access to the entire input text sequence. In the decoder, causal or masked self-attention (CSA or MSA) is used to ensure that the prediction in the current position depends only on the known outcomes in previous positions. This plays a significant role in tasks of NLP, where it is best that future tokens are not known while training the model. To prohibit the model from looking ahead, the attention scores for future tokens are obfuscated. In transformer model, multiple transformer blocks are stacked together, each of them having two main components: a multi-head attention mechanism and position-wise feed-forward neural network.
Depending on their architecture and purpose, transformers have evolved into a diverse family of architectures, some of which are bidirectional transformers (BERT), generative pretrained transformers (GPT), or vision transformers (ViT).

As a result of this analysis, the convolutional network and three more efficient recurrent networks LSTM, BiLSTM, GRU were chosen.

Based on the current state of the fake news detection problem using neural networks, the following methods were selected for detection model training: LSTM, BiLSTM, GRU, and CNN. Those methods were trained and tested first by themselves and in the second round, in combination with attention mechanisms. Those results were also compared to the efficiency of transformer, which naturally contains a multi-head attention mechanism. All models were trained on two datasets of fake news, LIAR and WELFake, and tuned based on their performance on the validation set. When they achieved the best results, they were tested on the test set.
The standard measures of efficiency, e.g., accuracy, precision, recall and F1-rate, were used as metrics to evaluate the models. Accuracy is a basic metric that expresses the percentage of correctly classified examples out of all examples in the test set. Precision measures what proportion of the examples classified as positive are truly positive according to expert labeling. The recall metric measures what proportion of true positive examples were correctly classified as positive. The F1-rate is a harmonic means of precision and recall, making it useful for situations where both performance metrics are important. Formula (3) describes the calculation of the F1-rate. 4.1. LIAR Dataset LIAR (Large-scale Imitative Fake News Dataset) was collected from PolitiFact.com platforms in the form of manually labeled short statements in various contexts for the purpose of fake news research. The dataset was originally introduced in [28] and is currently accessible from Hugging Face [29].
It contains more than 12,000 manually labeled examples of short texts, which were classified into one of six categories: True (16.4%);
Mostly True (19.2%);
Half True (20.6%);
Barely True (16.2%);
False (19.5%);
Pants on Fire (8.2%).

The LIAR dataset contains statements that were collected from political debates in the United States, more specifically during the 2016 presidential election, and are accompanied by metadata such as speaker identification, context of the statement, and time and location of the event, allowing for further study and analysis. This dataset is often used to train and test machine learning methods for generating models from text data to recognize the degree of truthfulness of texts. The dataset is divided into training, validation, and testing subsets, which are necessary for training neural networks.
In this dataset, at first, records with empty values were removed. The next step was to create binary classes, since the original dataset contained 6 classes. The classes barely true, false, and pants on fire were merged into one class labeled “0” (43,9%), and classes true, mostly true, and half true into a class labeled “1” (56.2%). The results are two classes that are balanced and thus it enables us to avoid several problems without any special treatment of class imbalance and limitations during model training. The standard text data pre-processing steps were performed: Conversion of characters to lowercase letters;
The removal of special characters and stop-words;
Tokenization;
Lemmatization;
Padding.

For this dataset, the GloVe (Global Vectors for Word Representation) embeddings were used to create a vector representation of words in texts. The GloVe embeddings were provided loaded a pre-trained GloVe Twitter file, which contains 200-dimensional vectors for words representations. These vectors were created using statistics from large corpora of texts from Twitter, which allowed us to capture specific linguistic features and context from this social media platform. After retrieving the GloVe embeddings, an embedding matrix was generated. This matrix has dimensions (the number of words in our vocabulary) × (the dimensions of the GloVe embeddings), where each word in our vocabulary has an associated vector from GloVe. If a word is not in the GloVe embeddings, its assigned vector in the matrix is zero. The benefits of using GloVe embeddings are considerable. They capture the meaning and context of words from Twitter text data. These representations can help us improve the performance of our model because they already have insight into the language and context used on Twitter. Using pre-trained embeddings also allows us to save time and resources that would otherwise be required to train vector representations of words from scratch. In this way, it was able to easily and efficiently exploit the rich linguistic information that was captured in the GloVe embeddings.
4.2. WELFake Dataset WELFake (Word Embedding over Linguistic Features for Fake News Detection) is another example of a dataset targeting fake news, containing 72,134 text data with false (48.6%) and true texts (51.4%). Since the numbers of examples are approximately the same for both classes, no special treatment of class imbalance was necessary.
This dataset was compiled from several popular datasets such as Kaggle, McIntire, Reuters, and BuzzFeed Political for the purpose of fake news research and recognition. The dataset was originally introduced in [30] and is currently accessible from Hugging Face [31].
It contains a wide range of fake information, including fake headlines, news, and information on social media. Unlike the LIAR dataset, this dataset contains a larger number of more extensive texts of news articles rather than short Twitter posts, which may bring new and interesting results. On the other hand, unlike the LIAR dataset, this data is not as widely used and validated.
In the pre-processing of the WELFake dataset, the standard data pre-processing procedures were performed: Changing characters to lowercase.
The removal of special characters: Special characters (e.g., punctuation characters) as well as digits were removed.
The removal of stop-words: A predefined list of English common words (typically not carrying much semantic weight) was used—the list was provided by the natural language toolkit NLTK.
Tokenization: A word tokenization method was used (based on white characters identification).
Lemmatization: Lemmatization was based on a Porter stemming algorithm provided by the natural language toolkit NLTK.
Padding: All list of tokens were padded to the same length.

Unlike the LIAR dataset, there was no need to modify the classes. The classes in this dataset are already binary and are also balanced. Finally, a transformation of the data into a form usable for training deep learning models was provided, i.e., a creation of vector representation of the text, which was achieved in the same way as for LIAR, and again, GloVe was used. But in this case, they were not pre-trained vectors of words from twitter but the vectors of regular words. After this pre-processing, the data was divided into training, test, and validation sets, where the training set constituted 65% of the total data, the test set constituted 20%, and the validation set 15%. The data was split randomly using the “train_test_split” function from the scikit-learn library, enabling the random split of a dataset into random two disjunctive subsets (e.g., train and test subsets or train and validation subsets). To use the same split into subsets for all tested network structures, the function was called “random_state”, enabling reproducible output across multiple function calls.
5. Basic Models of Neural Networks Training 5.1. LSTM Basic Model The LSTM on the LIAR dataset was the first trained model. The first layer of this model is the Input Layer, for transforming the input data into the model. This layer can receive datasets of different sizes. The next layer is the Embedding layer; it transforms the input words into vectors of fixed length, i.e., for each word, it generates a vector with a length of 200. The third layer is Dropout; this is a regularization technique used in training neural networks to minimize overtraining. Overtraining can occur when a model overfits the training data and loses its ability to generalize new, unseen data. Subsequently, the LSTM layer with the number of 128 memory cells was used. The other values (32, 64, and 256) were also tested, but the performance was not improved using them. After this layer, the Dropout layer was applied again to further reduce the possibility of overtraining the model. The last layer was the fully connected Dense layer, which generates the output value. This enters a sigmoidal function, which converts the output into a binary classification. The Output Layer has one neuron that represents the probability of belonging to a positive or negative class. The topology of the LSTM neural network is outlined in Table 1.
The model was trained in 50 epochs, using Early Stopping. The Early Stopping principle is based on monitoring the development of the model’s performance on the validation dataset during training. If the model’s performance on the validation data stops improving or starts to decrease, the model training is stopped, thus avoiding overtraining. In the Early Stopping function, there was a Patience parameter with value 3, which means that if the performance does not improve during the next 3 epochs, the training will stop and revert to the state 3 epochs before. Thanks to this, the training time of the models was significantly reduced and the optimal number of epochs for each model was found.
In this case, the model stopped training after 7 epochs, as illustrated in Figure A1 (Figure A1, Figure A2, Figure A3, Figure A4, Figure A5, Figure A6, Figure A7, Figure A8, Figure A9, Figure A10, Figure A11, Figure A12, Figure A13, Figure A14, Figure A15, Figure A16, Figure A17 and Figure A18 can be found in the Appendix A), since the error on the validation set did not decrease during next three epochs, but on the contrary increased, and the accuracy of the model on the validation set decreased. Learning was finished even though on the training dataset, the error decreased and the accuracy increased. Thanks to this, the learning avoided overtraining the model and saved time.
Then the same model LSTM was used to train on the WELFake dataset, in which it achieved better metric values and only needed to be trained over a few epochs. Even with the use of one dropout layer, this model achieved good results, but the accuracy on the validation set increased significantly compared to the model used on the LIAR dataset, so in this case it was not used early stopping but trained the model on WELFake data for 20 epochs (Figure A2).

5.4. CNN Basic Model This model was built on convolutional and fully interconnected layers of neural networks. It started with the Embedding layer, which was used to represent the words in the input data. In this case, the vector representation of each word had a size of 200 and was initialized using the pre-trained weights of the embedding matrix file. This was followed by a Dropout layer with a value of 0.4, which was used to control overfitting by randomly turning off approximately half of the outputs of each neuron in this layer. After the Dropout layer, a convolutional layer (Conv1D) was added, which was used to detect patterns in the input data. This convolutional layer had 128 filters with a kernel size of 3 and used the ReLU activation function to obtain nonlinearity. To reduce the size of the representations, the convolution was followed by a MaxPooling layer, which extracted the maximum value from each set of values from the convolution layer for each filter. This process helped to reduce the dimensionality of the data and extract the most important features. The MaxPooling layer was followed by the Flatten layer, which was used to convert the output of the convolutional layer into a one-dimensional vector so that they could be connected to the fully connected layers. Finally, a fully connected (Dense) layer with a single output neuron was added, which used a sigmoid activation function. This layer performed binary classification, where the output neuron predicted the probability of occurrence of class 1. When compiling the model, the loss function was defined as a binary cross-entropy, optimizing the algorithm Adam by a value of 0.0001 (1 × 10 −4 ). The topology of the CNN model of neural network is shown in Table 4.
The training process of the CNN model on the LIAR dataset is illustrated in Figure A7 and on WELFake dataset, it is illustrated in Figure A8.

6.1. LSTM Model with Attention Mechanizm Within this group of experiments, the LSTM network was first trained with an attention mechanism on LIAR data. The model contained attention layers. Using the layers, input values are dynamically weighted based on their importance, which is learned during model training. The attention layer consists of several steps. First, weights are computed that determine the importance of individual steps in sequence. Attention scores are then calculated using the tanh activation function and calculated weights. These scores are then normalized using a SoftMax function, which produces probability weights. Finally, a weighted average of the input values is calculated according to these weights, generating the output of the attention layer. Overall, this layer allows the model to selectively focus on relevant information of the input sequence. The question was whether the mere addition of the attention layer can positively or negatively affect the performance of the basic model. As with the basic LSTM model, the Early Stopping was used, thanks to which the optimal number of epochs was obtained. Compared to the basic model where the attention mechanism was not used, the model using the attention mechanism needed training for more epochs when using the LIAR dataset (Figure A9). The learning parameter used is 0.0001 (1 × 10 −4 ) and the optimizer was Adam. The topology of the LSTM neural network with the attention mechanism is presented in Table 5. There were 36,718,721 (140.07 MB) total parameters.
After adding the attention mechanism, the LSTM model was trained on the WELFake dataset in the same way as on the LIAR dataset, and a similar situation occurred. Therefore, the training was kept within 20 epochs but early stopping finished learning earlier (see Figure A10).
The difference was that the attention mechanism did not provide as much benefit when the LSTM model was trained on the LIAR dataset (values of nearly all metrics were less than 70%), but it was beneficial when model was trained on WELFake dataset (all metrics achieved values greater than 96%), as can be seen in Table 10.

7. Discussion With the use of the LIAR dataset, all the models achieved comparable results, without any of them being satisfactory. The highest achieved accuracy of 0.611 was with the LSTM model with the attention mechanism and the highest achieved precision was with BiLSTM, at 0.649. The highest achieved recall of 0.888 and F1-rate of 0.715 were achieved by the GRU model using the attention mechanism. Therefore, we ascertain that each model was unable to correctly detect fake news. The reason may be that this dataset tries to show the problem in detecting fake news in a too general manner, encompassing too wide a selection of topics while covering them in an insufficient number of samples. Another reason may be the character of the included posts from Twitter, which are short by their nature. This, accompanied by the volume of the data, contributes to the model being unable to find the relationships critical to the successful classification
The results of experiments with models combined with the attention mechanism on LIAR showed that the attention mechanism sometimes does not improve the results of the basic models as was assumed. The reason may be due to the dataset itself. The LIAR dataset contains 12,791 entries divided into six classes, depending on the level of truthfulness. The target classes range from the completely fake to the completely true. The statements are made in the range of 143 unique topics, meaning each topic contains about 89.5 statements on average in all six target classes. To make matters even more complicated, each statement has 10 additional associated features which aim at capturing additional info on the speaker. Since there are 2910 unique speakers in the dataset, each speaker has around 3.5 associated statements on average. Certain speakers are, however, represented more often than others in the dataset. While our goal was to assess the possibilities of fake news classification with and without the attention mechanism, we do believe that the LIAR dataset covers a wide range of topics, speakers, and information insufficiently, meaning that the underlying information for models to learn whether the particular statement is true or false is not present, thus leading to low performance, even in the case of models equipped with the attention mechanism.
Also, other studies from related works section represented weak results on LIAR dataset. For example, in [5], the proposed model C-BiLSTM and attention mechanism achieved an accuracy of 35.1% and a micro-F1-rate of 39%, showing the difficulty of obtaining good results in this dataset. In [6], CNN and LSTM models with an attention mechanism were trained on the LIAR dataset. The models achieved the highest success rate of 56% and 57% accuracy. Our model with attention layers achieved better results on the LIAR dataset than in the aforementioned studies. More specifically, LSTM achieved 61.1%, BiLSTM achieved 59.9% and CNN achieved 58.6% accuracy.
On the other hand, models with attention layers trained on data from the WELFake dataset had higher accuracy and the difference is up to 36.8% in comparison with LIAR training, when comparing the average values for all models. The BiLSTM model had the highest recall, 0.977; the GRU model had the highest precision, 0.975; and the CNN model had highest F1-rate, 0.973, and accuracy, 0.972. These results may be so good due to the fact that the WELFake data contain longer text articles and so the models are better able to find relations between words in these texts; also, they were not spread by ordinary internet users who think they are spreading the truth but probably by people who spread this news with the intention of deceiving people and so the texts may have a certain style and contain more information which makes models better able to identify whether it is fake news or not. Again, the attention mechanism did not have much effect on the results of models trained on LIAR, but it has significant effect on models trained on WELFake.
Since enriching the basic neural network models with the attention mechanism did not yield the assumed results on LIAR data, the transformer model was also trained—a model whose architecture is based on the attention mechanism and of which the attention mechanism is a fixed part. The results of the transformer model obtained on the test set are shown in Table 11 and the training process on the LIAR dataset is illustrated in Figure A17, while Figure A18 illustrates the training of transformer on the WELFake dataset.
The testing results of transformers (using multi-head attention) on LIAR data are comparable with the testing results of basic models (LSTM, BiLSTM, GRU, and CNN) and basic models with the attention mechanism and with multi-head attention, which can also be seen in Table 12. Table 12 presents the results of basic models in the first column (Basic Models (BM)). The second column (BM and Attention) contains the results of extended models—basic models with attention mechanism. We have also experimented with multi-head attention. The results of basic models extended with multi-head attention are presented in the third column (BM and multi-head attention). The transformer is also based on multi-head attention. The table contains only values of F1-rate for more transparent comparison. Also, the results of experiments with multi-head attention applied on basic models are provided (last column in Table 12) for better comparison with transformer results, since one of the key components of the transformer block is multi-head attention.
When applied to the WELFake dataset, the transformer did produce comparable results, on average, to the basic models with attention. But it was worse than basic models with multi-head attention. Thus, it seems to make sense to enrich the basic neural network models with the attention mechanism. For the attention mechanism, the best results on LIAR data were achieved by the GRU model (F1 = 0.715) and for the multi-head attention, it was the BiSLTM model (F1 = 0.688). The best results on WELFake data were achieved by the BiLSTM model with the attention mechanism (F1 = 0.901) and for the multi-head attention, it was the CNN model (F1 = 0.973)—see Table 12.
The table highlights that improvement brought by adding multi-head attention to traditional architectures (convolution or recurrent) strongly depends on the way the dataset is processed—it can be significant, as for the WELFake dataset, but also quite limited, as in case of the LIAR dataset.
In the future, it may be beneficial to experiment with multi-self-attention and include sentiment analysis in the fake news detection methodology [32]. Another possibility to improve the model’s efficiency is to focus on the issue of overfitting when the model has insufficient results in the recognition fake news that were not presented during the training. The paper [33] proposes a solution to this problem—an innovative method for constructing the parameters of deep neural networks based on appropriate BNF grammar.
8. Conclusions This paper focused on the attention mechanism in deep learning for fake news detection. The attention mechanism was applied on four different deep learning methods, namely LSTM, BiLSTM, GRU, and CNN. To train these models, two different datasets created for fake news detection were used: the LIAR dataset, which contains posts from twitter during the 2016 US election, and the WELFake dataset, which contains data from longer articles and is created by combining several popular datasets for fake news detection. Based on our experiments, it was found that the results of the models trained on the LIAR dataset containing short posts from Twitter were not very successful. Although the attention mechanism greatly improved recall and F1-rate, there were limitations in accuracy and precision, indicating the difficulty of detecting fake news in extremely short text data.
In contrast, the results on the WELFake dataset, which contained longer and more complex articles, were significantly better. The models trained on these datasets achieved higher accuracy, precision, recall, and F1-rate.
Despite both being used for fake news detection, the WELFake and the LIAR datasets have significant differences, which are summarized in Table 13. The first difference is the scope. WELFake is significantly larger than LIAR, both in terms of average text length and number of samples. While WELFake comprises news articles, LIAR only contains short statements. This is visible in Table 13, where average text lengths, median text lengths, and the numbers of unique words are compared. The LIAR dataset, on the other hand, encompasses author-related metadata about previous statements. Another difference is the number of classes. Both datasets generally distinguish between false and true labels; however, the LIAR dataset separates these two into finer sublabels, resulting in six classes. The class imbalance has been handled in both datasets. In the case of the LIAR dataset the most extreme class, “pants on fire”, is underrepresented in relation to the others.
For both datasets, it was seen that after using the attention mechanism, the two sets performed similarly in training and validation; however, without the attention mechanism, the training was less consistent. Regarding the metrics, the model using the attention mechanism performed at least slightly better for LIAR dataset and significantly better for WELFake. In addition to theoretical results, the work also brought practical knowledge that when we do not have a suitably compiled dataset available, the impact of the latest technologies on improving a model can be very limited.
Finaly, we can conclude that attention mechanisms, especially multi-head attention, can indeed enhance fake news detection performance when applied to high-quality, well-balanced datasets. The presented research confirms that incorporating attention is a worthwhile improvement in such contexts.
Another possibility for future research directions is to train deeper basic models with multi-headed attention and to focus on more sophisticated transformer architectures.
For the future, it may also be beneficial to experiment with multi-self-attention and include sentiment analysis. Another possibility to improve the model’s efficiency is to focus on the phenomenon of overfitting using a method for learning the weights of artificial neural networks based on BNF (Backus–Naur Form) grammar.
