1 Introduction
Vaccination has been widely recognized as one of the most effective public health interventions [ 1 ]. However, vaccine hesitancy remains a persistent challenge in public health [ 2 ]. The World Health Organization (WHO) has identified vaccine hesitancy as one of the top ten threats to global health, emphasizing its detrimental impact on vaccination coverage and herd immunity [ 3 ].
Among the different vaccines, the human papillomavirus (HPV) vaccine is particularly notable. HPV is one of the most common sexually transmitted infections, with certain high-risk strains linked to cervical, anal, and oropharyngeal cancers [ 4 , 5 ]. Prophylactic HPV vaccines have demonstrated high efficacy in preventing HPV infection and reducing the incidence of high-grade premalignant lesions (e.g., CIN2) [ 6 ]. However, HPV vaccination rates remain suboptimal due to vaccine hesitancy driven by factors such as safety concerns and misinformation [ 7 , 8 , 9 ]. The issue of vaccine hesitancy is further complicated by political polarization, which has become a significant determinant of public health behaviors and policy decisions in the United States [ 10 ]. Research indicates that political ideology influences HPV vaccine-related beliefs and behaviors, with liberals demonstrating higher awareness and uptake compared to conservatives [ 11 , 12 ].
Social media has emerged as a critical battleground for vaccine-related discourse, facilitating the rapid spread of both accurate and misleading health information. The interactive nature of social media platforms, where users engage with posts through comments or other reactions, has significant implications for public perception and decision-making [ 13 , 14 ]. Given that parental acceptance is crucial for HPV vaccine uptake, understanding how social media discourse influences parents' vaccine-related decision-making is essential. Prior studies have demonstrated that exposure to anti-vaccine messages, particularly those invoking conspiracy theories and misinformation, can increase vaccine hesitancy [ 15 ]. However, less is known about the role of further correction comments, that is, responses that counteract misinformation in comment sections, and their potential to mitigate the negative effects of anti-vaccine discourse. The interactivity component of Modality-Agency-Interactivity-Navigability (MAIN) model [ 13 ] posits that digital media cues, such as social media comments, influence individuals' message evaluations through heuristic processing. Specifically, the presence of opposite comments under a public health post can trigger a bandwagon effect, leading users to perceive the original message as less credible and impeding health behaviors [ 16 , 17 ]. Furthermore, individual differences, such as the need for cognition (NFC), may moderate responses to social media content, influencing susceptibility to heuristic cues and emotional appeals [ 18 , 19 ].
This study seeks to examine the impact of anti-vaccine misinformation comments on parents' HPV vaccine hesitancy, with a focus on the mediating role of negative emotions and the moderating role of NFC. Additionally, the study explores whether correction messages from the CDC can counteract the negative influence of anti-vaccine comments.
2 Literature review
2.1 HPV and HPV Vaccine hesitancy
Vaccines are among the most effective public health interventions, preventing millions of deaths annually by reducing the prevalence of infectious diseases [ 1 , 20 , 21 ]. The success of vaccination programs has led to the eradication of smallpox, the near-eradication of polio, and significant reductions in the burden of measles and hepatitis B. [ 22 , 23 , 24 , 21 ]. Despite their proven efficacy, vaccine uptake is often hindered by hesitancy, which is a phenomenon that has gained increasing attention in recent years [ 2 , 25 , 26 ].
Vaccine hesitancy has emerged as a significant public health challenge in recent years, exacerbated by various factors including but not limited to misinformation, political ideologies, and emotional responses [ 27 , 28 , 29 , 30 , 31 ]. Vaccine hesitancy refers to a delay in acceptance or refusal of vaccines despite the availability of vaccination services [ 32 ] as defined by the World Health Organization (WHO) [ 33 ]. The WHO has recognized vaccine hesitancy as one of the top ten threats to global health due to its impact on vaccination coverage and herd immunity ([ 34 ] l; [ 3 ]).
Human papillomavirus (HPV) is one of the most common sexually transmitted infections with certain high-risk strains linked to cervical, anal and oropharyngeal cancers [ 4 , 5 ]. HPV vaccines are designed to prevent the transmission of HPV infections that can lead to cervical and other types of cancer [ 35 , 36 ] and have been shown to be highly effective in reducing the incidence of these cancers [ 37 , 38 ]. More specifically, the development of prophylactic HPV vaccines, including but not limited to Gardasil and Cervarix, represents a major advancement in cancer prevention, with studies demonstrating their high efficacy in preventing HPV infection and reducing the incidence of high-grade premalignant lesions (e.g., CIN2) [ 6 ].
As aforementioned, among other vaccines, HPV vaccination rates remain suboptimal in many countries, partly due to widespread vaccine hesitancy (e.g., [ 39 , 40 , 41 , 42 ]). For instance, parents' concerns about vaccine safety, misconceptions regarding the necessity of vaccinating adolescents, and the stigma associated with a vaccine linked to sexual activity contribute to low vaccination rates (e.g., [ 7 , 8 , 9 ]). Additionally, inadequate healthcare provider recommendations and disparities in healthcare access further hinder HPV vaccine uptake [ 43 , 7 , 44 ]. Education campaigns that emphasize the vaccine's role in cancer prevention rather than its association with sexual behavior have shown promise in improving acceptance rates [ 45 , 46 , 47 ]. Additionally, addressing misinformation through fact-based communication strategies, including but not limited to the use of trusted healthcare providers and evidence-driven messaging, has been effective in counteracting myths surrounding vaccine safety and efficacy. Providing accessible, transparent, and culturally sensitive information can help dispel misconceptions and build public trust, which could ultimately lead to higher vaccination uptake [ 48 , 49 , 50 , 51 ].
2.2 Polarization of health issues in the U.S
Political polarization is a collective phenomenon, whereas “partisanship applies at the individual level, reflecting deep-rooted social and/or political identities” [ 52 ]. Political polarization has emerged as a significant determinant of public health over the past several years, influencing both policy decisions and individual health behaviors. Evidence showed that political polarization presents a structural challenge to public health in the U.S. (e.g., [ 10 ]), especially when individuals hold ideological views that are significantly more extreme than those of their surrounding communities. Van Bavel et al. [ 52 ] distinguished two major types of polarization, ideological polarization and affective polarization, and discussed how they influence public health through different mechanisms. Ideological polarization [ 53 , 54 ], characterized by the divergence of policy positions or ideological orientations to opposing extremes, has led to reduced consensus on public health policies and fragmented responses to health crises. Affective polarization [ 55 ], marked by strong animosity toward opposing political groups, further exacerbates distrust in public health institutions and experts.
Vaccination is one of the most polarized health issues in the U.S. The COVID-19 vaccine is one of the most prominent examples. It was found that during the COVID-19 pandemic, partisan divides were evident in vaccine uptake, with higher excess death rates among Republican voters compared to Democrats due to vaccine hesitancy [ 56 ]. Partisanship was also found to be a strong predictor of HPV vaccine-related beliefs and behaviors. A plethora of evidence revealed that liberals are more likely to be aware of HPV and the HPV vaccine, and have a higher HPV vaccination rate, compared to their conservative counterparts (e.g., [ 11 , 12 ]).
Polarized discussions about health issues on social media have been increasing over the past several years, posing huge threats to public health. For instance, they foster echo chambers that amplify politicized misinformation and weaken trust in public health authorities [ 57 , 58 ]. The algorithm further intensifies exposure to emotionally charged partisan content, heightening irritation, confirmation bias, and resistance to vaccines and other preventive measures. These self-reinforcing dynamics of selective exposure and emotional contagion ultimately undermine the effectiveness of evidence-based digital health campaigns.
To mitigate the adverse effects of polarization on health, scholars have emphasized the need for bipartisan communication strategies, engagement of trusted community leaders, and transparent public health messaging [ 57 , 51 , 59 ]. Interdisciplinary collaboration between social and medical scientists is essential to develop effective interventions that address polarization's impact on health systems and population well-being [ 52 ].
2.3 The main model and digital health campaigns
Social media platforms such as X have been widely used in public health campaigns. For example, the CDC's use of Facebook, Twitter, and other social media tools to disseminate health information has increased significantly over the last several years. It has been argued that each technology brings with it a set of affordances or capabilities that can shape the nature of content in a given medium, and these affordances also determine the way the content is typically presented via the medium and the receivers' states of mind while using it [ 13 ]. User comments are one of the most commonly used technological features of digital media [ 14 ], and they serve as a reflection of users' attitudinal positions (i.e., either align with the original post or against it). Consequently, audiences have shifted from passive information receivers to active participants who can interact with the original post.
Sundar [ 13 ] proposed the Modality-Agency-Interactivity-Navigability (MAIN) model, which asserts that digital media cues like user comments can trigger heuristic thinking that influences an individual's message evaluations. Interactivity, a core component of the MAIN model, refers to the ability of users to engage with content and influence message interpretation through reciprocal actions [ 13 ]. In a social media environment, comment sections represent a primary venue for interactivity, where users not only consume but also co-construct public meaning by posting, replying, or observing the conversation. Particularly, Sundar claimed that user comments can trigger a bandwagon effect, that is, “if others think this message is good, then I should think so too”. Therefore, when people find opposing comments under a post, they may use this as a mental shortcut, perceive the post as unreliable, and generate negative evaluations of the message. This argument has been supported by empirical evidence. For example, Shi et al. [ 17 ] found that the negative uncivil comments under anti-smoking PSAs on YouTube could evoke negative attitudes toward quitting and decrease the perceived risk of smoking. Conversely, Li and Sundar's study (2021) demonstrated that supportive comments under anti-drinking PSA videos could reduce reactance to the video through the bandwagon perceptions that other people liked the video.
The anti-vaccine misinformation comment in our study serves as the first-layer interactive cue, which signals dissent and invites further engagement, both from institutional actors (e.g., CDC) and from observing users. This further engagement (i.e., replies to first-order comments) then becomes the second-layer interactive cue. In many social media comment-related studies, researchers manipulate the presence or tone of first-order comments (e.g., positive vs. negative) and measure the audience's perceptions of the original post (e.g., [ 16 , 17 ]), which assumes the comment space as static. However, real-world comment sections are usually dynamic and interactive. Users encounter not only initial comments but also second-order replies (e.g., rebuttals, endorsements, counterarguments), which can further influence how they process the post. This ongoing interactivity may reinforce or mitigate the initial bandwagon effects. However, replies to first-order comments remain underexplored in the literature. Our study tries to address this gap by explicitly examining both anti-vaccine misinformation comments and further misinformation corrections within an interactive comment environment, providing a more ecologically valid test of how comment interactivity influences vaccine hesitancy.
2.4 Politized anti-vaccine misinformation comments on social media
One consequence of polarization of vaccine topics is the abundance of misinformation on social media, where user-generated content often circulates without verification. On these platforms, people can potentially be exposed to a torrent of partisanship-driven misleading health information (e.g., conspiracy theories). For example, Sharma et al. [ 60 ] analyzed COVID-19-related misinformation in Twitter data, and they found that misinformation is predominantly spread by far-right communities with a significantly higher ratio of conspiracy theories. These anti-vaccine messages often contain conspiracy theories, like the idea that health institutions are hiding the truth about vaccines' side effects or that the government is using vaccines to control its citizens [ 61 ]. Exposure to these anti-vaccine messages can cause negative health outcomes. Betsch et al. [ 15 ] found that viewing anti-vaccination information for 10 min would increase the perceived risk of vaccination and decrease the perceived risk of vaccine refusal as well as vaccination intention. A lot of anti-vaccine conversations on social media happen in the comments section underneath posts. Prior studies examined the features of these anti-vaccine comments. For example, Klimiuk et al. [ 62 ] analyzed vaccine-deniers' comments on Facebook and their results showed that anti-vaccine comments centered on several specific topics such as conspiracy theories, misinformation and unreliable premises, safety, and vaccination efficacy.
Because HPV vaccination targets children, parental acceptance or consent is critical to ensuring uptake. If parents hold concerns or misperceptions about the HPV vaccine, they will be less likely to vaccinate their children. As claimed by Atwell and Salmon [ 63 ], parents' vaccine decision-making is complex and associated with a series of factors such as perceived susceptibility, severity of the disease, perceived safety, and effectiveness of vaccines. Moreover, parents' decision-making can also be influenced by heuristics such as a tendency to overestimate very rare but severe risks of adverse events. Prior studies have shown that parental vaccine hesitancy is a growing public health problem in the U.S. Many parents hold concerns about the safety and effectiveness of vaccines as well as their potential side effects, such as autism (e.g., [ 64 , 65 ]). Vaccine-hesitant parents expressed low confidence and trust in vaccine information from public health agencies and the government (e.g., [ 66 ]). Beavis et al. [ 67 ] found that the concerns about the safety and necessity of the HPV vaccine impede parents from vaccinating their children. Moreover, even those parents whose children receive all of the recommended vaccines also have questions, concerns, or misperceptions about the vaccinations [ 68 ].
Given the high level of vaccine uncertainty among parents as well as the negative impacts of anti-vaccine comments, it is important to understand how parents respond to anti-vaccine comments on social media. Under the framework of the MAIN model, we hypothesize that the anti-vaccine comments could evoke vaccine hesitancy among the parents, which is H1:
H1: Participants who are exposed to politized anti-vaccine misinformation comments will show higher HPV vaccine hesitancy than participants who are not.
2.5 Negative emotion as a mediator
The valence and civility of social media comments are crucial determinants of emotional reactions. Research consistently indicates that negative comments elicit stronger emotional responses compared to positive ones (e.g., [ 69 , 70 , 71 ]). Negativity bias literature also suggests that comments with negative information have a stronger effect on attention and are more arousing (e.g., [ 72 ]). Additionally, recent studies show that exposure to negative messages influences information processing. For instance, Kohout et al. [ 73 ] conducted an eye-tracking experimental study examining how much attention social media users allocate to emotionally charged comments and to what extent the content of these comments is memorized and their findings were mainly in line with the existing negativity bias scholarship, reinforcing that negative messages capture more attention (e.g., [ 72 ]).
Negative emotions elicited by negative comments may further undermine the effectiveness of persuasive health messages. Prior research has often examined emotional responses within the framework of psychological reactance, suggesting that heightened reactance can lead to persuasion failure (e.g., [ 69 , 70 , 74 , 75 ]). However, these studies have typically treated anger as the primary emotional indicator and conceptualized it as one dimension of psychological reactance, alongside negative cognition. This approach presents certain limitations, as it overlooks other relevant emotions such as fear, anxiety, and nervousness. Moreover, treating emotion as a sub-component of psychological reactance may obscure its distinct role in shaping attitudinal outcomes. To address these limitations, the present study conceptualized negative emotion as an independent construct and examined its mediating role in the relationship between anti-vaccine comments and vaccine hesitancy.
In the current study, we focused exclusively on negative emotion for both theoretical and practical considerations. Theoretical frameworks such as psychological reactance theory suggest that negative user-generated content primarily evokes threat-based emotional responses, which are mainly negative. Research about negativity bias (e.g., [ 72 ]) also shows that the influence of negative information on an individual's attitude and behavior is much stronger than positive information. In our study, we developed the manipulation (i.e., anti-vaccine comments) with the aim of inducing skepticism and distrust toward the original post. This approach is based on the purpose of simulating the vaccine-related conversation in real social media settings. As such, we exclusively focused on negative emotion for conceptual clarity and external validity. Six discrete negative emotions (i.e., angry, outraged, disgusted, anxious, afraid, and nervous) were examined in this study.
Grounded in previous literature, we hypothesize that the anti-vaccine comments could evoke vaccine hesitancy through triggering negative emotional reactions toward the original post, which are H2 and H3:
H2a: Participants who are exposed to politized anti-vaccine misinformation comments will show higher negative emotions toward the original post than participants who are not.
H2b: Participants with higher negative emotions will show higher HPV vaccine hesitancy than participants with lower negative emotions.
H3: The impact of politized anti-vaccine misinformation comments on vaccine hesitancy is mediated by negative emotions.
2.6 Need for cognition as a moderator
Need for cognition (NFC) is defined as an intrinsic motivation to “understand and make reasonable the experiential world” ([ 76 ], p. 291). Individuals high in NFC exhibit a pronounced propensity to structure complex, information-rich environments in a coherent and integrative manner [ 18 ]. Closely aligned with intellectual engagement and cognitive curiosity, NFC reflects a stable dispositional tendency toward analytical reasoning, characterized by a preference for tasks that necessitate sustained deliberation and mental effort ([ 77 ]; Lins [ 78 ]). Extensive research has examined NFC's impact in information processing and media use, consistently finding that high-NFC individuals are less susceptible to superficial heuristic cues. Instead, they engage in effortful, systematic processing—critically evaluating message content and synthesizing complex information to arrive at well-substantiated conclusions [ 79 , 80 ].
The effect of NFC on message evaluation has also been widely documented in the literature. Cacioppo et al. [ 81 ] found that individuals high in NFC exhibit greater sensitivity to argument quality, invest more cognitive effort, and demonstrate superior recall of message content compared to their low-NFC counterparts, underscoring a stable individual difference in message elaboration and persuasion. Similarly, See et al. [ 82 ] demonstrated that high-NFC individuals are more motivated to engage with messages they perceive as complex, whereas low-NFC individuals tend to prefer simpler, more cognitively accessible content. These findings collectively suggest that NFC shapes message evaluation by determining individuals' motivation to engage in effortful cognitive processing.
Moreover, decades of empirical research have established NFC as a critical moderating variable in the interplay between cognitive and affective processes, particularly in contexts involving emotion-affected decision-making. Individuals high in NFC consistently demonstrate greater resistance to the influence of incidental emotions during evaluative judgments. Lin et al. [ 19 ] found that low-NFC individuals are more susceptible to mood-based fluctuations in decision-making, whereas high-NFC ones exhibit greater emotional stability and cognitive control. Similarly, Cacioppo et al. [ 83 ] suggest that high-NFC individuals are more likely to engage in deliberative processing, thereby attenuating the affective bias elicited by emotional states such as fear or anger. Rodríguez-Hernández et al. [ 84 ] further reinforce this pattern, showing that high-NFC individuals engage more readily in elaborative processing of persuasive messages, reducing the persuasive power of emotional appeals. Taken together, NFC can mitigate the impact of emotional interference in decision-making processes.
The moderating role of NFC has been consistently confirmed within the domain of health communication. Individuals with higher levels of NFC have been shown to exhibit stronger positive outcomes in response to health-related media messages, including increased mammography utilization [ 85 ], enhanced smoking cessation self-efficacy [ 86 ], more favorable attitudes toward fruit and vegetable consumption [ 87 ] and food product evaluations [ 88 ], heightened resistance to health misinformation [ 89 ], and greater consistency between implicit and explicit attitudes toward the COVID-19 vaccine [ 90 ].
In a nutshell, NFC can serve as a cognitive disposition that shapes individuals' receptivity to health messages, ultimately influencing the persuasive efficacy and behavioral impact of health communication interventions. More specifically, people with varying levels of NFC use distinct cognitive and affective processes when making decisions. To date, no attempt has been made to explore the interaction effects between individuals' emotional activities and their NFC on vaccination intentions. To address this research gap, we propose RQ1:
RQ1: Does need for cognition (NFC) moderate the association between negative emotions and vaccine hesitancy?
2.7 Misinformation corrections on politized anti-vaccine comments
Given the negative impact of the anti-vaccine comments, misinformation corrections are needed to mitigate these effects. Health misinformation correction strategies have been widely examined across different topics such as smoking (e.g., [ 91 ]), vaccine (e.g., [ 69 , 70 , 92 ]), and food safety (e.g., [ 93 ]). Commonly examined misinformation correction strategies include correction source (e.g., expert source vs. non-expert source), correction format (e.g., narrative vs. non-narrative), emotional appeals, etc. Health institutions have been found to play a particularly important role in correcting health misinformation due to their high perceived reliability (e.g., [ 94 , 95 ]). A meta-analysis by Walter et al. [ 96 ] demonstrated that overall, the misinformation correction interventions on social media are effective.
Recent work increasingly recognizes that social media affordances create new venues for misinformation correction. One emerging form is rebuttals within comment threads, that is, direct replies to misleading user comments. For example, Sun and Lu [ 95 ] found that rebuttals by the CDC helped combat misinformation contained in social media comments and increased individuals' vaccination intention. Batista et al. [ 97 ] found that a brand's reply to negative comments on social media could influence consumers' evaluation of said brand. Such rebuttals represent the correction in an interactive form, taking place within the ongoing conversations among users rather than at the level of the initial post. This type of correction leverages the interactivity affordance of social media, allowing institutions or other users to engage with misinformation in real time, visible to other observers of the comment thread.
However, the effectiveness of misinformation correction in comment sections remains underexplored. Most prior studies have focused on first-order comments, while relatively few have examined how second-layer interactive cues (e.g., replies to first-order comments) influence audience attitudes and emotional responses. Given that social media users often observe entire comment threads, including both misinformation itself and correction messages, understanding the effects of these dialogic interactions is particularly important. To address this gap, the present study investigates whether the CDC correction message mitigates the negative impact of anti-vaccine comments on audience emotions and vaccine hesitancy. We therefore pose RQ2:
RQ2: Does a further correction comment from CDC help to mitigate the negative effects caused by user-generated politicized anti-vaccine misinformation comments?
3 Method
Upon approval of the institutional review board (IRB), data were collected using an online between-subjects experiment. A power analysis using G*Power indicated that a sample size of 395 would be sufficient to detect small effects (f 2  = 0.02) with 80 % power at α = 0.05. Participants ( N  = 1039) were finally recruited on Lucid, a large online survey panel. Inclusion criteria were (a) currently living in the U.S., (b) were parents or guardians of any child below 18, and (c) their children had not fully received the HPV vaccine by then.
Participants first answered the screening questions. After that, eligible participants reported demographic information and then were randomly assigned to one of the three conditions: post-only condition ( n  = 349), misinformation comment condition which includes an anti-vaccine comment under the post ( n  = 346), and CDC correction condition which includes one more CDC correction comment ( n  = 344). The original data was collected through an experiment having six conditions with one more factor: gain and loss frame of the original post. In this analysis, we combined the gain and loss conditions. Therefore, each condition in our study includes an equal number of gain-framed and loss-framed posts. The loss and gain frame was also treated as a message-level covariate in subsequent statistical analyses. Before reading the stimulus, participants were told that they would see a screenshot of a Facebook post and be asked to read it as if it were on their own feed. Then, they read the stimulus and finished the assessment of outcome variables (i.e., Child HPV vaccine hesitancy and negative emotions). HPV vaccine hesitancy and negative emotions were measured only once, immediately after participants were exposed to their assigned experimental condition. No baseline measure was collected prior to exposure.
3.1 Experimental stimuli
We created three types of stimuli. Examples of stimuli can be found in the appendix, Fig. 5 . For the post-only condition, the stimulus was a Facebook post from the CDC encouraging people to get their children vaccinated. In the misinformation comment condition, an additional anti-vaccine comment was added to the same post. The politized anti-vaccine misinformation comment used in this study aligns with conspiracy theory–based misinformation, a subtype of health misinformation that frames vaccines as part of a hidden agenda by powerful actors. In our stimulus, the comment portrayed the HPV vaccine as unnecessary and framed it as a “political power game” by one political party and made extreme allegations that the vaccine is “killing people” and causes “severe side effects”. This framing reflects two key features of conspiracy theory-based misinformation: (1) institutional distrust, which casts doubt on the credibility, intentions, and transparency of official health agencies, and (2) risk exaggeration, which aims to amplify perceived dangers/side effects of the vaccine. These rhetorical strategies are designed to undermine one's confidence in vaccination programs and mobilize opposition by appealing to negative emotions such as fear and anger. In the CDC correction condition, CDC replied to that anti-vaccine comment, clarifying that the vaccines don't have side effects and should not be considered a political issue. All other factors in the stimuli (e.g., number of likes, name and picture of the user, time) were held consistent across all conditions. (See Figs. 1–4 .) (See Tables 5.2 and 5.3 .)
3.2 Measures
3.2.1 Child HPV vaccine hesitancy
To measure child HPV vaccine hesitancy, participants were asked to indicate their level of agreement with the nine items adapted from a previous study [ 98 ]; one example is “I am concerned about serious adverse effects of children's HPV vaccines.” Responses were recorded using a 7-point Likert scale (from 1 = strongly disagree to 7 = strongly agree). Higher values represent higher HPV vaccine hesitancy ( M  = 3.53, SD  = 1.23, Cronbach's α = 0.90).
3.2.2 Negative emotions toward the original post
We measured six negative emotions, including angry, outraged, disgusted, anxious, afraid, and nervous. The mean score of these six emotions was calculated to represent a general negative emotion construct ( M  = 2.61, SD  = 1.58, Cronbach's α = 0.94). Participants were asked to indicate to what extent they felt each emotion in relation to the original post using a 7-point semantic differential scale (from 1 = not at all to 7 = extremely).
3.2.3 Need for cognition
NFC was captured using three items from the Need for Cognition Scale [ 77 ]. One example item is “I really enjoy a task that involves coming up with new solutions to problems.” Participants indicated their agreement with these items using a 7-point Likert scale (from 1 = strongly disagree to 7 = strongly agree). Higher values represent higher NFC ( M  = 4.57, SD  = 1.23, Cronbach's α = 0.74).
3.2.4 Covariates
Previous literature has demonstrated political ideology's impact on individual's vaccine intention; thus political ideology was set as an individual-level covariate in this study. Considering our initial study design, we also set the gain/loss frame as a message-level covariate.
3.3 Data analysis
Frequency and descriptive analyses were run to describe the samples. Regression analysis using Hayes' Process Model 14 was run to test H1-H3 and answer RQ1. A one-way ANOVA was conducted to answer RQ2.
4 Results
The average age of participants was 42.48 ( SD  = 10.05), and the average age for their children was 10.71 ( SD  = 5.15). More than half of the participants (64.4 %) were female ( N  = 669). Among the participants, 73 % were Caucasian ( n  = 758), 18.4 % were black/African American ( n  = 191), 4.5 % were Hispanic/Latino ( n  = 47), 4.3 % were native American ( n  = 45), 3.7 % were Asian American ( n  = 38), 0.7 % were Arab American (n = 7), 2.4 % selected ‘Other’ for this question ( n  = 25).
4.1 Demographic equivalence across conditions
To assess the equivalence of demographic characteristics across experimental conditions, we conducted one-way ANOVAs for continuous variables (i.e., age, political ideology) and Chi-square tests for categorical variables (i.e., gender, race/ethnicity). Results can be seen in Table 5 . One-way ANOVAs showed no significant differences across the three conditions in participants' age ( F (2, 1036) = 0.24, p  = .79) and political ideology ( F (2, 1036) = 0.75, p  = .47). Chi-square test showed that gender (χ 2  = 9.08, p  = .17) and race/ethnicity (χ 2  = 10.25, p  = .59) distribution did not significantly differ across the three conditions.
4.2 H1-H3 and RQ1
Results of model 14 (see Table 1 ) showed that participants in the anti-vaccine comment condition reported significantly stronger negative emotions toward the post ( b  = 0.27, p  = .03, 95 % CI [0.03, 0.50]), which supported H2a. Negative emotion toward the post was positively associated with HPV vaccine hesitancy ( b  = 0.41, p  < .001, 95 % CI [0.21, 0.61]). Moreover, this association was moderated by NFC ( b  = − 0.07, p  = .001, 95 % CI [− 0.11, − 0.03]). Specifically, this relationship was statistically significant only among individuals with low-to-medium NFC (see Table 2 ). The relationship was slightly stronger among individuals with low NFC ( b  = 0.18, p  < .001, 95 % CI [0.10, 0.26]) compared to those with an average level of NFC ( b  = 0.08, p  < .01, 95 % CI [0.03, 0.14]), though both were statistically significant. Among individuals with high NFC, the association between negative emotion and vaccine hesitancy became non-significant ( b  = −0.01, p  = .82, 95 % CI [− 0.08, 0.06]). To sum up, the predictive capability of negative emotion on vaccine hesitancy was significant yet eventually became non-significant as individuals' NFC increased. Thus, H2b was partially supported and RQ1 was answered.
The direct effect of anti-vaccine misinformation comment on HPV vaccine hesitancy was not significant ( b  = 0.02, p  = .81, 95 % CI [− 0.15, 0.19]), while a significant moderated mediation effect was captured. Results (see Table 3 ) showed that for people with low to average NFC, the indirect effect of anti-vaccine comments on vaccine hesitancy through negative emotions was significant, with negative emotion's mediation effect getting weaker as NFC increased from a low ( b  = 0.05, 95 % CI [0.01, 0.10]) to average level ( b  = 0.02, 95 % CI [0.00, 0.05]). However, this indirect effect became non-significant when people's NFC increased to a high level ( b  = −0.00, 95 % CI [− 0.02, 0.02]). Therefore, H1 and H3 were partially supported.
4.3 RQ2
A one-way ANOVA with LSD (Least Significant Difference) post hoc tests was conducted to answer RQ2. We compared two outcome variables, vaccine hesitancy and negative emotion, across three conditions. Results (see Table 4 ) showed that there was no significant difference across the three conditions for either outcome, except that negative emotion in the misinformation comment condition ( M  = 2.72, SD  = 1.62) was significantly higher than post-only condition ( M  = 2.46, SD  = 1.51), p  = .03, 95 % CI [− 0.50, − 0.03]). Therefore, the CDC's correction to the anti-vaccine comment did not help to decrease either negative emotions or vaccine hesitancy.
4.4 Sensitivity analysis for each single discrete emotion
Considering that different discrete emotions may predict behavioral outcomes through different mechanisms, we also conducted a series of exploratory sensitivity analyses, treating each discrete negative emotion as an individual mediator in Hayes' Process Model 14. Results (see Table 6 ) indicated that when each emotion was tested separately, none of the indirect effects reached statistical significance after accounting for the moderated mediation structure. This pattern suggests that it may be the combined experience of multiple negative emotions, rather than any single emotion alone, that most strongly influences responses to health misinformation on social media.
5 Discussion
The HPV vaccine has faced significant politicization in recent years, leading to the proliferation of politically charged, misleading, anti-vaccine comments on social media. However, it remains uncertain how these misinformation comments interact with public health messages to shape audience decisions about vaccination. Using the MAIN model as a framework, this study investigated how anti-vaccine comments influence parents' decisions regarding HPV vaccination for their children, focusing on the emotional responses driving this effect, and whether social media comment serves as a promising format for misinformation correction.
Our results provide evidence that, compared to participants who only read the health campaign post, those exposed to politized anti-vaccine comments reported higher vaccine hesitancy, with negative emotions toward the original post mediating this association. This finding echoes the bandwagon effects captured in previous literature, that is, “if others disagree with this argument in the post, then I should think so as well”. Within the framework of the Modality-Agency-Interactivity-Navigability (MAIN) model, user comments serve as an interactivity cue that activates heuristics like bandwagon, thereby influencing audiences' evaluation of the original post. Our study also highlights the central role of affective heuristics in this process. Specifically, we found that the impact of anti-vaccine comments on vaccine hesitancy was mediated by negative emotions, including anger, outrage, disgust, anxiety, fear, and nervousness. These negative emotions might be driven by the linguistic properties of the anti-vaccine comment. Consistent with Yang et al. [ 99 ], emotionally charged language, particularly language conveying threat and blame, plays a central role in triggering user emotions on social media. In our study, the anti-vaccine comment included highly affective languages (e.g., “killing people,” “severe side effects,” “political power games”) which may function as linguistic triggers, eliciting a series of negative emotions. This perspective highlights how specific language features can amplify the psychological impact of misinformation in interactive digital settings.
Additionally, while prior research has established that first-order comments can trigger bandwagon effects and shape audience perceptions of public health messages (e.g., [ 16 , 17 ]), it has largely overlooked second-layer interactive cues, such as replies to first-order comments. Our study also explicitly examined this dimension by including CDC corrections to anti-vaccine comments, thereby capturing a more ecologically valid form of social media interactivity. We found that the CDC correction comment failed to mitigate the negative impact of misinformation on negative emotions and vaccine hesitancy. In other words, even when followed by fact-based CDC corrections, the initial misinformation comment continued to exert influence. This result highlights the enduring effects of early user-generated content in interactive media environments. Early misinformation comments could anchor user perceptions, and subsequent correction comments are interpreted in light of the initial misinformation's impact. This dynamic necessitates a reconsideration of how message effects operate in social media environments. It also suggests that simple correction comments may not be adequate to combat misinformation. Several possible reasons can explain this result. First is the potential first-mover advantage in interactive comment spaces, wherein early visible comments disproportionately shape subsequent audience processing, then later corrections will struggle to shift entrenched perceptions. Another reason may have something to do with affective responses, given the finding that the anti-vaccine comment could evoke negative emotions toward the original post. If users are already emotionally aroused, then it will be hard for a simple fact-based (i.e., not emotionally appealing) correction to counteract these negative emotional reactions. Future research may consider alternative correction strategies that move beyond neutral, fact-focused languages, such as emotionally appealing corrections, or narrative-based corrections that incorporate personal stories. That said, previous research by Sun and Lu [ 95 ] did find that corrections from the CDC were effective in reducing psychological reactance to persuasive messages and increasing vaccine acceptance. Possible explanations of these mixed findings, such as the tone of the correction messages, source credibility, or the number of likes and endorsements the comments received, should also be carefully examined in future research. Moreover, one crucial characteristic of this misinformation comment is that it is politized in nature. As a result, it maybe even more difficult to mitigate the impact of the misinformation.
Finally, our results revealed that the mediation effect of negative emotions was moderated by an individual's NFC. The association between negative emotions and HPV vaccine hesitancy was only evident among individuals with low-to-medium NFC. For those with high NFC, there was no significant link between negative emotions and vaccine hesitancy, nor was the mediation effect observed. This finding suggests that people with high NFC are less susceptible to the misleading effects of anti-vaccine misinformation comments. Although they may experience emotional reactions toward the original post, as those with lower NFC do, these emotions do not really drive their final decisions. This finding aligns with previous research indicating that high-NFC individuals tend to engage in more deliberative, logical thinking rather than relying on emotional responses when making decisions (e.g., [ 83 , 84 ]). The presence of emotional reactions among high-NFC individuals is explainable, as emotions are often automatic, fleeting, and difficult to regulate [ 100 ]. Encountering opposing comments can naturally trigger negative emotions. However, the key distinction is whether they rely on these emotional reactions for the final decision-making. Our results suggest that individuals with low-to-medium NFC tend to rely on their immediate emotional responses when making decisions, making them more vulnerable to misinformation comments. This finding has important implications for designing effective corrections. Since the most vulnerable individuals are those low in NFC, fact-based and cognitively demanding corrections may not resonate with them. Instead, emotionally engaging correction strategies may be more effective at mitigating the emotional impact of misinformation and guiding attitudes in a corrective direction. Tailoring the tone and format of corrections based on the audience's cognitive style is therefore a critical aspect to be taken into consideration in future research.
5.1 Theoretical contributions and practical implications
Theoretically, this study advances misinformation correction research by emphasizing how interactivity shapes the impact of health misinformation and correction messages on social media. Our findings highlight how politicized vaccine misinformation in comment threads can independently drive negative emotional responses and lead to negative health outcomes. By incorporating second-layer corrections (i.e., CDC replies to anti-vaccine misinformation comments), the study extends the MAIN model's interactivity cue into a more dynamic and dialogic context. It demonstrates that social media comment threads can function as active spaces where emotional and cognitive mechanisms jointly shape health behaviors and underscores the need to further investigate how health misinformation can be effectively countered through corrective strategies embedded within comment-based interactions. Furthermore, by identifying NFC as a moderator, this study contributes to a more nuanced understanding of how individual differences interact with layered media environments to shape either resistance or susceptibility to health misinformation.
Our findings also have important implications for comment-based health misinformation corrections on social media. Our findings underscore the limitations of neutral, fact-based corrections in combating health misinformation within interactive comment sections. Given the strong emotional impact of politicized misinformation comments, particularly among low-NFC individuals, corrections that fail to emotionally engage audiences may be insufficient. Public health practitioners should consider emotionally appealing rebuttals to more effectively counteract misinformation. Ultimately, this study highlights the need for a strategic shift from static information correction to a psychologically attuned correction that fits the interactive nature of today's social media environments.
5.2 Limitations and future directions
This study is not without limitations. First, the conceptual model has limitations. HPV vaccine hesitancy was used as the outcome variable, which primarily captures individuals' reluctance or uncertainty regarding HPV vaccination. To address this limitation, future research should consider incorporating objective behavioral measures (e.g., vaccination records) or adopting longitudinal studies to track actual behaviors. The R 2 values in our regression model are small. However, we believe this is typical for experimental and survey-based empirical studies in communication and health behavior research, where the outcome is often shaped by many unmeasured variables (e.g., prior experiences, broader sociopolitical context). That being said, we do encourage future research to incorporate additional predictors such as baseline attitudes and prior experiences into the conceptual model to capture a larger predictive power of the variance and get a more comprehensive picture.
Additionally, we aggregated multiple discrete emotions into a single index, which may obscure potential differences in how specific emotions influence health behavior, decrease or cancel effects if emotions operate in opposite directions, and limit the ability to identify which emotions should be targeted in interventions. As such, we recommend future research examine individual discrete emotions separately to explore whether specific emotions can independently shape the impact of health misinformation on vaccine attitudes.
Our experimental stimulus design has limitations as well. Though we tried to simulate the real-world social media settings in our manipulation, the stimuli used in our experiment still simplified the dynamics of real-world social media interactions, which poses threats to external validity. In practice, corrective comments on social media often occur within a multi-level and interactive environment, where other users may also express support, opposition, or indifference, and where visible engagement metrics (e.g., number of “likes”) may create social pressure and in turn influence perceptions. Our experiment only presented a single corrective comment without simulating such user-generated responses or social endorsement cues. Future research should explore how such complex social dynamics shape the impact of user-generated misinformation on social media.
Finally, in our study, white participants accounted for 73 % of the total sample, which means minority groups such as Black/African American and Hispanic/Latino participants were underrepresented. This imbalanced racial distribution reduces the statistical power for detecting subgroup differences or interactions and limits the generalizability of our findings to populations most affected by HPV-related health disparities. Previous research indicates that racial and ethnic minority communities often exhibit distinct patterns of vaccine hesitancy, shaped by structural inequities (e.g., [ 101 ]), differential access to care (e.g., [ 102 ]), and varying levels of trust in healthcare providers (e.g., [ 103 , 104 ]). Overrepresentation of white participants may therefore obscure the unique patterns that exist in underrepresented populations. Although there was overrepresentation of white participants, our sample still represents the overall patten in the US population. Future research may consider employing targeted recruitment strategies or oversampling techniques to ensure adequate representation of highly vulnerable populations, which is a necessary effort for improving health equity.
6 Conclusion
This study examined the impact of anti-vaccine misinformation comments and corrections on social media. Results indicated that individuals exposed to politized anti-vaccine misinformation comments reported higher HPV vaccine hesitancy, compared to those not, and this impact was mediated by negative emotions toward the original post. However, these effects were observed only among individuals with low-to-medium NFC, as those with high NFC are less likely to rely on emotional responses in their decision-making. Additionally, the study found that further correction comments from CDC were ineffective in mitigating these negative effects, suggesting that the influence of anti-vaccine misinformation comments on social media can be persistent and resilient. Despite some limitations, this study offers valuable insights into how health misinformation and correction messages shape vaccine decision-making in an interactive social media environment.
