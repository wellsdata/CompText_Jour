1 Introduction
New prospects for digital inclusion entrepreneurial innovation and economic growth have been brought about by the Arab world's financial systems becoming more digitally connected. Nations like Saudi Arabia and the United Arab Emirates have started large-scale FinTech projects to establish the area as a global center for digital finance (Arab Monetary Fund, 2023). Mobile payment adoption rates in Gulf Cooperation Council (GCC) countries are expected to surpass 65 percent and the Arab world's FinTech market is expected to reach $2. 05 billion by 2025 (McKinsey and Company, 2024). On the other hand, financial disinformation has flourished due to the growth of digital lending services cryptocurrency platforms and mobile banking. This study addresses a pressing question: How can Arab societies strengthen their citizens ability to assess digital financial content critically and protect themselves from false information? Digital transformation has brought opportunities, but it has also brought with it a wave of fraudulent investment schemes, financial scams and unregulated cryptocurrency promotions. According to recent reports the MENA region saw a 78% increase in financial fraud losses between 2022 and 2024. Of these 42% were social media-based investment scams (Arab Banking Federation, 2024). People who use digital devices frequently and lack basic literacy in media criticism and financial reasoning are disproportionately vulnerable to these dangers. In this context media literacy and financial literacy are two critical literacies. Media literacy enables people to evaluate the veracity of messages and fend off deceptive content while financial literacy enables people to manage resources, comprehend risks and make future plans (Lusardi and Mitchell, 2014). However, none of the literacy skills alone can adequately address the complexity of the current digital misinformation environment. In order to strengthen personal resilience this study presents and evaluates the idea of dual literacy in which media and financial literacy complement one another. The study makes use of four theoretical frameworks:Bandura's (2001)social cognitive theory explains how digital environments influence learning and decision-makingLusardi and Mitchell's (2011)financial capability model connects knowledge with behavior Potters media literacy theory provides insights into message evaluation andEntman's (1993)framing theory explains how misinformation is strategically structured. A large and varied Arab sample is used to test these theories which are operationalized within a structural model.
2 Literature review
2.1 Rise of financial misinformation in the Arab world
Globally digital misinformation has increased but because financial misinformation directly affects the economy it is especially dangerous. Regulation of digital financial content is still uneven in Arab nations. Telegram Instagram and other platforms are commonly used to advertise Ponzi schemes fraudulent trading apps and cryptocurrency investment schemes (Sultanbayeva et al., 2024). These difficulties are made worse by the particular socioeconomic circumstances of the Arab world. High rates of youth unemployment (29 percent on average throughout the region) make people susceptible to schemes aimed at making quick money and cultural elements like a strong belief in social media can help spread false information about money (Al-Rashid and Hassan, 2023).
According to recent studies by theArab Monetary Fund (2024)there are three main ways that financial misinformation spreads: WhatsApp groups sharing false success stories fake news websites imitating reputable financial publications and social media influencers pushing unregulated investment products. The COVID-19 pandemic increased exposure to false information while also speeding up the adoption of digital finance.Elmahdi et al. (2023)revealed that during their first three months of using digital financial services 67% of new FinTech users in the Arab world experienced at least one instance of financial misinformation. Similarly, during the pandemic misinformation about cryptocurrencies rose by 340 percent in Gulf countries according toQasemi and Al-Kuwari (2024).
2.2 Financial literacy deficits
In the Arab world financial literacy is remarkably low. According toOECD (2022)most adults in the MENA region have difficulty understanding fundamental financial concepts like risk inflation and interest. Limited financial education integration into national curricula particularly at the secondary and tertiary levels exacerbates this deficiency (Ajami et al., 2025).
There are glaring differences in the area according to recent thorough assessments. Countries like Yemen (31 percent) and Sudan (29 percent) lag far behind the United Arab Emirates which leads with 71 percent of adults exhibiting basic financial literacy followed by Qatar (68 percent) and Bahrain (65 percent) (World Bank Financial Inclusion Database, 2024). These differences have a strong correlation with the evolution of digital infrastructure and legal frameworks.
From straightforward knowledge-based models to increasingly intricate frameworks that take behavioral and contextual factors into account theoretical perspectives on financial literacy have changed over time. The three pillars ofLusardi and Mitchell's (2014)model—risk diversification inflation awareness and numeracy—remain fundamental although more recent research byFernandes et al. (2024). The significance of digital proficiency and financial confidence in contemporary financial decision-making is emphasized in (2024). In the Arab world there is a particularly noticeable gender gap in financial literacy. On standardized financial literacy tests women score 23% lower than men and the difference grow in more conservative societies according toAl-Tamimi and Kalli (2023).
Women are more likely than men to rely on social media for financial information which has important implications for their susceptibility to financial misinformation (Abdulla and Mahmoud, 2024). The development of financial literacy is also influenced by cultural factors. Islamic financial concepts can be confusing when applied to contemporary digital financial products even though they offer useful ethical frameworks. Scholars such asHassan and Al-Rawi (2023)promote culturally aware financial education that connects modern FinTech applications with traditional Islamic finance ideas.
2.3 Media literacy and its role in digital trust
People who are media literate are better able to recognize manipulation and critically assess information. In media-rich environmentsPotter (2010)highlighted the value of media literacy in fostering individual agency. Media literacy is crucial in the field of digital finance for identifying deceptive tactics such as data fabrication clickbait headlines and deepfake endorsements (Ugochukwu, 2025).
Language diversity disparate media environments and varying degrees of press freedom present particular media literacy challenges for the Arab world. Four essential media literacy skills for identifying financial misinformation were identified byTawfik and Mansour (2024): evaluating the reliability of sources identifying persuasive strategies comprehending algorithmic content curation and being able to cross-reference data. The latest study byKhalil et al. (2023)showed that conventional media literacy frameworks need to be modified to fit the social media landscapes that are common in Arab nations.
Conventional media literacy skills which were developed for television and print media demonstrated limited transfer to social media platforms where financial misinformation typically spreads according to their study of 1,200 participants in six Arab countries. Digital media literacy has become a separate concept that includes both technical and critical thinking skills. A culturally tailored digital media literacy scale for Arab populations was created byReza and Al-Hashimi (2024)who included aspects of religious and cultural sensitivity in addition to technical skills.
In keeping with collectivist cultural values their framework places a strong emphasis on the importance of community verification and consultation.Mahmoud et al.'s (2024)neurological study. According to a 2024 study conducted with fMRI technology when exposed to financial disinformation people with higher media literacy exhibit greater activation in prefrontal cortex regions linked to critical thinking. The theoretical claim that media literacy training can improve cognitive resistance to misleading content is supported by this biological evidence.
2.4 Dual-literacy and integrated resilience
Academics have started to realize that combating misinformation requires interdisciplinary approaches.Huang et al. (2024)demonstrated that combining media analysis and financial reasoning results in more robust defenses against false information. The ways in which this dual literacy functions in FinTech-heavy culturally heterogeneous environments like the Arab world are not well understood though. Complexity theory and systems thinking approaches to education give rise to the idea of dual literacy. Dual literacy acknowledges the synergistic interaction between media literacy and financial literacy in fostering cognitive resilience as opposed to treating them as distinct fields.
Chen and Rodriguez (2023)provided evidence in favor of the theoretical underpinnings of integrated approaches by showing that the interaction effect between media literacy and financial literacy competencies is larger than the sum of their individual effects. The effectiveness of dual literacy is supported empirically by a number of international studies. In comparison to those who received single-domain training participants who received combined financial and media literacy training performed 45% better on misinformation detection tasksWang et al. (2024). Likewise dual-literacy interventions proved especially successful for populations with higher baseline levels of digital engagement according to European research byKowalski and Müller (2023).
There are special chances for the development of dual literacy in the Arab world. The region's high social media usage rates and youthful demographic profile (65% of the population is under 35) make it the ideal place for integrated digital literacy initiatives. Nonetheless cultural elements like deference to authority and group decision-making might necessitate modifying dual-literacy frameworks created in the West. The Arab League Educational Cultural and Scientific Organization (ALECSO, 2024) has recently started creating dual-literacy curricula tailored to a particular region that take into account Arabic linguistic considerations as well as Islamic ethical principles. Participants in early pilot programs in Morocco and Jordan showed encouraging outcomes improving their ability to detect financial misinformation by 38% after completing integrated literacy training.
2.5 Theoretical framework integration
This study integrates four theoretical perspectives to provide a comprehensive understanding of dual literacy effects. 1) The Framing Theory (Entman, 1993) which explains how financial misinformation is strategically constructed to bypass critical thinking. Financial scams often frame investments as “limited time opportunities” or use authority figures to create urgency and reduce scrutiny. 2) The Financial Capability Theory (Lusardi and Mitchell, 2011) offers the foundation for understanding how financial knowledge translates into protective behaviors. The theory's emphasis on the knowledge-behavior gap is mostly relevant in digital environments where decision-making occurs rapidly. 3) The Media Literacy Theory (Potter, 2010) offers frameworks for understanding how individuals process media messages. It explains how active audience engagement and critical evaluation skills directly apply to financial misinformation contexts. The last 4) Social Cognitive Theory (Bandura, 2001) is explaining how digital environments shape learning and decision-making through observational learning and self-efficacy beliefs. This theory helps in explaining why social media-based financial misinformation is particularly persuasive.
3 Research questions and hypotheses
The following research questions were designed to understand the impact of media literacy on financial misinformation and combating fraud.- RQ1: What are the current levels of financial and media literacy in selected Arab FinTech users?
- RQ2: How does dual literacy affect the ability to detect financial misinformation?
- RQ3: What roles do education, digital exposure, and FinTech training play in shaping literacy outcomes?
- RQ4: How do cultural factors moderate the relationship between dual-literacy and misinformation detection?
- RQ5: What are the most effective intervention strategies for enhancing dual literacy in Arab contexts?
To answer the above question, the following hypotheses were developed to statistically test and validate the relationship among the various factors.- H1: Dual literacy significantly predicts misinformation detection accuracy. Dual-literacy functions as a dual-shield mechanism in FinTech environments: financial literacy enables users to identify the implausibility of unrealistic returns and assess risk exposure, while media literacy allows them to deconstruct the persuasive framing techniques—urgency appeals, fabricated authority endorsements, and social proof manipulation—that fraudulent FinTech content routinely exploits. Together, these competencies create a synergistic cognitive defence that neither literacy affords independently.
- H2: FinTech exposure has a significant positive effect on dual literacy. Sustained interaction with digital financial platforms provides learning-by-doing opportunities that simultaneously sharpen financial reasoning (through direct exposure to product terms, returns, and risk structures) and media evaluation skills (through repeated encounters with promotional content, algorithmic recommendations, and peer-shared investment advice). This dual experiential learning pathway aligns with social cognitive theory's emphasis on observational and enactive mastery (Bandura, 2001), whereby skill acquisition is embedded in real-world digital contexts rather than formal instruction alone.
- H3: Education level significantly influences dual literacy. Formal education builds abstract reasoning, numerical competency, and source-evaluation skills that constitute the cognitive infrastructure underpinning both financial and media literacy. Higher educational attainment is associated with greater exposure to critical thinking frameworks, structured argumentation, and information literacy curricula—all of which enhance an individual's capacity to interrogate financial content and identify deceptive signals in digital media (Lusardi and Mitchell, 2014). In Arab contexts where secondary and tertiary curricula are increasingly integrating digital competencies (ALECSO, 2024), formal education provides a structural pathway to dual-literacy development.
- H4: Dual literacy mediates the relationship between education and misinformation detection. If dual-literacy mediates the education–detection relationship, the protective benefits of formal schooling are operationally channeled through integrated literacy competencies rather than through direct knowledge transfer alone. This mechanism has direct policy implications: enhancing the dual-literacy content of educational curricula may yield greater misinformation-detection gains than increasing general educational attainment per se . This aligns with framing theory's insight (Entman, 1993) that resistance to strategically framed financial misinformation requires domain-specific cognitive tools beyond general education.
- H5: Cultural orientation moderates the relationship between dual-literacy and misinformation detection. Specifically, within the Arab FinTech context where collectivist cultures foster group-based verification of information, culturally oriented individuals are expected to leverage social networks to cross-check suspicious financial content, amplifying the protective effect of dual-literacy against online fraud. This synergy is particularly pertinent given that social media financial scams in the MENA region disproportionately exploit collectivist trust dynamics (Al-Rashid and Hassan, 2023).
- H6: Age negatively moderates the relationship between FinTech exposure and dual literacy. Older adults encounter compounding barriers to converting FinTech engagement into dual-literacy gains, including technology anxiety, reduced cognitive flexibility in adapting to novel digital interfaces, and limited access to age-appropriate digital training resources (Mahmoud and Al-Zahra, 2024). Consequently, the same level of FinTech exposure that builds robust dual-literacy competencies in younger users may yield significantly attenuated literacy gains among older populations, making age a meaningful negative moderator of the exposure–literacy relationship in Arab digital finance contexts.
4 Methodology
4.1 Research design
This study is based on a quantitative cross-sectional survey design to investigate the relationships between financial and media literacy and misinformation detection. The dual-literacy construct was operationalized as a second-order latent factor comprising financial literacy and media literacy as first-order factors, following the approach recommended byHair et al. (2019)for hierarchical constructs. The analysis will be conducted using structural equation modeling (SEM) in the AMOS software to test the proposed hypothesized relationships amongst the constructs. To address common method bias (CMB), Harman's single-factor test was conducted, with the first unrotated factor accounting for 28.3% of the variance (well below the 50% threshold), indicating that CMB is not a significant concern in this study. As a further check, a Common Latent Factor (CLF) model was estimated in AMOS, adding a single unmeasured latent factor with paths to all manifest indicators (Podsakoff et al., 2003). The constrained factor loadings on the CLF were all small (range: .04–.18) and non-significant, confirming the absence of systematic method variance. Procedurally, CMB was also mitigated by separating item blocks with instructional filler screens, guaranteeing respondent anonymity, and using the objective Misinformation Detection Task (behavioural scoring) rather than self-report for the outcome variable.
4.2 Sample and sampling strategy
The survey targeted 750 participants from Egypt, Saudi Arabia, Jordan, UAE, and Bahrain, selected to represent different stages of FinTech development and regulatory environments. A stratified sampling approach was employed, with quotas ensuring balance across gender, age, education level, and urban/rural digital access.
4.2.1 Inclusion criteria
Participants must have regular internet access (minimum of three hours daily) and be an active user of at least one FinTech application. Furthermore, the individuals must be fluent in either Arabic or English and between 18 and 65 years of age and have been a resident in one of the target countries for a minimum of two years.
4.2.2 Exclusion criteria
We have excluded all the participants who are employed within the financial services or media sector and have had participated in a financial literacy program in past six months.
Sample size calculation: Power analysis using G*Power 3.1.9.7 software program indicated that 650 participants would provide 80% power to detect medium effect sizes (f 2 = 0.15) with α = 0.05. So, 650 is the minimum sample size to achieve a significant real-world effect. We have decided to go for a final sample of 750 to ensure adequate power for these analyses and achieve meaningful findings confidently.
4.3 Instrumentation
4.3.1 Demographics questionnaire
Comprehensive 25-item instrument capturing age, gender, education, income, employment status, digital device usage, FinTech experience, and cultural orientation measures.
4.3.2 Financial literacy scale
15-item instrument measuring financial knowledge, attitudes, and behaviors. Items cover compound interest, inflation, risk diversification, insurance, and digital payment systems. Cronbach's α = .86 in pilot testing. The originalOECD (2022)scale comprised 20 items; 15 items were retained based on item-total correlation analysis ( r > .40) and expert review for cultural relevance. Arabic translation followed a standard back-translation protocol with two bilingual subject-matter experts, and cultural equivalence was confirmed via a 30-participant cognitive interview. Pilot testing was conducted on a sample of 50 Arab FinTech users not included in the final sample.
4.3.3 Media literacy scale (adapted fromPotter, 2010)
12-item scale assessing critical evaluation skills, source verification abilities, and understanding of media production and distribution. Items adapted for social media and digital content contexts. Cronbach's α = .89 in pilot testing. The originalPotter (2010)framework provided 16 candidate items; 12 were retained based on item-total correlation screening ( r > .40) and expert review confirming relevance to digital and social media contexts prevalent in Arab FinTech ecosystems. Four items referencing broadcast and print media exclusively were replaced with equivalent digital-media items validated by a panel of three media literacy scholars. Arabic translation followed the same back-translation protocol used for the Financial Literacy Scale (two bilingual experts, reconciliation by a third), and cultural appropriateness was confirmed through a 30-participant cognitive interview with Arab FinTech users not included in the main sample. Pilot testing ( N = 50 per country; total N = 250) was conducted separately from the main data collection.
4.3.4 Digital financial misinformation detection task
12 carefully crafted vignettes presenting authentic-looking financial content, half containing misinformation. Vignettes were developed through expert consultation and pilot testing. Inter-rater reliability for correct identification = .94.
4.3.5 FinTech exposure index
8-item scale measuring frequency and depth of interaction with digital financial services including mobile banking, cryptocurrency platforms, peer-to-peer lending, and robo-advisors. Cronbach's α = .82.
4.3.6 Cultural Orientation Scale
10-item instrument measuring individualism-collectivism orientation, adapted for Arab contexts and validated byAl-Rashid and Hassan (2023). Cronbach's α = .78.
4.4 Data collection procedures
Data collection occurred over six months (September 2024–February 2025) using multiple channels to ensure representative sampling. We have employed web and mobile-optimized survey deployed through google form in both Arabic and English language. We also collaborated with universities in each target country to access diverse student and staff populations.
4.5 Data analysis
The data analysis will begin with a descriptive analysis then ANOVA, to examine group differences. AMOS will be used to conduct Confirmatory Factor Analysis (CFA) and to evaluate validity and reliability that would be followed by Structural Equation Modeling (SEM) to test the hypotheses, including model comparisons using fit indices. The study will also employ mediation analysis using bootstrapping to examine indirect effects and multi-group SEM for moderation tests.
4.5.1 Model identification
All constructs were identified through multiple indicators with factor loadings constrained appropriately. Model identification was verified through positive definite covariance matrices and proper convergence.
4.5.2 Fit assessment
Model fit was evaluated using multiple indices: χ 2 /df ratio, Comparative Fit Index (CFI), Tucker–Lewis Index (TLI), Root Mean Square Error of Approximation (RMSEA), and Standardized Root Mean Square Residual (SRMR).
4.5.3 Final model Fit indices - χ 2 /df = 1.84 (acceptable <3.0)
- CFI = .95 (good fit >.90)
- TLI = .94 (good fit >.90)
- RMSEA = .034 (excellent fit <.05)

4.6 Structural equation modeling (SEM)
SEM was employed to test the hypothesized relationships between constructs. The structural model included direct paths from education and FinTech exposure to dual-literacy, and from dual-literacy to misinformation detection. Indirect effects were tested through dual literacy as a mediator. The structural model was developed based on existing theoretical relationships and empirical evidence from extant literature. All paths were theoretically justified and statistically tested using AMOS.

The effect is significantly stronger for younger individuals (.51) than for older ones (.32) which indicates that younger people convert their FinTech usage into combined literacy skills more effectively. The interaction term for Age × FinTech Exposure was statistically significant ( β = −.09, SE = .027, t = −3.33, p = .001, ΔR 2 = .03), confirming that age significantly and negatively moderates the FinTech exposure–dual-literacy relationship. Simple slopes analysis revealed that the positive effect of FinTech exposure on dual literacy is stronger for younger users ( β = .51, p < .001) than for older users ( β = .32, p < .001), with the difference between slopes being statistically significant ( p = .001).
6 Discussion
6.1 Principal findings The results support the theoretical claim that media and financial literacy work together to affect users' capacity to recognize financial disinformation. In line with earlier research byHuang et al. (2024)dual literacy significantly predicts detection accuracy ( β = . 61 p. 001), as well asLuo et al., 2022.
Given this robust correlation, people who are proficient in both media analysis and finance are likely to be far more capable of spotting and thwarting financial disinformation. According to the mediation analysis one important way that education and exposure to FinTech affect the detection of misinformation is dual literacy.
The high percentage of mediation (52 percent for education and 64 percent for exposure to FinTech) suggests that improved literacy skills rather than direct effects are the main way that formal education and digital financial experience contribute to positive outcomes.
These findings must be understood against the dual-sided nature of digital financial technology in the Arab context. While FinTech exposure positively predicts dual-literacy (H2: β = .44, p < .001) — confirming the beneficial role of engaged platform use — the same digital environments that foster literacy competencies simultaneously expose users to sophisticated financial misinformation including AI-generated endorsements, algorithmically amplified investment scams, and social media influencer promotions of unregulated products (Arab Banking Federation, 2024). The increasingly sophisticated use of information technology, particularly social media within FinTech ecosystems, therefore, carries a genuine dual risk: it develops protective cognitive skills while also multiplying the misinformation threats those skills must defend against. The net protective benefit of FinTech exposure is thus contingent on concurrent dual-literacy development rather than exposure alone. This finding has direct implications for platform designers, who should integrate educational nudges and fraud-alert features within FinTech interfaces, and for regulators, who should mandate minimum digital-literacy disclosures at the point of user onboarding.
6.2 Country-level disparities
The differences between nations draw attention to opportunities and challenges in the context. Egypts lower performance (M = 3. 38) indicates curricular and infrastructure gaps whereas the UAEs higher literacy rates (M = 4. 18) are a result of strong regulatory frameworks and state-sponsored digital education initiatives.
These observations highlight how important it is to recognize distinct national contexts while promoting policy uniformity throughout the Arab world. The UAE and Saudi Arabia have performed well for a number of reasons including significant government spending on digital infrastructure programs requiring financial education and vigilant regulatory monitoring of the FinTech industry. On the other hand, issues facing nations with lower scores include inadequate educational resources, restricted internet access and regulatory flaws that permit the spread of false information.
6.3 Cultural considerations
Important subtleties in the way dual-literacy function in various cultural contexts are revealed by the notable moderating effect of cultural orientation. There were stronger correlations between dual-literacy and misinformation detection among those with more collectivistic orientations ( β = . 67 vs. β = . 52) indicating that cultural elements might strengthen the protective benefits of literacy.
This result is consistent with studies byAl-Rashid and Hassan (2023)that suggest community-oriented financial decision-making and group-based verification procedures may be advantageous for collectivistic cultures. The implication is that literacy interventions should be culturally tailored to leverage existing social structures and communication patterns.
6.4 Age-related patterns
The association between dual-literacy and FinTech exposure is negatively moderated by age ( β = −.19 p. 001) indicating that different strategies might be needed for older adults to acquire media and financial literacy.
Older adults may require more structured support to reap the same benefits as younger users who can more readily translate FinTech experience into literacy competencies. The Arab worlds larger problems with the digital divide are reflected in this age-related pattern. Technology anxiety decreased cognitive flexibility and restricted access to age-appropriate training materials are just a few of the obstacles that older adults frequently encounter when trying to develop their digital literacy according to research byMahmoud and Al-Zahra (2024). These results imply that age-sensitive pedagogical strategies ought to be incorporated into dual-literacy interventions.
6.5 Theoretical implications
The integrated theoretical framework integrating media literacy, financial capability framing and social cognitive theories is strongly supported by the study findings. The high explanatory power of the model (R 2 = .67 for misinformation detection) suggests that this multi-theoretical approach effectively captures the complexity of financial misinformation resistance.
The dual-literacy concept is validated as having more than just additive effects due to the substantial interaction between media literacy and financial literacy components. Perspectives from complexity theory that stress emergent properties resulting from component interactions rather than linear combinations are supported by this synergistic relationship.
6.6 Practical implications
The robust mediation effects imply that rather than treating media and financial literacy as distinct domains, educational interventions should concentrate on fostering integrated literacy competencies. This finding suggests that integrated programs may be more effective than siloed approaches which has immediate implications for curriculum design in Arab educational institutions.
Different implementation strategies are required as indicated by the variations at the national level. While nations with lower performance might need more basic infrastructure and policy investments high-performing nations like the United Arab Emirates and Saudi Arabia might concentrate on preserving and growing their current programs.
7 Implications
7.1 Implications for regulation and policy
Complete policy and regulatory reform are the cornerstone of the country's dual-literacy development. The development of Arabic-language resources and national certification standards along with the inclusion of required financial and media literacy modules in secondary and higher education curricula are the main recommendations. At the same time, it is recommended that financial regulators set up strong frameworks requiring transparency on FinTech platforms develop quick response systems to combat false information and create agreements for cross-border cooperation. This approach emphasizes the need for ongoing government funding for digital infrastructure such as increasing broadband availability and creating mobile-first learning environments in order to guarantee the initiatives efficacy and equitable reach.
7.2 Implications for education and pedagogy
For dual-literacy competencies to be successfully developed educational systems must adopt a transformative strategy. Peer-to-peer learning models and realistic simulations are used to strategically integrate media literacy and financial literacy as foundational interdisciplinary subjects. Implementing comprehensive teacher training programs that include dual-literacy pedagogy integration into pre-service teacher education and professional development for existing teachers is a crucial element. This approach also highlights the value of expanding education outside of traditional educational settings by addressing intergenerational knowledge gaps through partnerships community-based initiatives and public digital literacy centers, all of which promote a culture of lifelong learning.
7.3 Industrial implications
Proactive involvement and collaboration with important industry sectors are necessary for the successful development of dual literacy. The FinTech sector is expected to create in-app evaluation tools to support research on patterns of disinformation and integrate instructional content directly into user interfaces. Likewise, partnerships with both traditional and digital media outlets are crucial for creating specialized fact-checking materials, moral advertising standards and training courses for truthful financial reporting. This approach emphasizes a shared obligation whereby industry participation is essential to building a safer and better-informed digital ecosystem for customers going beyond simple CSR.
7.4 Implications for innovation and research
There must be a dedicated agenda for continued research and technological innovation to support a forward-looking approach. The cost-effectiveness and long-term effects of different literacy interventions on actual decision-making require longitudinal research. In order to compare results across various regions and modify programs to local contexts cross-cultural research is equally important. Finally, guaranteeing that dual-literacy programs continue to be at the forefront of educational efficacy and engagement research into cutting-edge technologies is advised. Examples of these technologies include blockchain for credential verification virtual reality for immersive experiences and artificial intelligence for personalized learning.
8 Limitations and future directions
8.1 Study limitations
This study has a few important limitations to consider while generalizing its findings. The sample of participants was mostly from cities and included people who were already comfortable with technology, so the findings might not apply as well to rural or less digitally engaged populations. The study was done at a single point in time so there is uncertainty like one factor caused another and the relationships is vice versa. Finally, the tools used to measure literacy, and misinformation has been developed in Western countries and might not perfectly capture all the cultural details of the Arab region. Moreover, people's own reports of their skills might not always be completely accurate.
8.2 Future research directions
The main goals of future research should be proving and implementing the dual-literacy concept. It is recommended that researchers should carry out controlled experiments to evaluate the effectiveness of various training programs that can further examine integrated courses format and duration. It is also quite important to look beyond test results and examine how better dual literacy affects people's actual financial behavior including the caliber of their investment choices, financial decisions and susceptibility to fraud. Future research should also examine contemporary and inter-cultural aspects. This requires creating and evaluating fresh approaches to teaching these skills like virtual reality, social media and smartphone apps. Lastly by contrasting the Arab world's experience with that of other parts of the world, it is possible to understand how various customs laws and policies affect dual-literacy and develop more efficient regionally specific teaching methods.
9 Conclusion
Dual-literacy or integrated financial and media literacy is essential for thwarting financial disinformation in Arab FinTech ecosystems as this study shows. Dual-literacy accounts for 67% of the variation in misinformation detection accuracy demonstrating the strong evidence that people with both financial knowledge and media evaluation abilities are significantly better able to recognize and fend off financial misinformation. Several significant additions to the literature are made by research.
First of all, it supports the idea of dual literacy as a synergistic blend of media literacy and financial literacy skills rather than merely additive effects. Second it illustrates the critical mediating function of dual literacy in converting experiential and educational benefits into useful resistance to misinformation. Thirdly it highlights significant contextual and cultural elements that moderate these associations offering guidance for designing culturally aware interventions. Both opportunities and challenges for regional development are highlighted by the study's findings regarding country-level disparities. The lower performance in other nations indicates the need for focused interventions and international cooperation while nations like the United Arab Emirates and Saudi Arabia show the advantages of coordinated policy approaches and infrastructure investment.
These results imply that differentiated approaches that take into consideration local contexts and abilities may be more effective than a one-size-fits-all approach to dual-literacy development. For many stakeholders the study's practical implications are important. All levels of curricula should incorporate dual-literacy competencies and educational institutions should embrace interdisciplinary frameworks rather than compartmentalized approaches. The development of literacy should be supported by infrastructure investment and educational mandates and policymakers should create comprehensive regulatory frameworks that address digital financial misinformation. Industry players such as media outlets and FinTech firms ought to take an active part in dual-literacy programs by collaborating on research creating instructional materials and conducting ethical business. As digital finance spreads throughout the Arab world dual literacy will become even more crucial. An urgent need for ongoing investment in dual-literacy development arises from the anticipated expansion of FinTech markets, changing disinformation strategies and new technological advancements. According to the research these investments have the potential to provide significant benefits for consumer protection, financial security and general economic stability. A framework for comprehending digital financial resilience in various cultural contexts is provided by the study's theoretical contributions which go beyond the Arab context.
Framing theory, financial capability theory, media literacy theory and social cognitive theory are all integrated to provide a thorough understanding of how people negotiate complex information environments. Other areas dealing with comparable issues might test and modify this framework. These findings should be expanded upon in future research by cross-cultural comparisons intervention trials and longitudinal studies. Further research into the potential of emerging technologies, the creation of more advanced measurement instruments and behavioral outcome analysis will all contribute to our growing understanding of the effects of dual literacy.
The best way to develop and execute dual-literacy interventions should also be investigated in research taking sustainability technological delivery methods and cultural adaptation into account. The ramifications of this study go beyond personal financial stability to include more general collective objectives of social cohesion economic growth and digital citizenship. As Arab societies proceed with their digital transformation dual literacy serves as a safeguard against present dangers as well as a basis for future innovation and success. For digital financial services to reach their full potential while minimizing risks, media literacy and financial literacy skills must be developed in tandem.
In summary this study offers empirical support for the crucial role dual literacy plays in the digital age as well as helpful recommendations for its advancement throughout Arab FinTech ecosystems. Teachers, legislators and business executives must act quickly to adopt integrated literacy programs that can adequately prepare the public for the opportunities and challenges of digital finance according to the findings. Arab societies can safeguard vulnerable populations from financial fraud and misinformation while simultaneously developing the human capital required for long-term digital economic growth through such concerted efforts.
