1. Introduction
Fake news can be defined as the false or fabricated or camouflaged information or rumor, either deliberate or accidental, which misleads people (Muigai, 2019). In business, fake news refers to all kinds of false contents and misleading information with or without the aim of damaging the reputation of a business (Zhang and Ghorbani, 2020). The severity and frequency of fake news on social media is growing (Bermes, 2021) and has become a serious concern (Siwakoti et al ., 2021; Bermes, 2021; Park et al ., 2021a; Velichety and Shrivastava, 2022). Particularly, during COVID-19, fake news on social media (Bunker, 2020; Dwivedi et al ., 2020; Rao et al ., 2020) relating to the shortage of essential supplies (e.g. toilet paper, sanitizer and dried foods) and images of empty shelves in supermarkets panicked consumers to purchase more. It shocked the supply chains (SC), globally (Naeem, 2021; Prentice et al ., 2020), and thus spawned another new crisis – shortage of essential items – during a crisis (Kadam and Atre, 2020; Spencer, 2020). For example, Tokyo-based “Japan household paper products industry association” and other sources propagated misleading information on social media that “masks and toilet paper are made from the same raw materials, as a result an increase in the production of masks will cause shortage of paper products,”. Such information had created huge panic buying of toilet paper rolls and created store shelfs empty (Kohei Shimizu, 2020). Similarly, following the false tweets of many people about toilet paper shortage and posting images of empty store shelves a local drugstore in Chuo Ward in the southwestern Japan city of Kumamoto experienced a sudden increase in customers buying toilet paper and tissue and the items sold out in about an hour on the evening of Feb. 27 (Kohei Shimizu, 2020). For another instance, “India's battle against the coronavirus has many obstacles – large crowds, a stretched health system and inadequate infrastructure … But beyond these, familiar foes are rearing their heads: misinformation and fake news …. Through its vast social media networks” (Purohit, 2020). Thus, social media fake news (hereafter “fake news”) becomes an important research agenda among SC researchers (e.g. Wisker, 2020; Spanaki et al ., 2021; Mustak et al ., 2023).
Fake news can have irremediable effects to firms (Atkinson, 2019) including damage to reputation and consumer trust (Di Domenico et al ., 2021). It also has the potential to cause serious SC disruptions (SCDs) (e.g. Bermes, 2021; Shanker et al ., 2021), which eventually makes SC unsustainable (Churchill, 2018) and incur financial losses of firms (Jay, 2018). Despite the serious consequences of fake news on firms, especially during crises, the literature has devoted little attention to this critical phenomenon, and research gaps remain to be fulfilled. In general, extant research mainly focused on the impact of fake news on political movement, and upon marketing and consumers (Kumar et al ., 2021; Di Domenico et al ., 2021). More specifically, first , in marketing and SC discipline, a major stream of research on fake news concerns customer behavior including panic buying (e.g. Arafat et al ., 2021; Bentall et al ., 2021). Although customer behavior may also be influential to understand SCD (Chua et al ., 2021), they are too general. We have little knowledge regarding firms' response to manage SC during disruptions through different mitigation strategies (Butt, 2021b), for example, developing resilience capability (Barua et al ., 2020). Research on this may provide us with more closely scrutinized theoretical and practical meanings. Second , research on fake news, SCD, and firm performance is fragmented. As firms need to deal with the cause-effect of SCD and apply associated mitigation strategies, it is necessary to understand the complete picture in one frame of research. Third , although SC learning (SCL) is considered as a longstanding strategy for firm performance (Ponomarov and Holcomb, 2009; Flint et al ., 2008), its role for improving dynamic capability to manage firm performance in a turbulent business context has yet to be understood properly. In a given dynamically changing environment, firms differ in terms of organizational learning (Murray and Donegan, 2003). Learning from disruptions and previous failures is imperative for firms to adapt their existing procedures and routines, which improves SCR capability (Jüttner and Maklan, 2011; Fiksel, 2015). Along this line we posit that firms' resilience capability in combination with learning capability is effective in mitigating disruptions and enhancing performance. However, prior research is limited in explaining how firms foster the combined effect of SCR and SCL to preserve performance when dealing with SCD. More specifically, we have little knowledge on whether high and low SCL have particular effects on improving dynamic capability (e.g. SC resilience) to manage performance during disruptions.
Therefore, the objective of this study is to understand how fake news during COVID-19 affect firm performance, and how the associated dynamic capabilities of firms can assist firms in this regard. Specifically, this study examines two issues from the business context when firms are exposed to severe disruptions caused by fake news. First , it examines the indirect (serial mediation) effect of fake news on firm performance via SCD and SC resilience (SCR). Second , it examines the moderated mediation effect of SCL on the relationship between SCD and SCR. Given the complexity of our research model with direct associations as well as serial mediation and moderated mediation effects, we investigate the effectiveness of using partial least squares structural equation modeling (PLS-SEM) over other competing methods.
Responding to the call for research on the impacts of fake news on SCD and firm performance (e.g. Spanaki et al ., 2021), this study has the potential to contribute to the literature in several ways. First, our research identifies the complex relationships (e.g. moderated serial mediation) among factors (e.g. fake news, SCD, SCR, SCL and firm performance) that have theoretically meaningful and statistically significant relationships. More specifically, this work enriches our knowledge by understanding how fake news enhances the resilience capability through SCD, which eventually affects firm performance. Second , this work advances our knowledge of dynamic capability view (DCV). We argue that, to tackle disruptions during crises, firms need to develop resilience, which is an essential dynamic capability for firms. Further, as an enabler of dynamic capability of firm, SCL contributes to firms' resilience capabilities to mitigate disruptions and enhance performance. Therefore, we extend the DCV by integrating the implicit role of learning in enhancing dynamic capability of firms to manage SCD. Third , this study demonstrates the suitability of using PLS-SEM path modeling to assess a complex model (i.e. with moderated serial mediation). This substantiates why PLS-SEM is considered better suited for explaining complex relationships (Chin, 2010; Hair et al ., 2022). Moreover, acknowledging Kaufmann and Gaeckler (2015)'s observation advising that PLS-SEM's potentials have not been fully exploited; our research is an initiative toward it. Finally, understanding the mechanism of fake news on SCD, SCR and SCL relationships, pragmatic implications are offered to SC practitioners.
The rest of the paper is organized in the following order. The next section discusses the background literature, leading to the research model. This is followed by discussing the research method followed by presenting the results of the data analysis. The final section discusses the findings, theoretical and managerial implications and concludes with the limitations.
2. Background and the theoretical lens
Proliferation of fake news on social media and its negative impacts have become a cause of concern for society and a burning research agenda among scholars in recent years (Tandoc, 2021). The concept of fake news is analogous with numerous issues such as hoaxes, conspiracies, propaganda, manipulation of information, satire, etc. (Tandoc et al ., 2018). In this research, social media fake news is defined as the fabricated information that are disseminated through social media platforms to deceive and mislead others (McGonagle, 2017). Fake news may have tremendous adverse effects on different facets of economic systems. For example, it may affect business reputation and financial performance of a firm (Di Domenico et al ., 2021). Given the deleterious impact of fake news during COVID-19, the World Health Organization (WHO) identified it as one of the major threats to control the spread of COVID-19 (Pulido et al ., 2020). Although numerous studies investigated the impact of fake news on social media but little has explored the impact of it on SC mechanisms (Dulam et al ., 2021).
Extant literature define SCD as unplanned and unanticipated events and their consequential effects, which disrupt the normal flow of goods and materials in a SC (Craighead et al ., 2007). SCDs are uncertain, less frequent, less controllable and unplanned but having severe impact. These can be men-made (e.g. economic downturns, technology changes, labor strikes and terrorist attacks) or natural (e.g. hurricanes) (Shekarian and Mellat Parast, 2021). From SC process perspective, SCD can be caused by supply side uncertainties (e.g. Zsidisin and Wagner, 2010), demand side issues (e.g. Chen and Xiao, 2009), and production disorder (e.g. Chopra and Sodhi, 2014; Ivanov et al ., 2017). Through men-made mechanisms, fake news creates SCD. For example, during COVID-19 pandemic, social media posts of empty shelves in supermarkets have triggered psychological stress of missing out, i.e. social panic (Chua et al ., 2021; Islam et al ., 2020). Consumer panic buying created disruptions in SC, e.g. shortage of products, delay in fulfilling customer order (Prentice et al ., 2020). It ultimately affected firm performance such as dissatisfaction of customers and losing sales (Prentice et al ., 2020). However, awareness and understanding about the threats posed by COVID-19 and setting combat strategies may help organizations and their SCs to tackle the situation and manage performance (Craighead et al. , 2020). This situational awareness and strategic action can be explained by awareness-motivation-capability (AMC) framework of Chen (1996). AMC framework theorizes that the responses by firms are shaped by the degree to which they are aware of the nature/type of threats, motivated to combat threats, and capable of effectively mitigate those. More specifically, it guides managers to adopt a systematic approach of awareness about threat, motivation to respond to threat and finally the extant of capability deployment to respond to threats.
Though AMC framework was mainly used to explain rivals' competitive moves and countermoves (e.g. Chen et al. , 2007) but apart from competitive rivalry it can be extended to explain various other threats such as threats posed by pandemics (Craighead et al. , 2020). Along this line we argue that the threats posed by social media fake news to SC during COVID-19 and the management action to tackle such situation can be explained by AMC framework. We posit that by creating awareness about social media fake news, managers can understand what combat strategies and capabilities to develop to manage performance against external threats. Otherwise, lack of understanding and awareness about threat (e.g. COVID-19 led social media fake news) may leave them unprepared and less capable to overcome unintended situations that can deteriorate organizational performance (Chen, 1996). Acknowledging the extant research on fake news, consumer panic behavior, and associated consequences, a theoretically grounded and empirically tested strategic roadmap to tackle the deleterious impact of fake news on SC is yet to be an emerging field of research. Therefore, theorizing the threat posed by social media fake news on SC and corresponding management action, in the lens of AMC framework, substantially contribute to existing body of knowledge.
Prior studies suggest that developing right capabilities are effective in managing the SCD in general (Chowdhury and Quaddus, 2017), and COVID-19 led disruptions in particular (Zheng et al ., 2021; Ali et al ., 2022). Understandably, studies have been applying the DCV to tackle SCDs. Along with AMC we propose DCV to explain the capabilities to mitigate SCDs caused by social media fake news during COVID-19. AMC framework guides a systematic process of awareness, motivation and capability deployment but it does not offer comprehensive information about capability development process for a very complex unforeseeable situation such as COVID-19. DCV, which has been widely used theoretical framework for managing complex disruptions caused by COVID-19 (e.g Ali et al ., 2022; Belhadi et al. , 2022; Kähkönen et al. , 2021), can complement the capability development perspective of AMC framework in mitigating SCD due to COVID-19 social media fake news. Dynamic capability refers to firms' ability to integrate, build and reconfigure internal and external competences to address rapidly changing environments (Teece et al ., 1997). DCV posits that organizations and their SC attempt to engender portfolios of idiosyncratic and difficult-to-imitate resources (e.g. processes, skills, structures and routines) to leverage sensing, seizing, and reconfiguring capabilities for gaining sustained competitive advantage in the event of uncertain business environment (Teece, 2007). Extant studies (e.g. Chowdhury and Quaddus, 2017) propounded that firms and their SC need dynamic capabilities to mitigate SCD. Similarly, Ali et al . (2022) ascertained that COVID-19-led SCD triggered firms and their SC to develop dynamic capabilities to mitigate disruptions and enhance competitive advantage. More specifically, Prentice et al . (2020) and Zheng et al . (2021) posit that demand planning, inventory management and order management strategies are effective in managing SCD during COVID-19. Similarly, recent studies find that flexibility is salient for minimizing COVID-19 inflicted SCD (Hobbs, 2021; Skipper and Hanna, 2009). Despite myriads of evidences of disruptions due fake news on social media and the potentials of dynamic capabilities in managing sudden disruptions (Ali et al ., 2022; El Baz and Ruel, 2021), surprisingly, the role of dynamic capability in managing fake news-led SCD to preserve performance has yet to be explained in the extant literature.
Firms and their SCs build resilience capabilities – an essential dynamic capability – when exposed to disruptions (Ali et al ., 2022). Resilience capability helps organizations and their SC to get back to normal or even better position once they are disrupted (Chowdhury and Quaddus, 2017). In line with prior studies (e.g. Chowdhury and Quaddus, 2016; Wong et al ., 2020), we define SCR as the capability of a SC to prepare, respond and recover from crises while maintaining required level of control, coherence and connectedness with the SC members. Extant literature discusses numerous enabling factors for building resilience capability. One such enabler is learning from disruptions. Flint et al . (2008) refers SCL as a capability to learn SC events actively to manage and solve SC problems. Relying on Flint et al . (2008), SCL can be defined as a process of acquiring knowledge about significant events and sharing it with SC partners so that a similar event can be managed better in future. We argue that SCL from disruptions enhances firms' ability to improve resilience capability because disruptions offer new learning opportunity for the managers to manage future disruptions more efficiently (Fiksel, 2015). Though SCL has been studied in explaining SCD and SCR, most of the studies are conceptual (e.g. Ponomarov and Holcomb, 2009; Jüttner and Maklan, 2011). Therefore, empirically testing the operational context of SCL in harnessing SCR to manage disruption is vital.
3. Hypothesis development and proposed model
As mentioned before, during COVID-19, fake news has fueled consumer panic buying, which created SCD. Fake news also have direct and tremendous consequence on firms, e.g. stock-out, customer dissatisfaction and losing profits (Prentice et al ., 2020; Zheng et al ., 2021). In separate efforts, previous studies (e.g. Di Domenico et al ., 2021; Kumar et al ., 2015; Butt, 2021b) document that SCD affect firms' financial performance and reputation. Along this line, we argue that, fake news creates SCD, which in turn affects firm performance. We, therefore, propose the first hypothesis as, H1. SCD mediates the relationship between social media fake news and firm performance.

As mentioned in hypothesis 1 that fake news creates SCDs, to minimize the detrimental effects of SCDs, empirical studies claim that building dynamic capabilities of firms is critical (El Baz and Ruel, 2021). Recently, Ali et al . (2022) demonstrate that exposure to SCD triggers firms to develop dynamic capability to manage performance during crises. It is understood that dynamic capabilities enable firms to sense disruptions, develop readiness and response mechanisms to tackle disruptions and eventually assist in recovering from crises (Ponomarov and Holcomb, 2009; Chowdhury and Quaddus, 2017). In other words, on-time intervention by adopting right strategies can mitigate SCD disruptions (e.g. Zheng et al ., 2021; Dulam et al ., 2021). Accordingly, recent studies prescribe that resilience strategy is critical for managing SCD (e.g. El Baz and Ruel, 2021). Thus, our model argues that exposure to SCD influences firms to develop SCR, which in turn improves firm performance. We, therefore, propose the following hypothesis: H2. SCD and SCR sequentially mediate the relationship between social media fake news and firm performance.

While SCD can be managed by building resilience, however, it may not be always sufficient to mitigate highly unpredictable and high impact disruptions, e.g. COVID-19 pandemic (Scholten et al ., 2019). Recognizing that SCD are inevitable, every disruption presents new learning opportunity for firms. Therefore, firms must possess SCL to adjust and adapt their existing procedures and routines to foster SCR (Jüttner and Maklan, 2011; Fiksel, 2015). Prior studies (e.g. Hung et al ., 2010) construed organizational learning as an essential antecedent for dynamic capability. Similarly, studies on SCD and SCR (e.g. Ponomarov and Holcomb, 2009; Azadegan et al ., 2019) assert that learning from past incidences is an essential capability for building SCR and enhancing firm competitiveness. In other words, learning from disruptions may set a boundary condition in influencing organizational ability to respond to disruption and build resilience capability. Along this line, our research model posits that learning from disruptions helps SC managers in reshaping and reconfiguring their response and recovery mechanisms to manage the impacts of SCD. We, therefore, propose, H3. SCL moderates the relationship between SCD and SCR.

As discussed in the previous section, existing literature exerts importance on SCL. Learning from disruptions and previous failures is imperative for firms to adapt their existing procedures and routines, which improves SCR capability (Jüttner and Maklan, 2011; Fiksel, 2015). Such resilience capability, enabled by learning from disruptions, eventually helps firms to improve performance (Ponomarov and Holcomb, 2009). We argue that firms' resilience capability coupled with learning capability is effective in mitigating disruptions and enhancing performance. In spite of the practical significance of the operational relationship between SCL, SCD and SCR, prior research is limited in explaining how firms foster SCR to preserve performance when dealing with SCD. We, thus, propose the following hypothesis, H4. A higher level of SCL strengthens the mediating effect of SCD and resilience between social media fake news and firm performance.

4. Methodology
4.1 Research design
In line with our research objective, a quantitative research design was adopted for this study. To serve our research objective of the study, at first, an extensive literature review was conducted to summarize the definition and explanation of variables (e.g. FkN , SCD, SCR and SCL) with the use of DCV. Then, a research model was proposed with argument and explanation of four main hypotheses. Next, this study applied an anonymous, self-reported online survey-based quantitative research approach. A quantitative research approach was used because it can quantify our research problem by producing data that can be transfigured into useable statistics. Further, this approach can quantify opinions, behaviors and other explained variables – and generalize outcomes from a huge sample population (Nardi, 2018).
4.2 Participants and data collection
Data have been collected from Australian retail SC, which has been disrupted and overwhelmed with many fake news and misconceptions during COVID-19 via social media. In general, fake news and misleading information is a huge concern in Australia (Park et al ., 2021b). Like worldwide (Ahmad and Murad, 2020; Al-Zaman, 2021), 23% of Australians have encountered “a great deal of” (and 30% with “some”) misinformation about COVID-19, and for 66% of people such misinformation were obtained from social media, mostly Facebook (Park et al ., 2021a, b). “Facebook has removed more than 110,000 pieces of COVID-related misinformation generated by Australian accounts in the first year of the pandemic” (Taylor, 2021). Also, the case of panic buying in all over Australia was repetitive (Chua et al ., 2021), which affected the retail SC (Ho, 2020) and firm performance (May, 2022), regularly.
We have collected responses from manufacturers, retailers, and buying agents via an online survey. We used the “List of Australia Company Profiles | IBISWorld” database that only represents firms that are entirely from Australia and New Zealand. The self-report questionnaire technique is commonly-used in SC studies (e.g. Zsidisin and Wagner, 2010; Barua et al ., 2020). The online survey tool Survey Monkey has been used to administer the survey. A total of 500 potential respondents have been approached via emails to participate in the study. A systematic random sampling method was used. Information about the research and instructions on the survey have been provided to the potential respondents on the front page of the survey. The unit of analysis was firm supervisors/managers from the chosen companies. The survey questionnaire includes demographics and the measures of the variables. Respondents' consent have been obtained before they started the survey. In total 324 respondents have responded; however, 313 are useable after screening out responses with significant missing values. The respondents' demographic profile is presented in Appendix 1.
Before data analysis, we check common method bias (CMB). We have applied procedural remedies to minimize and statistical tests to check CMB (Podsakoff et al ., 2003). Regarding procedural remedies, we have used already-developed and tested measurement items. Yet, we have conducted a pre-test with three academicians, two Ph.D. students and two SC practitioners to refine the items. In the survey, we have kept the instructions simple, clear, specific and concise. Also, respondents were not allowed to revisit the responses they already made. To minimize the threat of social desirability bias, respondents were assured in the consent form that their responses would be anonymous and kept confidential.
Regarding statistical remedies, first , Harman's one-factor test is performed. The first factor accounted for 25.683% of the variance and none stated the majority of the total variance. Second, we have applied marker variable (MV) technique. A MV (color choice: I like blue cloths, I prefer blue to other colors), which is theoretically unrelated to the nomological network, was included in the model. The result shows an insignificant effect of the MV on firm performance ( β  = 0.046, p  > 0.05). Third , in the correlation matrix, the correlation between MV and other variables are significantly below the 0.9 threshold. Finally , a t -test is conducted to examine differences between early and late respondents. We have not found any significant differences. The result indicates that our study has not been suffered from CMB.
4.3 Questionnaire and measures
The constructs in the research model have been measured with scales from existing literature where minor modifications have been made to fit the current research context. Specifically, social media fake news is measured with three items from Apuke and Omar (2021). Participants have been asked to choose the extent to which they saw hoax, made-up, and exaggerated information on social media related to COVID-19. To measure SCD , six items from Bode et al . (2011) have been adapted. The items highlight on procurement, product quality, production, responsiveness and delivery related issues. Likewise, we measure SCR with seven items from Chowdhury and Quaddus (2016) and Wong et al . (2020). The items emphasize on readiness, response, recovery, control and connectedness of SC . Next, we adapt five items from Flint et al . (2008) to measure SCL , focusing on better ways to manage disruptions. Finally, adapting from Rai et al . (2006), firm performance was measured with five items including firm's overall customer satisfaction level, service level, operational performance, sales volume and net profit . The measures are provided in Appendix 2.
Social media fake news and SCL have been modeled as reflective whereas SCD, SCR and firm performance as formative constructs. The decision of operationalizing a construct as reflective or formative is based on four major criteria: (1) direction of causality from construct to items; (2) interchangeability of items; (3) covariation among items; and (4) nomological net of construct items (Jarvis et al ., 2003). All the constructs used a five-point Likert-scale from “strongly disagree” (1), “neutral” (3) to “strongly agree” (5), except SCD. Five-point Likert scale is used because “literature suggests that five-point scale appears to be less confusing and to increase response rate” (Bouranta et al ., 2009, p. 280). The SCD used a five-point Likert-scale of “not at all” (1) to “to a very large extent” (5).
4.4 Analysis
To operationalize the research objectives and to test the hypotheses in the research model, we use variance-based SEM that is PLS-SEM. PLS-SEM is suitable for complex models (Sarstedt and Cheah, 2019) than covariance-based SEM (CBSEM) (Chin, 2010; Hair et al ., 2022; Hwang et al ., 2020), and its prediction capability is robust (Shmueli et al ., 2016; Chin et al ., 2020). It has thus become a popular method among researchers (Kaufmann and Gaeckler, 2015; Ciavolino et al ., 2022). With the following rationales, we consider PLS as the most appropriate data analysis tool for this study. First , given the complexity of our research with moderating and mediating effects (i.e. in our study model: moderated serial mediation), PLS-SEM is favorable (Becker et al ., 2022). Second , PLS modeling is based on an algorithm that firstly estimates the best weights of each block of the measurement model, and then estimates the path coefficients in the structural model (Chin and Newsted, 1999). Thus, the latent variable component scores or weight estimates depend on how well the measurement model and structural model are specified. Third , it is better suited to apply within a new context of measurement, e.g. fake news during COVID-19. Fourth, in PLS-SEM, all links (i.e. multiple dependent and independent variables) can be modeled simultaneously rather than one at a time (Gefen et al ., 2000). These causal modeling techniques by using PLS-SEM have made it possible for scholars to simultaneously test theory and measures (Hulland, 1999). Fifth, the capability of handling formative as well as reflective constructs is one of the greatest incentives for adopting PLS (Chin, 1998). Finally, PLS is popular among researchers because it can handle complex model analysis with small sample sizes under non-normality conditions (Chin, 1998), which suits the sample size and constructs of this study.
Nevertheless, to handle a complex model like ours, previous studies deployed a combination of PLS-SEM and SPSS PROCESS (e.g. Chowdhury et al ., 2022; Chowdhury and Quaddus, 2021) or AMOS and SPSS PROCESS (e.g. Osei et al ., 2019; Fatima et al ., 2017) to assess the measurement and structural model, respectively. As far the authors' knowledge go, this current research is a sole initiative to apply a single method, e.g. PLS-SEM in testing and analyzing a moderated serial mediation model. Furthermore, PLS-SEM assists this research to explore how well various antecedents and their interactions explain firm performance instead of testing and confirming an extant theory (Hair et al ., 2011).
5. Results
5.1 Assessment of the measurement model
For data analysis, we have used SmartPLS 3.2.7 (Ringle et al ., 2015). For the reflective constructs, we assess internal consistency reliability, convergent validity and discriminant validity tests. Internal consistency is checked with Cronbach's α and composite reliability (CR). The α values are 0.867 and 0.826 for FkN and SCL , respectively. Similarly, the CR for FkN is 0.919, which is 0.878 for SCL . The values of both α and CR are higher than the 0.7 threshold for both constructs (Hair et al ., 2022). Next, to check convergent validity, we consider the outer loadings of the indicators and the average variance extracted (AVE). Outer loading of each item of both FkN and SCL satisfy the ≥ 0.6 threshold (Igbaria et al ., 1995) (See Table 1). Similarly, the AVE values of FkN (0.790) and SCL (0.594) are well above the required minimum level of 0.50. Finally, we have relied on three measures of discriminant validity. First , in the cross-loading matrix, the outer loadings of FkN are greater than their correlations on SCL and vice versa. Second , we have applied the Fornell-Larcker criterion, which shows that the correlation between FkN and SCL is 0.309, which is less than the square root of the AVE values of FkN (0.889) and SCL (0.771). Finally , as Henseler et al . (2015) suggest, we have assessed the heterotrait-monotrait ration (HTMT) of the correlations. In terms of discriminant validity, HTMT value for SCL is 0.380, which is less than the 0.85 threshold.
For the formative constructs, first we check for collinearity of indicators by looking at their indicators' VIF values. According to the results in Table 1, fkn_2 has the highest VIF (2.375); hence, VIF values are uniformly below the threshold value of 5 (Hair et al ., 2022). Next, we analyze the outer weights for their significance and relevance. Table 1 summarizes the results for the formative constructs by showing the outer weight estimates, t values, and p  values. Looking at the significance levels, we find that all formative indicators are significant at a 5% level, except scd_2, scd_5, scd_6, scr_2, scr_3, scr_5, and frp1, frp3, frp4 . However, the respective p values of these indicators are clearly below 0.05 (see Table 1), suggesting that all loadings are significant at a level of 5%. Moreover, prior studies provide support for the relevance of these indicators for capturing the respective constructs (Bode et al ., 2011; Chowdhury and Quaddus, 2016; Wong et al ., 2020). Thus, following Hair et al . (2022), we retain the indicators in the formative constructs, even though their outer weights are not significant. Considering the results, all reflective and formative constructs exhibit satisfactory levels of quality. Thus, we can proceed with the testing of the structural model.
5.2 Assessment of the structural model
To evaluate the structural model, we check the R 2 value of the endogenous variables. The R 2 values of SCD, SCR , and FrP are 0.263, 0.433, and 0.362, respectively, representing that the model has good prediction accuracy. Then, to measure the model's predictive relevance, we estimated the Stone-Geisser Q 2 value by using the blindfolding method. The Stone-Geisser Q 2 values of SCD, SCR , and FrP are 0.148, 0.142, and 0.144, respectively, which are larger than zero and indicate an acceptable level of predictive relevance.
We further assess our model's predictive relevance by presenting “out-of-sample” predictions. For this, we have used PLSpredict , which checks a model's accuracy when predicting the outcome value of new cases (Motamarri et al ., 2020; Shmueli et al ., 2019; Wong et al ., 2022). We have used PLSpredict with 10 folds and 10 repetitions to mimic how the PLS model could be used to predict a new observation. Comparing the RMSE values from the PLS-SEM analysis with the naïve linear model (LM) benchmark, we find that the PLS-SEM analysis produces lower prediction errors for all the indicators. For example, when using PLS-SEM to estimate the model, the indicators of FrP have RMSE values of 1.013, 0.939, 0.898, 0.862, and 1.055 whereas the LM produces RMSE values of 1.025, 0.947, 0.924, 0.877, and 1.063 for these indicators. Similar results were observed for other endogenous variables, i.e. SCD and SCR. The results show that our model has higher predictive accuracy (Hub, 2021).
We then evaluate the structural model (path coefficient, t values, and p values obtained from bootstrapping). First, we evaluate the direct effects . The results show that, FkN has non-significant impact on FrP ( β  = 0.077, t  = 1.422, p  > 0.05); it has a significant positive impact on SCD ( β  = 0.512, t  = 9.314, p  < 0.001). Similarly, SCD increases SCR ( β  = 0.544, t  = 8.265, p  < 0.001); however, it reduces firm performance but not significantly ( β  = − 0.009, t  = 0.084, p  > 0.05). Finally, SCR has positive impacts on FrP ( β  = 0.583, t  = 6.482, p  < 0.001).
Next, we evaluate the mediation effects . A non-significant indirect effect has been confirmed from FkN to FrP through SCD ( β  = −0.005, t  = 0.083, p  > 0.05). Hence, H1 is rejected. We further found that the path from FkN to FrP through SCD and SCR (i.e. three-staged path of FkN → SCD → SCR → FrP ) is significant ( β  = 0.163, t  = 4. 489, p  < 0.001); thus, H2 is supported.
Next, we run the moderation test using SmartPLS's “moderating effect” option by selecting two-staged calculation method, standardized product term generation, and automatic weighing mode. The path coefficients shows that SCL positively moderates the relationship between SCD and SCR ( β  = 0.228, t  = 3.838, p  < 0.001); hence, our H3 has been accepted. SmartPLS also generates simple slope analysis for the moderating effect; see Appendix 3 for the slope plot. We also have performed a pseudo-F test to assess the effects of excluding SCL from the model, along with the resulting change in variance explained for FrP . For this, we perform the effect size of the endogenous variable of the research model (i.e. FrP ) with respect to the exogenous variable (i.e. SCL ). It appears that the f 2 effect size is “small” [1] (0.025), which means the exclusion of SCL from the model leads to a drop in variance (even though less significantly) to explain FrP . Finally, the moderated mediation – the conditional indirect effect of FkN on FrP , through SCD and SCR at different levels of SCL – is statistically significant ( β  = 0.106, t  = 2.448, p  < 0.05). The conditional indirect effect results show that the dependent indirect effect decreases as the moderator increases. Thus, our H4 is accepted.
6. Discussion and implications
6.1 Summary of results
The main objective of this study is to examine a moderated serial mediation model by considering the several variables–SCD, SCR, FkN and SCL. Fundamentally, this study has successfully test a moderated serial mediation model by establishing four hypotheses. First, this study confirms the none-meditating role of SCD between FkN and firm performance (H1). This finding is different form past studies (e.g. Di Domenico et al ., 2021; Kumar et al ., 2015; Butt, 2021b). Second , this study confirms the sequential meditating role of SCD and SCR between FkN and firm performance (H2). This finding is consistent with previous studies (e.g. El Baz and Ruel, 2021; Zheng et al ., 2021; Dulam et al ., 2021). Third, this study also confirms the moderating role of SCL between SCD and SCR (H3). This result is similar to previous studies (e.g. Ponomarov and Holcomb, 2009; Azadegan et al ., 2019). Finally , our study confirms the moderated serial mediation hypothesis (H4). This result is supported by past studies (e.g. Ponomarov and Holcomb, 2009; Azadegan et al ., 2019).
Overall, our results show that fake news can reduce the performance of firms (Atkinson, 2019) through disrupting SC (Churchill, 2018). However, during SCD, firms acquire and develop dynamic capabilities, specifically resilience capabilities, to minimize the impact of disruptions (Ali et al ., 2022; Chowdhury and Quaddus, 2017), which ultimately assist them to perform better. Logically, SCD have greater effect on SCR for the firms with high learning capability compared to those firms who are laggards on learning (Jüttner and Maklan, 2011; Fiksel, 2015). As a causal (mediation) link, we summarize that fake news increase disruption, which incentivize firms to develop resilience strategies to enhance firm performance. More interestingly, the mediation link works better for the firms with better learning capability. As a causal (serial mediation) link, we summarize that fake news increase disruption, which incentivize firms to develop resilience strategies to enhance firm performance.
6.2 Theoretical implications
This study offers several important theoretical implications. First, as far the authors' knowledge are concerned, this is one of the first studies (if not the unique) to investigate the effect of fake news during COVID-19 on firm performance mediated via SCD and SCR, and thus expands our knowledge. Most of the previous studies have considered fake news as a driver of SCD (e.g. Churchill, 2018) and inhibiter for firm performance (e.g. Atkinson, 2019). In a similar vein, studies relate SCD and SCR with firm performance (e.g. El Baz and Ruel, 2021; Prentice et al ., 2020). However, the complex relationships of these factors are not explained well in SC literature with empirical data. Our study advances a step further and attempts to investigate the ultimate effect of fake news on firm performance with complex causal relationships. Based on the aspects of DCV, we both theoretically and empirically demonstrate that SCL and SCR have particular effects on managing firm performance when they are affected by SCD due to fake news.
Second , one of the excuses of not dealing such a complex model in the past could be the scarcity of easy to use data analysis tool to explain the complex relationships. This study shows the efficacy of PLS-SEM in handling a complex model with moderated serial mediation relationships. Leveraging the integral functions of SmartPLS 3.2.7 , we prove that the models with complex relationships on SC domain can readily be handled using PLS-SEM. It thus explains the rigor of PLS path modeling and shows the advantage of using it to produce robust solutions. This paper additionally serves future researchers demonstrating how to handle a moderated serial mediation model.
Third , the current study applies dynamic capability as a mechanism to manage SCD emanating from fake news, and thus extends existing knowledge. Specially, DCV has been extensively employed to explain firm performance in the wake of dynamic changes in business environment (e.g. Teece, 2007; Teece et al ., 1997), and enhancing SCR to mitigate SCDs (e.g. Ali et al ., 2022). Surprisingly, despite myriads of evidences of SCD due to fake news, the role of dynamic capability has been under explored. This current study formally assesses it to illuminate firms' and their SCs' responses toward fake news and the associated disruptions. Thus, this study extends the generalizability of DCV to manage social media-led disruptions in the field of SC and provides an insightful theoretical lens for future research.
6.3 Pragmatic implications
Our study explores the effects of SC dynamic capabilities on eliminating the effect of fake news on firm performance. The results provide insightful practical implications for SC practitioners. First , it has been observed as well as empirically proven that fake news creates SCD. As a counter-measure of fake news, SC communications managers should devise appropriate communication strategies to minimize its effects. For example, to combat with fake news on the shortage of supplies during COVID-19 (that leading to panic buying), the chief executive officers of supermarkets sent emails to subscribed customers, posted ads, and posted short videos on social media, etc. and assured the consumers (Kelly, 2020; Davey, 2020). In a separate effort, the social media platforms should take out the fake news as soon as they are identified, as Facebook does (Taylor, 2021). Finally, the respective government agencies should identify the fake news and inform its citizens. For example, during COVID-19, Australian government agencies warn citizens about various forms of fake news flooded on social media regarding lockdowns (McLeod, 2021), supermarket closures (Swain, 2021), and more.
Second , our study suggests that SCL is a “must have” strategy when it comes to enhance SCR and firm performance. It also contributes to minimize the consequences of fake news on firm performance. Thus, we suggest that SC managers should seriously consider strategies to promote SCL, as this is a key to enabling organizational resilience toward SCD. The more and quicker a firm learns, the more it is capable of managing the disruptions (Flint et al ., 2008). Such learning processes should inspire acquiring new knowledge as well as disseminating the extant knowledge with SC practitioners so that everyone has a clear picture about the SCD and the mechanisms to handle it. In this instance, for example, continuous and instant share of learning from every day operations, with respect to managing SC, among SC stakeholders (e.g. product replenishment) while responding to SCD can be useful. Retail managers may share demand-related information about fast moving products with the SC members so that all network members revise their capacity plan to adapt with the new demand pattern. In summary, SCL can pave a new path for SC managers to tackle SCD through building SCR.
Third , our study conveys an important message to the SC managers that build resilience capabilities based on SCL. More specifically, SC managers can focus on improving readiness mechanisms such as training staffs to tackle crises, forecasting demand and contingency planning, etc. (Chowdhury and Quaddus, 2017). They may also adopt various response and recovery strategies as follows: (1) demand management by rationing product purchase (e.g. not more than two toilet rolls at a time); (2) supply management (Ali et al ., 2022) i.e. maintaining multiple suppliers, sourcing from both local and international suppliers; (3) inventory management (Zheng et al. , 2021) i.e. careful decision of order quantity and safety stock; (4) logistics management, i.e. maintaining third-party logistical services as well if own logistical support is not enough to maintain over demand; (5) improve connectedness with the SC network members by enhancing information sharing, communication and collaboration; and (6) monitoring and controlling overall SC performance. As SCR improves firm performance, SC managers need to continuously review their resilience strategies (Butt, 2021a), based on new learning from disruption and check appropriate strategies to preserve and improve performance.
6.4 Limitations and future research direction
Despite its significant implications, our study has several limitations that advise future research directions. First, this research has collected data from a cross-sectional survey. As fake news and its related impacts on SC and firm performance are dynamic in nature, future research can adopt a longitudinal design. In this regard, we can compare the effects of fake news at the early stage of COVID-19 with current times given that consumers may not react to fake news on a constant fashion over periods, and thus SCD may either not happen or happens with lesser impacts. Second, as our study has centered on three social media (i.e. Facebook, Twitter and Instagram); hence, we cannot generalize the results, which can be different for other social media platforms (e.g. WhatsApp). Interrogating our results with data collected from other social media is warranted. Third, we have collected and used data from one country (i.e. Australia). Given the prominence of fake news in Australia during COVID-19, it is a plausible representative sample. Some other countries alike (e.g. the UK, New Zealand) have experienced similar circumstances. Yet, we cannot generalize the results. The consumers and SC of a developing country may behave differently for various demographic, economic and cultural differences (e.g. Bentall et al ., 2021). For example, consumers in Iraq have not changed their buying habits because of social media fake news and have not participated in panic buying (Arafat et al ., 2021). Therefore, future studies are needed, which can incorporate other relevant factors (e.g. SC innovation, socio-demographic variables) before claiming generalizability of our findings. Finally, the issues related to impact of fake news on firm performance should also be examined in relation to emerging technologies such as artificial intelligence and metaverse (Dwivedi et al. , 2019, 2022). For example, future studies should explore “how does the misinformation, disinformation, and fake news share and spread in the metaverse?” (Dwivedi et al ., 2022, p. 48) and what impact it can have on firm performance.
7. Conclusion
This research investigates the impact of fake news on firm performance. In addition, to combat with the negative consequences of fake news (e.g. SCD), we have explained how SCL and SCR can assist firms to perform well. Drawing on DCV and by applying PLS-SEM, this study provides unique empirical evidence of indirect mediation effect of fake news on firm performance through SCD and SCR, which is further moderated by SCL. The findings of this research provide important insights and beneficial implications for SC stakeholders and researchers. In short, we now have a better understanding, from SC perspective, on the effect of fake news on SC and the required SC strategies to tackle the consequences of fake news. Yet, further research is required to understand the effect of fake news on upstream and downstream SCs separately and thus devise different strategies.
