Introduction
Fake news is defined as “fake stories that might appear to be news, spread on the Internet or other media” 1 , and such fabricated content often resembles actual news, making it difficult for audiences to distinguish between truth and falsehood 1 . Globally, the rapid rise of fake news on social networking sites (SNS) has become a significant public health concern 2 . In today’s digital era, SNS function not only as spaces for communication but also as primary sources of news, education, entertainment, and political discourse 3,4 . However, algorithms designed to maximise user engagement frequently amplify sensational or emotionally charged content at the expense of factual accuracy, fuelling the spread of fake news at rates that outpace verified content and creating “echo chambers” where falsehoods are reinforced and normalised 5, 6, 7, 8–9 . Large-scale analyses show that false news spreads six times faster than verified information and that 70% of users struggle to distinguish truth from falsehood. As Marcia McNutt, President of the US National Academy of Sciences, cautioned, “Misinformation is worse than an epidemic: It spreads at the speed of light throughout the globe, and can prove deadly when it reinforces misplaced personal bias against all trustworthy evidence.” 10 .
Given this global concern, international surveys further underscore the magnitude of the problem. For instance, a 2025 Pew Research Center study across 35 countries reported that over 80% of surveyed expressed concern about made-up news, while earlier research in 11 emerging economies found that around 70% of SNS users frequently encounter fake news at least occasionally in most countries 11,12 . Beyond its prevalence, exposure to fake news erodes trust and contributes to various mental health problems, such as stress, anxiety, and confusion, as well as poorer sleep quality (SQ) and health-related quality of life (HRQOL) 13, 14, 15, 16–17 . These patterns highlight the double-edged nature of SNS. Indeed, alongside the undeniable benefits of SNS, such as rapid access to global information, instantaneous connectivity across borders, and opportunities for civic engagement, it also accelerates the spread of harmful falsehoods that undermine public health, social trust, and mental well-being. Coping responses to fake news, such as ignoring, verifying, sharing, or disengaging, may mitigate or exacerbate these harms. Yet evidence suggests that adolescents and young adults, the most intensive users of SNS, often lack the digital literacy, critical thinking skills, and support systems required to employ effective strategies 18, 19–20 . Understanding the extent of exposure to fake news, the coping mechanisms adopted, and their implications for mental well-being in this population is therefore a pressing public health priority, particularly in rapidly digitalising settings such as Vietnam 21 .
The issue is especially pressing in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), where rapid digitalisation has often outpaced advances in media literacy and mental health infrastructure. In Vietnam, with internet penetration exceeding 78.8% and nearly universal mobile connectivity, adolescents and young adults spend on average 4–6 h daily online, primarily on Facebook, Zalo, and YouTube 22, 23–24 . These platforms offer opportunities for learning and connection but also expose youth to unverified content at scale. Meanwhile, school-based digital literacy education remains limited, and structural barriers in mental health services, including stigma and inadequate resources, further heighten risks 25 . Despite the global recognition of these challenges, empirical research on how Vietnamese adolescents and young adults experience fake news, how they cope with it, and how it affects their mental health is still scarce. This study addresses this gap by examining the extent of fake news exposure, coping strategies, and associated psychological outcomes among Vietnamese adolescents and young adults, providing important information for future strategies that strengthen digital literacy and mental health support in rapidly digitalising contexts.

Regarding SNS usage characteristics, 51.5% of participants had used SNS for more than 5 years, followed by those who had used it for 3–5 years with 37.8%. Approximately 60% of participants spent more than 6 h daily on the Internet (29.8% used 6–8 h, and 29.7% more than 8 h). Meanwhile, those who used SNS for 3 to 5 h daily accounted for the highest proportion (36.1%). Furthermore, there were significant differences ( p  < 0.5) in the average amount of time spent using the Internet and SNS between participants who occasionally/regularly/always or never/rarely encountered fake news, with the higher average usage time being witnessed in the former group.
Table 2 summarises participants’ experiences, coping strategies regarding fake news, and mental health characteristics of participants. Regarding coping strategy, nearly half of the participants (47.1%) reported ignoring fake news at least occasionally, whereas only a small proportion actively intervened to prevent its spread (16.2%) or report to share it (7.1%). With respect to mental health, the mean PHQ-9 and GAD-7 scores were 7.4 (SD = 6.2) and 4.9 (SD = 5.3), respectively. Importantly, participants who reported frequent exposure to fake news demonstrated significantly higher scores of depression and anxiety, alongside differences in coping strategies and digital literacy domains, compared with those reporting little or no exposure ( p  < 0.05).

Discussion
This study is perhaps one of the first comprehensive assessments of fake news exposure, coping strategies, and associated mental health outcomes among adolescents and young adults in Vietnam. Most participants reported encountering fake news on SNS, with regular exposure associated with higher PHQ-9 and GAD-7 scores. Poorer mental health was observed among those with higher daily internet or SNS use, particularly excessive users, and greater exposure risk was linked to longer online engagement and higher problematic internet use. In terms of coping, frequent or excessive SNS users were more likely to share fake news, whereas individuals with larger online networks were more likely to engage in active interventions to counter its spread. Notably, participants with greater digital literacy reported more frequent exposure, possibly reflecting greater awareness and recognition of fake news. This study provides critical evidence for designing targeted interventions that contribute to the cope with fake news and mitigate its negative impacts on mental well-being among young populations in Vietnam.
The current findings revealed that most participants encountered fake news on social networking sites at least occasionally, a prevalence comparable to global estimates. Data from the Pew Research Center in 11 emerging economies place Vietnam among the highest-burden countries, with 72% of respondents reporting at least occasional exposure to fake news, alongside similarly high figures in Tunisia (78%), Lebanon (75%), and Jordan (71%) 12 , underscoring that fake news being problems globally. Interestingly, greater digital literacy was linked to more frequently reported exposure to fake news. This is likely because people with higher digital literacy can recognise and distinguish fake news and misinformation more effectively 26,27 . As a result, they may report seeing fake news more often, not because they are exposed to more of it, but because they can identify it when it appears. Therefore, alongside efforts to control or limit the spread of fake news on the Internet and SNS, there should be more emphasis on educational programmes that strengthen individuals’ capacity to navigate these platforms, fostering skills such as accurate recognition, critical thinking, and appropriate responses.
One of the key findings that arose from this work was how the excessive use of SNS and the internet, and frequency of encountering fake news might be associated with one’s mental health, as reflected in higher PHQ-9 and GAD-7 scores. This aligns with a growing body of evidence showing that high-intensity online engagement, particularly in unregulated information environments, can contribute to psychological distress through mechanisms such as cognitive overload, exposure to emotionally charged or polarising content, and disruption of sleep and offline social connections 28, 29–30 . Repeated encounters with fake news may amplify uncertainty, erode trust, and heighten anxiety, especially among young users who rely on these platforms as primary sources of news 31 . These findings underscore the need for integrated interventions that address both digital well-being and fake news resilience, including time management strategies, mental health literacy, and media literacy training to reduce the psychological burden associated with persistent online fake news exposure.
An important contribution of this study lies in its examination of coping strategies for fake news, an area that remains unexplored in previous literature. In the current study, we distinguished behavioural responses into three types: ignoring, intervening to prevent the spread, and joining in the dissemination of false content. The predominance of ignoring behaviour in our sample is consistent with earlier studies 32,33 , which found that passive non-engagement is a common default response when users encounter fake news. Previous qualitative research shows that respondents often choose to scroll past or deliberately ignore suspicious content on SNS, partly to avoid reinforcing algorithmic “echo chambers” and partly due to low confidence in distinguishing real from fake news, particularly among younger teens 32,34,35 . However, there is the fact that ignoring may only reduce the extent of amplifying false content in the moment; it does little to correct fake news within one’s network and may allow false narratives to persist unchallenged.
In contrast, active interventions such as challenging fake news or sharing corrective information were fewer. Similar to prior studies, low intervention rates may stem from limited self-efficacy, uncertainty about corrective strategies, and reluctance to confront others online 36 . Social concerns emerged as a central barrier: participants feared negative evaluations, conflict, or damaging relationships, reflecting broader evidence that users carefully manage their online identities and tend to avoid behaviours that might threaten social harmony 37, 38–39 . Effort and interest considerations also shaped decisions; adolescents often viewed challenging as requiring excessive time and cognitive effort compared to the perceived benefit, consistent with cost–benefit perspectives such as Protection Motivation Theory 40 . Additionally, prosocial concerns, such as avoiding embarrassment or offense to peers, and the perceived irrelevance of certain content further reduced the likelihood of corrective action. These barriers mirror findings from Gurgun et al. (2024), who identified multiple personal and contextual factors shaping reluctance to challenge fake news 41 . Overall, our results support the perspective that even when the internet and SNS users recognize fake news, coping strategies remain skewed toward avoidance rather than constructive correction. This indicates a critical need for interventions that strengthen self-efficacy and equip young people with both the skills and the social support to engage proactively. Encouraging positive norms around respectful correction, providing accessible fact-checking resources, and integrating digital literacy training into schools may help shift coping strategies from passive ignoring toward active and confident engagement with fake news.
Notably, a minority of participants reported sharing fake news, whether intentionally or unintentionally. This aligns with broader evidence indicating that SNS users rarely disseminate false content, especially with the deliberate aim of deceiving others 42 . Rather, sharing appears to be shaped by emotional reactions, social pressures, or the perceived alignment of content with pre-existing beliefs, as documented in prior behavioural studies. Notably, our finding that prolonged SNS was associated with stronger endorsement of fake news echoes the “echo chamber” effect, whereby repeated exposure to homogenous content reinforces existing convictions, including false ones 43,44 . Though only a few instances of fake news sharing, this highlights an important dimension of coping strategies. For example, unlike ignoring or challenging, which are defensive or corrective in nature, sharing might inadvertently contribute to the amplification of fake news. This phenomenon reflects the complex social dynamics of online communication, where sharing serves not only as information dissemination but also as a means of signalling group identity and belonging. Previous research suggests that when content resonates with personal beliefs or community norms, users may overlook accuracy in favour of social alignment 45 . This suggests that sharing behaviour cannot be understood solely as a matter of individual choice but is also shaped by structural features of online environments. These insights reinforce the need for interventions that address not only the cognitive aspects of fake news detection but also the relational and motivational drivers of sharing. Equipping adolescents with critical evaluation skills, while simultaneously fostering norms of accuracy-oriented sharing, is crucial to curbing the amplification of fake news.
Based on the current findings, several implications emerge at the policy, programmatic, individual, and healthcare system. At the policy level, national strategies should prioritize the integration of digital literacy into formal education and establish clearer responsibilities for SNS platforms in curbing the spread of fake news. Embedding curricula on fake news detection and response within school systems would strengthen adolescents’ resilience and equip them to navigate increasingly complex information environments. At the programmatic level, community-based initiatives and extracurricular interventions are needed to enhance critical thinking, encourage deliberate “critical ignoring” rather than passive avoidance, and provide accessible verification tools alongside safe spaces for dialogue to reduce social pressures when confronting fake news. A notable example is Quang Ninh province’s pilot model “Safe Social Media Use for Students,” which has demonstrated promising results in raising awareness and shaping positive online behaviors, and is now being scaled up to other locations and target groups 46 . Importantly, interventions at this level should be tailored to socio-cultural contexts, traditions, and community practices. In settings where family influence and community norms play a central role, involving parents, teachers, and local organizations can enhance program uptake and sustainability. At the individual level, adolescents should be empowered to build confidence in verifying information and adopt proactive coping strategies, shifting from emotionally driven or socially pressured sharing to accuracy-oriented behaviors. Finally, at the healthcare level, professionals can serve as trusted sources of guidance by incorporating digital literacy into consultations. In resource-limited settings, this may be operationalized through brief counseling during routine visits, the use of simple educational materials in waiting areas, or collaboration with community health workers to reinforce safe online practices. Coordinated actions across these levels hold the potential to cultivate a healthier online communication environment, mitigate the amplification of false information, and strengthen the overall information health of communities.
There are several strengths of this work. We used an online platform to disseminate the questionnaire, and we managed to sample a good proportion of participants. We also managed to not only capture their SNS usage characteristics but also their experience with fake news and how they cope with it. While there are clear strengths, there remain several limitations. Firstly, we used snowball sampling methods and an online mechanism to recruit participants. While there might be a large sample of participants, this sampling approach may cause selection bias, which restricts the generalizability and does not accurately reflect the diversity of the entire Vietnamese population. Secondly, the cross-sectional nature of the study limits the ability to infer causal relationships. Furthermore, the questionnaire was based on self-reports that are subjected to biases, such as recall bias, where participants may not accurately remember or report past events, leading to distorted responses. Lastly, previous evidence has highlighted the role of potential confounding variables, such as socioeconomic status, family background, and pre-existing mental health conditions, in shaping both exposure to fake news and mental health outcomes 47, 48, 49–50 . These factors were not assessed in the current study. Therefore, future research should incorporate such variables to provide a more comprehensive understanding of the observed associations. Furthermore, to mitigate biases inherent in self-reported data, future studies could employ objective measures (e.g., face-to-face interviews, in-depth interviews), employing validated instruments, and ensuring anonymous data collection to reduce social desirability bias. In addition, probability sampling methods should be applied to ensure diversity and representativeness, and longitudinal designs are needed to clarify the long-term effects of fake news exposure on mental health.
Conclusions
In conclusion, this study underscores the high exposure of adolescents and young adults to fake news and the multifaceted strategies they employ to cope with fake news on SNS. The findings highlight an urgent need for interventions that develop digital literacy, promote accuracy-oriented norms, and equip young people with the skills and confidence to adopt proactive, informed strategies in navigating today’s digital information environment. A coordinated call to action is needed from educators, policymakers, and healthcare providers to translate these insights into sustainable practices that safeguard the information health of young people.
Materials and methods
Study design and participants
In the period spanning from September to October 2021, an online cross-sectional survey was conducted within three Vietnamese cities, namely Hanoi, Tuyen Quang, and Can Tho. This research is part of a survey by the Vietnam Youth Research Institute on issues related to current trends in using and communicating via social networks among young people. Vietnam Youth Research Institute focuses on research, organization, and management of scientific research on youth and Youth Unions with a network covering throughout Vietnam 51 . In this study, participants invited to participate in the study have fully explained the content, purpose, and benefits when participating in the study. Participation in research is completely voluntary. Participants can refuse to participate in the study at any time. During the participation process, they can still withdraw from the study if the research they feel uncomfortable.
Eligible participants for this research were identified based on the following inclusion criteria: (a) residency within the geographical boundaries of Vietnam; (b) having the capacity to provide informed consent, thereby signifying their willingness to participate in the study; (c) being able to access the survey online link by using using a computer/mobile device; (d) having not ever been diagnosed by physicians with severe cognitive impairments, such as those who were diagnosed with advanced stages of dementia or intellectual disabilities that significantly hinder cognitive functions.
Out of a total of 1929 individuals invited to participate in the study, a 95.9% completion rate was achieved, with 1850 respondents completing the questionnaire.
Sampling and recruitment
This study employed the snowball sampling methodology in a four-step procedure, as follows:
- The study was initiated by targeting student union presidents of educational institutions as core groups.

- Subsequently, these individuals were provided with a website link containing comprehensive information about the study and the survey instrument.

- Their participation extended to disseminating the provided link to others in their network through various channels such as email, SNS, and other online platforms.

- The ensuing participants perpetuated this iterative process by participating in the survey and actively promoting its completion among their connections.


Measure and instruments
Our methodological approach to developing the research instrument followed a systematic framework to ensure both reliability and coherence. First, we conducted a comprehensive review of the literature on fake news and coping strategies, as well as their impacts on mental well-being, which informed the development of a contextually relevant instrument tailored to the Vietnamese setting. Regarding the translation process, for selected scales such as PHQ-9, GAD-7, and BSMAS, an approach called “back translation” was applied to ensure that translations into Vietnamese meant the same questions as the original questionnaire. Particularly, a translator translated these scales from the source language (English) into the target language (Vietnamese). After that, a different translator translated the scale from the target language back into the source language. This process was looped until the meaning of the back translation was aligned with the source language.
After drafting the initial questionnaire, we invited a multidisciplinary panel of experts in public health, psychology, health services, policymaking, and linguistics. The panel collaboratively refined, translated, tested, and simplified the instrument to enhance clarity and validity. The final version consisted of five components: (1) socio-demographic characteristics, (2) internet and SNS usage, and digital literacy, (3) experience with fake news and recognition of fake news, (4) coping strategies with fake news, and (5) depression and anxiety (PHQ-9 and GAD-7).
Before the formal questionnaire distribution, a preliminary pilot testing phase was conducted involving 20 students in the network of the Vietnam Youth Research Institute with various age groups, genders, and demographic backgrounds. During this stage, the issues related to the language, logical order, and meaning of each research question, as well as technical issues with the online platform, were thoroughly tested and revised until the final version of this instrument was approved by all 20 students. Before officially starting the data-collecting process, the pilot data was removed. For recruitment, the link to the questionnaire was then disseminated to all participants. The completion of the questionnaire typically necessitated approximately 10 to 15 min of participant engagement.
In this study, the survey instrument was administered via an online platform, specifically employing Survey Monkey (surveymonkey.com). The selection of this platform was underpinned by several advantages, including its efficiency in terms of time and cost, as well as its user-friendly interface, enhancing the participant experience. Additionally, this approach allowed access to a broader and more diverse sample of respondents.
Demographic and socioeconomic status
Respondents provided information on their age, gender, residential location, and occupation.
SNS usage and digital literacy
Participants’ SNS engagement was assessed through several indicators. The average daily duration of use was reported on a four-point scale (< 2 h, 3–5 h, 6–8 h, > 8 h). In addition, the size of participants’ online networks was measured by the number of people connected or added through SNS, categorised as < 150, 150–300, 300–500, and > 500 contacts. Digital literacy was evaluated using 15 items covering two domains: basic digital literacy (7 items) and advanced digital literacy (8 items). Each item was rated on a 5-point Likert scale (1 = “Strongly disagree” to 5 = “Strongly agree”). Domain-specific scores were calculated by summing item responses, with higher scores indicating greater digital literacy. The Cronbach’s alpha of each domain was good at 0.91 for the basic domain and 0.92 for the advanced domain. Furthermore, the detailed content of items related to digital literacy is represented in Fig. 3.
The Bergen social media addiction scale (BSMAS)
The BSMAS scale was utilized to assess the severity of social media addiction among the participants in the current study. This scale consists of 6 items, each of which was ranked on a Likert-rating scale with options ranging from 1 (very rarely) to 5 (very often) 52 . The total score ranged between 6 and 30, with higher total scores indicating more severe social media addiction. The Cronbach’s alpha was reported at 0.97. In Vietnam, this sacle has been widely applied and validated in previous studies 52,53 .
Assessment of depressive & anxiety symptoms
The Patient Health Questionnaire (PHQ-9) (9 items) assessed the severity of depressive symptoms experienced during the past 2 weeks. The short module scores the nine DSM-IV criteria for depression 54 . This instrument has gained extensive utilization within the context of Vietnam 55, 56–57 . In this study, we use the PHQ-9 Vietnamese version to assess depression in adolescents. Items consist of measurements for cognitive/affective and somatic disorders to screen depression and suicide risk. Responses were on a 4-point Likert scale (“not at all-0”, “several days-1”, “more than half the days-2”, and “nearly every day-3”). The total score ranged from 0 to 27. Participants who had a higher PHQ-9 score indicated a higher depression severity. For participants intending to self-harm or suicide, a referral for psychological counselling was sent to respondents or their parents. Up until now, this scale has been widely applied and validated in Vietnamese context, showing good reliability and validity 57, 58, 59–60 . In the present study, the Cronbach’s alpha was good at 0.92.
Generalized Anxiety Disorder Assessment (GAD-7): We use the GAD-7 scale to assess for generalized anxiety symptoms. In the present study, the GAD-7 Vietnamese version is employed for the assessment of anxiety among adolescents. There were 7 items, and each item had a score of 0, 1, 2, and 3 with corresponding response categories of “not at all,” “several days,” “more than half the days,” and “nearly every day,” respectively. The total score was calculated by a sum score of the 7 items, and the total score ranged from 0 to 21. Participants who had a higher score indicated a higher risk of anxiety disorder 61 . In adolescents, the GAD-7 can assess anxiety symptoms and distinguish between mild and moderate GAD 62 . This instrument has garnered widespread usage within the Vietnamese context 63,64 . The Cronbach’s alpha was 0.94.
Recognition of fake news
The ability to identify fake news was assessed using 13 items categorized into two domains: (1) Direct information from news (5 items) and (2) Indirect information from news (8 items). Direct information refers to cues that can be directly verified from the content of the news itself, such as whether the sender works at a reputable organization, holds professional certification, clearly states the source, or uses appropriate grammar. In contrast, indirect information refers to contextual or external cues related to the news, such as the sender’s address, the location of the article, the number of shares, or whether the poster is an active internet user. Each item was rated on a 5-point scale (1 = “Totally unreliable” to 5 = “Very reliable”), with higher scores indicating better recognition of fake news. The possible score ranges were 5–25 for the direct domain and 8–40 for the indirect domain. Internal consistency was good, with Cronbach’s alpha of 0.85 and 0.87, respectively. Furthermore, the detailed content of items is represented in Fig. 2.
Experience with fake news
We used a 5-point Likert question “Have you ever encountered fake news on SNS? If yes, how is the frequency?” to measure participant’s experiences and encounters with fake news. The question was scored from 1 “Never” to 5 “Always”.
Coping strategies with fake news
Participants’ coping strategies when encountering fake news were assessed using nine items, grouped into three domains: (1) ignore the fake news (this domain encompasses efforts to counter fake news and prevent its spread), (2) intervene to prevent the spread of the fake news (This domain encompasses efforts to counter fake news and prevent its spread), and (3) join in to share the fake news (This domain refers to engagement with fake news, where participants contribute to its spread). For each item, participants self-reported information on their coping strategies with fake news by using a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (“Never”) to 5 (“Always”). The possible score ranges were 2–10 for ignore domain, 5–25 for intervene domain, and 2–10 for join-in to share domain. Higher scores indicated a greater frequency of adopting the respective coping strategy. The Cronbach’s alpha of this question was reoported at 0.81. Furthermoree, the detailed content of items is presented in Fig. 1.
Data analysis
Data analysis was performed using STATA version 16 (Stata Corp. LP, College Station, United States of America). For missing data, we used the Listwise Deletion method to clean data before analyzing it. A total of 1929 individuals participating in the study, after data cleaning, data of 1850 research subjects were included in the analysis (79 cases were removed due to missing data). Continuous variables were presented as mean and standard deviation (SD), while categorical variables were presented as frequencies with percentages.
We used the Chi-squared and Kruskal - Wallis Tests to test the difference between participants who were never/rarely/occasionally had experiences with fake news and regularly/always have had experiences with fake news. To identify the underlying structure of digtal literacy and recognition of fake news, we utilized the exploratory factor analysis (EFA) 65,66 . The principal component analysis (PCA) was used to determine the optimal structural model of the instrument according to the observed data. The Scree plot, and parallel analysis, along with eigenvalues and the proportion of variance explained were used to calculate the number of factors. Items with a loading value ≥ of 0.4 were included in the relevant component. The Cronbach’s alpha was measured to identify the internal consistency reliability of the SNS literacy scale.
Upon comprehensive review of the extant literature, we discerned several salient factors that contribute to engagement with and coping strategies for experiences with fake news. These factors encompass individual characteristics, SNS characteristics, digital literacy, and one’s degree of recognition of fake news 67, 68–69 . Furthermore, it is noteworthy that factors associated with fake news also exert an influence on the mental health status of adolescents 70,71 . Potential covariates for full models of experiences with fake news and coping strategies with fake news included individual characteristics, SNS characteristics (the number of years used SNS, the number of people who were connected/added through SNS, time average used Internet per day, time average used SNS per day), SNS literacy, and one’s extent of recognition of fake news. In terms of potential covariates for full models of depression, all potential covariates, as well as additional variables such as experiences with fake news and coping strategies with fake news, were included. Multivariate ordered logistic regression was applied to examine factors associated with experiences with fake news, which was measured as an ordinal outcome (from 1 = “Never” to 5 = “Always”). In addition, coping strategies and depression were continuous outcomes; thus, multivariate linear regression was applied. These models were then combined with the stepwise forward strategies to find the optimal regression models. Specifically, in this study, variables with p  < 0.2 were considered as the threshold for retaining variables in the final models according to the suggestion of K. Dietz et al. 72 . A p-value (P) < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.
