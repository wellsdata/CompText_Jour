Introduction
In the real world, information diffusion occurs through 'word-of-mouth (WoM)' [1]. However, information dissemination occurs through 'word-of-media (WoMe)' in the digital world. The Internet has become an information hub for users due to its low cost, easy access, and practicality [2]. Especially in healthcare, the information is readily available to the users in an immense size. The Internet has attracted medical practitioners for online consultancies, education or learning purpose, writing blogs, etc. People also surf the Internet for information on sicknesses and symptoms and hunt for preliminary treatments. A survey observed that 65% of users use the Internet to explore health-related subjects. Thus, maximum users prefer the Internet for information access [3].
However, the information may only sometimes be credible and leads to misinformation generation. Such incredible information has long-lasting effects on the lives of people. In the healthcare domain, this may lead to severe damage. For example, through the current COVID-19 pandemic, misinformation about the COVID-19 vaccine suppresses the immune system making people more susceptible to HIV, shingles, and herpes or 5G networks generate radiations that triggers the virus or statement like "coronavirus is just like the flu" [4] or “coronavirus is an engineered bioweapon” or “study proving that children’s heart destroyed by COVID vaccine” had an impact on people that they started believing the misinformation. Such a half-truth grounds panic situations and may lead to a decrease. The most effective strategy for preventing the spread of misinformation is to fact-check the claims with reliable information from credible sources. Thus, false information, credibility, and fact-checking are interrelated terms [3].
False information is inaccurate information that gets spread over the web. The web user distributes incorrect information either intentionally or unintentionally. The intention behind spreading false information may involve gaining financial profit, fun activity, promoting ideology, or could be political intent. False information may appear in the form of fake news, rumor, satire news, hoaxes, misinformation, disinformation, and opinion spam. These types are not mutually exclusive and hence show heterogeneous behavior in a way that a thin line of difference exists between them [5].
Credibility consists of the objective and subjective components of the quality, believability, and trustworthiness of a source or content. For example, the researchers consider knowledge extracted from websites Politifact, Snopes, and Fact-check credible as they provide already fact-checked and verified claims. In contrast, the knowledge extracted from the website The Onion, a satirical news organization, has low credibility and is unreliable. In the literature, researchers have detected fake news based on the credibility of the news author, news publisher, and social media users as a source. The feature-based approaches focusing on topic-based, user-based, propagation-based, and sentimental features detect the credibility of contents [6]. Recently, authors have proposed Fair Ensemble Regression with Optimization (FERMO) to check the credibility of movie reviews [7].
Fact-checking aims to assess the truthiness of information under investigation to identify whether the information is factual. The knowledge-based approach requires previously fact-checked data to determine the credibility of contents [8]. Fact-checking involves examining the facts to verify the legitimacy of the information [9, 10]. Self-executing fact-checked algorithms ascertain the veracity of the data with previously confirmed fact-checked contents and further classify them into legitimate or non-legitimate. The knowledge or fact-based approach is of two types: reference-based and graph-based. In the reference-based approach, the model identifies the semantic similarity between the claim and previously verified knowledge of facts. Features are extracted from the textual claim and matched with the characteristics of verified factual data by finding similarity using algorithms, viz—Euclidean, Jaccard, Cosine, etc. In the graph-based approach, a network model consists of nodes, edges, and information dissemination to perform fact-checking. However, the accuracy of the opinion and graph-based strategies decreased by 50% when tested on different datasets. At the same time, the referencing method outperformed other processes with an accuracy of 82%. Thus, the reference approach is best suited to detect misinformation with fact-checking, although it's a demanding task to resolve completely [9].
The critical challenges in referenced-based automated fact-checking consist of (1) the unavailability of a large structured dataset of URLs in the healthcare domain and (2) the referenced-based approach depends on semantic similarity measures to perform fact-checking. However, standard similarity measures such as Jaccard and Cosine can be less accurate or biased when dealing with sparse data, null values, or term frequencies. (3) No algorithm in the literature to label websites as authentic or malicious. Therefore, there is a pressing need to design a dynamic and automatic fact-checking model to detect and verify healthcare misinformation [9, 11, 12].
Thus, in this research, authors have developed two novel datasets, namely 'Credibility Dataset for Healthcare Misinformation Detection (CredHealth)’ and ‘Fact-checked Dataset for Healthcare Misinformation Detection (FactHealth)’ by verifying the credibility of contents and categorizing them into true and false. Further, authors have proposed a “Content Similarity Measure (CSM)” algorithm based on feature extraction and Closeness-Factor Based Algorithm (CFBA) [13] using a reference approach.
The prime objective of the research is to detect misinformation in healthcare web URLs and ultimately classify them into legitimate or non-legitimate. Following is the primary research objective:
RO 1. Design a healthcare misinformation detection and classification framework using automated fact-checking based on the 'Content Similarity Measure (CSM)' algorithm with a feature-based and algorithmic approach.
This research aims to design a novel healthcare misinformation detection and classification framework that involves a feature-based and algorithmic approach. In the feature-based method, apart from linguistic and sentimental features, domain-specific and fact-based features are incorporated to detect misinformation in healthcare URLs. Researchers have developed machine learning models to classify URLs as legitimate or non-legitimate. In an algorithmic approach, a Content Similarity Vector (CSV) is built for actual URLs and matched with a CSV of fact-checked URLs to generate a Content Similarity Score (CSS). The CSS calculates a threshold value to classify healthcare web URLs into legitimate or non-legitimate. The authors evaluate the robustness of the proposed algorithm based on different datasets. Further, the authors assess the proposed model against state-of-the-art similarity measure algorithms and existing studies.
Following are the research contributions of the proposed CSM architecture: - Information retrieval of URL related to healthcare domain to generate misinformation dataset. The dataset consists of 5000 URLs. Further, applying NLP techniques to pre-process the text and generate a sentiment-based CSV.

- Novel features, viz- CSS, domain-specific sentimental words, and domain-specific sentiment polarity scores are introduced for misinformation detection.

- Authors have introduced a novel first-of-its-kind CSM algorithm implemented using CSS for misinformation detection and classification. The authors show empirically how misinformation detection and classification efficiency can be improved with a novel fact-based feature CSS and an algorithmic approach to automated fact-checking.

- A novel algorithmic approach follows journalistic fact-checking rather than relying only on a feature extraction strategy. Journalistic fact-checking involves detecting misinformation, verifying it with existing facts, and transforming URL classification into legitimate and non-legitimate.

- Authors perform extensive experiments on three benchmark datasets to validate the robustness of the proposed model. The evaluation shows that the proposed CSM-based algorithmic approach attains the highest performance compared to the best available algorithm in the literature.

Related work
In this Section, authors briefly review two related topics: misinformation detection using a feature-based approach and misinformation detection algorithms developed to verify the fact of contents with a knowledge-based automated fact-checking approach.
Misinformation dataset in the healthcare domain
It is challenging to retrieve information from document collections that contain false information. Users may need help to distinguishing between accurate and inaccurate information when a search engine returns documents that include disinformation. The false information may cause them to make bad decisions. The TREC Health Misinformation track encourages research on information retrieval techniques that favor accurate and reliable information [14]. Therefore, retrieving reliable and correct information from the web in the healthcare domain takes time and effort. A recent survey [15] discusses datasets generated for misinformation detection, including the healthcare domain. Researchers observed that, before 2019, there was only one dataset for misinformation detection in the healthcare domain. After the COVID-19 pandemic, researchers focused on generating datasets related to COVID-19 URLs resulting in the generation of 21 datasets of COVID-19 misinformation detection. However, only seven datasets were available publicly, consisting of a maximum of 2000 URLs [15].
Misinformation detection using a feature-based approach
According to the literature, misinformation or false information is incorrect information verified by humans or machine models with existing evidence. The researchers are keenly driving many efforts to detect misinformation. Machine learning algorithms' effectiveness for misinformation detection primarily depends on feature extraction. Table 1 shows the list of features used for false information detection like fake news, rumor, misinformation, checking the credibility of information, etc. According to the literature, the most widely and commonly used features are syntactical or textual, sentimental, and grammatical features while dealing with textual contents [5, 16]. However, based on the type of false information, the platform used, and the domain, additional features are added. For example, to detect fake news or disinformation, authors have developed new features like specificity and informality, which capture properties like cognitive and perceptual processes [17]. For rumor detection, features with specific patterns like inquiry and correction patterns in textual contents are considered [18]. Researchers found that based on platforms like social media, propagation features, user-based features, tweet level features, post-based features, and network features are more effective in detecting fake news, rumors, etc. [6, 16, 19]. In another study related to the healthcare domain, authors used domain-specific features like normalized count of medical terms, commercial terms, hyperlink count, etc.[20]. In [21], behavioral features are extracted from commonly used features to detect healthcare misinformation. Moreover, authors extracted similarity-based features using cosine similarity to find the similarity between the claim and the evidence at the sentence level or title and the body of the news, etc. [21, 22]. In another research, authors have used link-based, commercial, page rank, presentation, and word features to check the reliability of web pages in the medical domain [23]. Authors in [8, 17] found user-based features, sentimental features, syntactic, part-of-speech, and propagation features more suitable for finding the credibility of contents. In [2], authors introduced novel linguistic features with persuasive, comparative, uncertain, and emotional words. In [24], authors detected misinformation based on novelty, i.e., outliers and emotions. However, the textual, grammatical, or syntactic features alone are less potent in capturing the fine-grained information present in the text and unable to detect complex interdependence [22]. In essence, handcrafted features must be more vital to see false information as they lack knowledge of facts [18, 25]. Thus, researchers need to incorporate more relevant features related to factual data into the feature set to precisely detect and classify textual data into legitimate or non-legitimate based on actual facts. Therefore, the authors have included novel features in terms of similarity measures and CSS along with healthcare domain-specific words in the proposed system. After identifying and extracting the required feature set, the researchers use significant machine learning classifiers to perform the classification, viz-Support Vector Machine, Naïve Bayes, Random Forest, Decision Tree, Logistic Regression, k-Nearest Neighbors, etc. [3, 6, 25]. Misinformation detection using algorithmic knowledge/fact-based approach
Detecting misinformation using a knowledge-based approach requires a process of fact-checking. Fact-checking assesses the authenticity of knowledge based on known facts. There are two approaches to fact-checking: manual or traditional fact-checking and automated fact-checking.
Manual fact-checking process
In this approach, a group of domain experts verifies the article's contents. Manual fact-checking is a time-consuming and costly activity considering the scale of information flow over the Internet. Another way of manual fact-checking is expert-based fact-checking websites. The websites like Politifact, Snopes, FactCheck, TruthOrFiction, GossipCop, etc., perform fact-checking of news or articles or claims and provide ground truth on the credibility of contents. These websites have mainly contributed to the development of datasets like LIAR, FakeNewsNet, etc. [5, 16, 27, 28].
Automated fact-checking process
The automated fact-checking techniques rely on information retrieval, NLP, and machine learning techniques to develop fact-checking models. There are three ways to perform automatic fact-checking: graph-based, reference-based, and contextual approach [9, 29]. The knowledge graph-based approach consists of fact-extraction involving creating a knowledge base with a set of facts verified by this approach. Further, the fact-checking process verifies the claim with the knowledge base of points and performs classification. For example, in recent research, authors have developed a temporal propagation network that updates the emerging new nodes, edges, and information dissemination network for fake news detection [30]. The reference approach uses integrated knowledge to verify the claims. In [31], authors have used a reference-based approach with similarity measures for misinformation detection. The contextual approach does not perform fact-checking but identifies the contexts of the relevant claim from social media or the web. For example, in the study, the authors have used information quality with education and users' domain knowledge to detect fake news [1]. However, as mentioned in the introduction Section, the reference-based approach is the best method for the automated fact-checking process.
Researchers have recently developed several fact-checking platforms with automated fact-checking techniques using a reference-based approach. For example, in [32], the authors have introduced the task of fact-checking and detailed the construction of available datasets. Further, they highlighted the baseline approaches, which mention that statements are to be matched with already fact-checked claims to perform fact-checking. However, they said that these approaches are unsuitable for handling newly arriving allegations as they would involve manual fact-checking of those claims. TruthTeller is one study that follows this approach and classifies statements using the kNN algorithm.
Moreover, the authors suggested that an extensive collection of textual data of factual statements can help overcome the baseline approach's drawback to some extent. A similar approach is adopted by the ClaimBuster algorithm, which detects relevant claim from the web and matches it with fact-checking websites to find their relevance. Also, it verifies the claim with knowledge graphs and databases using structured queries and keywords [26].
Thus, the reference-based approach to fact-checking requires the use of integrated facts. These combined facts must have been verified from known sources or should be from previously verified facts [9]. Apart from verified facts, there is a need to find similarities between new and previously fact-checked claims. The two steps for finding textual similarities are (a) feature extraction and (b) finding semantic similarity between documents. Step 'a' can be achieved with the techniques of NLP, such as sentiment analysis, grammatical features, linguistic features, etc. However, step 'b' is similar to information extraction and text similarity, which resembles document similarity measures, viz—cosine similarity, Euclidean distance, etc. [33]. In the literature, [34] authors have used the cosine similarity measure to compute the score between each text snippet and the tweet to assign a ranking to web pages and further classify the text snippet as true or false based on the maximum score gained by the web pages. In another research, authors have proposed an automatic fact-checking algorithm to spot sentences that provide evidence and further classify based on the collected evidence using a neural network-based approach.
Further, the authors have computed the cosine similarity of the claim with the best sentence and classified them using the Hexa-F system [35]. In the research [36], authors have considered statements and evidence and checked their relevance by computing Term-Frequency-Inverse Document Frequency (TF-IDF) for feature extraction and then comparing with HerBERT. Further, the authors have used cosine similarity between claim and reference vectors and support vector machine (SVM) to classify the claims. In the research by [3], authors have compared the web URLs related to the healthcare domains with verified fact-checked web URLs using distance measures, viz- Jaccard Distance, Cosine Similarity measure, and Euclidean distance measure to find the similarity between the URLs and further classify them as legitimate or non-legitimate. Thus according to the literature, researchers have used similarity measures such as Cosine, Jaccard, and Euclidean to perform fact-checking and classify the documents. However, these similarity measures have certain limitations. For example, cosine similarity is less accurate or biased if the terms are zero or unknown. Similarly, Euclidean distance shows less accuracy for sparse data with high dimensions. Also is the case with Jaccard distance when dealing with term frequencies in text documents [37].
To eliminate the need for similarity measures, authors [13] have developed the parameter-free Closeness Factor-Based Algorithm (CFBA) to manage the newly arriving data. Researchers have extended CFBA with various versions, viz-probabilistic CFBA, Naive Bayes-based CFBA, correlation-based CFBA, threshold-based FCBA, distributed CFBA, etc. [38]. However, researchers have used CFBA and all its variants to cluster newly arriving data using incremental clustering techniques. But, misinformation detection using fact-checking is ultimately a classification problem. The bibliometric survey [39] found that CFBA is best suitable for detecting and fact-checking misinformation compared to existing text classification techniques. In this research, the authors have proposed a CSM algorithm to perform fact-checking of the healthcare web URLs and classify them into legitimate or non-legitimate URLs.
Proposed methodology
This Section elaborates on the proposed methodology in terms of data collection, architecture, and algorithm to accomplish the objective of misinformation detection with automated algorithmic fact-checking.
CredHealth and FactHealth dataset generation
The authors have divided dataset CredHealth and FactHealth generation into three parts: data collection, data annotation, and data validation. In this Section, the authors elaborate on the detailed dataset creation process.
Dataset collection
The dataset collection process started on 1st January 2021 up till 31st March 2021. The authors have developed a crawler module in python that accepts healthcare domain-specific keywords and searches for healthcare-related URLs in the Google search engine. The crawler module picks all the URLs listed by Google and filters them using domain-specific sentiment keywords. The crawler maps the domain-specific sentimental keywords with the textual contents of the URL [40]. Further, the crawler removes the duplicate and irrelevant URLs resulting in a fetch of 5032 URLs specific to the healthcare domain.
Data annotation
According to the literature [41], the sources like government organizations and educational institutions are considered reliable sources of healthcare-related information. Therefore, the authors have considered URLs from these sources valid, resulting in 4112 URLs in a true category. Next, a python-based module verifies the URL and its textual contents with URLs from existing datasets, viz- CoAID, ReCOVery, and FakeHealth. Upon finding the URL in these datasets, the model assigns respective annotations to the URL. When the model fails to find URLs in these datasets, the URL and its contents are verified with compiled URLs from fact-checking websites PolitiFact.com and Fact-check.org. The authors have previously collected 100 sample URLs and stored URL names, contents, and labels. Upon finding a match with these URLs, the model assigns corresponding tags to the URL. If the module fails to capture URLs from the above two places, the module checks the parameters anonymity, publisher, and percentage of misinformation of URL based. Suppose the model finds the URL as anonymous (not having email, name, or contact number), with no publisher information, and having a percentage of misinformation above 90. In that case, the model labels the URL as False. If the percentage of misinformation is below 40, they are then marked as true, neutral, or undecided otherwise.
Data validation
The annotators validated the URLs based on two criteria: topic and content. Annotators categorized the URLs into five categories, viz- True, Likely True, Neutral, Likely False, and Immensely False, with a point scale of 1–5, respectively. Further, the authors generated a score which is a sum of points by each annotator for a URL. A threshold of nine categorizes the URL as false, as the maximum value of a neutral score would be nine, and true value is six. Thus, a URL gets a false label if a score exceeds nine. Authors considered a threshold value of three to six to annotate true URLs, considering the maximum number of true points a URL can get. Proposed automated fact-checking architecture
Figure 1 shows the diagrammatic representation of the proposed architecture. The architecture is based on misinformation detection using a feature-based approach and algorithmic fact-checking approach. The first step is to fetch the URLs from the URL repository of 5000 URLs. The model pre-processes the URLs to convert text to lowercase, remove punctuations, stop words, and duplicate words. After cleaning the data, the next step is extracting basic features, which consist of sentiment, syntactic, and grammatical features. According to the literature from among basic features, sentimental features play a crucial role in detecting misinformation. According to the literature, true information has more positive words, and misinformation consists of more negative words [2, 3, 42]. Therefore, the authors manually developed a sentimental bag of words with 793 words. These words are collected from authentic healthcare web URLs with medical-related terms and labeled with sentiment polarities of positive and negative sentiments. The Term-Frequency (TF) of the URL is computed and matched with the sentimental Bag-of-Words to generate novel domain-specific features. The frequency of terms matching with sentimental bag-of-words is a Content Similarity Vector (CSV) consisting of 793 features. The CSV is fed as an input to similarity score computation and fact-checking part of the algorithmic fact-checking approach to generate fact-based features. In this part, the model computes the similarity between CSV of URL and CSV of fact-checked data using proposed CSM and standard similarity measures, viz- Jaccard, Euclidean, and Cosine. Upon finding similarity, the model generates Content Similarity Features (CSF), namely Content Similarity Score (CSS), Jaccard Distance Score (JS), Euclidean Distance Score (ES), and Cosine Similarity Score (CS). Further, all the basic features and CSV and CSF are trained on five different machine learning classifiers, viz- Logistic Regression (LR), Random Forest (RF), Decision Tree (DT), Support Vector Machine (SVM), and Naïve Bayes (NB). The six performance metrics (confusion matrix, accuracy, precision, recall, F1-score, and tenfold cross-validation) evaluate the model's performance. [See PDF for image]

Content similarity measure (CSM) algorithm
This Section defines the Content Similarity Measure (CSM) Algorithm. CSM algorithm has two modules to (a) compute Content Similarity Score (CSS) and (b) classify URLs by computing threshold value.
Figure 2 shows the algorithmic steps to compute CSS using equations Eqs. 1–6. The model calculates CSS for U1 concerning U2, where U1 belongs to the URL repository while U2 is the URL from fact-checked URL repository. Initially, URLs are fetched and pre-processed to remove duplicates, stop words, etc. Further, the model generates CSV1 and CSV2 using Term-Frequency (TF) for URL U1 and U2, respectively. Next, the model computes term-wise sum using the formula of T ( j ) for each term j , as shown in Eq. 1. The next step is calculating the vector-wise sum by adding all the term values per vector. While dealing with the textual data, there could be terms with zero value in both the vectors CSV1 and CSV2. In such a case, the probability computation results in an undefined value. Therefore, the authors have used the updated formula of T ( j ) as per the textual version of CFBA [8, 30]. Thus, if CSV1 or CSV2 contains terms with zero, then according to the formula, one is added to the sum. If both CSV1 and CSV2 include terms with zero, then a value of 2 is added to the sum. An algorithm expects a minimum of value two for T ( j ); otherwise, probability and error lead to an undefined value [13, 43]. The probability is computed for each term in CSV1 concerning CSV2 using Eq. 2. If CSV1 contains 0, the authors devised a formula with the addition of one to handle the problem of undefined results in Eq. 4.
Similarly, the algorithm computes vector-wise probability using Eq. 2. Equation 3 calculates the expected value for each term concerning vector CSV1 by multiplying vector-wise probability with total T ( j ) for the j th term. The next step is to compute the error value using Eq. 4. The weight value is the square root of the total of each term in CSV1, as shown in Eq. 5. Finally, Eq. 6 delivers the computation of CSS of U1 concerning U2. Figure 3.shows the steps to compute threshold value based on CSS to classify URLs into legitimate or non-legitimate. Experimental setup
In this Section, the authors discuss the baseline datasets and baseline studies from the literature used for the performance evaluation of the proposed CSM model.
Baseline datasets
The authors have used three state-of-the-art datasets to test the proposed CSM model's robustness. CoAID, ReCOVery, and FakeHealth [41, 44, 45] along with a newly generated dataset by authors ‘CredHealth’.
- CoAID [41]



CoAID is a dataset containing information about news articles based on web URLs related to COVID-19. The authors have collected a true set of URLs from authorized sources of organizations and institutes. To collect URLs with false information, authors have used fact-checking websites.
- ReCOVery [44]



The repository comprises multi-modal features, including text, visual, temporal, and network information about COVID-19 news articles.
- FakeHealth (Story/Release) [45]



Fake Health consists of two datasets, viz. Health Story and Health Release. The Health Story is a news dataset containing articles with text, images, tags, authors, etc., collected from Reuters Health and similar media. Health Release captures news from universities, research centers, and companies. Every article is assigned a credibility score based on ten questions. The model marks the article as false if the credibility score is below three; true otherwise.

Baseline studies
To find the relevant studies based on baseline datasets, authors have collected the papers having citations of baseline datasets. The authors have crawled 214 articles, filtered studies built with machine learning approaches, and used news articles from the baseline datasets, not Twitter posts or images. The authors did not find complete matching research having similar dataset size, pre-processing techniques, features extracted, and machine learning classifiers used. Therefore, the authors have compared relevant researchers' general performance based on machine learning classifiers. In the first baseline study [20], authors detected misinformation in the healthcare domain using machine learning classifiers on three baseline datasets CoAID, ReCOVery, and FakeHealth. The features extracted include linguistic, textual, and medical terms. The machine classifiers used were Logistic Regression, Gradient Boost, Naïve Bayes, and Random Forest. The second and third baseline study includes [44] and [45], the ReCOVery and FakeHealth datasets tested with machine learning classifiers. In the fourth baseline study [46], the model translates newly received articles into target languages, and cross-lingual news is retrieved.
Further, top N articles evaluate the authenticity of the news. Authors have used n-gram, linguistic, and readability features and tested them on the ReCOVery dataset. The model is a feature-based comparison, and the newly proposed classifier classifies the articles. Apart from dataset-based studies, authors have also compared studies in the healthcare domain using similarity measures as a feature.
Experimental results
This Section extensively analyzes results generated with the proposed CSM algorithm. Section 5.1 discusses the results created based on the CredHealth dataset. Section 5.2 elaborates on results generated on existing state-of-the-art datasets viz. CoAID, ReCOVery, and FakeHealth. Discussion
This Section interprets and analyzes the results found in Sect. 5. Further, it discusses the implications of the study in theory and practice.
Interpretation and analysis of results
In the feature-based approach, the proposed model showed the highest accuracy of 88.86% with CSS, followed by 81.86% with CSV in combination with BF. Thus, sentiment-based domain-specific keywords in CSV and newly generated fact-based CSS have improved the model's performance. Therefore, apart from handcrafted features used in the literature, newly developed features with knowledge of facts (CSS) are required, and such features can improve the model's performance in detecting misinformation. These findings resemble the research that states handcrafted features with knowledge of facts are necessary to enhance the performance of misinformation detection [25]. In the algorithmic approach, reference-based automated fact-checking without using similarity measures, i.e., the proposed CSM algorithm, showed the highest accuracy compared to other state-of-the-art similarity measures. The probabilistic approach of CSM finds semantic similarities between two URLs without the need for standard distance measures to perform automated fact-checking with a reference approach resulting in higher accuracy [9]. A value of CSS is within a range of 0 to 1. A value of CSS = 0 predicts two properties. First, two series of features are precisely similar; thus, two URLs are the same. Secondly, it indicates that two URLs have the same behavioral pattern, helping classify URLs. As CSS value increases, authors observed that the behavioral way of URL differs due to the extensive scaling of data points and leads to different URLs. The proposed CSM algorithm deals with sparse and zeroes data appearing in text and improves the model's accuracy. Thus, updating the CFBA [13] algorithm in the literature as a proposed CSM classifier eliminates the necessity of standard similarity measures for reference-based automated fact-checking for misinformation detection. Therefore, the CSM algorithm is superior to others in the literature in several ways. First, it generates novel veracity-based features, and second removes the necessity of standard similarity measure algorithms for the reference-based approach of fact-checking algorithms. Thirdly, CSM deals with sparse and zero data and improves the algorithm's accuracy compared to existing baseline studies. Thus, CSM is undoubtedly an augmentation to the literature on the automated fact-checking approach of misinformation detection and also an advancement in the version of the CFBA life cycle.
Theoretical and practical implications of the study
Although there are realistic dimensions of previous research to perform automatic fact-checking of data, CSM is the first attempt to achieve automated fact-checking of healthcare URLs eliminating the need for similarity measures.
The theoretical implications of the study are as follows: - The study incorporates novel domain-based and fact-based features knowing facts. Authors have proved with experiments that these features are essential to verify the facts and thus improve the model's performance.

- The study eliminates the need for similarity measure algorithms in the reference-based fact-checking approach with the CSM algorithm.

- The CSM is a first-of-its-kind version of CFBA used as a classifier to perform automated fact-checking and detect misinformation. CSM certainly is an advancement in the CFBA life cycle.

- CSM architecture follows the journalistic fact-checking task of finding knowledge of facts, evaluating claims concerning evidence, and deciding the article's truthfulness, thus overcoming the limitations of the feature-based approach, which merely predicts how the claim is written. Therefore, creating additional knowledge in this research area as it was a need of time [29].

- Although CSM works at the document level, the CSM architecture can undoubtedly work for sentence and paragraph level to find the similarity between claim and evidence, title and body of the news, etc.


These observations have certainly augmented the knowledge base of misinformation detection using algorithmic automated fact-checking in the healthcare domain.
The practical implications of the study are as follows: - The CSM algorithm can work with other domains like politics, business, and news.

- The CSM algorithm can classify text documents over the web.

- The outcomes of the algorithm can help researchers, practitioners, doctors, and organizations to employ them to detect the credibility of information.


Conclusion, limitations, and future enhancements
In this study, the authors have proposed a novel CSM algorithm to detect misinformation with the fact-based approach in two ways. First, using a feature-based approach, and second algorithmic fact-checking approach. In the feature-based approach, authors have contributed to incorporating novel features based on domain and fact-based, viz. CSV and CSF. Further, the authors trained the model with five machine-learning classifiers. In the algorithmic approach, the threshold value is computed based on CSF, and URLs are classified. The findings of the study are as follows: - The algorithmic approach outperformed the feature-based technique.

- Standard similarity measures failed to improve the accuracy compared to the feature-based approach. However, CSM outperformed the feature-based approach to misinformation detection.

- The experimental results show that domain and fact-based features play a significant role in improving the performance of the model.


The study has a few limitations in terms of the model's inability to generate and publish fact-checking reports. Another limitation is that the proposed algorithm cannot work for the multi-classification of URLs in its current form. Also, CSM architecture does fact-check at the document level and not at a sentence or paragraph level.
In the future, authors want to develop a dashboard that would display the reports of fact-checking wherever possible. Also, the authors wish to show the fact-checking reports to the users. Further, the authors want to implement a hybrid classification of models in the future.
