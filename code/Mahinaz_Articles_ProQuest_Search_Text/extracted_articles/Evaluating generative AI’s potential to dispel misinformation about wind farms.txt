Introduction To meet key sustainable development goals, it is crucial that there is a fast and unprecedented uptake of renewable energy, including wind. The successful large-scale deployment of wind farms ultimately depends on broader public support, yet our knowledge of wider societal attitudes toward wind energy remains surprisingly limited. Research to date has mostly been conducted on those directly affected by wind farm developments, focusing on legitimate local concerns about visual impact 1 , noise 2 , real estate prices 3 , and trust issues rooted in failures of procedural justice 4, 5, 6–7 . However, as the scale and speed of wind-energy developments has ramped up in recent years, it has become apparent that negative sentiment about wind farms has expanded beyond legitimate, local concerns to incorporate misinformation and conspiracy theories circulated among the general public. In the current research, we adopt the definition of scientific misinformation as “publicly available information that is misleading or deceptive relative to the best available scientific evidence and that runs contrary to statements by actors or institutions who adhere to scientific principles” 8 .
Results from international surveys on responses to wind farm misinformation are alarming. For example, a 2024 survey found that at least one-third of people in Australia and the U.S. agreed with the notion that “climate scientists manipulate facts in order to portray wind energy in a particularly positive light” and “the construction of wind turbines releases more CO 2 than is saved by their operation” 9 . Of the 13 statements included in the survey, the least endorsed piece of misinformation – that “the underground power cables of wind turbines can cause cancer in local residents” – was still agreed with by approximately a quarter of respondents in Australia and the U.S. The results of this survey research dovetail with evidence that opposition to wind farms has become highly organised and influential in public discourse. Anti-wind farm lobby groups are gaining increasing political influence and media traction, 10,11 frequently disseminating misinformation 12,13 that is amplified via social media 14 and by senior political figures including U.S. President Trump 15 .
There is also evidence that misinformation is consequential 16 . In survey research, agreement with misinformation is strongly associated with lower levels of support for pro-wind policies and greater intentions to engage in collective action against wind farms 9 . Widespread opposition to wind farm development is a disincentive for investors and governments to invest their capital in expanding such projects 17 . As such, the mainstreaming of myths and conspiracy theories about wind farms threatens our collective ability to ramp up renewable energy at a speed required to mitigate climate change.
To develop communication strategies capable of reducing damaging misinformation about wind farms, agents of change need to understand the psychological makeup of the audience they are trying to influence. Interestingly, there is only weak evidence that people who believe misinformation about wind farms are poorly educated or lack objective scientific knowledge 9 . Instead, the strongest predictors of misinformation endorsement were measures of worldviews. For example, political conservatives were more likely to agree with statements about wind farms grounded in misinformation while people with pro-ecological worldviews were less likely to agree with them 9 . However, the variable that was by far the biggest predictor of misinformation agreement was conspiracy mentality, a measure capturing the general worldview that societal events are grounded in conspiracies developed secretly by powerful elites 18,19 . One study in Germany found that conspiracy mentality explained five times more variance in willingness to vote against wind farm developments than political conservatism and approximately 20 times more variance than age, gender, and education 20 . When considering participants’ beliefs in a specific conspiracy theory about wind farms (as opposed to the generic conspiracy mentality measure) the relationship was even more dramatic, explaining at least 25 times more variance in voting intentions compared with any other variable.
The fact that wind farms have become a target of attention among those prone to believing conspiracy theories is a concern because, historically, it has been difficult to “teach” people to reduce their feelings of systemic mistrust. Individual studies have reported some success with corrective information to rebut a specific conspiracy theory 21, 22, 23, 24, 25–26 , however there is little evidence that this spills over into attenuating the overall conspiracy worldview, or that corrective information is effective for people who have identified strongly with a particular belief 27 .
Over and above research on conspiracy theories, many studies show only modest effects of corrective information on negative attitudes about science and technology. A recent meta-analysis concluded that “attempts to debunk science-relevant misinformation were, on average, not successful” 28 . Another meta-analysis, conducted simultaneously, was somewhat more positive but still concluded that refutation interventions were, on average, only weakly effective 29 . Studies in Germany found that willingness to vote in favour of wind farm developments increased after being presented with positive information about wind farms 20 . However, when a mixed argument was presented – containing both valid positive and negative arguments, like a person would experience in the real world – there was much weaker evidence for attitude change, and this intervention was not effective at all for people who held a pre-existing conspiracy theory about wind farms. In sum, there are reasons to believe that informational interventions can be modestly successful, but effects are weak and unreliable, and concerns remain as to whether effects that do emerge are resilient in real-world contexts.
A recent study, published in Science by Costello and colleagues, showed some promise of large and durable effects of a text-based Generative AI (GenAI) intervention on one form of misinformation: willingness to believe conspiracy theories 30 . In this study, participants nominated a specific conspiracy theory that they believed in (the two most nominated conspiracies were about JFK and aliens). They then conducted a three-round dialogue with ChatGPT, a text-based GenAI program. After that experience, participants’ confidence in the truth of the conspiracy theory reduced by 20%, an effect that far exceeds what is typically achieved through informational interventions on conspiracy theories 25,26 . Encouragingly, this effect extended to belief in conspiracy theories other than the one that the participant nominated, the belief conversion did not substantially decay over two months, and the intervention spilled over to reduce participants’ self-reported willingness to spread conspiracy theories online.
While meta-analytic evidence suggests that AI can be broadly as persuasive as humans across a range of communication contexts 31 , in the case of the current study, there are several plausible reasons why ChatGPT interventions might be more effective than other informational interventions. First, the ChatGPT intervention is dynamic and interactive; it adjusts information live in response to participants’ concerns, unlike static fact sheets. Second, ChatGPT information is effectively “authorless”, which could be an advantage for people who dispute the integrity of sources of information such as scientific consortia, government departments, and industry bodies. Regardless of mechanism, the results of Costello and colleagues’ initial study are potentially transformative because they suggest a revolution in how information is delivered to the sceptical public, with a de-emphasis on mass communication efforts from official sources and an emphasis on directing people through GenAI conversations.
In short, GenAI has the potential to become the world’s biggest source of information for the general public, particularly as traditional internet search engines increasingly use GenAI to craft responses to queries. Despite this, there is currently no empirical insight into what happens when people interact with large language models like ChatGPT on issues related to wind farms. The current studies respond to this evidence gap.
We used ChatGPT rather than other AI tools for three reasons. First, ChatGPT is one of the most accessible and widely used LLMs by the general public. This aligns with our aim to test interventions that are readily available to people seeking information about wind farms. Second, to maximise ecological validity, we provided no background prompt and so the GenAI responded with its default behaviour. This approach tests a real-world scenario millions experience daily. Third, using ChatGPT enabled direct comparison with Costello et al.‘s 30 findings, allowing us to address two questions: (1) whether the large and durable effects of AI conversations would generalise to wind farm beliefs, and (2) whether ChatGPT would remain effective without explicit persuasion instructions. This third point warrants emphasis. Costello and colleagues provided a prompt to ChatGPT in the background, informing the AI that it was going to be interacting with a conspiracy theorists and instructing it to attempt to change their mind 30 . Without a background prompt, it remains to be seen whether conversations with GenAI could be part of the solution to misinformation, or whether GenAI tools will reproduce the type of misinformation and conspiracy theories it finds in the collective informational pool 32 .
Across two experiments, we investigated whether ChatGPT interactions could reduce agreement with wind farm misinformation. Participants first articulated their opinions on wind farms in open-ended comments, rated their confidence in the truth of these statements, and completed measures assessing agreement with wind farm misinformation and support for wind energy policies (see Supplementary Table S1 for a full list of measures). In one condition, participants engaged in a three-round conversation with GPT 4o-Turbo about windfarms. An example of one of these conversations – selected to be illustrative rather than representative – is depicted in Fig. 1. In Study 1 ( N  = 800) the control condition was a conversation with GPT 4o-Turbo about a neutral topic (participants’ favourite sport).
In Study 2 ( N  = 1605), the effectiveness of the GenAI intervention was compared to that of a 3-page wind farm myth-busting fact sheet developed by the U.S. Department of Energy (reproduced in Supplementary Fig. S1). We selected a fact sheet as our comparison condition because fact sheets represent the standard intervention currently employed by government agencies to combat misinformation. This provides a practical, real-world benchmark and addresses policy-relevant implications of implementing new technological approaches versus enhancing existing communication strategies.

Results Data were analysed using the LME4 package in R to fit linear mixed effects models. Results are summarised below. Full model output can be found in Supplementary Tables S2–S11. Descriptives and attrition analyses are located in Supplementary Tables S14 & S15.
Confidence in initial attitude We first coded the valence of participants’ initial views, given changes in confidence over time vary in meaning depending on whether the initial statements were positive or negative about wind farms. As elaborated in the Methods, we coded each set of open-ended responses as either “anti”, “pro”, “mixed” or “uncertain”. Because changes in confidence are difficult to interpret for “mixed” and “uncertain” participants, we focused these analyses on those in the “pro” and “anti” groups.
As shown in Fig. 2, there was a significant 3-way interaction in Study 1 between condition, timepoint, and belief valence (β = 0.36, p = 0.001, 95% CI [0.15, 0.57]). Among participants who initially opposed wind farms, confidence in their initial attitudes was 7.90% points (pp) lower following the AI conversation about wind farms (relative to participants’ pre-intervention baseline). This represents a 9.46% reduction in confidence when expressed as a ratio of the pre-intervention baseline ( p = 0.001, 95% CI [2.68, 13.12], d  = 0.65). However, this effect was temporary, with confidence levels returning to baseline 1 month later. Participants in the control (sport) condition maintained stable confidence levels throughout the study, with their follow-up confidence ratings nearly identical to their pre-intervention interaction levels ( p s > 0.56).
Among participants with initially ‘pro’ wind farm beliefs, confidence in their initial attitudes was 2.76pp (3.25%) higher immediately after the AI conversation about wind farms ( p =0.049, 95% CI [0.01, 5.15], d  = 0.22). At this timepoint, confidence was significantly higher than the sport condition ( p a.i. vs. sport =0.018, 95% CI [0.73, 7.64], d  = 0.34), although this advantage attenuated 1 month later ( p a.i. vs. sport =0.159, 95% CI [1.14, −6.99], d  = 0.24). In the control (sport) condition, confidence levels remained consistent across all time points ( p s > 0.91).

Misinformation agreement The next analyses (depicted in Fig. 4) examined whether the interventions influenced participants’ agreement with a diverse set of 14 misinformation statements about wind farms (i.e., this measure no longer focused on participants’ original open-ended responses). In Study 1, participants who interacted with the AI about wind farms displayed reduced agreement with misinformation immediately following the conversation ( p < 0.001, 95% CI [0.11, 0.24], d  = 0.44). Although this effect diminished slightly at follow up, misinformation agreement levels were still lower than at baseline ( p = 0.002, 95% CI [0.04, 0.19], d  = 0.29). For participants in the sport condition there were no significant changes in misinformation agreement throughout the study period ( p s >0.546).
Study 2 revealed a similar pattern. Following the AI interaction, misinformation agreement was 11.38% (3.7pp) lower from pre-intervention to post-intervention ( p <.001, 95% CI [6.33, 8.53], d  = 0.92), an effect that remained at 1-month follow-up ( p post – 1month later =0.096, 95% CI [−2.28, 0.12], d  = 0.13). The fact sheet, however, proved significantly more effective, displaying larger reductions in misinformation agreement than the AI conversation at every assessment point following the initial presentation ( p s <0.001).

Discussion Our collective ability to reach sustainable development goals is being compromised by misinformation about sustainability solutions such as renewable energy. Generative AI is rapidly becoming one of the most influential sources of information for the public. Yet, until now, there has been little empirical evidence on what happens when people actually engage with these tools when forming views about wind farms. By examining this interaction directly, the current studies begin to fill that critical evidence gap.
GenAI models, such as those used in conversational systems, learn from vast datasets that may include unreliable or misleading content, raising the risk that they could perpetuate or even escalate the spread of false information. Furthermore, GenAI systems may inadvertently mirror the biases present in the data they are trained on, leading to the propagation of these inaccuracies on a larger scale. This has led many commentators to express concerns about the unintended consequences of GenAI tools, particularly their potential to amplify misinformation and conspiracy theories embedded within the broader information landscape, or to deteriorate when trained on recursively generated data 33,34 .
One take-home message of the current studies is that, under the conditions tested here, unregulated ChatGPT conversations did not appear to make things worse. Although our fact-checking procedure, which combined automated LLM-based checking with independent expert review of 20% of conversations, found no clear evidence of misinformation being introduced, this should not be taken as a blanket assurance. Given that each conversation with ChatGPT is unique and dependent on the specific inputs provided by participants, it would require a far more extensive auditing programme to conclude with confidence that misinformation is never introduced. Our findings are best interpreted as providing provisional reassurance under the specific conditions of these studies, rather than a general guarantee of accuracy across all possible ChatGPT interactions on this topic.
A critical question is whether these conversations are associated with improvements in wind farm beliefs and attitudes. The answer, under the conditions of the current studies, is yes, particularly in the short-term. For people who reported exclusively negative views about wind farms in open-ended responses, confidence in the truth of their negative views decreased by 9.46% in Study 1 and 10.37% in Study 2 following a 3-round conversation with ChatGPT. Following the ChatGPT intervention, participants also showed increased pro-wind policy support and decreased agreement with misinformation.
Although modest, these effects are not an artifact of merely interacting with GenAI, as similar conversations about sport had no effect. In the context of informational interventions on science and technology issues, where meta-analyses typically find effects around d  = 0.1 28 , these results are encouraging. At population scale, such effects have the potential to shift beliefs among millions of people, meaningfully influencing the political and investment climate for renewable energy and potentially accelerating deployment of climate-critical infrastructure.
Further consistent with most one-shot debunking exercises in the literature 35,36 , some of these positive effects decayed over time. This pattern is consistent with broader evidence that attitude change is generally difficult to sustain, particularly for topics that carry emotional or partisan significance 37 . The relatively greater durability of effects on misinformation agreement compared to policy support and confidence measures may reflect the fact that correcting discrete factual claims is more tractable than shifting the emotionally grounded attitudes that underpin broader policy preferences.
For example, in Study 1, the observed differences in policy support and confidence in sceptics’ initial beliefs were no longer significant one month later. However, positive effects in terms of reducing belief in misinformation remained significant at follow-ups. In Study 2, where we conducted two follow-up sessions, the positive effects of the intervention were still observable after a month across all measures.
Across the range of dependent measures we used, the data present a more nuanced picture than the exceptional durability reported by Costello and colleagues in their studies on conspiracy theories. This difference may stem from several factors, but one critical distinction is the nature of the beliefs themselves. Generic conspiracy theories (such as those about JFK or aliens examined by Costello and colleagues) are relatively abstract and distant from most people’s lived experiences. In contrast, wind farm beliefs often emerge from or are reinforced by local community discourse, personal economic concerns about property values, or lived experiences with visual and noise impacts. This grounding in tangible, local contexts may make wind farm misconceptions more resistant to change through purely informational interventions, as they are intertwined with legitimate concerns and community identity.
A second key distinction is that we did not provide background prompts to the AI. Costello et al.‘s use of background prompts instructing the AI to change participants’ minds may have produced more persuasive conversations. However, our decision to test GenAI in its default state reflects how most people actually encounter these systems in the real world. The absence of explicit persuasion framing may have positioned our intervention as more informational than argumentatively compelling, potentially reducing both immediate effectiveness and sustained attitude change. This trade-off between maximal effectiveness and ecological validity represents an important consideration for real-world implementation.
Interestingly, recent work examining AI conversations for electric vehicle misinformation found similarly modest but persistent effects, with beliefs remaining improved at 10-day follow-up38. A second series of studies examining the impact of AI conversations on climate scepticism found equivalently modest effects that decayed over time 39 . Taken together with the findings of the present studies, it appears that sustainability-related misconceptions do respond differently to AI interventions than pure conspiracy theories, but further research is clearly needed to directly compare the long-term effectiveness of AI interventions against traditional one-shot debunking exercises across different domains and contexts. Such comparative studies would help determine whether the interactive nature of AI truly offers superior resistance to belief decay, and under what conditions these advantages might be maximised.
Study 2 tested the question whether the GenAI intervention was more or less effective than a traditional fact sheet intervention; a myth-busting document produced by the US Department of Energy. We chose this comparison over alternatives (such as multi-turn AI with different prompting strategies or human-moderated discussions) for several reasons. First, government-produced fact sheets represent the current gold standard for combating wind farm misinformation at scale. This makes our comparison immediately relevant for policy decisions about resource allocation and communication strategy. Second, while human-moderated discussions might possibly be more effective, they are prohibitively expensive and logistically impossible to scale to population level, rendering such comparisons less actionable for real-world implementation. Third, comparing different AI configurations (e.g., AI with persuasion prompts versus neutral prompts) would address mechanistic questions about optimal AI deployment, rather than whether AI conversations offer advantages over existing communication tools. By testing ChatGPT against the best current practice rather than against alternative AI implementations, we provide organizations with directly actionable insights: should they invest in developing AI conversation systems, or continue refining traditional fact-based materials?
On many indices there was no reliable difference between the effectiveness of the two interventions, however the fact sheet was more effective at reducing agreement with the misinformation scale. This is perhaps unsurprising given that the information carried in the fact sheet was broad-based, and so better equipped to address the diversity of myths captured by the misinformation scale than the GenAI tool, which was responding more narrowly to participants’ original concerns expressed in the open-ended responses.
The obvious applied question is whether GenAI is a useful population-level tool for addressing misinformation and conspiracy theories, given that an existing fact sheet was equally effective (and on agreement with misinformation, more effective). On one hand, GenAI tools such as ChatGPT have unique capabilities that make them particularly well-suited for addressing misinformation and conspiracy theories about wind farms. These tools can engage individuals in personalised conversations, tailoring responses to their specific concerns and misunderstandings. Unlike static fact sheets or one-size-fits-all informational campaigns, GenAI can adapt dynamically, providing nuanced explanations in real time. Another major advantage is accessibility, as GenAI is easy to access and operates in multiple languages. These qualities make it an effective tool for reaching diverse audiences, including audiences that might not be inclined to seek out and read fact sheets on government department websites. Furthermore, by facilitating conversations that are interactive rather than didactic, GenAI tools may foster a sense of agency and trust in users, potentially reducing resistance to wind energy initiatives.
However, several limitations warrant caution. First, the accuracy and reliability of these tools depend on the quality of the underlying training data and algorithms. Although we did not discover obvious cases of misinformation being produced during ChatGPT conversations, the “black box” nature of its algorithms and a lack of regulatory oversight makes it difficult for users to understand how information is being generated, which could erode trust if inconsistencies or errors arise going forward 40, 41–42 .
Second, because ChatGPT generates unique responses for each user, we cannot definitively isolate which specific features of the conversations (e.g., particular arguments, evidence types, conversational tone, or depth of technical detail) were most effective in producing the observed changes in beliefs and attitudes. Different participants received qualitatively different information and argumentative strategies, meaning the “active ingredients” of the intervention remain unspecified. This heterogeneity limits our ability to make strong causal claims about mechanism—we can demonstrate that conversations with ChatGPT were associated with belief change but cannot precisely specify why or how this occurred.
This variability raises questions about reproducibility. While we can replicate the protocol (having participants converse with ChatGPT about wind farms), we cannot guarantee that future participants would receive similar conversational content, as the AI’s responses depend on both the specific user inputs. This means that while the general pattern of effects may be reproducible (which it appears to be for both electric vehicle misinformation 38 and climate scepticism 39 the specific magnitude and nature of belief change could vary across implementations.
The lack of standardisation makes it difficult to identify boundary conditions for the intervention’s effectiveness. Without categorising response types or systematically varying conversational features, we cannot determine whether certain types of AI responses are more or less effective, or whether the intervention works equally well across different forms of wind farm misconceptions. This limitation is particularly relevant given that participants’ initial beliefs varied substantially—from conspiracy theories to concerns based on lived experiences—and the AI’s approach to these different belief types may have varied as well.
In relation to wind farm misconceptions specifically, one such boundary condition is the degree to which wind farms have become politically coded, particularly in countries like the United States where renewable energy is increasingly entangled with partisan identity. While we found no evidence that political orientation moderated the efficacy of the AI conversation in either study, this politicisation may shape how individuals engage with AI conversations on other polarising misconception-prone topics, with some participants’ responses reflecting motivated reasoning or identity-protective cognition rather than genuine information processing. Future research should examine whether the effectiveness of GenAI interventions varies as a function of the political salience of the topic and the strength of participants’ prior ideological commitments.
We emphasise that these limitations do not invalidate our findings, but rather reflect the inherent trade-off between experimental control and ecological validity. Our study demonstrates that AI conversations, as they naturally occur, are associated with meaningful improvements in wind farm beliefs. However, researchers and practitioners should recognise that optimising such interventions would require systematic investigation of conversational features and their effects. While this work falls outside the scope of our initial effectiveness study, it represents an important direction for future research.
When considering scaling AI-based interventions, it is important to acknowledge that the computational resources required to train and operate large AI models consume significant amounts of energy and water 43 . This raises ethical questions about the benefits of using GenAI to address misinformation, especially in the context of advocating for sustainable energy solutions. The computational energy costs of LLMs such as ChatGPT are estimated at 0.34 W-hours per prompt-response 44 , which is akin to running a household oven for a little over one second 44 . While this represents a meaningful environmental consideration, these costs must be evaluated against the potential climate benefits of increased renewable energy acceptance, while also acknowledging that existing interventions (such as fact sheets) also have a carbon footprint associated with development time, graphic design, translation services, and distribution infrastructure.
While our studies solely focus on ChatGPT due to its widespread public use, future research on AI-enhanced belief shift more broadly could integrate two promising alternative AI approaches to improve misinformation correction. The first promising direction involves ensemble learning, which is a technique that combines multiple AI models to produce more reliable outputs than any single model alone. Recent advances in unsupervised ensemble learning have demonstrated remarkable accuracy in complex classification tasks, such as achieving accuracy scores above 0.95 in medical image quality assessment by integrating multiple generative autoencoders 45 . Applied to wind farm misinformation, a similar architecture could deploy specialized models trained on different aspects of the issue—one focused on technical/engineering claims, another on health impacts, and a third on economic arguments—with their outputs synthesized through ensemble weighting. This would address a limitation of ChatGPT’s responses, which, while accurate, sometimes lacked high levels of technical depth. An ensemble approach integrated with Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) could retrieve current research on specific claims (e.g., the latest acoustic studies on turbine noise) before generating responses, ensuring corrections reflect cutting-edge evidence rather than potentially outdated training data.
A second promising direction is the potential graph-based models have for analysing the structure of information spread across social networks, and their ability to visualise how specific wind farm myths propagate through social groups. By identifying influential spreaders and understanding characteristic patterns of misinformation transmission on social media, these models could help target corrective information more strategically, similar to how public health officials might focus on key transmission points during disease containment. Such approaches could leverage recent developments in graph convolutional networks applied to social sentimental data analysis 46 and federated learning techniques in integrated deep learning pipelines 47 . These complementary approaches could address the limitations in the efficacy of the AI intervention examined in our current study, though this remains an empirical question for future research.
Third, a promising avenue for future research is exploring reinforcement mechanisms to address the decay patterns observed following AI interactions. Multi-session interventions represent one potential solution, where users engage with GenAI at strategic intervals to reinforce corrections and sustain belief change. While longitudinal experimental implementation faces challenges of participant attrition, several practical approaches merit investigation. One compelling approach is developing real-time AI assistance that provides immediate corrections in dynamic online discussions about renewable energy. This could be implemented as a browser extension that activates when users encounter websites flagged for high levels of wind farm misinformation. To maintain user engagement, such tools could employ minimally invasive design principles – perhaps offering unobtrusive notifications when misinformation is detected, with users retaining control over whether to access further corrective information.
In sum, GenAI shows potential as a complementary tool for addressing misinformation about wind farms. While our findings reveal both strengths and limitations compared to traditional approaches, its adaptability, accessibility, and capacity for personalised engagement suggest value as part of a diversified intervention strategy. However, its use must be tempered with caution. The modest effect sizes and decay patterns highlight the need for further research before widespread implementation. Ensuring the accuracy, transparency, and ethical use of these tools will also be critical to their success. Complementing GenAI interventions with community-based approaches and rigorous oversight will further help maximise their benefits while mitigating potential risks.
Methods Procedure Participants first recorded their demographics followed by a wind farm misinformation agreement scale and pro-wind farm policy support scale. In Study 1 only, participants were then asked to enter their favourite sport and (in a separate text entry box) to elaborate on what led them to this opinion. Participants in both studies were then given a definition of wind farms: A wind farm is a group of wind turbines in the same location used to produce electricity. Participants were then prompted to: […] share your overall perspective on wind farms in a few sentences. Consider the following points in your response: Do you generally view wind farms as a positive or negative technology? What do you see as the main benefits or drawbacks of wind farms? There are no right or wrong answers – we’re interested in your honest thoughts and opinions. Participants were then presented with their response and asked to […] share more about what led you to this opinion? For instance , are there specific pieces of evidence , events , sources of information , or personal experiences that have particularly influenced your perspective? Please describe these in as much detail as you feel comfortable.
We piped both responses (the initial opinion and the elaboration) into ChatGPT4-o Turbo which was prompted to summarise the participants’ views on wind farms in a single sentence (see Supplementary Fig. S2 for the prompt text). Participants were presented with this GenAI-summarised version of their view on wind farms and asked to indicate their level of confidence on a (0–100% scale) that this statement was true. Consistent with Costello and colleagues 30 , the piping of participants responses back and forth from Qualtrics into GPT 4o-Turbo was facilitated by the online AI aggregator ‘OpenRouter’ which was integrated into Qualtrics.
After participants provided their initial view on wind farms, they were then randomly assigned to one of two conditions. Half the participants were assigned to engage in a three-round conversation with ChatGPT about wind farms. In Study 1, the control condition was a conversation with GPT 4o-Turbo about their favourite sport (see Supplementary Table S16 for instructions). In Study 2, participants read a three-page myth-busting fact sheet about wind farms (see Supplementary Fig. S1 for details of this text). The fact sheet in Study 2 was chosen as an active comparator, not as a control condition – its static, image-inclusive style does not mirror the interactive but image-free conversations that ChatGPT provides. Instead, we construed the experiment as akin to a tournament, pitching the GenAI against the best collective efforts of the U.S.’s leading government agency responsible for wind energy.
After the intervention, participants were presented with their original GenAI-summarised opinion on wind farms and re-asked to indicate their confidence in it. Finally, participants completed the same wind farm misinformation agreement and policy support scales they completed before the intervention. In Study 1, participants were invited to complete the same measures one month later. In Study 2 participants were followed up twice: 10 days after the original intervention and again 1 month after the original intervention.

Participants Participants for Studies 1 and 2 were U.S. Prolific workers sampled using the pre-screening option to obtain a representative sample across age, sex, political ideology and, in Study 1, ethnicity (5.8% Asian, 11% Black, 10.1% Mixed, 58.5% White, 14.6% Other). All participants provided informed consent and were compensated an equivalent of £7.50 per hour for their time. In Study 1, we recruited 800 participants ( M age = 45.31, SD age = 16.14, 49.37% male, 48.75% female, 1.88% other). In Study 2, we recruited 1,605 participants ( M age = 46.67, SD age = 16.11, 48.72% male, 49.41% female, 1.87% other). Average scores on political ideology approximated the midpoint of the 7-point scale ( M  = 3.9 and 3.8 for Studies 1 and 2 respectively). In line with our pre-registrations, 30 participants in Study 1 and 216 participants in Study 2 were excluded for either failing attention checks or omitting demographic information. The higher rate of attention failures in Study 2 can be attributed to differences in attention check implementation between the studies. In Study 2, attention checks were embedded within the wind farm misinformation scale at every timepoint (a total of four attention checks across all timepoints). In contrast, Study 1 used only two attention checks: one standalone item, separate from the misinformation scale, and another embedded within the misinformation scale during the one-month follow-up assessment. Measures The following measures were taken at all timepoints in both studies. A complete list of items is provided in Supplementary Table S1.
Confidence in initial attitude Participants indicated their level of confidence that their GenAI-summarised view on wind farms was true on a scale from 0 (definitely false) to 100 (definitely true). This scale format was identical to the one used by Costello and Colleagues. We also presented a line of text: “ (NOTE: This question is not about the quality of the summary but rather about your overall take on climate change)” to direct participants to rate the statement based on how true it felt to them personally, rather than how accurately it reflected the conversation summary. A separate question: “Did the AI accurately summarize your perspective?: Yes / No” was included underneath this item to check the accuracy of the AI summary. Only 12% of participants reported the AI did not accurately summarise their perspective, and results remained unchanged regardless of whether these participants were included or excluded from the analyses.
Misinformation agreement We used a version of the 13-item wind farm misinformation scale used by Winter and colleagues 20 (e.g. The noise from wind turbines can cause health problems in humans; Climate scientists manipulate facts in order to portray wind energy in a particularly positive light. ). We modified the original scale by adding a new item: Offshore wind farms cause mass whale deaths. In Study 1, participants responded to each item on a Likert scale from 1 (Strongly disagree) to 5 (Strongly agree). In Study 2, the endpoints were changed to range from 0 (Strongly disagree) to 100 (Strongly agree). The scale was highly reliable at all timepoints (Study 1: 0.95 < α < 0.97; Study 2: 0.93 < α < 0.95) and so we averaged the items in each study to form a single measure.
Wind farm policy support We used the same 2-item scale used by Winter and colleagues 20 . I generally support political measures to expand wind energy in my country and The government should increase their efforts to expand wind energy in my country. In Study 1, participants responded on a Likert scale from 1 (Strongly disagree) to 5 (Strongly agree) whereas in Study 2 the scale ranged from 0 (Strongly disagree) to 100 (Strongly agree). The two-items showed good internal consistency across both Study 1 (α = 0.92 at both timepoints) and Study 2 (0.96 < α < 0.98 across timepoints) and so were averaged to form a single measure.
Wind farm true claims endorsement To check for pattern responding, we embedded four true (and positive) statements about wind farms within the misinformation items. Pattern responding might be indicated by perverse positive correlations between measures that should be negatively correlated (e.g., as participants respond exclusively down the right-hand or left-hand sides of the scale). However, inspection of the correlations in supplementary Table S12 offers no evidence that this was prevalent in our sample.
Demographics As well as recording their age and gender, participants indicated their education on a 5-point scale (1: Less than high school diploma or equivalent – 5: Completed an advanced degree ) (M study 1 =3.56, SD study 1 =0.97, M study 2 =3.62, SD study 2 =0.95). Participants also indicated their political orientation (1 =  very liberal , 7 = very conservative ) on two items: one related to social issues (e.g., immigration, homosexual marriage, abortion) and one related to economic issues (e.g., social welfare, government spending, tax cuts). The items displayed good agreement (α = 0.91 for both studies) and so were averaged to form a single measure of political orientation.
Protest intentions In Study 1 only, we also included a measure of intentions to protest against wind farms (e.g. If a wind farm was planned close to my home town I would protest against it ). However, scores were close to floor ( M  = 2.25, SD  = 1.13) and analyses revealed no significant effects. This measure was not included in Study 2. Results of these analyses are detailed in supplementary Table S13.
Exploratory moderations In each study we examined whether the effects of Condition over Time were moderated by age, gender, education, and political orientation. We also examined two exploratory variables that were measured at the end of the last follow-up survey: (1) conspiracy mentality 19 (taken from Bruder and colleagues), a 5-item scale measuring propensity to believe conspiracy theories, and (2) wind farm salience, a 3-item scale measuring the extent to which participants were thinking and talking about wind farms since the original intervention. In Study 1, none of these moderation effects were significant. In Study 2, 5 out of 18 moderation effects were significant. These effects are summarised in supplementary Fig S3.
Response coding After the initial survey data were collected, two research assistants and Anthropic’s AI, Claude 3.5 Sonnet, coded each initial wind farm attitude as either “anti” (exclusively negative comments), “pro” (exclusively positive comments), “mixed” (a blend of positive and negative comments) or “uncertain” (responses characterised by self-professed ignorance about wind farms). There was good agreement between the human and AI coders in both Study 1 (Rater 1 & Claude: 81.76%, Rater 2 & Claude: 85.46%, Rater 1 & Rater 2: 82.65%) and Study 2 (Rater 1 & Claude: 74.39%, Rater 2 & Claude: 79.13%, Rater 1 & Rater 2: 82.61%). In cases where there was disagreement between the three raters, we used the consensus of two raters. In cases where there was no consensus between at least two raters, a third rater made the decision.
Fact checking To verify the accuracy of ChatGPT-4 Turbo’s responses, and in line with recommendations 48 , we conducted systematic fact-checking using Anthropic’s Claude 3.5 Sonnet model. Each response was evaluated through the API with a single prompt: “Please analyse if this response is likely to be true based on known facts and logical consistency. Respond with only ‘Likely true’ or ‘Likely false’.” The analysis revealed that 98.70% of responses were classified as “likely true.” Upon manual inspection by our research team, the remaining responses marked as “likely false” were determined to be artifacts of API errors rather than actual inaccuracies in the content.
We acknowledge that relying solely on another LLM for fact-checking has limitations, as LLMs may share similar training data and systematic biases. To address this, we engaged an independent climate science expert (who teaches climate literacy and sustainability to postgraduate and executive audiences) to manually review 20% of randomly sampled ChatGPT outputs from both studies (600 ChatGPT responses total). This expert review identified no instances of misinformation or factual inaccuracy in the sampled responses, providing independent validation of our automated fact-checking approach.
