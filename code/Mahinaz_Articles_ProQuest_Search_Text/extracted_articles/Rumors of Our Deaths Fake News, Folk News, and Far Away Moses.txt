Headnote This paper addresses the suggestion that "fake news" would be better called "folk news."It explores the tale of the death and subsequent replacement by an impostor of a nineteenth-century celebrity, comparing that story to similar tales about Paul McCartney and Mark Twain. The legends and proverbs created in the context of these news reports suggest that "fake news" is not the same as "folk news," largely because "folk" does not mean "fake."
Keywords
AFS ethnographic thesaurus: Legends, proverbs, news, death, hoax
In 2016 and early 2017, as the topic of "fake news" was coming to dominate the real news where I work in Washington, DC, I was doing research into a nineteenth-century tour guide and merchant named Far Away Moses. I didn't expect to come across "fake news" in my research into Moses, but as it happens, I did. It got me thinking about some of the issues involved in studying fake news, and particularly about the way in which fake news has always been part of what folklorists study.
Far Away Moses was an interpreter, tour guide, and merchant active in Istanbul, Cairo, and several American cities during the nineteenth century. He was best known for having been Mark Twain's guide in Istanbul and is thus mentioned in The Innocents Abroad (Twain 1869:381), but he was also written about by many contemporary writers who traveled in the Ottoman Empire. At the time, it was common for one's guide, commonly referred to as a dragoman, to act as a negotiator for his clients when haggling in the bazaar, and in this way, Moses came to be known to Ottoman merchants. He then became a merchant himself, managing shops in several cities, and he visited the United States when his company opened a chain of Oriental rug stores in major American cities with Moses as their manager; he seems to have lived in the United States for most of the 1870s before going home to Constantinople. He returned to the United States in 1893 for the World's Columbian Exposition, where he was one of the leaders of the Ottoman delegation.1
From the 1860s on, Far Away Moses was also the subject of legends and folk stories. One of Twain's traveling companions, John Franklin Swift, pointed out that Moses was legendary before the expedition even met him:
While we are yet in the land of Egypt, vague and indistinct rumors floated down the waters of the great sea and up the sluggish current of the Nile, about the inestimable qualities of one Far-away-Moses, who resided in Constantinople. This phenomenon, so the report went, was honest to a degree never before known in the East. And many were the indistinct stories we heard of amber mouthpieces, of embroidered slippers, or of Persian carpets, refused absolutely to the Giaour in person with money in hand, at one sum, but brought in triumphantly at a later day by the ingenuous Moses, who had in the mean time been dispatched on a secret mission to the bazaar for that purpose, at one-half, or even one-fourth, of the money before contemptuously declined. . . . As we worked our way slowly along the Syrian coast, approaching gradually the scene of Moses's operations, his fame grew more distinct and decisive. That which had been whispered at Cairo was muttered at Damascus, and statements which at Beyrout were ventured with mysterious reluctance, were boldly and defiantly proclaimed at Smyrna. (1868:425)
Another set of legends documented by travelers were stories about how Moses got his name from Twain. The anonymous article "Finishing the Fair" in the April 19, 1893, San Francisco Chronicle told the story this way:
The humorist and the guide strolled along together, the former being so intensely interested in what he saw that he often stood stock still in the road to admire the strange architecture of the houses and shops. But Moses kept plodding away, with the result that when the humorist resumed his tramp the Turk was so far ahead of him that it was with difficulty that he was called back. So often did Twain find himself alone in his tramps through the crooked streets of Constantinople that he facetiously dubbed his guide and chaperone "Far Away."
Another variant was told by Thomas James Donlon:
In 1876, Robert Levy, a Turkish Jew, conceived the idea of bringing to America a collection of rugs. He also brought with him a native of Constantinople who had acted as guide to Mark Twain and who was known as Faraway Moses. Thus he had been dubbed by the humorist, who always found himself left so far behind by his eager guide that he was compelled perpetually to shout after him, "Don't go so far away, Moses" in order to keep him somewhere within hailing distance. He put Moses in his book, which had a great vogue at that time, and made him famous. Levy located on Broadway and widely advertised the presence of the notorious cicerone of the famous humorist. Crowds flocked to the store to see Faraway Moses, and the beauty, durability, and value of the Oriental rug were thus brought to the notice of the American public. (1915:14)
Amusingly, these authors think they are recounting something from Twain's book, yet nothing like it exists in Twain's account. In fact, Twain clearly states that the dragoman was already called "Far Away Moses" by the time the two met, and there are accounts of Moses' activities that use the name Far Away Moses at least 4 years before he met Twain.
What's the fake news? In late 1893, soon after the Exposition, Far Away Moses was the subject of what today might be called a "celebrity death hoax" A strange conspiracy theory emerged involving Moses' death and replacement by an impostor. The story is recorded, as far as I can tell, in just one source: an unsigned article entitled "Exposition Items" in the December 9, 1893, Scientific American weekly supplement:
Every visitor at the Exposition heard a great deal about fakes in Midway Plaisance, and no doubt encountered several of them, but one deception has just come to light which will disappoint many people. Probably no character in the Midway was talked about more than Far Away Moses, who was connected with the Constantinople bazar [sic]. This individual was made famous by Mark Twain, and nearly every American who has visited Constantinople since Mark Twain's memorable visit has made use of this guide. When the Constantinople bazar was opened it was heralded broadcast that Far Away Moses was on hand to receive his old friends and patrons and scores of these people have hunted him up. Since the Exposition has closed, it has been discovered that the original Far Away Moses died some three years ago and that this counterpart is an individual resembling him who was brought to Chicago because of the trade he might draw because of his name.
To be clear, I do not find this story remotely plausible. Over 200 people made up the Ottoman delegation to the Exposition, four-fifths of them Jews (Kirshenblatt-Gimblett 1997:68). Most of them would have known Moses as a celebrated member of their own small community and would have been aware if this ersatz Moses were not the genuine article. Scores of American tourists who knew Moses personally from meeting him in Cairo or Istanbul met him again on the Midway, so he would have had to fool them as well. Moses had lived in the United States for a good part of the 1870s, with some of that time spent in chicago, so he would have had visitors who knew him quite well. All of these people would have had to have been either deceived or in on the deception.
Among guests who visited Far Away Moses on the "Midway Plaisance" at the Expo, there were some who claimed intimate acquaintance with him. To give one example, the writer and artist Francis Hopkinson Smith wrote of a visit with Moses during the Expo:
My old and valued friend, Far-away Moses, I say, invited me to dinner. I have enjoyed this especial privilege very often in his own bazaar in Stamboul, and the aroma of the Mocha and the soothing qualities of his Narghilehs have haunted me ever since. Now thanks to his courtesy I can enjoy them every day.
We talk of the old days in Stamboul and of the morning we spent at the bath where I was parboiled and rubbed full of holes by two insufficiently clad Greeks; and then of the festival night at Saint Sophia when, as a member of his household, I entered the Sacred Mosque barefooted and befezzed. Later on a lighted lantern is brought in and we follow another gorgeous slave into the mysteries of my host's private apartments, where a repast of kebabs and boiled rice is served. (1893:113-6)
It certainly doesn't seem plausible that Smith could have had this long session of reminiscences without suspecting he was visiting an impostor.
After Moses returned home, people continued to report seeing and meeting with him until the early twentieth century, including William Eleazar Barton (1902:103) and Mary Frances Willard (1914:194). Apparently, whoever was known after the Exposition as "Far Away Moses" was retained by the same company Moses had long worked for, until he died in 1908 or early 1909. Given all this, the story in Scientific American simply can't be true without a massive conspiracy to keep it quiet.
So is this an example of nineteenth-century "fake news"? Russell Frank defines "fake news" as "intentionally false reports" "written in conformity with journalistic style" (2015:315-6). This story about Far Away Moses was written in the style of a mainstream magazine. We don't know who the author was, and we consequently can't prove the reporting was intentionally false. On the other hand, the "celebrity death hoax" is a recognized genre of fake news,2 and this story fits right into that mold. Meanwhile, the Collins Dictionary definition quoted by Tom Mould in the introduction to this issue of JAF does not insist that the news be "intentionally false," instead requiring fake news to be "false, often sensational, information disseminated under the guise of news reporting" (Collins 2017). By some definitions, then, this story is certainly "fake news."
On the other hand, "fake news" may not be a useful way to frame matters anyway. In a recent article for Culture Digitally, Whitney Phillips points out that "fake news" stories may be fascinating for reasons that have nothing to do with their objective truth (2017). She proposes the term "folkloric news" or even "folk news" as a substitute, a suggestion subsequently picked up by Jesse Singal (2017) on New York Magazine's blog, the Cut.
Although the term "folk news" seems handy, I am uncomfortable with a simple substitution of "folk news" for "fake news." For too long, "folklore" was used as a substitute for "popular error" or "untrue stories believed to be true." Since (by our folkloristic definition) folk stories are not necessarily false, "folk news" should not necessarily be "fake." Folklore and news intersect in many interesting ways, after all, and many real news stories have folkloric traits as well. Thus, "fake news" may be no more folkloric than "real news."
Still, Phillips makes a good point that applies to the Far Away Moses story when she says that it would be better to use "a frame that foregrounds why a particular story is believed, and what that belief reveals about the broader cultural landscape-not just whether or not the story is true" (2017). True or not, it's easy to imagine why such a story about Moses might arise and why it would be appealing and believable in America in 1893.
As revealed in a blog post I wrote on Folklife Today, for years there had actually been impostors who pretended to be Moses (or to be related to Moses) in order to pick up tourist business in the bazaars of Cairo and Constantinople (Winick 2017).
Some Americans knew this, which could have led to stories about impostors replacing the celebrity guide. Some Americans might have been deceived by an ersatz Moses in Istanbul and thus might not have recognized the real Moses upon meeting him in Chicago.
There were also people who had long wished to discredit Far Away Moses, including department stores that he had competed with since the 1870s. In another blog post (Winick 2016), I pointed out that Wanamaker's department store (with whom Moses had directly competed by opening a Philadelphia store in the 1870s) ran an ad in the Philadelphia Times on April 6, 1892, that tried not only to discredit Moses specifically, but also suggested that anyone with Ottoman origins was untrustworthy:
The humbug of Far-away Moses has often been punctured and yet foreign travelers in Turkey are still swindled in Constantinople. Devious and crafty Armenian ways are grafted upon American trading in Oriental Embroideries and Rugs. Childlike and bland, dignified and solemn, mysterious and mystical are the manners; artful and subtle the methods by which the credulous are cajoled into purchasing "bargains" that pay fabulous profits.
For those who were aware that Moses was Jewish, anti-Semitism probably played a role as well. Some observers, while admitting that Moses himself was probably honest, nevertheless distrusted the whole class of Jewish merchants from which he came. For example, Richard Ferguson wrote:
Travellers, almost invariably, are victimized by their dragoman who, of course, gets a percentage on their purchases, and has his favourite tradesmen, or they fall into the hands of Jew brokers, of whom the most famous, much patronized by Americans, is Far-Away-Moses of Constantinople and Cairo, a man as honest as an Eastern can be expected to be. (1873:27)
Like both fake and real news, then, the story of Moses' death and replacement by an impostor might have spread because it confirmed people's preconceived biases. "We knew Jews and Ottoman merchants were untrustworthy," they might say, "and here is one more outrageous way they have deceived us!"
To use Sandy Hobbs' (1987:141) term for one of the functions of contemporary legends, there was even "poetic justice" in the idea that Far Away Moses was an impostor: for supporters of Wanamaker's, who had complained of "the humbug of Far-away Moses," how sweet it must have been to find evidence that he literally was a humbug!
Clearly, the legend of Moses' death was affected by the prejudices of his age, but it also clearly transcends them. In fact, it's hard not to notice how much this story resembles one of the most popular journalistic legends of about 70 years later, which still lives on today in current "fake news": the story that Paul McCartney was killed in 1966 and replaced by an impostor. This combination of legend and conspiracy theory first arose in 1969. It is so well known that it has its own acronym (PID for "Paul Is Dead"), entire books devoted to it, numerous websites and articles, and its own Wikipedia page.3 According to the website Turn Me On, Dead Man, one of the most detailed accounts was published in a 1969 fan magazine called Paul McCartney Dead: The Great Hoax. The magazine (as quoted by the website) claims that Paul was killed in a car accident in November 1966, after which the Beatles' record company became involved:
Not wanting to lose potential record sales, record company executives suppressed the story of Paul's death and brought in a lookalike to replace him. Paul's stand-in was a man named William Campbell, who had won a Paul McCartney lookalike contest.
With a little plastic surgery, William Campbell had taken Paul's place in photos of the group. The surgery had been successful except for a small scar above his lip. And, as luck would have it, William Campbell could also sing and just happened to be a songwriter with an exceptional ear for pop melodies.
The common features between the stories of Paul and Moses are clear: the untimely death of a celebrity, partners who wish to continue making money from his fame, the scheme to hire an impersonator, and the successful impersonator retaining the new role for the rest of his life. To be true, both stories would require the existence of a large conspiracy over many years. To a folklorist, the similarity of these two stories indicates first of all that Far Away Moses was a true celebrity of his day, subject to the same processes of legend formation and the same appeal to writers of both fake and real "folk news" that affect modern celebrities like Paul McCartney. It also suggests that while the celebrity death story can be seen more broadly as a genre or subgenre, the more specific tale in which the celebrity is replaced by an impostor who lives out his days as the celebrity might be a migratory legend, traveling from place to place and becoming attached to real people along the way.
To understand the difference between folk news and fake news, let's look at an interesting coincidence: Mark Twain, Far Away Moses' old friend and the man who had done the most to make him a celebrity, was also once incorrectly rumored to have died. A few years after Moses' brush with fake news, on May 31, 1897, a tale circulated in the New York press that Twain had passed away. Twain, at that time in London, was immediately contacted by the newspaper editor Frank Marshall White. According to White, Twain scrawled a hasty letter in reply:
James Ross Clemens, a cousin of mine, was seriously ill two or three weeks ago in London, but is well now. The report of my illness grew out of his illness, the report of my death was an exaggeration. (quoted in White 1910:966)
White sent this report to New York by telegram, and the paper printed it. White writes that "this by process of repetition became 'The reports of my death are grossly exaggerated'" (Twain, quoted in White 1910:967), and indeed it later appeared, slightly embellished, in Albert Bigelow Paine's biography of Twain as "the report of my death has been grossly exaggerated" (Twain, quoted in Paine 1912:1039).
Twain's phrase was soon adopted by all kinds of people wishing to dispel the notion of a person's death-and, by extension, the metaphorical "death" of an activity, an attitude, a project, or an idea. In time, the process of both oral and written transmission created many variants of the phrase. In my notes, I have documented it varying as to whether the statement is ascribed to Twain or not; whether there is one report (as in Twain's original) or more than one; whether they are "reports" or "rumors"; whether the reports are of the speaker's "death" or "demise"; and whether they are "an exaggeration" (as in Twain's original), "a gross exaggeration," "exaggerated," "greatly exaggerated," or "grossly exaggerated." There are probably other variations I have missed.
The recurrent uses of this phrase in popular media, beginning with newspapers, made it into a traditional way to address an untrue rumor of the death of a person or practice. As a traditional sentence that is sometimes metaphorical and usually employed to address a recurrent type of social situation, the phrase fits many folkloristic definitions of the proverb.
Seventy years after Twain coined this proverb, news of the "Paul Is Dead" legend reached Paul McCartney. According to The Beatles Bible, he used a variant of Twain's proverb, telling Beatles' assistant Peter Brown to tell the press: "Rumours of my death have been greatly exaggerated."
Here, then, is an example of "folk news" that is not fake: a new folk saying emerging through transmission in news stories, undergoing the same process of variation typical for orally transmitted folklore. In both Twain's and McCartney's cases, the folklore was created and deployed in the context of "real news," accurately capturing the speaker's intended meaning. And in both cases, the "folk news" that was "real news" was published in response to other "folk news," which was "fake news": inaccurate reports about a celebrity death. The simplistic substitution of "folk" for "fake" obviously doesn't reflect the complexity with which folklore moves through the news. "Folk news" and "fake news," it turns out, are entirely different categories, although they might sometimes overlap. Most importantly, "folk news" does not mean "fake news" because "folk" does not mean "fake."
What about Far Away Moses? There's no evidence he ever commented on (or even heard of) the story that he had died. On the other hand, who needs evidence in the age of fake news? I like to imagine this little scene:
It's December 1893, and Samuel Clemens, better known as Mark Twain, is sitting down to read his copy of the Scientific American supplement. He sees a startling note to the effect that his old friend Moses has been dead for years. He immediately sends a telegram to the US consulate in Constantinople: "I hear my old friend, Far Away Moses, is dead. If this is true, please convey my condolences to his family."
A few days later, he receives a puzzled reply: "Have just had a visit from Far Away Moses. Touched that you remembered your old friend. Says the report of his death was an exaggeration."
"Good old Moses," Clemens chuckles, folding the telegram and placing it in his pocket. "The report of my death was an exaggeration! That's a good one! I'll have to remember that!"
