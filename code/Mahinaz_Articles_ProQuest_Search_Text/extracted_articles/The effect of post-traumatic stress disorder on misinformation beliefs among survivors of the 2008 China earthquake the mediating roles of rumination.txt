Introduction
The Wenchuan County in Sichuan Province, China, experienced a major earthquake of magnitude 8.0 on May 12, 2008 [1]. This destruction created by the earthquake resulted in a significant loss of human life, with thousands of fatalities, as well as serious economic damage amounting to $150 billion [1, 2]. The quantity of research on earthquakes is numerous but there is a noticeable scarcity of articles specifically addressing the topic of post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) among survivors of the Wenchuan earthquake, especially how they coped during the COVID-19 epidemic. This unexpected global public health crisis led to widespread physical isolation and a misinformation deluge about the epidemic (which may have caused mass panic). For the survivors of the Wenchuan earthquake, more than a decade has passed, and little research has been conducted on how they coped during other psychological public health emergencies or how they dealt with the resulting internet information deluge. To clarify, regarding the Wenchuan earthquake, the task of rebuilding Wenchuan County’s infrastructure has been accomplished but there exists a lack of awareness regarding the psychological dimensions of the survivors’ reconstruction efforts. Additionally, numerous existing studies have mainly concentrated on the immediate effects of COVID-19 while ignoring the distinct challenges and dynamics of the post-pandemic era [3].
According to Musheer et al. [4], participants with PTSD show a higher level of negative mood. Recent reviews indicate that the combined effects of the Wenchuan earthquake and COVID-19 may have resulted in heightened “double stress” and exacerbated PTSD symptoms [5]. After the earthquake, numerous victims had to alter their personal habits, such as their lifestyle and exercise patterns; this situation was mirrored in the challenges encountered by the public during the COVID-19 pandemic [6, 7]. A study examining changes in suicide rates during the East Japan earthquake and COVID-19 revealed that survivors of the earthquake experienced greater psychological distress compared to individuals who were anxious but did not endure the earthquake, resulting in a more significant rise in suicide rates during this public health crisis [7]. Research also indicates that earthquakes have more prolonged adverse effects on fear and well-being than COVID-19 [8]. Changes in the social climate (catastrophe of insecurity) may also exacerbate PTSD, in contrast to anxiety or depressive mood [9]. Moreover, alterations in health service utilisation patterns following a crisis or disaster may manifest in both short- and long-term effects on an individual’s PTSD [10]. Consequently, scholars have emphasised the necessity of concentrating public health interventions and therapeutic interventions on populations that are impacted by both earthquakes and pandemics.
In conclusion, the aforementioned studies demonstrate changes in PTSD among earthquake survivors during COVID-19, offering mood comparisons with the general population; the results suggest that prior trauma exposure and ensuing post-traumatic stress symptoms may heighten an individual’s susceptibility to further stressors [11]. Waves of misinformation can similarly diminish an individual’s resources and coping mechanisms, consequently heightening their susceptibility to stress [12]. Therefore, it is reasonable to question whether individuals who have endured a natural disaster (e.g. an earthquake) face an increased risk of poor mental health when confronted with extensive misinformation. In this research, we surveyed a sample of survivors of the Wenchuan earthquake in China, primarily since the Wenchuan earthquake is the most significant seismic event in China’s history and is a highly representative case. Secondly, the earthquake disaster period coincided with the swift advancement of social networks, so this study will not only look to corroborate prior findings but also furnish evidence regarding the media psychology of Wenchuan’s PTSD population in this social media era.
Research suggests that the impact of PTSD symptom-triggering events on internet addiction varies [13]. Similarly, researchers refer to the overuse of smart devices in individuals with PTSD as an “iDisorder” [14]. This non-adaptive technology use is also related to the fear of missing out (FOMO). FOMO is characterised by an individual’s persistent desire to keep connected to the enjoyable experiences of others and an apprehension about missing out on such experiences [15]. This phenomenon, although promoting the use of social media, has been found to have negative effects on both physical and mental well-being [16]. Because previous research on social media use suggests that it causes psychological and emotional confusion, Park [17] examined the complex relationship between Facebook use intensity, FOMO, and depression. The study revealed that individuals with PTSD experience fear and distrust caused by information overload on social media platforms. Furthermore, specific social media content induces triggers for traumatic experiences, thereby further destroying the trust in these platforms [18]. The strategy of using social media to mitigate symptoms is frequently counterproductive, resulting in increased dependence on social media. This can impair the ability of those with PTSD to critically evaluate information [18] and increase the likelihood of them trusting and sharing potentially misleading information. As FOMO is derived from the satisfaction of psychological needs in chronic insufficient circumstances [15], individuals who dedicate more time to social networks are more susceptible to feelings of “missing out” or exclusion from beneficial experiences as a result of their PTSD symptoms [19]. Overall, if PTSD individuals use social networking sites (SNS) to mitigate social deficiencies and relieve fears of isolation, this may result in increased FOMO or potentially inadequate capabilities in managing internet disinformation (e.g. they trust and disseminate such information).
Moreover, certain studies provide evidence that memories of significantly negative or traumatic incidents may be distorted [20]. Individuals experiencing higher distress levels (e.g. PTSD) exhibit a significant correlation between diminished accuracy and misleading information [21]. This phenomenon is referred to as the misinformation effect [22]. Studies [20,21,22] indicate that PTSD correlates with heightened vulnerability to erroneous recollections of emotionally significant occurrences. However, there is a lack of research on the potential user response behaviours (e.g. trust and sharing) that underly this misinformation effect in mediated communication. The present study aimed to address this issue.
Rumination is a complex process consisting of various components encompassing both behavioural and cognitive aspects. It primarily revolves around the thoughts associated with a negative mental state and their subsequent consequences. This phenomenon is widely recognised as a psychological vulnerability factor associated with the development of depression and anxiety [23]. One study [24] discovered that depressed individuals frequently ruminate without developing solutions, and that ruminating increases with access to online information. Rumination may act as a consequential response to an unexpected stressful event (earthquake, etc.) [25]. This is mainly attributed to its function as a mediator between traumatic experiences and the increased experience of negative emotions, including but not limited to sadness, shame, and anger. However, although rumination may be a cognitive maintenance factor in PTSD, most research still focuses on general forms of rumination that involve negative, abstract self-evaluations, rather than rumination that is focused on post-disaster trauma. Therefore, studies on media use and internet information access are still unclear on why trauma survivors engage in rumination despite it having negative psychological effects [26].
Given this backdrop, it is essential to understand how FOMO, rumination, and other negative affective states influence the degree of misinformation beliefs among the broader population, especially in the post-pandemic context [3, 4]. This study aimed to fill a gap in the existing literature by examining the potential relationship between PTSD and misinformation beliefs among survivors of the Wenchuan earthquake, through the lens of FOMO and rumination.
Literature review and hypothesis
The independent variable role of PTSD
PTSD can manifest with diverse levels of influence on an individual’s social, academic, professional, familial, and legal progress, particularly when associated with social anxiety [27]. In specific cases of social isolation (e.g. natural disasters or global public health emergencies such as COVID-19), people may frequently encounter the FOMO phenomenon when they are physically isolated from their social milieu and have limited interaction with friends, spouses, or family members [28]. It is reasonable to speculate that the disruption of social networks resulting from the Wenchuan earthquake was a contributing factor to the phenomenon known as FOMO. Previous research examining the association between PTSD and FOMO has commonly incorporated mediator variables, such as problematic mobile phone use [14, 29], anxiety or depression [16, 30], and compensatory internet use [31, 32]. Studies on PTSD have broadened the scope of psychopathology-related conceptions. For example, Elhai et al. [33] explored the correlation between FOMO and the severity of PTSD among Asian participants, finding that FOMO could potentially serve as a significant factor in explaining this link. However, in another separate study [21], these authors worried about the limited number of studies investigating the relationship between non-social smartphone use and information sharing and mental health characteristics.
According to Janoff-Bulman [34], individuals who have experienced trauma may exhibit a state of internal instability both before and after the traumatic event, which can result in their heightened preoccupation with and repetitive thoughts about the painful experience. Therefore, it is likely that traumatic experiences are predictive of PTSD due to their propensity to induce rumination. However, there is little research on the specific factors that could potentially influence this relationship. Therefore, given the findings of previous research, given the independent role of PTSD and the limited population in certain investigations examining relevant correlations, specifically among earthquake survivors samples [13], we propose Hypotheses H1 and H2:
H1
PTSD caused by the Wenchuan earthquake will be positively associated with FOMO.
H2
PTSD caused by the Wenchuan earthquake will be positively associated with rumination.
The mediating role of rumination
Rumination is a complex process that includes various activities and thoughts focused on negative mental states and their consequences. It is widely recognised as a psychological vulnerability factor associated with the development of depression and anxiety. Rumination has been identified as a possible mediating factor in the influence of traumatic experiences on PTSD. The study of rumination could potentially enhance open discussion about PTSD trauma and its related triggers [35].
However, limited research has been conducted to evaluate the potential mediating influences of rumination in PTSD, particularly among youth and adults who have been experiencing rapid developmental and societal changes since 2008. Wu et al. [36] observed that intrusive rumination (IR) regarding an earthquake had a significant influence on the development of PTSD. The researchers characterised IR as involuntary thoughts that infiltrate an individual’s cognitive processes without deliberate intention and that typically involve a negative focus on the traumatic event.
When people encounter difficulties in their everyday experiences, those who are unable to accomplish their objectives despite persistent effort will engage in a process of reassessment or propose alternative courses of action. According to Liu and Ren [37], if the thought process persists in a repetitive and self-perpetuating manner, it is categorised as rumination. In general, rumination can be classified into two categories: intrusive rumination (IR) and deliberate rumination (DR). The former is the uncontrollable recollection of trauma-related cues, whereas the latter is the deliberate consideration and re-examination of trauma events [38]. Numerous studies on the Chinese earthquake [39,40,41] indicate that for the majority of individuals, IR typically arises soon after the traumatic incident, whereas, over an extended timeline, DR tends to prevail. Therefore, we must differentiate between the intrusive symptoms of PTSD and trauma-focused rumination. Although rumination in the context of PTSD has the same characteristics (e.g. abstractness, repetitiveness) as rumination in other conditions (e.g. depression), the content of ruminative thought is distinct in PTSD, as the emphasis is on the trauma and its repercussions, which include PTSD symptoms [42]. The primary distinction is that intrusive symptoms are essentially recurrent “flashes” of the trauma that are highly sensory and arise unintentionally, whereas rumination refers to abstract, persevering chains of thought about the traumatic event itself, as well as its antecedents and consequences. In this way, rumination in PTSD may well be indicative of a conscious effort to understand the traumatic event and “work through” it, albeit in an unproductive manner. This may evolve into a more automatic, default response as time progresses. In line with previous earthquake–rumination research [38, 39, 41, 42], we also reasonably distinguish rumination as a psychological variable that is distinct from certain rumination symptoms of PTSD.
Additionally, it was previously believed that rumination is related to negative trauma outcomes due to the tendency of individuals who ruminate to maintain their attention on the traumatic event and associated emotions, rather than engaging in proactive behaviours. However, recent studies [38, 43] suggest that rumination has the potential to induce errors in information processing and heighten the intensity of symptoms associated with PTSD. According to Hertel et al. [44], the activation of rumination memories can influence the ability to access information online, which indicates a potential positive correlation between rumination and misinformation beliefs. Nonetheless, there is a lack of research examining the impact of social media messages on specific ruminant populations (such as the PTSD–rumination population) after the COVID-19 pandemic [45]. Therefore, based on the above studies and considering the mediating role of rumination, we propose the following hypothesis:
H3
Rumination will be positively associated with misinformation beliefs.
H4
PTSD caused by the Wenchuan earthquake will be indirectly and positively associated with misinformation beliefs through rumination.
The mediating role of FOMO
FOMO gained mass media attention during the early 2010s. This phenomenon involves concerns about missing out on others’ experiences and the constant checking of SNS and message applications to stay updated and is recognised as a longstanding issue. FOMO prevents enjoyable activities, disrupts social engagements, and involves a constant desire to share news.
The phenomenon of misinformation has been widely analysed by scholars in relation to cognitive-psychological factors and FOMO [46,47,48]. What are the reasons behind the dissemination of misinformation? Talwar et al. [38] examined the relationship between negative aspects of social media usage and the impulse to believe misinformation or share fake news among people who engage with social media platforms. They found that factors such as online trust, self-disclosure, FOMO, and social media fatigue are positively associated with the dissemination of misinformation. Another study [19] showed that there is a negative correlation between the length of time people spend on SNS and their self-esteem. More recent research [46] indicates a positive correlation between the usage of social media for news consumption, the experience of FOMO, and the intentional sharing of deepfake content, and suggests that individuals with lower cognitive capacities will exhibit higher levels of FOMO and engage in more frequent sharing behaviours on social media platforms. Additionally, the study revealed a novel insight, such that individuals with a higher analytical mindset are also vulnerable to the dissemination of misinformation.
In general, the identified association between FOMO and sharing news behaviour is valuable as it establishes an empirical basis for the formulation, arrangement, and mitigation of potentially harmful actions [48]. Expanding upon this, our study aimed to investigate the correlation between psychological factors, outcomes, and beliefs or trust in information disseminated through social media platforms. Based on previous research and considering the mediating role of rumination, we propose the following research hypotheses:
H5
FOMO will be positively associated with beliefs in misinformation.
H6
PTSD caused by the Wenchuan earthquake will be indirectly and positively associated with misinformation beliefs through FOMO.

Methods
Participants and procedure
This study utilised a survey approach and was approved by the Ethics Committee of Mianyang College of Administration at the time of data collection. Participants were recruited from Beichuan County, an area particularly hard-hit by the Wenchuan earthquake. All the participants gave written informed consent and were compensated with ¥10 for their time. The data collection process took place over a six-month period, from January to June 2023, and was conducted through field visits. A total of 500 questionnaires were distributed during this time. To determine the sample size, we referenced prior studies in comparable contexts [49, 50] and deemed the sample size (N = 356) to be appropriate due to the relative simplicity of the statistical model examined (i.e. absence of chain mediation structure). After eliminating questionnaires with significant amounts of missing data, as well as those completed in an unusually short duration, 373 valid responses remained: 185 men and 188 women between the ages of 20 and 51 (31.59 ± 5.72). The effective questionnaire response rate was 74.6%.
The prevalence of PTSD was 24.4%, which is consistent with Wenchuan earthquake studies such as those by Wang et al. [1] and Ying et al. [51], which had 28.4% and 42.5% respectively. Note that our study was not purely a PTSD population survey, because it was restricted by sampling issues and a limited survey area; thus, the level of PTSD symptoms experienced by survivors 15 years after the earthquake must be interpreted with caution.
Data analysis
Measurement of the structural relationship between variables was conducted using a partial least squares-structural equation modelling (PLS-SEM) methodology. Under the PLS-SEM process, two assessment models were developed: an outer model and an inner model. The outer model refers to a measurement model that is used to predict the correlation between indicators or parameters that have been estimated with their corresponding latent variables. The evaluation of this measurement model is to evaluate the validity and reliability of the measures used to assess the concepts, thereby supporting their inclusion in the path model [52]. The second model, referred to as the inner model, refers to a structural model that provides predictions regarding the causal relationship existing among latent variables.
The reliability test for the indicators in the partial least squares (PLS) analysis involved assessing the factor loading values, which served as a measure of the construct. In the context of assessing the reliability of scale-type measurement instruments, it is common to employ Cronbach’s alpha (α) as the measure of internal consistency. According to Aiche et al. [53], a measurement dimension is deemed to possess satisfactory internal consistency if its Cronbach’s α exceeds the threshold of 0.7. Validity refers to the degree of accuracy exhibited by the collected data. In the context of PLS structural equation modelling, it is imperative to assess the factor loadings associated with the latent variables that correspond to the questionnaire items. Factor loadings exceeding 0.6 are indicative of higher quality. Furthermore, as proposed by Hair and previous research with similar analytical methods [52, 54,55,56], the composite reliability (CR) and average variance extracted (AVE) of the dimensions were computed using standardised factor loadings. It was determined that a measured dimension exhibited satisfactory convergent validity if the CR value exceeded 0.7 and the AVE value exceeded 0.5.
Measures
PTSD scale
For the PTSD scales, we referred to prior research that employed questionnaires in Chinese [33, 57]. Hence, we chose the commonly translated and validated adult iteration of the PTSD Checklist – Civilian Version (PCL-C) [58], which exhibited ideal levels of validity and reliability in samples from the Chinese population. The participants were asked to rate each item on a 5-point Likert-type scale, ranging from 1 = “Strongly disagree” to 5 = “Strongly agree”. The internal consistency of the PTSD Scale in the study was classed as good (Cronbach’s α = 0.960).
FOMO scale
We adopted Przybylski’s [7] Fear of Missing Out (FOMO) Scale in this study. An example item is “I fear others have more rewarding experiences than me”. Higher total scores indicate a more severe experience of FOMO. The scale used a 5-point Likert-type scale ranging from 1 = “Not at all true of me” to 5 = “Extremely true of me”. Cronbach’s α = 0.935, suggesting a good internal consistency in this scale.
Rumination scale
The assessment of rumination was conducted using the Cognitive Emotion Regulation Questionnaire, specifically applying the revised Chinese version developed by Zhu et al. [59]. The questionnaire is a comprehensive 22-item instrument designed to assess different cognitive coping strategies, including self-blame, acceptance, rumination, positive refocusing, refocus on planning, positive reappraisal, putting into perspective, catastrophising, and other-blame [60]. All the items are rated on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 0 = “Completely disagree” to 4 = “Completely agree”. Cronbach’s α = 0.964.
Misinformation beliefs scale
Utilising established questionnaire items relating to social media usage [46, 61, 62], a scale consisting of five items was developed to measure beliefs in misinformation. An example item is “You consider it fake news if the news you follow goes against your expectations”. The questionnaire had good internal consistency (Cronbach’s α = 0.865). Each item was rated on a 5-point Likert-type scale ranging from 1 = “Strongly disagree” to 5 = “Strongly agree”. Higher scores represented higher levels of misinformation beliefs regarding social media.

Evaluation of structural model (inner model)
Covariance analysis
The model covariance was analysed using SmartPLS3 software. The analysis revealed that the variance inflation factor (VIF) values for the external model ranged from 1.841 to 2.794, while the VIF values for the internal model ranged from 1.000 to 1.035. Notably, all the VIF values were found to be less than 3. Therefore, there was no issue with covariance in the model.
Path coefficient analysis
The findings from the path coefficient analysis indicate that PTSD exerted a statistically significant and positive influence on FOMO (β = 0.372, SE = 0.046, t = 8.061, p < .001), PTSD had a significant positive effect on rumination (β = 0.390, SE = 0.041, t = 9.552, p < .001), FOMO had a significant positive effect on misinformation beliefs (β = 0.420, SE = 0.038, t = 11.001, p < .001), and rumination had a significant positive effect on misinformation beliefs (β = 0.396, SE = 0.040, t = 9.918, p < 0 0.001). The ultimate explanatory capacity of the variance was found to be 13.8% for FOMO, 15.2% for rumination, and 39.4% for misinformation beliefs (see Table 4 for more details).

Discussion
The primary objective of this study was to investigate the role of rumination and FOMO as a mediator in the relationship between PTSD and misinformation beliefs. The demographic composition of survivors of the Wenchuan earthquake in China predominantly consisted of individuals in the young and middle-aged adult age groups. In the past, there has been limited investigation into the correlation between these three factors with regard to Asian samples. To our knowledge, this study is the first attempt to investigate the mediating influence of rumination and FOMO on the association between PTSD and misinformation beliefs in the Chinese context. The results of this study suggest that PTSD caused by the Wenchuan earthquake directly impacted survivors’ FOMO and rumination. Additionally, the results suggest that PTSD had an indirect effect on misinformation beliefs by triggering increased rumination and FOMO. Significantly, the results also indicate that while rumination does not exert a significant influence on the direct pathway to FOMO, it does mediate the indirect association between PTSD related to the Wenchuan earthquake and misinformation beliefs.
Hence, the results of this study indicate a significant positive relationship between FOMO and PTSD, as well as between FOMO and misinformation beliefs. These findings provide support for H1 and H5. This finding aligns with research conducted by Elhai et al. [33, 63], who propose that FOMO could serve as a significant factor in elucidating specific forms of psychopathology. Also in accordance with prior research [64], our results indicate a direct association between rumination and PTSD, as well as between rumination and misinformation beliefs. Traumatic experiences have the potential to disrupt individuals’ established belief systems, leading to a rise in increased negative cognition and emotion [34]. Consequently, this can contribute to the manifestation of increased symptoms associated with rumination.
Additionally, this paper demonstrates the significant role that negative cognitive processes, such as rumination and FOMO, may potentially have in the development of misinformation beliefs. Consistent with Janoff-Bulman [26], our study indicated that there is a mediating effect of rumination on the relationship between experiences related to misinformation beliefs and PTSD. Experiencing trauma has the potential to disrupt individuals’ previously established and stable perceptions of themselves and the world. Consequently, this disruption often compels individuals to engage in the cognitive process of reconstructing their belief systems in the aftermath of trauma. During this process, repetitive and passive thoughts, which are specifically referred to as “rumination”, are activated.
According to Nolen-Hoeksema [65], rumination can intensify and extend distress through various mechanisms. Primarily, it may increase the impact of negative mood on cognitive processes, leading individuals who have experienced trauma to turn to negative thoughts and memories as they perceive their present situations. Furthermore, our rumination measurement scale and the mediation results emphasise the positive relationship between PTSD and trait rumination, which may expand research horizons on IR (intrusive rumination) and DR (deliberate rumination). As some disaster research on the cognitive theory of stress and coping indicates [e.g. 68], the profound emotions of fear and helplessness, accompanied by exposure to further adversities (e.g. entrapment beneath debris or bereavement), may exacerbate the traumatic stress burden on survivors. This heightened traumatic burden may also amplify the propensity for ruminative contemplation of events. Over time, IR develops into a pervasive symptom [66,67,68]. Our findings suggested that trait ruminativeness might be an ignored but significant factor in PTSD research. Future clinical efforts should focus on the enhancement of DR after IR is decreased.
The mediation analysis results of this current study also support H4 and H6. It was observed that both motivational factors (PTSD and misinformation beliefs) exhibited a positive correlation with FOMO, subsequently resulting in increased levels of involvement with social media platforms. The findings also demonstrate the strong mediating influence of FOMO on the relationship between experiences related to misinformation beliefs and PTSD. One reason for this may be the widespread impact of the COVID-19 pandemic during these years, which led to a significant upsurge in the individual utilisation of social media platforms on a global scale so that individuals with and without PTSD could maintain connectivity in their professional and personal lives. Hence, while it is likely that FOMO prompts individuals to engage with social media platforms, their personal predispositions and motivations may facilitate the acquisition of preferred information, potentially leading to a greater susceptibility to misinformation, and this is where the mediating role of FOMO is reflected. On the other hand, this discovery may be limited in its applicability to the sample under investigation, so further study is needed to support any broader generalisations.
It is imperative to acknowledge several design and measurement limitations. First, participants were not subjected to random selection but rather were chosen based on their convenience for the investigation. Consequently, there may be sampling bias in the study. Second, the measurement of all the variables was conducted through the use of self-report scales, so the associations between the measures may be influenced by common-method variance. The third limitation also pertains to the utilisation of self-report scales, a common approach employed in comparable studies [31, 33, 69]; the reliance on self-report measures to assess the psychopathological framework of the sample population may have introduced potential inaccuracies. Consequently, implementing a structured diagnostic interview specifically designed to evaluate depression and anxiety would provide a more precise assessment [31]. Thus, it is crucial to prioritise the examination of direct and indirect effects, rather than focusing exclusively on the comprehensive structural equation model.
Despite these limitations, this study makes a valuable contribution to the existing body of knowledge concerning the association between PTSD and misinformation beliefs, as well as providing insights into psychopathology-related variables, such as FOMO and rumination among PTSD patients, and trust in misinformation on social media platforms. This study also has significant implications for people following natural disasters from the perspective of intervention design and health enhancement. Clinical works should concentrate on reducing rumination and facilitating attentional redirection among PTSD patients, especially regarding the negative aspects associated with earthquakes. To alleviate the adverse effects of misinformation on individuals with PTSD, future research could investigate viable psychological therapies such as positive thinking therapy. Meanwhile, as a rising topic to be explored in the development of theoretical and empirical studies, we conclude that future research should investigate the effects of FOMO and rumination on personality traits and social media [70] in specific populations.
Conclusion
By adopting a mediated structural equation model, this study showed that PTSD symptoms in Wenchuan earthquake survivors are indirectly associated with believing misinformation. Specifically, FOMO and rumination were found to mediate the association between PTSD and misinformation beliefs. This research provides a significant reference for those who aim to analyse psychological variables in social media use among PTSD individuals. Future studies could enhance the generalisability of findings by improving the assessment of potential bidirectional relationships among these psychological variables.
