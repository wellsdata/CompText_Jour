1 Introduction
The rapid expansion of digital platforms and artificial intelligence (AI) has transformed contemporary information environments, amplifying the scale, speed, and sophistication of disinformation. Electoral processes have become particularly vulnerable, as algorithmic recommendation systems, generative AI tools, and platform-based communication facilitate the production, circulation, and amplification of misleading, manipulated, or false content. Deepfakes, synthetic media, and AI-generated information have intensified concerns about information integrity, democratic participation, and public trust in institutions.
From a Media Ecology perspective, these developments reflect broader transformations in the communication environments that shape how information is produced, distributed, and interpreted. Digital platforms function as complex socio-technical environments that influence visibility, credibility, and public discourse through algorithmic processes. At the same time, General Semantics highlights the challenges associated with meaning-making in increasingly mediated information ecosystems, where representations, interpretations, and realities may become blurred by misinformation, manipulation, and synthetic content. Together, these perspectives underscore the need to examine the technological and communicative environments in which disinformation emerges and circulates.
In response to these challenges, Media and Information Literacy (MIL) has gained increasing prominence as a framework for strengthening citizens’ critical capacities to access, evaluate, interpret, and create information. UNESCO has positioned MIL as a key component of democratic resilience in the digital age, emphasizing its role in fostering informed citizenship and resistance to information disorders. Moreover, UNESCO's MIL Cities framework expands the concept beyond individual competencies by recognizing the importance of institutions, universities, media organizations, observatories, and civil society actors in building information resilience and strengthening democratic communication ecosystems (Mateus, Andrada and Quiroz, 2019).
In Latin America, fact-checking organizations have adopted methodological standardization to strengthen their legitimacy (Rodríguez-Pérez et al., 2023). This development reflects broader structural challenges in the region, including limited journalistic autonomy, political influence over media, and precarious labor conditions (Moreno-Gil, Ramon, and Rodríguez-Martínez, 2021).
Since 2017, the media organization Verificado in Mexico has implemented strategies to monitor and counter disinformation, while simultaneously promoting MIL as a response. Concurrently, the proliferation of social media and AI has accelerated MIL initiatives within media observatories and universities, exemplified by the Digital Media Observatory at Tecnológico de Monterrey.
Despite growing scholarly attention to disinformation, artificial intelligence, and MIL, limited research has examined how institutional actors develop and implement strategies that integrate fact-checking, media observation, and MIL in response to AI-mediated disinformation. In particular, empirical studies are still needed to explain how organizations engaged in verification and media monitoring contribute to broader MIL ecosystems during electoral processes, especially in the context of AI-mediated disinformation.
This article addresses this gap by examining two complementary institutional responses to disinformation in Mexico: Verificado, the country's pioneering fact-checking organization, and the Digital Media Observatory of Tecnológico de Monterrey (OMD-Tec), which developed an innovative methodology for monitoring digital media ecosystems during the 2024 Mexican elections.
Utilizing UNESCO's MIL framework, along with Media Ecology and General Semantics, this article analyzes the contributions of these organizations to the development of MIL capacities, digital media observation practices, and strategies for addressing disinformation in contemporary electoral contexts. The study prioritizes institutional strategies, methodologies, and collaborative practices for addressing information disorders, rather than evaluating the effectiveness of specific interventions among end users.
The research is guided by UNESCO's MIL framework, particularly its ecosystemic perspective, which understands MIL as a collective endeavor involving educational institutions, media organizations, fact-checking initiatives, civil society actors, and knowledge networks.
This analysis highlights the role of fact-checking organizations and media observatories as key actors in constructing MIL ecosystems that strengthen democratic resilience within increasingly complex digital environments.
2 Literature review
2.1 Media and information literacy as a response to disinformation
Defined byUNESCO (2023)as “a set of competencies that enables individuals to understand, use, and critically evaluate information and media in all their forms,” media and information literacy (MIL) has become an essential skill for global citizens in the digital age.
In Latin America, MIL has been shaped by educational policies since the 1990s, initially focusing on traditional media and later, in the 2000s, expanding to include digital literacy as a response to disinformation in an increasingly complex media environment (Garro-Rojas, 2020). Across the region, various organizations have played a key role in developing resources and educational initiatives to foster critical thinking among citizens. As noted in a literature review byGarro-Rojas (2020), MIL initiatives in Latin America are frequently linked to non-formal education, popular education, and civil society (Peguer and Martínez 2016, cited inGarro-Rojas, 2020).
In Mexico, however, there is no established media and information literacy framework within formal, state-sponsored curricula. Moreover, the topic has yet to gain prominence in the public agenda (Cortés et al., 2016, cited inGarro-Rojas, 2020).Esquivel Lozano and Tejedor Calvo, S. (2011). highlight the importance of integrating cyberjournalism literacy into journalism education, a component that remains largely absent in the Mexican context. This situation is not unique to Mexico but reflects broader regional trends (Garro-Rojas 2020).
According toHernández-Pérez and Guzmán-Contreras (2025), disinformation during electoral periods represents a persistent and growing challenge in Latin America. While increasingly sophisticated technologies are being used to exploit country-specific vulnerabilities, an encouraging response exists in the form of a wide range of actors actively collaborating to mitigate the harmful effects of disinformation and safeguard democratic processes. Among these strategies, fact-checking has emerged as the most common approach, often involving collaboration between platforms, media organizations, civil society, governments, and electoral institutions.
The use of online media and social platforms as news sources in Mexico has increased significantly (Newman et al., 2025). Facebook is the most widely used platform for news (52%), followed by YouTube (35%). WhatsApp is the most used messaging app for news (28%), while TikTok has experienced notable growth, rising by 6 percentage points. News podcasts are used by 13% of respondents, and AI chatbots by 7% (Gutiérrez-Rentería and Montalvo, 2025).
Monitoring conducted by the Digital Media Observatory at Tecnológico de Monterrey (OMD-Tec, 2024) on social media conversations during the 2024 presidential campaigns in Mexico identified strategies employed across platforms such as X, Facebook, and TikTok to disseminate ideological messages and polarize the population. At the same time, an increasing level of sophistication was observed in the use of AI and bots to influence public opinion (OMD-Tec, 2024).
Similarly, the Complutense Observatory of Disinformation at the Universidad Complutense de Madrid identified “technological threats that compromise the integrity and transparency of the process” and emphasized the need to foster public confidence in the legality, legitimacy, authenticity, competitiveness, and transparency of elections. As noted in their report, “electoral processes in Mexico have been marked by information disorders, and electoral authorities have been targeted by attacks aimed at delegitimizing their decisions” (Rubio-Núñez et al., 2024b, 36).
In its electoral observation report on Mexico 2024, the OCD analyzed a sample of fact-checking and anti-disinformation sources, including Animal Político, the National Electoral Institute (INE), and Infodemia, and found that the most common formats for disseminating disinformation were tweets and videos, followed by hybrid formats combining tweets with other media. This suggests that X remains a key platform for the spread of disinformation. The most common objective identified was “distortion,” significantly exceeding other categories (Rubio-Núñez et al., 2024b, 55).
The OMD-Tec also identified manipulated social media profiles of candidates such as Xóchitl Gálvez and Claudia Sheinbaum, which were used to spread false campaign promises or discredit them through altered, decontextualized, or AI-generated images. Additionally, decontextualized tweets from official accounts were used strategically to construct narratives aimed at undermining candidates’ proposals (OMD-Tec, 2024).
Artificial intelligence has further contributed to the creation of false information through advanced tools that generate synthetic content, including manipulated videos, images, and audio. Coined deepfakes, these types of content are designed to make it appear that a person said or did something they never actually did. These technologies have reached a level of sophistication with a twofold effect. On one end, detection becomes increasingly difficult for the human eye, while on the other, fact-checkers and their organizations face an additional burden on what is already a complicated social endeavor (Vinogradova E. A., 2023).
Also, generative AI systems significantly increase the scale and speed of disinformation production by enabling automated text generation, realistic synthetic media, and highly personalized political messaging. These technologies challenge traditional verification practices because misleading content can now be produced and disseminated faster than fact-checking organizations can respond (OECD, 2024).
According to a report by the Aeon Institute and the Friedrich Naumann Foundation on the influence of AI in Mexico's 2024 elections, there was a notable increase in disinformation and false content amplified by tools such as deepfakes and manipulated audio, posing risks to public trust in electoral processes. The report also highlighted the growing difficulty of real-time verification, as the production and dissemination of false content outpace the capabilities of fact-checkers and automated tools. Additional risks include cybersecurity threats, such as automated attacks and the potential manipulation of electoral databases through AI systems, as well as gender-based political violence, particularly through the proliferation of intimate deepfakes and harmful content disproportionately targeting women in politics (May del Pozo and Rojas Arroyo, 2025, 16).
In the Mexican electoral context, the amplification capacity of social media has enabled candidates to reach broader and more diverse audiences. However, it has also facilitated the proliferation of false or biased information, significantly shaping public perceptions of candidates (Arias, E. et al., 2019).
The analysis of mentions and sentiment surrounding political figures such as presidential candidates has become an important category for examining ideological influence and the impact of specific narratives on voters. During the 2024 presidential election period in Mexico, social media served as a critical battleground where influence, public perception, and information (both accurate and manipulated) circulated at scale (Rivera Salas, P. E. and Flores González, R., forthcoming).
At the same time, the phenomenon of filter bubbles contributed to electoral polarization. This effect was reflected in high levels of negative mentions directed at candidates such as Xóchitl Gálvez and Jorge Álvarez Máynez, in which criticism and attacks outweighed positive mentions, particularly following presidential debates (Rivera Salas, P. E. and Flores González, R., forthcoming).
This scenario represents a significant challenge: “The increasing sophistication and accessibility of generative AI compel Mexico to adopt anticipatory measures to protect electoral integrity and strengthen its democracy in this new technological landscape” (May del Pozo and Rojas Arroyo, 2025, 20).
Increasingly, scholars advocate for integrated approaches that combine fact-checking with MIL to address disinformation more effectively.Hobbs (2020)emphasizes the significant role of educators in fostering critical thinking and student autonomy, arguing that education promotes knowledge creation when it encourages critical thinking and the exploration of diverse perspectives supported by evidence and reasoning.
Raquel Recuero (2025)has also emphasized that disinformation should be understood as a systemic phenomenon embedded in digital communication ecosystems. Recuero argues that disinformation emerges from coordinated networks of actors, algorithmic amplification, platform affordances, and affective dynamics that reinforce polarization and shape public debate. From this perspective, addressing disinformation requires systemic responses that combine institutional collaboration, technological governance, fact-checking, and Media and Information Literacy.
Collaborative MIL plays a fundamental role in enabling active citizenship in democratic societies by promoting critical engagement with media messages (Buckingham, 2003). In this regard, institutions such as Tecnológico de Monterrey and the Complutense University contribute to the efforts of media organizations committed to fact-checking and of civil society groups like Verificado to address one of the most significant global threats identified by theWorld Economic Forum (2025).
From this perspective, MIL assumes a central role both as a medium and a long-term mitigation strategy. The central concept of MIL, is understood by UNESCO as a composite concept that integrates media literacy, information literacy, and other literacies related to the digital environment. The UNESCÓs guidelines for the governance of digital platforms focuses on learning outcomes: understanding the role of media and other information providers in democracy, recognizing information needs, locating information, critically evaluating it, organizing it, communicating it ethically, and engaging with it in processes of democratic expression and dialogue (UNESCO, 2023a).
In this article, MIL is understood as a societal endeavor. Following UNESCO's MIL framework, and particularly the MIL Cities initiative, MIL encompasses formal education, higher education, media organizations, fact-checking initiatives, civil society, and public institutions. The initiatives analyzed in this study are intended to strengthen critical competencies within academic settings but also across broader civic and democratic contexts, fostering collaborative responses to disinformation.
Understanding how platforms operate, including algorithmic incentives and amplification dynamics, is essential for audiences to be able to critically evaluate electoral information. In the current age of AI, this literacy must extend beyond content analysis to include the ability to recognize synthetic manipulation, understand algorithmic bias, and engage in informed digital citizenship.
Therefore, the analysis of Electoral disinformation on social media cannot be separated from broader reflection on the role of AI in political communication, nor from the need for comprehensive responses that integrate technology, education, and verification. This framework helps explain why initiatives developed by fact-checking organizations and academic observatories are relevant not only as mechanisms for correcting misinformation but also as key actors in building democratic resilience in the face of contemporary information disorders.
2.2 Recommendation algorithms and bias: adverse effects on MIL
The need to strengthen MIL can be understood from an epistemological perspective grounded in General Semantics, as developed byKorzybski (1933). This approach posits that humans do not interact directly with reality, but rather with symbolic representations of it, summarized in the principle “the map is not the territory.” In today's digital ecosystem, characterized by the rapid circulation of content, this distinction becomes particularly relevant. Media messages function as abstractions, simplifications, and narratives of complex events. From this perspective, disinformation can be understood as an extreme distortion of these abstraction processes, where representation replaces reality and emotional narratives displace empirical evidence. Integrating this perspective reinforces MIL's role as a form of critical awareness of representation, interpretation, and symbolic construction.
This perspective can be further expanded through media ecology, which examines how communication technologies shape the cultural, cognitive, and political environments in which information circulates. Originating withMcLuhan (1964)and further developed byPostman (1970)andMeyrowitz (1985), this approach conceptualizes media not merely as channels of transmission but as environments that influence perception, social practices, and civic participation. In the contemporary context, digital platforms and algorithmic systems constitute a media environment that reorganizes the production, circulation, and reception of information. From this standpoint, disinformation can be understood as an ecological phenomenon emerging from the structural conditions of the digital media ecosystem.
Social media platforms have become central infrastructures of contemporary public debate and, in electoral contexts, strategic spaces for the construction, disputation, and amplification of political narratives. In countries such as Mexico, these platforms function not only as channels of information but as algorithmically mediated environments in which content visibility is determined by automated systems of recommendation, segmentation, and prioritization.
From a media ecology perspective, social media can be understood as informational environments able to reshape the dynamics of public debate.McLuhan (1964)argued that “the medium is the message,” emphasizing that the technical characteristics of media exert a deeper influence than the content they carry. In today's digital ecosystem, algorithmic recommendation systems, attention economies, and platform architectures favor short, visual, and emotionally intense content. These structural conditions create an environment particularly conducive to the spread of disinformation and polarizing narratives.
An additional significant challenge arising from the use of AI in the information environment is the development of recommendation algorithms that customize the content users encounter. These algorithms, employed by platforms such as YouTube, Facebook, and X, are designed to maximize user engagement by prioritizing content that aligns with individual interests and past behavior. However, this personalization can lead to the formation of “filter bubbles,” in which users are primarily exposed to information that reinforces their existing beliefs while encountering less content that challenges them (Pariser, 2011).
Social media platforms have been widely used to influence opinion formation and political decision-making. Manipulation has been conducted through the deliberate dissemination of false or low-quality content, the creation of fake and automated accounts (bots), and the use of algorithms that bias information flow and reinforce homogeneous viewpoints.
Filter bubbles describe a phenomenon in which social media platforms and search engines use algorithms to present personalized content based on users’ interests, opinions, and prior behavior. Platforms such as X, Facebook, and TikTok have been used to disseminate ideological messages and polarize the population. AI-based systems tend to reinforce informational bubbles and polarization by prioritizing content that generates higher levels of engagement, which is often associated with more extreme or polarizing discourse (Elorza-Saravia, 2023, cited inRubio-Núñez et al., 2024b).
Algorithmic recommendation systems prioritize emotionally charged and highly engaging content, which often favors sensationalist or polarizing narratives over verified information. These dynamics contribute to the rapid spread of false information and intensify ideological fragmentation in online environments (Vosoughi, Roy, and Aral, 2018).
These algorithmic dynamics are closely connected to the economic logic of contemporary digital platforms. Most major platforms operate under business models based on the attention economy, where user engagement and advertising revenues depend on maximizing time spent on the platform. Consequently, recommendation systems tend to privilege emotionally engaging, controversial, or sensational content, regardless of its informational quality. In this sense, the amplification of disinformation can be understood as a structural consequence of platform governance and commercial incentives (Forum on Information and Democracy, 2024;UNESCO, 2023a).
Moreover, the automation of messaging and algorithmic personalization in electoral campaigns can generate phenomena such as information overload (“infoxication”) and the spread of disinformation or hate speech. These dynamics can erode public perceptions of the integrity and legitimacy of electoral processes (Rubio-Núñez et al., 2024b).
From the perspective of General Semantics, these processes can be understood as a chain of abstraction levels in which each stage removes contextual information while adding interpretation. In digital environments, this chain is accelerated and amplified: an event becomes an image, the image becomes a headline, the headline becomes a short post, and eventually a meme or viral narrative. Each transformation entails a loss of complexity and an increase in emotional intensity, which are conditions that favor the spread of disinformation. This perspective helps explain why short, visual, and emotionally charged content tends to achieve greater visibility in algorithmic systems designed to maximize interaction.
Similarly, the phenomenon of filter bubbles can have adverse effects on MIL by limiting users’ exposure to diverse perspectives and sources of information.
The virilization of narratives shaped by ideological, social, and gender biases represents another dimension of disinformation in these contexts. As noted byRubio-Núñez et al. (2024b), AI can reproduce and amplify existing biases, thereby harming electoral and democratic processes.
These biases may stem from multiple sources, including training data that reflect existing social inequalities. As a result, algorithmic systems can contribute to discriminatory patterns in content selection and targeted messaging, ultimately affecting democratic representation and equity. These dynamics contribute to social fragmentation and the deterioration of democratic dialogue (Becker Castellaro, S. et al., 2025, p. 10–13, 15).
Challenges related to MIL, particularly in critical contexts such as Latin America, also include the need to integrate perspectives centered on diversity and social inclusion, such as gender perspectives, not only within educational systems but also across technological industries, major platforms (e.g., Google and Meta), and other strategic sectors. These perspectives are essential for shaping public policy, decision-making processes, and protecting users’ rights.
As Gustavo Gómez, Executive Director of Observacom in Uruguay, notes, content moderation policies implemented by major digital platforms raise concerns about their impact on democracies, public debate, and freedom of expression. These concerns are intensified in polarized contexts characterized by structural inequalities, rising authoritarian tendencies, and still-developing public policy frameworks in many countries across the region (Forum on Information and Democracy, 2024, 13).
From a MIL perspective, these phenomena highlight the need to expand public understanding of how algorithmic systems function. It is not sufficient to identify false content; it is equally important to develop competencies that enable individuals to recognize how platforms mediate access to information, what incentives shape content visibility, and how algorithms influence perceptions of political reality.
In this sense, recommendation algorithms should be understood not only as a technical or regulatory challenge but also as an educational one. Strengthening audiences’ critical capacities regarding algorithmic personalization is essential to mitigating the effects of disinformation and preserving a pluralistic public debate in electoral contexts shaped by artificial intelligence.
Together, this literature shows that disinformation in AI-mediated environments has been widely examined in relation to electoral integrity, algorithmic amplification, and fact-checking practices. However, less attention has been paid to how civic and academic actors in Latin America articulate MIL, verification, and AI-supported monitoring within a collaborative model for democratic resilience. We address that gap through the cases of Verificado and the Digital Media Observatory at Tecnológico de Monterrey.
3 Materials and methods
This research used a qualitative case study design with two embedded cases. It examined how civic and academic actors in Mexico articulate media and information literacy, fact-checking, and AI-supported monitoring as responses to electoral disinformation. The case study approach allows in-depth analysis of contemporary phenomena in real-life contexts and is especially valuable where institutional practices, technological infrastructures, and democratic communication closely intersect (Yin, 2014).
To this end, two representative cases were selected: Verificado, a pioneering Mexican civil society organization dedicated to fact-checking, and the Digital Media Observatory at Tecnológico de Monterrey University (OMD-Tec), an academic initiative established in 2024 to systematically monitor electoral narratives on social media using AI.
These cases provide complementary perspectives: Verificado represents civil society's role in fact-checking and MIL, while OMD-Tec reflects the academic institutionalization of MIL through technologically driven media monitoring. Their selection is based on relevance, trajectory, and capacity to illustrate cross-sectoral responses to disinformation in Mexico.
To examine these cases, the study draws on empirical material and documentation. The authors conducted a 60-minute semi-structured expert interview with Daniela Mendoza, founder and representative of Verificado, on June 13, 2025. The interview explored Verificado's institutional trajectory after the 2024 Mexican elections, focusing on the evolution of its fact-checking practices, the challenges faced by verification organizations amid declining resources and growing resistance to fact-checking, and the implications of recent platform governance changes.
In addition, the authors had access to methodological documentation and analytical reports on the development and implementation of the OMD-Tec methodology during the 2024 electoral observation process, which informed the present study. These materials are treated as institutional sources. Specifically, the Digital Media Observatory methodological report (Rivera Salas, P. E. and Flores González, R., forthcoming) is cited as a primary source. Finally, the empirical material was contrasted with the literature review, documentary evidence, and public initiatives, which served as supporting sources.
Consistent with UNESCO's MIL Cities framework, the unit of analysis in this study is the institutional ecosystem that supports MIL. Therefore, the study does not measure the impact of these initiatives on end users but analyses how MIL, fact-checking practices, and technological monitoring are articulated as complementary dimensions, drawing on insights from Mexico to understand responses to disinformation.
4 Results
4.1 Verificado: a representative case of fact-checking in electoral contexts
Within the proposed conceptual framework, the case of Verificado provides a valuable lens through which to analyze how the articulation of MIL, fact-checking, and the strategic use of emerging technologies can function as a mechanism of democratic resilience in the face of AI-mediated disinformation in electoral contexts.
Verificado emerged in Mexico to address the growing need for reliable information. According to Mendoza (interview by authors, June 13, 2025), the organization was founded by four women in Monterrey with the aim of conducting fact-checking initiatives in northern Mexico. However, following an earthquake that struck central Mexico in September 2017, the organization joined broader verification efforts coordinated under the initiative Verificado19. Although this initiative was temporary, it helped consolidate Verificado's mission to counter disinformation. Inspired by the experience of Chequeado in Argentina, considered a pioneer in fact-checking in Latin America, Verificado set out to verify political data and public statements. Eight years after its founding, the organization has achieved significant impact within the Mexican media landscape.
The organization's work has been particularly relevant in electoral contexts. During the 2018 elections, Mexico faced a highly active and organized disinformation environment, amplified by bot networks and characterized by the prominent involvement of civil society and the National Electoral Institute (INE) in implementing verification-based responses in collaboration with technology platforms (Hernández-Pérez and Guzmán-Contreras, 2025).
The Verificado 2018 project, an innovative initiative for fact-checking during that electoral process, in which Verificado actively participated, demonstrated the importance of collaboration among electoral authorities, respected media organizations such as Animal Político, and digital platforms such as Facebook and Twitter in addressing disinformation in complex electoral environments.
According toHernández-Pérez and Guzmán-Contreras (2025), Verificado 2018 illustrates the need to develop context-specific responses tailored to national conditions, with a strong emphasis on both technological tools and educational strategies. Following this experience, educational campaigns were developed to improve citizens’ MIL about fake news, along with technological tools to identify and report misleading content.
Although Verificado was initially created to address a specific national context, its scope expanded significantly after the 2018 elections, evolving from a regional initiative into a national reference point for fact-checking. Its verification methodology, which is based on five steps (content selection, consultation of the original source, cross-checking, contextualization, and labelling), has enabled the organization to address disinformation beyond strictly political content.
During the 2018 presidential elections, given the broader context of discrimination and gender-based violence, Verificado diversified its work to also counter hate speech, complementing its fact-checking methodology.
In 2024, Mexico experienced an unprecedented electoral scenario, with two women emerging as leading presidential candidates: Claudia Sheinbaum, representing the MORENA party, and Xóchitl Gálvez, representing the opposition coalition Fuerza y Corazón por México, composed of the PRI, PAN, and PRD parties. While this configuration suggested progress in gender inclusion within Mexican politics, national statistics from 2021 indicated that 70.1% of women aged 15 and older had experienced at least one incident of gender-based violence (INEGI, 2021). Against this backdrop, Verificado anticipated that gender-based violence could also manifest within the electoral process.
In response, during the 2024 electoral campaign, Verificado launched the Observatory on Hate Speech and Disinformation. This initiative aimed to monitor, verify, and analyze sexist and hate speech directed at women candidates and members of the LGBTQ + community. Between March and May 2024, the Observatory produced 26 fact-checking reports focused on identifying false narratives that incorporated gender-based violence, xenophobia, racism, or anti-diversity discourse (Mendoza, interview by authors, June 13, 2025).
The relevance of the Observatory is illustrated by the findings generated during the 2024 Mexican electoral process. According to the Executive Summary of the Observatory on Disinformation and Hate Speech, disinformation and hate speech functioned not as isolated incidents but as systematic mechanisms aimed at undermining the political participation of women and historically marginalized groups. The report further documented patterns of gender-based political violence, online harassment, and coordinated disinformation campaigns targeting female candidates and vulnerable communities (Verificado, 2024).
According to Mendoza (interview by authors, June 13, 2025), the organization structured its electoral coverage around two main lines of work. The first involved live fact-checking of the three presidential debates, while the second focused on verifying and analyzing disinformation and hate speech, including sexist content targeting candidates. These efforts revealed clear patterns of disinformation with gender bias.
The disinformation identified targeted candidates, including both leading presidential contenders, by referencing their ethnic origins, nationalities, personal decisions, and other aspects unrelated to their policy proposals. These narratives frequently incorporated elements of hate speech, shifting attention away from substantive political debate (Mendoza, interview by authors, June 13, 2025).
The following electoral process, the 2025 Judicial Elections (TEPJF, 2025), represented another unprecedented moment in Mexico, with 3,423 candidates competing for positions within the federal judiciary. In a context characterized by limited access to clear, reliable information, Verificado implemented a strategy called “explainers.” This approach provided users with accessible answers to key questions such as how to vote, what political positions were being contested, and how the electoral process would unfold. These initiatives exemplify how MIL can be operationalized to address information gaps that might otherwise be exploited by misleading narratives (Mendoza, interview by authors, June 13, 2025).
For Mendoza, collaboration alone is not sufficient; media organizations and fact-checking initiatives must continuously reassess their strategies to address disinformation. One of the most promising approaches, she argues, lies in the practice and promotion of MIL as a complementary and long-term strategy.
4.2 Media and information literacy initiatives of verificado
The development of critical thinking through MIL initiatives has been a central way in which fact-checking organizations contribute to society and strengthen their legitimacy. These initiatives often include workshops, training sessions, public events, and other forms of civic engagement designed to equip citizens with the skills needed to make informed decisions about the content they consume (Moreno-Gil, Ramon, and Rodríguez-Martínez, 2021).
In 2022, Verificado launched the first edition of the media and information literacy academy (MILA), a project developed in collaboration with the Mexican American Institute for Cultural Relations and supported by the U.S. Consulate General in Monterrey. Structured as a multi-session workshop, the program trained both adolescents and adults in MIL through five learning modules: access, analysis, reflection, creation, and action (Orozco, 2022).
According to Mendoza (interview by authors, June 13, 2025), MILA operates as a “living communication laboratory” with two core components. On one hand, it provides training in topics such as digital footprints, digital security, and disinformation. On the other hand, it promotes the development of practical skills for creating multimedia content. The objective is for participants to learn how to identify disinformation and verify information before sharing it with diverse audiences. The most recent edition of MILA was held in 2025 in Monterrey, where a cohort of 40 participants completed five weekly training sessions (Cavazos, 2025).
While MILA represents an in-person initiative, Verificado has also sought to expand its MIL tools beyond the local level. In April 2025, in collaboration with Chequeado, the organization launched Verificaula (Orozco, 2025), a digital learning platform designed for users interested in combating disinformation and strengthening their critical analysis of online information. This initiative serves as an open-access, free educational resource. The platform includes a repository of digital materials—such as specialized guides, e-books, research reports, and manuals—available to anyone interested in developing critical skills to counter disinformation (Verificado, 2023). These resources were developed through collaboration between Verificado and a range of governmental and non-profit organizations, including DW Akademie in Mexico, the International Centre for Journalists (ICFJ), It Gets Better Project, Border Hub, Consejo Nuevo León, and the U.S. Embassy in Mexico (Verificado, 2023).
In addition, as part of International Fact-Checking Day 2025, Verificado collaborated with Tecnológico de Monterrey to organize the first Fact Fest, described as a “celebration of truth.” This event combined two components. The first was a panel discussion with experts addressing current challenges in combating disinformation. The second was a “Verificatón,” an interactive activity in which students participated in fact-checking exercises. These activities included challenges designed to develop digital competencies, such as identifying manipulated images, verifying videos, and detecting misleading content.
According to Mendoza (interview by authors, June 13, 2025), Fact Fest has the potential to serve as a replicable model at the regional level, particularly if adapted to local contexts and supported by networks of experts. Future editions are expected to expand this initiative, including a planned event at the University of Monterrey in September 2025. However, for initiatives such as MILA, Verificaula, and Fact Fest to scale and sustain their impact, Mendoza emphasizes the importance of building strategic alliances with diverse stakeholders.
Verificado trained faculty members from a wide range of public and private universities, including the National Autonomous University of Mexico (UNAM), Tecnológico de Monterrey, the University of Guadalajara (UDG), and Universidad Iberoamericana, among others. As a result of these efforts, the Universidad Marista de Yucatán announced the creation of a new course on disinformation for journalism students, while the Autonomous University of Chihuahua has begun incorporating fact-checking methodologies into its curriculum.
Active collaboration among educators, technology developers, fact-checkers, media observatories, and public actors is essential for designing inclusive policies that strengthen societal resilience against information disorders. In this regard, the 2024 annual report of the Forum on Information and Democracy highlights actions such as policy advocacy, research mobilization, coalition-building, regional partnerships, platform accountability, and international collaboration as key mechanisms for fostering reliable and democratic information environments (Forum on Information and Democracy, 2024).
4.3 The digital media observatory at Tec: strategic use of AI for MIL
The Digital Media Observatory at Tecnológico de Monterrey, established during the 2024 electoral year, builds upon the experience of fact-checking organizations and MIL initiatives while leveraging the growing availability of technological tools, particularly artificial intelligence.
This case illustrates that, despite the challenges posed by AI within the media ecosystem, it also offers significant opportunities to enhance media and information literacy. One of the most promising developments lies in the creation of advanced tools for monitoring, analyzing, and verifying information in digital media environments.
For the observation of the 2024 elections, OMD-Tec implemented a web scraping tool (Information Tracer) that enabled researchers, following training aligned with specific research objectives, to extract large datasets from social media platforms for analysis. In addition, the Observatory employed AI-powered tools to assess and verify the accuracy of online content, enabling users to identify misleading or false information more efficiently (Rivera Salas, P. E. and Flores González, R., forthcoming).
Furthermore, OMD-Tec developed publicly accessible dashboards that highlighted which candidates generated the most online engagement and the reasons behind these patterns. These tools facilitated a deeper understanding of each candidate's reach and influence across different social media platforms. For example, variations in popularity across platforms could indicate targeted communication strategies directed at specific demographic groups or the presence of echo chambers, known as online communities in which like-minded individuals reinforce shared beliefs while excluding dissenting perspectives (Rivera Salas, P. E. and Flores González, R., forthcoming).
Using histogram-based visualizations, the Observatory also analyzed the cumulative number of posts across different sentiment categories (positive, neutral, and negative), as well as the daily volume of content on each platform for each candidate. Additionally, it identified the most influential accounts and highlighted the dominant topics of conversation within each platform (Rivera Salas, P. E. and Flores González, R., forthcoming).
From the perspective of the Digital Media Observatory, AI can accelerate the detection and debunking of disinformation circulating online. Platforms such as X and Facebook have already begun implementing AI-based systems to identify and remove false content, though these efforts have not been without challenges and controversy. As these technologies continue to evolve, they are likely to become more effective and accessible, offering users increasingly powerful tools to counter disinformation (Ciampaglia et al., 2015).
Simultaneously, fact-checking requires a combined approach that integrates technological tools with essential human skills. AsBuckingham (2003)argues, critical thinking is the cornerstone of media and information literacy, and AI can support its development by enabling learning experiences that foster reflection and analysis.
More broadly, addressing the systemic risks AI poses to the quality and integrity of electoral processes and political communication requires a comprehensive, multidimensional strategy. Such an approach must integrate updated regulatory frameworks, ethical principles grounded in human rights, platform accountability, citizen education, independent verification mechanisms, international coordination, and democratic participation (Rubio-Núñez, Franco, and De-Andrade-Monteiro, 2024a).
Technology developers and policymakers also play a crucial role in designing AI systems that promote diversity and inclusion. This includes addressing biases in training data and ensuring transparency in platform practices. Only under these conditions can the full potential of AI be realized to strengthen MIL and enhance society's capacity to respond to disinformation.
Overall, the analysis demonstrates that civic actors play a vital role in building MIL capacities in urban contexts. Their initiatives combine fact-checking, MIL programs, and collaboration with academic institutions and digital platforms. During recent electoral processes, these strategies have enabled the identification of disinformation narratives amplified by AI, as well as hate speech shaped by gender bias. At the same time, educational programs have strengthened public understanding of how social media platforms, recommendation algorithms, and synthetic content generation operate.
5 Discussion: MIL and democratic resilience in the age of AI
The findings of this study can be interpreted through the lens of General Semantics, which emphasizes the importance of developing whatKorzybski (1933)called an “awareness of abstraction” in communication processes. In the context of AI-mediated disinformation, this awareness involves recognizing that digital content does not constitute reality itself but rather represents reality, shaped by technological, economic, and editorial decisions. From this perspective, MIL can be understood as a contemporary form of cognitive hygiene, aimed at distinguishing between facts, interpretations, and narratives. This epistemological dimension reinforces the idea that democratic resilience depends not only on technological or regulatory tools but also on citizens’ capacity to critically interpret mediated representations.
The analysis of Verificado, informed by the proposed conceptual framework, demonstrates how integrating MIL, fact-checking, and the strategic use of emerging technologies can serve as a mechanism of democratic resilience in the face of AI-driven disinformation. Beyond its corrective function, the initiatives examined reflect a shift toward more comprehensive approaches that combine immediate intervention, civic education, and critical reflection on digital environments.
One of the case's central contributions lies in the explicit integration of MIL as a transversal axis in strategies to counter electoral disinformation. Unlike approaches focused solely on debunking false content, Verificado incorporates educational programs, workshops, and learning platforms to strengthen audiences’ critical capacities. This approach acknowledges that fact-checking, while essential, is insufficient to address the scale, speed, and sophistication of disinformation amplified by AI.
At the same time, the case highlights the role of civil society as a key factor in information governance, particularly in contexts where state regulatory capacity and platform self-regulation remain limited. Collaboration between fact-checking organizations, universities, and media observatories not only expands monitoring capabilities but also generates applied knowledge that contributes to understanding algorithmic dynamics and their effects on public debate.
Another significant contribution is the incorporation of a gender perspective in both the analysis and response to disinformation. The empirical evidence shows that AI can amplify pre-existing biases and forms of violence, disproportionately affecting women candidates and members of sexual diversity communities. Initiatives such as the Observatory on Hate Speech and Disinformation illustrate how MIL can be adapted to address these structural inequalities and promote more equitable political participation.
From a technological standpoint, the findings reveal both opportunities and limitations in the use of AI-based tools for electoral monitoring. While automated systems for data analysis, web scraping, and visualization expand the scope and scale of information monitoring, their effectiveness ultimately depends on human interpretation, socio-political context, and the ethical frameworks guiding their use. This reinforces the notion that AI should be understood as a complement to, rather than a substitute for, critical judgment and educational intervention.
The findings further illustrate how disinformation and hate speech operated as interconnected phenomena during the Mexican electoral process, particularly targeting women and historically marginalized groups, thereby reinforcing the need for institutional responses that combine fact-checking, media observation, and media and information literacy initiatives (Verificado, 2024).
The findings also suggest that addressing electoral disinformation in the age of AI requires multidimensional strategies that go beyond isolated technical solutions. MIL emerges as a structural component of democratic resilience, enabling citizens to understand, question, and respond to information disorders. In this sense, the case of Verificado offers relevant insights for other Latin American contexts, demonstrating that the combination of education, verification, and cross-sector collaboration can significantly contribute to democratic sustainability.
In this context, MIL should also be understood as a form of algorithmic literacy that enables citizens to critically evaluate how digital platforms prioritize, recommend, and amplify information. Beyond identifying false content, democratic resilience increasingly depends on the public's ability to understand the sociotechnical infrastructures that shape online visibility and political discourse (OECD, 2024;UNESCO, 2023a).
From a media ecology perspective, contemporary cities can be conceptualized as hybrid communicative ecosystems where physical infrastructures, digital platforms, and social practices converge. The UNESCO MIL framework aligns with this view by framing urban environments as spaces where MIL serves as essential civic infrastructure. Within this framework, the initiatives analyzed do not merely respond to specific instances of disinformation; they intervene in the broader media environment in which such content circulates, thereby strengthening the informational resilience of the urban ecosystem.
The cases examined also demonstrate that implementing MIL does not depend solely on formal public policy. The coordinated action of civic, academic, and technological actors is crucial to translating the principles of the MIL model into concrete practices.
Integrating gender perspectives and human rights frameworks reinforces the inclusive dimension of the MIL approach. AI, rather than being viewed solely as a threat, can become part of a civic infrastructure when embedded within educational, ethical, and participatory strategies.
At the same time, the expansion of digital networks and AI presents significant challenges. The use of these technologies must be guided by ethical principles, human rights frameworks, and regulatory mechanisms that prevent the reproduction of biases, ensure algorithmic transparency, and promote accountability among both platforms and governments.
Finally, this findings should be interpreted as an analysis of institutional responses rather than an evaluation of the impact of MIL interventions. Future research could build on this work by examining audience reception, voter perceptions, and the effectiveness of MIL initiatives through surveys, focus groups, experiments, or mixed-methods approaches.
6 Conclusions
This article argues that MIL constitutes a structural component in the construction of more democratic societies in the age of AI. Drawing on the UNESCO MIL approach, the analysis demonstrates how indicators related to civic actors and technological innovation can be operationalized through initiatives that integrate fact-checking, education, and the responsible use of AI.
In the context of expanding social media and AI technologies, MIL should not be understood solely as the ability to critically interpret media content. Rather, it must be conceptualized as a multifaceted competency that includes understanding algorithmic systems, exercising digital rights, identifying, dismantling harmful narratives, and acting responsibly within interconnected digital environments. At the same time, MIL initiatives must remain accessible and inclusive to effectively reach key audiences.
The analysis of the two institutional cases examined in this study, particularly the Verificado case, demonstrates that regional collaboration and the strategic use of technological and AI-based tools can serve not only as effective responses to disinformation but also as catalysts for broader social transformation.
Verificado, through its multifaceted approach combining electoral fact-checking, a gender perspective, civic education, and the responsible use of technology, represents an innovative model for addressing contemporary information disorders. Initiatives such as MILA, Verificaula, and Fact Fest not only democratize access to knowledge about information verification but also empower diverse social groups, especially young people and women, to become active agents of change within their digital communities. These initiatives illustrate how MIL, when implemented through a collaborative, integrative framework, can serve as a tool for epistemic justice, social inclusion, and democratic strengthening.
The potential of AI to reinforce these efforts is also considerable. Initiatives such as the Digital Media Observatory at Tecnológico de Monterrey demonstrate how tools such as web scraping, data visualization, and automated detection of misleading content enable broader, more precise, and real-time monitoring of the information ecosystem, particularly in electoral contexts.
Nonetheless, the construction of a more just, equitable, and resilient information ecosystem cannot depend on isolated efforts, regardless of their individual effectiveness. It requires a comprehensive strategy that articulates diverse actors, including governments, universities, media organizations, technology platforms, civil society organizations, and citizens. This collaborative framework should promote public policies that recognize MIL as both a civic right and a structural component of educational systems, while also fostering applied research and socially oriented technological innovation.
This finding is consistent with UNESCO's MIL Cities framework, which conceptualizes MIL as an ecosystem sustained through collaboration among universities, media organizations, observatories, civil society, and public institutions.
Diversified, and evolving MIL strategies are essential for addressing the challenges posed by disinformation in Latin America. The experience of Verificado and the media observatory analyzed in this study provides valuable insights into how to mitigate the risks associated with disinformation and strengthen civic engagement. Regional collaboration and inclusive policy design remain key to enhancing social resilience and ensuring the sustainability of democratic systems (Cruz, González, and Martínez 2025, 139–167).
Recent global discussions on AI governance (UNESCO, 2023;World Economic Forum, 2025) increasingly emphasize that combating disinformation cannot rely exclusively on content moderation or automated detection systems. International organizations highlight the need for coordinated strategies that integrate regulation, transparency, platform accountability, digital rights, and MIL as complementary dimensions of democratic governance in AI-mediated societies.
By examining Verificado and OMD-Tec together, this article shows that MIL in Latin America is not only an educational practice but also a collaborative infrastructure for democratic resilience in AI-mediated information environments.
