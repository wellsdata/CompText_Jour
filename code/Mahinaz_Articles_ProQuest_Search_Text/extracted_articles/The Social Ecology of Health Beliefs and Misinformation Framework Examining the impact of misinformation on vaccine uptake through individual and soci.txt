1 Introduction Misinformation presents a critical concern for academic and public health discourse, particularly around vaccine response [1,2] . Misinformation about vaccines, such as Covid-19 and HPV, is related to lower rates of vaccine uptake for adults and their children [3–6] . This study explores how misinformation relates to vaccine decisions through the Health Belief Model (HBM) and the Socio-Ecological Model frameworks. HBM is a psychosocial framework that has been used to explain health-related behavior change and maintenance, as well as provide a framework for health behavior interventions [7–9] . HBM considers several constructs that predict why people will take action to avoid diseases including perceived susceptibility, perceived severity, perceived benefits, perceived barriers, cues to action, self-efficacy, and demographic or sociopsychological variables (see Fig. 1 ).
Several health studies have relied on HBM to understand COVID-19 vaccination hesitancy. While there have been additions to the HBM over time, multiple studies have examined the importance of original and added variables in the HBM in health decisions, such as COVID-19 vaccination intent. A systematic review of HBM scholarship found that perceived barriers and perceived benefits were the most common HBM constructs significantly associated with vaccine hesitancy [10] . The study found various HBM constructs to be associated with COVID-19 vaccination intent, and modifying variables like gender, prior COVID-19 diagnosis, education, and other demographics also played a role [10] . Another systematic review found perceived benefits, perceived barriers, and cues to action to be the three most frequently demonstrated predictors of COVID-19 vaccination intent, but that predictors depended on demographics like nationality and role such as parent [11] . A separate study found women and higher levels of perceived benefits, and both internal and external cues to action, were related to COVID-19 prevention behaviors, while perceived barriers were negatively related to those behaviors [12] . The HBM has been successfully employed to understand vaccination intentions and behaviors among other viruses, including influenza and HPV, and in different cultural contexts [13,14] .
Houlden and colleagues theorized that the HBM could be used as a lens to examine and address misinformation about health in general and COVID-19 in particular [15] . For example, a study has shown how conspiracy theories can interact with perceived effectiveness of the vaccine to affect COVID-19 vaccination intentions [16] . However, the HBM does not effectively capture the complexity of individual behaviors situated around contextual, or sociological, factors that operate in relational and reciprocal ways and at varying degrees of distance [14,17] .
One way to address this gap is Bronfenbrenner’s Socio-Ecological Model [18] . The Socio-Ecological Model describes multiple factors that impact healthcare decisions as it conceptualizes different levels of hierarchical influence including intrapersonal, interpersonal (e.g., parents and peers), institutional (e.g., schools or healthcare clinics), and structural levels of society (e.g., media and government policy) (see Fig. 2 ) [18–21] .
Public health research has relied on the Socio-Ecological Model to understand multi-level factors associated with vaccination intentions [17,19] . A study that examined parental beliefs about the COVID-19 vaccine found that parents who talked to others at least weekly about the COVID-19 vaccine were less likely to be uncertain about their intentions to vaccinate their children [21] . Another study found that low trust or confidence in government authorities, healthcare professionals, and the media were barriers to COVID-19 vaccine acceptance, while high trust or confidence in government authorities or healthcare professionals made one more likely to get a COVID-19 vaccine [22] . Circumstances such as job loss due to COVID-19 and the presence of healthcare workers in the family can moderate parents’ COVID-19 vaccine intentions for their children [23] . Structural barriers, such as access to vaccines and cost, are contributors to hesitancy for routine childhood vaccinations [24,25] . Other research shows such structural issues are not as important in decision making for other vaccines, such as influenza [13] . Currently, research on the impact of misinformation on vaccine decision in the context of structural levels is limited.
The purpose of our study was to qualitatively explore the effect of misinformation on Colorado residents’ COVID-19 vaccination beliefs. Colorado represents a number of structural factors that may shape how individuals respond to misinformation. Colorado is one of 16 states that allow for non-medical exemptions for school-aged vaccines, and such exemptions are associated with lower routine vaccination rates [26–28] . Colorado was included in an interview-based study that examined sociological factors that shape large disparities in vaccination rates between white and African-American/Hispanic residents [29] . Covid-19 vaccine stakeholder interviewees pointed to a number of structural factors that shape vaccine decision making, with concerns over vaccine safety, remote locations, and access to technology for vaccine appointments as top barriers to vaccine uptake [29] . Demographic analyses of school-aged immunization rates in Colorado present a complex picture of routine vaccine uptake in the state, however. Predominantly white school districts have lower immunization rates than school districts with larger Hispanic populations and more use of free and reduced lunch [30] . It is likely that such sociological factors are one element of the complex individual characteristics that shape vaccine decision making. At the same time, scholars are increasingly concerned with the foundation of distrust among BIPOC communities that may enable misinformation to spread among communities in – for instance – Hispanic-serving social media [31] . Efforts to systematically examine best practices for preventing the impact of misinformation in BIPOC communities in culturally relevant ways is growing [32] .
While previous studies have utilized quantitative approaches to explicate this relationship between misinformation and vaccine beliefs [16,21,33] , we present a post-positivist, triangulated perspective that contextualizes the relationship between misinformation and vaccine hesitancy. We argue that misinformation could permeate these structural levels of influence and skew an individual’s perception of illness severity/susceptibility, which can increase vaccine hesitancy. Our study is grounded in the HBM and the Socio-Ecological Framework to identify both individual and societal factors that shape the influence of misinformation on vaccination intentions.
2 Methods
2.1 Sample and Recruitment In this multi-method study, we used semi-structured interviews and focus groups to understand how misinformation shapes vaccine decisions in Colorado. We conducted three focus groups, two with community leaders in the public health profession ( n  = 13) and another with family practice physicians ( n =  9), and semi-structured interviews with parents ( n  = 9) in Colorado. Prior to data collection, the research protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board at the authors’ institution.
For the focus groups with community leaders in the public health profession, we purposively sampled individuals who worked in roles where they encountered and addressed misinformation about the COVID-19 vaccination, such as the elderly care community, K-12 school nursing, a Veteran’s hospital, county and city public health offices, and university extension offices. Participants were identified using publicly available contact information, snowballing, and through the researchers’ networks. These individuals attended a full-day workshop in spring 2022, and participated in 2 90-minute focus groups during the day. A shorter follow-up interview of about 60 min was conducted with a workshop participant who was unable to attend at the last minute due to illness. A group of physician residents in family medicine at a hospital near the authors’ institution was recruited for 1 90-minute focus group.
The parents were provided a $15 gift card for the semi-structured interviews that lasted approximately 30 min and were recruited at a county fair through the 4-H network of a university extension agent. This sample was overwhelmingly female, but this aligns with previous reports that women make 80 % of healthcare decisions, including vaccination decisions, for their family [34] . Recruitment ceased when we believed the sample recruited and interviewed provided sufficient information to reach saturation on explication of misinformation and vaccine decision making in Colorado.
2.2 Data collection Researchers collected data during the late spring and summer of 2022. The focus groups with community leaders and physicians and the semi-structured interviews parents were audio recorded and transcribed.
Public health professionals and physicians were asked about their perceptions of examples of misinformation, groups susceptible to misinformation, and sources of misinformation, in addition to their thoughts on successful examples of addressing misinformation. Parents were asked questions about vaccine decision making, perspectives on misinformation, and media use. The interview protocol was piloted among parents in the researchers’ personal networks, after which questions were refined for clarity.
2.3 Data analysis Transcripts were coded inductively using thematic analysis, aided by MAXQDA. Initial analysis involved multiple readings and discussions of the findings among the five team members to identify emergent factors that influence vaccination decisions in Colorado. We began with organizing the dataset by identifying units of data. These included words, sentences, and in some cases paragraphs. The units of analysis were organized around mentions of misinformation as it relates to decision making primarily around vaccination decisions, but also allowing for decisions in other contexts to account for historical and sociocultural contexts a prominent place in the data analysis – which was a primary research goal for this study. The data organization was data-driven and was then followed by the codebook-generating phase, which was theory-driven. Specifically, the codebook was driven by the Health Belief Model and Socio-Ecological Model, and we aimed to uncover relationships between misinformation and Colorado residents’ COVID-19 vaccination beliefs. Some data units were assigned more than one code to capture the richness and complexity of the data. Team members met weekly to discuss the coders' understandings of the definitions of codes applicable to each level of influence. These team discussions sometimes led to adjustments of the codebook structure and definitions which furthered coders’ abilities to apply codes accurately. The factors were then deductively classified by consensus into four broad levels of influence according to the Socio-Ecological Model: intrapersonal, interpersonal, institutional, and structural. The codebook contains a description of each factor, including its level, frequency of occurrence in the dataset, and a clear definition with examples that illustrate the factor’s use [35] .
3 Results The findings provide evidence that vaccination decisions in Colorado are based on intrapersonal factors (routine vaccine hesitancy), interpersonal factors (social networks), institutional factors (mediated platforms, distrust in institutional sources of information, portrayals of uncertainty, and political influence), and structural factors (economic barriers to vaccine uptake).
3.1 Intrapersonal factors Studies of health decisions at the intrapersonal level consider people’s personal characteristics or preferences, concerns with vaccines, history of and experiences with vaccination, and knowledge or perceptions of vaccination as factors that influence vaccination uptake [22] . The findings from this study provide evidence intrapersonal factors were related to routine vaccine hesitancy and concern about novelty of the vaccine, particularly among our sample of parents in Colorado.
3.1.1 Routine vaccination hesitancy The focus groups with public health professionals and physicians revealed concerns about a growing trend of parents’ hesitancy towards routine vaccination for their children. Specifically, they worried that diseases that were once eradicated could reemerge. Public health professionals and physicians connected parents’ routine vaccine hesitancy or refusal to adopt the vaccines to misinformation about COVID-19 vaccines. Parents cited two reasons for their vaccine hesitancy: negative reactions to vaccines and concerns about vaccine efficacy. As a result, parents were cherry-picking vaccinations for themselves and their children based on the perceived severity of vaccine-preventable diseases. One parent mentioned she stopped vaccinating her child after her child was diagnosed with autism. She eventually found out that vaccinations were not the reason behind her daughter’s autism diagnosis. However, her peer networks blamed her daughter’s diagnosis on vaccination, which was rooted in the assumption that vaccines could alter a person’s genetic structure after they were born. The mother of three eventually found out that vaccinations did not do that.“I’ve dealt with this quite a bit more than most parents because my daughter’s special needs and when she was very little, I had a lot of people, even friends and family, tell me it was my fault that she had the disability because I gave her vaccinations, or I gave her Tylenol or things like that. I'd say for about a year I stopped vaccinating her. I didn’t vaccinate her anymore. We lived in California so that was acceptable. They didn't require it. And I decided to do my own research on it. And the more I looked at the research and listened to the doctors, and I understood that, yes, there are things that can cause issues in a small population. But when it comes to my daughter, what they said is that’s not going to change her genetic structure after she’s already born. It’s not going to cause her to become mute and genetically different.”
Another parent mentioned she chose what vaccines to administer to her children based on their perceived severity of the disease. The mother of four perceived her family’s healthy lifestyle would keep her family safe from vaccine-preventable diseases, such as chickenpox. She also made comparisons between the risks of the diseases and the vaccines when deciding which vaccines to get.“I did not vaccinate my children against COVID-19. They were exposed and did not contract it. I believe in being very health-minded. We watch our diet. None of my children are obese. They are all martial artists, so we have the fitness piece nailed down. I know that, again, it isn't a 100 % guarantee that they would not have a bad outcome. But I feel like the risk is higher to take the shot than it would be for them to survive COVID-19 at this point.”“My children are all up to date on their tetanus shots now. But I do take a delayed and prayerful approach… Like chickenpox, I survived chickenpox. The likeliness of dying of chickenpox is fairly low. I have opted personally to not vaccinate my children for chickenpox. But I understand why we would like to develop herd immunity. I have done that homework. There are certain ones that I have chosen to take the risk versus getting the vaccination. Then also some that I have not chosen to take the risk such as tetanus because it is a real risk where we live, but not before they’re (her children are) one year old.”
A mother of three teenaged boys mentioned she was vaccine-hesitant because of previous negative experiences with vaccinations. These concerns were specifically for COVID-19 and tetanus vaccines. She connected tetanus vaccine to autism and asthma, and COVID-19 vaccine to sterility.“…When my oldest was very young, I challenged why we give a one-year-old four rounds of tetanus when the risk of tetanus to a one-year-old that cannot walk yet is extremely low. After he received his four rounds of tetanus, he developed asthma. I did some research that caused me to do some questioning about how much that may be a factor in future health because my family with being autoimmune is highly sensitive to metals, to other things that could be in the vaccinations. I took a very detailed research approach. In fact, I fired a doctor over it because he forced the issue. I decided to wait to do the MMR shot with my oldest son because I did not have a good gut feeling about it.”“And then, other people that are not vaccinated and have not encouraged their children are the ones that have heard about sterility, and it [the Covid-19 vaccine] hasn’t been around long enough, the science isn't there….”“I believe children’s vaccinations are very useful. I think that they have served a great purpose over time. I’m grateful that they exist. At the same time, I have a friend that I worked with who had twin sons who were vaccine-harmed and became autistic.”
Another parent mentioned concerns over vaccine efficacy, particularly because she (as well as people she knew) still got COVID-19 after vaccinating against it.“Most of the people that I knew that had the vaccination ended up with COVID-19 at some point…. I didn't really feel the need after.”
Moreover, some parents expressed concerns about COVID-19 vaccine development that spilled over into routine vaccine hesitancy. Specifically, they expressed concern about insufficient long-term studies on new vaccines and cited insufficient research, particularly around long-term COVID-19 vaccine side effects.“All the [vaccines] that they have gotten have been around for so long that it’s easier for me to trust those than it is to trust the COVID-19 one. Because for me, until there’s like 10 years of information of large groups of people…. Even though maybe there’s not a lot of bad or severe side effects for kids right now, I feel like there's not enough to know.”
Other parents recognized the importance of vaccines but were concerned with too many vaccinations at once.“I don’t know that [scientists have] done enough research to satisfy me personally of mixing the vaccinations like [parents] do and giving children five or six of them at a time. [Scientists] isolate and try to do the studies on the individual vaccination, but not the long-term effects of throwing 12 of [vaccines] all at once. I like letting [children] grow, giving them a scheduled process of the vaccinations that they have to have versus throwing it all at them at once and see if their body can handle it.”“There are certain kids that you don’t want to risk that kind of stuff with, but a normally healthy child, I think they need to be exposed to some of the stuff in order to build their immunities. And I think that over vaccinating and trying to protect them too much is harmful.”
3.2 Interpersonal factors Participants noted an interpersonal factor that influenced vaccination decisions -- social networks including in-person and mediated communication with people in family, professional, and community circles. Social networks, including peers and other influential individuals -- particularly in mediated communication spaces -- were also mentioned as origins of misinformation by public health professionals:“…I'm skeptical, fearful, kind of family members. I get a lot of info from people who will say my cousin sent me this. Is this real?”.“It's your people that are around you that you socialize with on a regular basis, or even in a community sometimes, will, “Oh, I don't really believe that, but everybody else around me does, so I'm going to go with the flow…”.
Public health professionals also pointed to the influences of misinformation from mediated communication platforms -- such as WhatsApp, Facebook, Instagram, Twitter, and TikTok -- on vaccine hesitancy.“A lot of the misinformation…or the information that folks are getting is coming from social media first and then that kind of segues into the questions that people are bringing to these one-on-one conversations. So, someone might hear COVID-19 vaccines are going to make me infertile on social media. And then that’s one of the questions that they’ll bring to these one-on-one conversations… in these group settings.”
3.3 Institutional factors Our interviews and focus groups revealed several institutional factors that influenced vaccine decision making.
3.3.1 Mediated platforms Misinformation originating from mediated platforms like podcasts, websites, television, and radio sources resulted in vaccine hesitancy. Public health professionals mentioned influential sources in social media and podcasts, such as Joe Rogan, and individual sources from the Disinformation Dozen [36] .“The other thing I wanted to just call up to was podcasts, like Joe Rogan … I've heard that the auditory media plays into our brain chemistry more because it feels like we're talking to or hearing from a friend. I think especially during that COVID period when we couldn't see anyone, I think podcasts really were a big source of that.”
They also pointed to the influence of misinformation in Spanish-speaking sources.“We find that we’ve encountered a lot of vaccine misinformation over the radio, particularly in Spanish….I think people are going to listen to whatever is in their language, and if what happens to be in their language is misinformation, then... what can we do about that?”
3.3.2 Distrust in institutional sources of information Public health professionals shared that they recognized some minoritized groups distrusted government, public health, and healthcare entities due to sustained systems of oppression in the United States. They discussed how black, indigenous, and people of color (BIPOC) and refugee communities had low vaccination rates and were reluctant to trust medical systems and healthcare professionals. One public health professional described why immigrant communities might be fearful of government organizations and related that concern to their fear to get vaccinated.“It’s like they have this fear, there’s a reason why they have fear. Whether it’s something that they themselves in the past have experienced or something that they have witnessed in the past….… I lived in the Fort Lupton-Brighton area. So, a lot of farming and agricultural stuff and having a lot of migrant workers. And there’s this one farmer down there that, I mean, it still makes me really angry every time I think about it. But he had the migrant workers. Before he [paid] them, he called ICE (Immigration and Customs Enforcement) and said, I have a whole slew of undocumented workers here.”
Parents also expressed concerns with the politicization of public health recommendations. First, they believed their autonomy and agency were not taken into consideration, and second, they suspected financial motives behind the mandates.“[Covid-19 vaccination] is pretty much mandated. And so, [public health recommendations are] just a tough thing. I feel like bodily autonomy is very important and I believe everybody should have the right to vaccinate or not vaccinate, no matter what. And I think that’s hard when you get into politics. We mix [politics] with medical. Because there’s no rhyme or reason to political matters involving vaccines, because then [vaccines] become conflated with money.”“I genuinely feel like [public health recommendations have] made me question so many things. Why this vaccine? Where were the mandates for measles and mumps and for polio? For things that were truly killing people and crippling them … There’s a world-wide pandemic still going on. Dengue fever, West Nile virus. There are horrible, horrible diseases that are ravaging and pandemics that are ravaging our planet every day. Obesity, heart disease, depression. Alcoholism… has killed more people over COVID-19. Our spikes of addiction went up and suicides went up…. [Those diseases] are so important. Why this vaccine?... Because I feel like there’s so many vaccines that are more valuable to my children. But then it makes me question, was [the COVID-19 vaccine] all for money? Was it all for power?”
Some parents were suspicious of pharmaceutical companies and doctors. They felt doctors and pharmaceutical companies were corrupt and wanted to extort them.“I read the statistics and learn about the diseases that we’re vaccinating for. I’m not going to just take one person saying, ‘Please put this in my child because it says so on some kind of protocol.’ I believe that the pharmaceutical industry is a little bit over-the-top corrupt and that a lot of the doctors are sold out. For that reason, I don’t look to them as my only source of information.”“How many boosters do we need with it? Every time we turn around, we hear booster number three, booster number four…When does it end? When is the last of the boosters? And I find it interesting that when you listen to the radio… I hear Moderna and I hear Pfizer at the ends of those commercials. They are making money off of it.”
Healthcare providers also mentioned that an increasing number of patients were suspicious and distrustful of doctors, which explains why people were hesitant to trust new vaccines. They described how patient’s distrust of healthcare providers increased over time in the course of the pandemic.“I feel like healthcare has a bad publicist… At the start of the pandemic when people were banging pots for healthcare, there was at least a shift in which there was a modicum of respect towards the advice you gave and people heeded it. Now it's a little bit more antagonistic in some ways, because the nuance that is expressed more often nowadays is your doctors are just working to get you sick so that way they make money, and it can create a difficult work environment… We are dedicating our lives to learn things to help them get better, and then when you have someone come in and just mouth you off and say that you're the bane of the existence of why they're sick, it can make it difficult.”
Moreover, participants from the community leaders focus group identified politicians as potential sources of misinformation. Specifically, they mentioned politicians who discuss science issues without any professional health background resulted in distrust in the government and ultimately negatively impacted vaccine uptake.“Politicians are kind of the the least likely to be trusted sources of vaccine or health information. Not only do they not have any sort of health background, but they also have an agenda and could be pushing their agenda.”
3.3.3 Portrayals of uncertainty Physicians mentioned that some public health guidelines were contradictory and inconsistent with science, which ultimately impacted physicians’ relationships with their patients. These portrayals of uncertainty in public health guidance contributed to physicians’ distrust in public health officials.“That [inconsistency] also technically caused distrust between providers and public health officials… Because when the CDC was like you need 14 days of quarantine and all of a sudden, they dropped it down to five and we didn’t really have the science to back that up, and we were all kind of wondering why, and it seemed like [the new recommendation] was more influenced because we just needed people back in the labor force.”“I think there have been times where it’s felt like the information that was coming out of the CDC directly contradicted what they said a week ago …But I think a lot of what I saw in medical school was adapting to what the public health department said. And so, I think there was a lot of push from faculty to always be aware of what the CDC and county is saying, and that is the messaging you should be giving at all times.”
3.3.4 Political influence in vaccine decision making The results of the focus group with physicians revealed that some patients were aligning their decision to vaccinate with their political beliefs. Patients were choosing to vaccinate against COVID-19 only after the politician they supported was vaccinated.“Yeah, that was the tough thing because even when the one-on-one conversations with patients did line up with medical experts, they were oftentimes aligned with public opinion or party-line opinions and that was really the problem. I had a patient literally say, ‘Did you only get vaccinated after Biden got vaccinated or something?’ I was vaccinated when Trump got vaccinated.”
3.4 Structural Factors: Economic barriers to vaccine Uptake This study identified the structural factor of economic barriers as influential in shaping vaccine uptake.
Public health professionals were concerned that some people were hesitant to receive the COVID-19 vaccine because they were worried about missing work if they had a severe reaction to the vaccination. Public health workers mentioned they were empathetic towards this concern and adjusted their vaccination schedules to accommodate working people with those concerns.“…If somebody gets sick from COVID-19 vaccine for two days and they can’t work and if you're working at an hourly job, it’s like… you don’t pay your bills. You can’t pay your rent; you can’t buy food. So, we try, like doing clinics on Fridays for people who work like Monday through Friday and will have like the weekend to recuperate.”
Based on these results, we propose the Social Ecology of Health Beliefs and Misinformation Framework ( Fig. 3 ) to account for the important influence misinformation can have on vaccine uptake. Specifically, the Health Belief Model Misinformation Framework combines Socio-Ecological factors with the Health Belief Model in its investigation of misinformation and vaccine hesitancy. We organized the factors identified in the interviews ( Table 1 ) into this framework as shown in Fig. 3 . This framework provides a means to account for structural factors connected to misinformation alongside individual factors typically considered in understanding vaccine uptake. This holistic approach accounts for the multifaceted influences that shape individuals' decisions and attitudes toward vaccines, enabling more effective interventions and communication strategies.
4 Discussion Our study presents the Social Ecology of Health Beliefs and Misinformation Framework which combines the Socio-Ecological Model with the Health Belief Model in its explication of misinformation and vaccine hesitancy. Socio-ecological factors are important as they play a crucial role in shaping individuals' attitudes, beliefs, and behaviors related to vaccines. The HBM is a psychological framework that focuses on individual perceptions of health risks and benefits, but it does not capture the complex interplay of institutional and structural influences that contribute to vaccine hesitancy. Our findings therefore provide evidence that misinformation shapes several HBM and socio-ecological constructs that are important to the uptake of a vaccine. For instance, misinformation influences the risk–benefit calculations individuals make when comparing the perceived threat of contracting a disease such as COVID-19 or chickenpox to the vaccine’s side effects or to the perceived efficacy of the vaccine. Our study also demonstrates that accounting for structural factors provides new understanding of how misinformation introduces barriers to vaccine uptake. For instance, distrust in governmental organizations might direct individuals’ attention toward information coming from spaces where misinformation is more common, such as social networks -- either online or offline -- or politicians. Future studies are encouraged to apply the Social Ecology of Health Beliefs and Misinformation Framework to misinformation-related investigations as our study provides support for the idea that susceptibility to misinformation is a complex dynamic of assessing the accuracy of claims against information that confirms the social goals of groups with which individuals identify [37] . Our findings also demonstrate support for the relationship between distrust in government, or official sources, will lead people to alternative sources in social media, politically motivated media, and members of their social networks [38] . Therefore, our model also introduces accounting for interpersonal sources and institutional sources of information as an integral part of understanding the influence of misinformation on barriers to vaccine uptake.
4.1 Limitations A small sample size can limit the generalizability of the findings. A recent review of qualitative health research, however, has found saturation of themes in the data occurs in studies with sample sizes similar to ours [39] . Additionally, our study triangulates multiple perspectives across public health, physicians, and parent populations. Our study also complements existing studies using similar approaches with small sample sizes to uncover perspectives on structural influences on vaccine decision making by addressing the critical missing component of misinformation [29] . Future, large-scale studies could investigate the extent to which misinformation types, based on the HBM, are found across the Socio-Ecological Model’s levels of influence. This knowledge will help us better understand the misinformation landscape affecting vaccine decision making. A second limitation for consideration is that qualitative research can be subject to social desirability bias. We addressed this limitation through techniques such as establishing rapport in the communities of research, including involvement in the county fair at which the parent population was recruited and networking at the full-day workshop for the public health professionals [40] .
Additionally, our findings from this small qualitative study are validated in their congruence with previously published analyses from larger survey studies. For instance, a survey study of 6518 adults in the United States that focused on factors associated with beliefs in various types of misinformation found associations between interpersonal and media sources of information about COVID-19, trusted community relationships, and belief in general misinformation and COVID-19-specific misinformation [41] . The relationships between these factors differed for people of different demographics [41] , supporting our findings about the importance of individual’s social ecology in their susceptibility to misinformation. Similarly, other studies of the influence of misinformation on vaccination decisions have shown that different sociodemographic groups are differentially impacted by misinformation, including a survey of 8001 adults in the United States and United Kingdom [42] . In addition to the impacts of sociodemographic factors, a survey of 1941 adults in the United States showed that misinformation about vaccination in general is correlated with reduced uptake of the COVID-19 vaccine [3] , which mirrors the findings in our model about individual susceptibility to misinformation in general being a factor influencing beliefs about COVID-19 vaccination. Finally, other studies have sought to examine the impact of online misinformation on vaccination rates by correlating geo-tagged online discourse with localized vaccination rates [43,44] , which is consistent with our findings about misinformation influencing health decisions. However, compared to these correlational studies of relationships between variables found in large surveys, our interviews and qualitative data analysis offer a richer examination of the influence and interaction of multiple factors on an individual’s health beliefs, social ecology, and vaccination decisions. Thus, our study’s findings, while validated by similar findings to those of larger studies, offer a further nuanced perspective that can be useful for guiding future messaging and culturally relevant interventions to addressing vaccine misinformation.
5 Conclusions This study provides a unique, triangulated, post-positivist perspective from public health professionals, physicians, and parents to help researchers understand the effect of misinformation on vaccine hesitancy in Colorado. The findings provide evidence that misinformation presents a barrier to vaccination uptake in Colorado. The study also reveals that misinformation can permeate multiple socio-ecological determinants/characteristics to influence vaccination behaviors at the intrapersonal, interpersonal, institutional, and structural levels. Thus, our study advances health communication scholarship in two important ways. First, our research identifies misinformation as an important barrier in the Health Belief Model [5] . Second, our study proposes a framework for involving structural factors in the Health Belief Model. Our study introduces the Social Ecology of Health Beliefs and Misinformation Framework that incorporates multiple levels of influence on health behavior including intrapersonal, interpersonal, institutional, and structural.
