Introduction
Recently, the advent of Digital media has revolutionized the usage of Data and Multimedia communication through minimal price and convenience of use among users. This has led to social media and online portals [1] (Twitter, Facebook, Instagram, YouTube, etc.) for news reading/information sharing among teenagers rather than printed media. Even though digital media has developed into a powerful educative weapon, it is causing a significant impact on society by disseminating false or Fake news [2]. In some instances, this type of fake news significantly impacts developed countries' democratic elections, in which voters may be deceived by counterfeit political statements and claims [3]. In another instance, Covid related Fake news spread over social media, causing panic among the people. As a result [4], it has been reported that these types of fake news messages have been causing damage to over 50 billion USD dollars annually in the stock market.
These problems arise mainly due to the human nature of believing false rumors to be true rather than facts. Manually identifying fake news takes a lot of work and domain experts in many professions. Consequently, a device is required to identify false reports. However, due to the sheer number of reports, creating such a tool is challenging.
However, this issue has been addressed in a variety of ways. Various Machine Learning (ML) techniques are employed to recognize fake news, such as information checking [5], linguistic processing [5, 6–7], and computational linguistics [8]. Early research used datasets derived from the post's content, such as quantitative text features [9] and sentimental data [10, 11–12].
Deep learning (DL), a recent innovation in the scientific world, has proven to be superior to conventional machine learning (ML) techniques at identifying fake news. DL does have unique benefits over ML, including the capacity to automatically retrieve robust features, dependence on data pre-processing reduction, and improved accuracy. Additionally, the widespread availability of programming and data frameworks today has increased the use and dependability of DL-based approaches. As a result, many articles on fake news detection have been published over the past five years, most of which are based on DL strategies [13]. It has been compared between the massive proportion of DL-based fake news detection in ongoing studies and a sincere attempt to review the most recent literature.
Systematic research has been done on classifying fake reviews—studies on tweet text classification using labeled data. Several machine learning techniques were used to classify emails automatically [14]. Using classification algorithms, [15] proposed an intelligent system that could identify fraudulent transactions. One of the critical problems in creating such classifier mechanisms for review platforms is the imbalance in the data. In this study, we used under-sampling of the more significant class instances or up-sampling of the lower-class cases to solve this problem.
To improve social networking websites' credibility and lessen fake news's catastrophic effects, it is essential to identify and quickly comprise phony content disseminated on social media. Automated affected news detection systems are utilized to disprove false information before it spreads and have a chance to cause harm [16, 17–18]. Despite this, it can be challenging to spot speculations and misleading facts that circulate on digital platforms [19, 20–21]. According to [22], the average score of the source as a defense against fake news could be effective. They also went into greater depth about how news sources are rated. They also stated that a low origin rating significantly impacts unknown sources' credibility.
Ma et al. [23] classified tweets as rumors and non-rumors using a variety of mechanically customized attributes depending on the written message, consumer, and dispersion journey of Twitter posts. Researchers use linguistic and tweet characteristics to categorize rumor and non-rumor tweets, such as whether a Twitter post confirms a fake news story [24, 25]. Using a deep learning model with an attention mechanism, Chen et al. [26] extracted word embeddings from Twitter posts to detect rumors in them.
The followers of active Twitter profiles can access news stories from these accounts. There are Twitter contributors that the community has acknowledged as reputable and reliable. On the other hand, some accounts might give the public wrong information. Individuals find determining whether a particular report is accurate challenging and time-consuming. It would be ideal if a clever app could evaluate the trustworthiness of a specific social media bio and provide an overall sense of how much that profile could be relied upon.
As was already discussed, when individual text content performances are lacking, the availability of multiple pieces of information on Twitter can improve the recognition rate. Fusion can combine various Twitter content, either early or late. Early fusion is the fusion of features, whereas late fusion is the fusion of outcomes. The most common early fusion method was to concatenate the feature vectors. Two fundamental methods for late fusion include the majority voting method, which also allocates to a situation the tag predicted by most of the models in use and averages the predicted class probabilities of various models.
This article suggests an approach for combining data from various cues using Attention-Long Short-Term Memory (A-LSTM), Recurrent Neural Network models, and a Whale Optimization Algorithm. The Glove word model was employed. Lemmatization is a crucial stage in human linguistic processing because it simplifies language by breaking down words into their simplest forms. We added each word's average from the Glove word model after lemmatization. We can more accurately convey the meaning of the words because numerical word representations with similar meanings are clustered together. We used tweet pre-processing techniques during the training and testing, like stemming, removing punctuation, stop words, Uniform Resource Locator (URL), Twitter controls, and tweet extension.

This work's novelty can be summed up as follows:
- To propose a Novel WOA-ABiLSTM framework consisting of layers: (a) Tweet pre-processing, (b) Tweet Extraction and representation Layer, (c) Fusion Layer, (d) Attention Layers, and (d) Output Layer.

Related works
Fake news is automatically identified and marked using deep learning algorithms in research known as "deep learning-based fake news detection." These algorithms examine the text, images, and other media in a news article or social media post using various methods, including computer vision and natural language processing.
The main objective is to create a model that can correctly distinguish between authentic and false news using various features like text, images, audio, and video. Models may be trained using supervised approaches, in which classified data are utilized to train the model, or unsupervised learning techniques, in which case the model is skilled to spot patterns in the data without being explicitly told what the patterns are. Using neural networks, specifically Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs) and Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs), to categorize news as fake or real is one of the popular strategies. While CNNs are used to process images, RNNs are used to handle datasets, such as text. Another strategy is to use a combination of deep learning and conventional machine learning to identify fake stories, which can increase the model's accuracy.
The complexity and the absence of labeled information for training make deep learning-based fake news detection difficult. To enhance the efficiency of the models, study in this field is ongoing, and new techniques are being created.
Recent studies on using deep learning for fake news detection are numerous. Sharma et al. [27] provide an overview of several techniques for identifying counterfeit stories, including deep learning and machine learning. Recent deep learning-based approaches for identifying fake news are reviewed in Hu et al. [28], covering both older supervised learning techniques and more recent unsupervised and semi-supervised ones. Often, researchers have utilized media posts compiled from publicly accessible datasets. The pre-processing strategy prepared the dataset for feeding into a neural network. Prior studies have mostly mapped words into vectors using the Word2vec and GloVe word embedding methods [29, 30–31].
The Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) is a well-known variation mode in the deep learning framework for artificial recurrent neural networks. Recurrent Neural Networks (RNN) cannot understand long-term relationships because back-propagation requires a lot of time, especially to account for the growing backflow of error. However, LSTM can keep "Short-Term Memories" for "Extended durations". Asghar et al. [32] proposed a Bidirectional LSTM (BiLSTM) with CNN for rumor detection. The model keeps both directions of the sequence data. The BiLSTM layer can be used to remember long-term dependency effectively.
A method for identifying false news on Facebook that considers both user profiles and news content was put forth by Sahoo and Gupta [33]. To identify false propaganda, the researchers utilized LSTM, and several new features were extracted using Facebook crawling and Facebook API. However, its main drawback is the model's need for considerable time to develop and evaluate it.
The attention mechanism in deep neural networks aims to mimic the exact behavior of preferentially concentrating on a few crucial things while disregarding others. The decoder receives information from each encoder's secret state via the connecting bridge, called attention. Using this framework, the model carefully focuses on the valuable elements from the input. According to experimental results from Long et al. [34] recommended attention-based LSTM with tweeter profile characteristics, it may be easier to spot fake news when using tweeter profiles.
Singhania et al. [35] utilized an algorithmic detector via a Three-Level Hierarchical Attention Network (3HAN) corresponding to levels like words, sentences, and headlines. 3HAN gives various parts of a news story weights based on its three attention ratings. Unlike other deep learning models, 3HAN yields outputs that are simple to comprehend. In contrast to 3HAN, which only utilized text data, Jin et al. [36] focused on RNN and visual features, such as sociocultural contexts (Att-RNN). Another study used RNNs with a soft-attention framework to filter out recognizable language components [37]. However, this approach lacks outside support and is based solely on unique field and character traits. As a result, it offers a constrained setting for credibility analysis.
Baydogan and Alatas [38, 39, 40, 41, 42, 43–44] conducted a series of studies focusing on hate speech detection and sentiment analysis in online social networks. The first study [38] aimed to automatically detect hate speech related to COVID-19 by employing a combination of shallow and deep learning models. The second study [39] involved the evaluation of various artificial intelligence-based algorithms for hate speech detection in online social networks, with a focus on assessing their effectiveness and efficiency. The third study [40] introduced a novel approach to hate speech detection by incorporating metaheuristic algorithms, specifically Ant Lion Optimization and Moth Flame Optimization. Lastly, the fourth study [41] concentrated on sentiment analysis in social networks using a metaheuristic algorithm called Social Spider Optimization, aiming to leverage nature-inspired optimization techniques for improved accuracy or efficiency in capturing sentiments expressed online. Dixit et al. [42] propose a novel fake news detection approach, utilizing a Levy flight honey badger optimization algorithm to fine-tune convolutional neural network (CNN) weights for enhanced accuracy. Guo et al. [43] introduce a model designed for mixed languages, employing a multiscale transformer architecture to adapt to different languages, addressing multilingual challenges. Dixit et al. [44] automated approach integrates Probabilistic Principal Component Analysis and a Levy flight-based LSTM for feature extraction and temporal dependency modeling in fake news detection. Overall, while these studies contribute to exploring innovative methods for hate speech detection and sentiment analysis, they may face limitations in terms of Context Sequence Modeling and Global Optimization.
By leveraging Attention BiLSTM with Whale Optimization and Late Fusion mechanism, we benefit from improved sequence modeling, context understanding, and efficient parameter optimization. These advantages could potentially address some of the limitations, such as enhancing the model's ability to handle dynamic language patterns in social media, improving accuracy, and providing more interpretable results by focusing on relevant parts of the input sequence. Our idea is to improve performance by combining the strengths of different modalities or features and leveraging the complementarity of other representations of the same data.

Tweet pre-processing
Tweet pre-processing is the standard procedure involving noise reduction and preparing tweets for further analysis. This includes removing special characters, Uniform Resource Locator (URL), and user mentions, converting text to lowercase, and tokenizing the tweets into individual words. Pre-processing aims to standardize the tweets and prepare them for linguistic processing methods like sentiment classification and topic modeling. Removing or replacing emojis and emoticons is done to normalize the data and make it easier to analyze. Overall, pre-processing aims to standardize and prepare the data for analysis, such as sentiment analysis or topic modeling. It is a crucial step that allows us to understand the underlying meaning of the tweets and make sense of the data. It can also help reduce the dimensionality of data and noise reduction.
Tokenization
A text is tokenized when divided into smaller pieces, known as tokens. Tokenization divides tweets into single words or phrases, as used in pre-processing. The resulting tokens, such as sentiment or topic modeling, can be used for further analysis.
There are several different approaches to tokenization, such as word tokenization and phrase tokenization. Word tokenization breaks down the text into individual words, while phrase tokenization breaks down the text into groups of words, also known as n-grams. For example, a tweet " The teacher teaches in the classroom” can be tokenized into the following words: "The," "teacher," "teaches," "in," "," and "classroom" by using word tokenization. Phrase tokenization can break down the text into the following n-grams: "The teacher," "teaches in, "and "the classroom."
Tokenization can also involve additional pre-processing steps, such as removing punctuation, memorable characters, and numbers and converting the text to lowercase. Stop words, such as "and" and "the," frequently used but with little meaning, should also be eliminated.
The tokens produced by the tokenization process can then be used for other types of analysis, including trend analysis, topic detection, and text classification. Tokenization is a crucial step in tweet pre-processing as it allows us to understand the underlying meaning of the tweets and make sense of the data.
Stop word removal
Stop word removal is a typical pre-processing step in natural language processing (NLP) that involves removing common words that do not carry much meaning from a text. To make the data less dimensional and more straightforward to analyze, all such words—"stop words"—are typically eliminated. Stop words are frequently used words like "and," "the," "is," "are," and "of," among others. These words do not help provide a context to the text, so they are removed from the text to make it more meaningful and relevant for further analysis.
Stop words can be eliminated by using an algorithm or a collection of pre-defined stop words. A list of words prevalently used in a language or application as stop words is a collection of stop words. The algorithm-based approach uses statistical methods to determine which words are stop words based on their frequency and importance within the text.
Removing stop words is a crucial step in tweet pre-processing as it helps to eliminate common words that do not carry much meaning, making the data more focused and relevant for further analysis. This can improve text analysis models' performance, making it easier to identify meaningful patterns and insights in the data.
URL removal
In the context of tweet pre-processing, URL removal is the process of removing URLs from tweets. URLs, or uniform resource locators, are web addresses that point to a specific webpage or file on the internet. They can often be found in tweets, especially those that contain links to external websites or articles.
Removing URLs from tweets is essential in pre-processing as they do not provide much context or meaning regarding sentiment analysis or topic modeling. URLs may also contain a lot of noise and unimportant information that could be disruptive when analyzing tweets.
There are several ways to remove URLs from tweets, such as using regular expressions or pre-defined libraries. One standard method is to use a regular expression to search for patterns matching URLs, then remove the matching text. Another method is to use pre-defined libraries such as NLTK (Natural Language Toolkit) that provide functions for extracting the Uniform Resource Locator (URL).
After removing URLs, the tweets can be further processed, such as by tokenizing them and removing stop words, before being used for sentiment analysis or topic modeling. Removing URLs is a crucial step in tweet pre-processing as it helps to eliminate any irrelevant information and improve the performance of text analysis models.
Special character removal
Exceptional character removal is a typical pre-processing step in the context of tweet pre-processing that involves removing special characters from tweets. Special characters are non-alphanumeric characters such as punctuation marks, emoticons, and other symbols often found in tweets.
Removing special characters is an essential step in tweet pre-processing because they can disrupt the analysis of tweets by introducing noise and unimportant information. For example, punctuation marks can change the meaning of a sentence, and emoticons can convey sentiment but can also be challenging to interpret by machine learning models.
There are several ways to remove special characters from tweets, such as using regular expressions or pre-defined libraries. One standard method is to use a regular expression to search for patterns matching special characters and remove the matching text. Another method is to use pre-defined libraries such as Natural Language Toolkit (NLTK) or regular expression (re) that provide functions for removing special characters. Removing special characters are a crucial step in tweet pre-processing as it helps to eliminate any irrelevant information and improve the performance of text analysis models.
Text feature representation
GloVe is a technique for creating word embeddings, vector representations of words that capture their meaning and context. The method is based on a statistical model that learns vector representations by analyzing co-existing patterns of textual terms in an extensive database of text. The vectors are known, and the dot product of any two vectors corresponds to a logarithm concerning the probability of the two words co-occurring in the corpus. This allows the vectors to capture the meaning of individual words and the relationships between words, such as similarity and analogy. The resulting vectors can be fed into machine learning algorithms like neural networks to perform tasks like text classification and language translation.
When processing tweets, GloVe can be used to create word embeddings that can be fed into machine learning techniques to complete actions like opinion analysis and text categorization. The gensim library provides a convenient way to use pre-trained GloVe models. We used the following steps to use a pre-trained GloVe model from gensim:
- Install gensim we installed the gensim library by running pip install genism in the Python terminal.

- Download a pre-trained GloVe model We downloaded pre-trained GloVe models from various sources, such as the official GloVe website or the genesis-data library.

- Load the pre-trained model Once you have downloaded the pre-trained model, you can load it into your program using the KeyedVectors.load_ word2vec_format() method from gensim.

- Fine-tune the model Make a vocabulary out of the terms utilized throughout the posts on Twitter. Create a new GloVe framework by training it using the tweet information and language. This can be done using the gensim library by creating a new Word2Vec object and preparing it on the tweet data using the train() method. We used the new embeddings discovered during the fine-tuning process to update the word embeddings in the pre-trained model after the new model had been trained. To achieve this, iterate through the pre-trained model's vocabulary and update the embeddings of phrases that also appear in the refined model.

- Extract the embeddings We first tokenized each tweet into a word before using the pre-trained model to extract an embedding on every phrase. This can be accomplished by using the framework to search up the word encoding to every word within the dictionary of the pre-trained framework.

- Create a feature vector We concatenated the embeddings for all the words in a tweet to create a feature vector for the tweet. Now, any artificial intelligence system can utilize this feature vector as input to train on a particular task, like sentiment analysis or text classification.

- Padding/ Truncating Padding refers to adding padding symbols to sequences shorter than a defined length. This is used to make all sequences have the same size so that they can be processed by machine learning models, which require fixed-sized inputs.

- Truncating refers to truncating sequences that are longer than a defined length. This limits the size of sequences, allowing models to process them more efficiently.

- The pre-processing of tweets using these techniques prepares them for use as input in algorithms for machine learning. By padding or truncating sequences, data can be designed for analysis in a consistent and standardized manner, improving the performance of models and reducing the risk of errors.



Tweet metric representation
Tweet metrics are a set of data that provides information about the performance of a Twitter post, including:
- Impressions The proportion of instances a tweet was shown to customers. The frequency at which a Twitter message appears for users on the platform is how we tally up tweet impressions.

- Engagement rate The percent of likes, retweets, replies, and clicks divided by the total count of impressions the Twitter post gained is used to calculate the tweet engagement rate. It is expressed as a decimal or a percentage.



The formula for tweet engagement rate is: Engagementrate = T o t a l n u m b e r o f e n g a g e m e n t a c t i o n s / T o t a l n u m b e r o f i m p r e s s i o n s ∗ 100
For example, if a tweet received 1000 impressions and 100 engagement actions, the engagement rate would be:
100/1000 * 100 = 10%
Note that the engagement rate can vary widely depending on the type of tweet, the account, and the audience and should be interpreted in the context of other metrics, such as the number of followers and the engagement rates of similar posts. Twitter uses algorithms to track engagement in real-time and updates the engagement metrics for each tweet in near real-time. The engagement metrics are important indicators of a tweet's popularity and impact on the platform.
- Clicks how many times a customer has pressed on links, hashtags, mentions, etc., in a tweet. Twitter calculates tweet clicks by counting the number of clicks on links, hashtags, mentions, photos, videos, etc., within a tweet. This includes:

- Link clicks A user initiates a click each time they select a link within a Twitter post.

- Hashtag clicks Whenever a user chooses a hashtag within a Twitter post, it counts as a click.

- Mention clicks Whenever a user selects a mention in a Twitter post, it is trusted as a click.

- Photo clicks Every time an individual presses on a photo within a Twitter post, it counts as a click.

- Video clicks It counts as a click every time an individual presses on a video contained within a Twitter post.



The click metrics are important indicators of a tweet's impact and the effectiveness of a user's call-to-action (such as clicking on a link).
- Retweets how many times has someone else shared a particular tweet? Retweets on Twitter are determined by counting the instances in which a tweet has been transferred (or "retweeted") by another service user. Each time a tweet is retweeted, it counts as a new tweet with metrics, including impressions and engagement.



When a user retweets a tweet, the original tweet is displayed along with the username of the user who retweeted it. Each retweet of a tweet counts as a separate impression and engagement action, which are recorded in the metrics for each tweet. The retweet metrics are important indicators of a tweet's popularity and reach on the platform, as retweets can help extend the reach of a tweet beyond the user's followers.
- Likes The number of users who like a tweet. The number of times that someone wanted a tweet upvoted by Twitter customers is utilized to calculate the number of likes, also referred to as "favorites," a tweet has received. Each time a user likes a tweet, it counts as a new engagement action.



When a user likes a tweet, the like is recorded in the metrics for that tweet, and the total number of likes is displayed on the tweet. Each like of a tweet counts as a separate engagement action, which is recorded in the metrics for each tweet. The like metrics are important indicators of a tweet's popularity and impact on the platform, as likes can help extend the reach of a post and increase its exposure to all Twitter users.
- Replies The quantity of occasions a tweet has received a response. The number of replies is determined by how many times a tweet has elicited a response from other Twitter users. Each time a user replies to a tweet, it counts as a new engagement action.



When a user replies to a tweet, the reply is displayed as part of the conversation thread, and the total number of replies is displayed on the tweet. Each reply to a tweet counts as a separate engagement action, which is recorded in the metrics for each tweet. The reply metrics are important indicators of a tweet's impact and engagement level, as replies can help extend a tweet's reach and increase its visibility to other users.

Bidirectional LSTM model
The Bidirectional Long Short-Term Memory (BiLSTM) architecture is used for tasks involving computational linguistic processing, like sentiment analysis and translation software. We initially start introducing LSTM in this section, then BiLSTM.
Long short-term memory
Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) is a Recurrent Neural Network (RNN) architecture widely used mainly for modeling sequential information in applications such as voice, multimedia, and language processing. LSTMs [45] were initially presented as an answer to the vanishing gradient issue in the basic RNN model.
The fundamental concept behind an LSTM is the use of memory cells, which oversee the storage and updating of data over time, and gates, which regulate the flow of data from and to the cells. Several interconnected layers with one or more LSTM cells make up the LSTM architecture.
The critical components of an LSTM cell (as shown in Fig. 2):
- Memory cell ( c t ) The memory cell c t maintains information over time. The cell has a self-loop that allows it to remember information from previous time steps.

- Input gate ( i t ) The input gate i t regulates the data flow from input toward the storage unit. The gate is a sigmoid function that controls what further new data are added to the cell.

- Forget gate ( f t ) The forget gate f t regulates data flow from the memory cell to the output. The gate is also a sigmoid function that decides how much old data are retained in the cell.

- Output gate ( o t ) The output gate o t regulates data flow from memory cell to output. The gate is a sigmoid function that decides how much of the cell's current state is passed to the output.

Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) benefit over simple Recurrent Neural Networks (RNN) is their capacity to manage long-term dependencies by carefully keeping or removing data from earlier time steps. The memory cell and the three gates help achieve this by enabling the network to regulate the data flow from and to the cell. The gates also allow the algorithm to recognize lengthy correlations while ignoring the vanishing gradient problem frequently occurring in simple RNNs. Thanks to the gates, the network can learn when to add new information to the cell and when to discard old data.
Bi-directional long short-term memory (LSTM)
Attention-based Bi-directional Long Short-Term Memory (ABiLSTM) belongs to the deep learning family. It is commonly used for computational linguistic processing jobs such as trend analysis and translation software.
This architecture's forward and backward LSTMs process their input sequence to collect contextual information in both directions. The BiLSTM layer processes the embedded sequence in two directions, forward and backward. The sequence is processed in the forward direction from left to right, then in the reverse way from right to left. This enables the LSTM to consider past and future data and comprehend each word's context in the tweet.
The forward LSTM computes the hidden state fh t using the preceding hidden fh t - 1 state, as well as the incoming data X t for every time step t , w. In contrast, backward LSTM computes the hidden state bh t using the reverse hidden state bh t - 1 and the initial input X t . Final hidden state of the BiLSTM model is created by appending the hidden state vectors in both directions. In BiLSTM networks, the two LSTM neural network variables are independent of one another and use the exact word embeddings. The BiLSTM model's step t final output, h t , is as shown in: 6
h t = fh t , bh t

In this work, the Glove model represents the feature vector.
The feature vectors from each source, i.e., BiLSTM features, text, and social network data, are concatenated into a single feature vector. This can be done by simply appending the feature vectors to create a more extended feature vector that includes all the features from each source. The combined feature vector is fed into a fully connected layer and an attention network for tweet classification.
The final feature map contains text and social network data information, allowing the model to leverage information from multiple sources to improve efficiency. The concatenated feature vector can have high dimensions, presenting computational difficulties and necessitating careful model architecture and optimization of training.

The WOA algorithm imitates humpback whales' cooperative oceanic hunting tactics, which cooperate to hunt prey in the ocean. The WOA algorithm considers every whale a potential solution to the optimization issue. The WOA algorithm maintains a collection of possible candidate solutions throughout the optimization process. The algorithm directs the movement of the whales toward potential areas of the solution space based on the location of the optimal method solution so far. The algorithm uses various techniques to iteratively update the position of the whales while assessing the fitness of each potential solution at every time step. The algorithm's output is the best solution ever discovered. Fake news detection method
The proposed algorithm, named Late Fusion WOA-Attention BiLSTM (WOA-ABiLSTM), aims to enhance fake news detection by integrating information from diverse sources as shown in Table 2. The WOA-ABiLSTM algorithm begins by collecting news text content from Kaggle [47] and FakeNewsNet [48] datasets, along with corresponding output labels. Additional features such as Tweet Metric Representation, Tweet User Demographic Features, Tweet User Follower Network Features, Tweet User Role Features, and Twitter Verification Features are obtained from APIs. The text data undergo pre-processing and embedding using an Embedding Layer. A Bidirectional Long Short-Term Memory (BiLSTM) neural network is then employed to capture contextual information from the embedded text. Table 2. Proposed late fusion WOA-attention BiLSTM (WOA-ABiLSTM) algorithm

The hidden states from the BiLSTM, both forward and backward, are concatenated to form a comprehensive representation S c l . This concatenated representation is then fused with features obtained from various sources, including metrics, demographics, follower network details, user roles, and Twitter verification. The dataset is split into training and testing sets for model evaluation.
The training phase involves optimizing a Binary Cross Entropy loss function using the Whale Optimization Algorithm (WOA). The optimization process determines attention weights α t , which are subsequently used to aggregate attention over the hidden states. The resulting attention-weighted sum Z is employed in a softmax function to derive class probabilities p .
The classification decision is made by comparing the probability p against a user-defined threshold δ. If the probability exceeds the threshold, the algorithm classifies the news as fake; otherwise, it categorizes it as non-fake. This decision process is repeated for each sample in the dataset. The algorithm effectively combines the strengths of deep learning (BiLSTM) and metaheuristic optimization (WOA) while leveraging attention mechanisms for feature fusion, ultimately enhancing the accuracy of fake news detection.

Comparison with other methods
K nearest neighbor (K-NN) [49]
The K-NN algorithm works by classifying new texts based on the similarity to a set of labeled texts in a training dataset. The K-NN algorithm determines how far apart the new text is from the training set's labeled texts and then, classifies the new text according to the positive class of those tagged texts.
Regarding text classification, the K-NN algorithm can classify documents, emails, social media posts, and more. By representing each text as a vector of word frequencies or other features, we can calculate the similarity between different texts using distance metrics such as Euclidean distance or cosine similarity.
Naive Bayes classifier (NB) [50]
Naive Bayes Classifier is a well-liked machine learning technique for categorization tasks. It is rooted in the Bayes theorem, which says that the relationship between the likelihood of a hypothesis given the evidence and the possibility of proof provided by the theory is proportional. The method multiplies the prior probability of a class with the probabilities of each feature in that class to determine the likelihood that a new data point will belong to each class when classifying it. The class decides the expected class label for the unique data point with a significant probability. The value of the parameter was 0.05.
Support vector machines (SVM) [51]
Support vector machines (SVM) are well-liked supervised learning techniques in machine learning that utilize text categorization. SVM divides input data into classes with the most significant possible margin by locating a hyperplane. The points from each class that is nearest to the hyperplane are known as "support vectors." The margin is the separation between the hyperplane and the support vectors, and SVM seeks to maximize this margin while still accurately classifying the data. If a linear hyperplane cannot separate the data, SVM uses a kernel function to move the data into a more significant space where it can be divided by hyperplane. The kernel function may represent a linear, polynomial, or radial basis function (RBF).
Convolutional neural network (CNN) [52]
Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) are a potent class of deep learning models that can be used as text classifiers. They have gained popularity for computational linguistic tasks like trend analytics and topic classification due to their capacity to retrieve information from text documents and spot patterns. Classifying documents, emails, social media posts, and other text types is possible by learning a CNN on a sizable corpus of text data. By doing so, the CNN can be trained to recognize the language and context used in various text types. With the help of a CNN, we can make sense of large volumes of text data, identify critical information, and gain insights into trends and patterns.
Evaluation settings
The effectiveness of the suggested method was assessed using the industry-standard metrics for classification models. TP stands for True Positive, TN stands for True Negative, FP stands for False Positive, and FN stands for False Negative.
Accuracy
The effectiveness of machine learning models is frequently assessed using accuracy, especially for classification tasks. A model's or classifier's accuracy estimates how well it recognizes or predicts the target variable or result. It is calculated by the ratio of the total number of predictions made to the number of accurate predictions. In other words, accuracy is the proportion of the model's predictions. 23
Accuracy = TP + TN TP + FP + TN + FN
Precision
Precision measures how many true positives (TP) out of all optimistic predictions are generated by a machine learning model or classifier (both true positives and false positives). Precision is determined as the ratio of TP to the total of TP and false positives (FP). A high precision value indicates that the algorithm correctly identifies positive cases and makes a few wrong positive predictions. 24
Precision = TP TP + FP
Recall
Recall, also known as sensitivity, is calculated by the ratio of total true positives to the sum of true positives and false negatives (FN). Although a high recall value suggests that the model correctly identifies most positive cases, it may also lead to more false positives. 25
Recall = TP TP + FN
F1-score
The F1 score is the harmonic mean of recall and precision, considering both metrics for a more thorough assessment of classification results. The F1 score is determined by multiplying the precision and recall products by two and dividing the result by precision and recall. A more significant number indicates better model performance; the range is 0 to 1. 26
F 1 - score = precision · recall precision + recall

Conclusion
In this study, we introduced a Novel Late Fusion framework, WOA-Attention BiLSTM, designed for the identification of fake news in Twitter data. Our investigation offers a unique approach, incorporating late fusion of diverse elements such as Tweet content, metrics, user demographics, user followers, user roles, user verification features, and attention weights optimized with the Whale Optimization Algorithm in neural networks.
The motivation for this innovative approach stems from the pressing need to enhance the accuracy of fake news detection, considering the complex interplay of various factors involved in disseminating misinformation. Recognizing the limitations of existing methods, we sought to address these challenges through a comprehensive late fusion framework that combines textual content, user-related features, and attention mechanisms optimized with the Whale Optimization Algorithm.
Employing tweet pre-processing techniques and glove word embedding, we transformed the data into a feature vector, utilizing BiLSTM to extract hidden state features. Late fusion of bi-directional hidden state features with tweet content and user-related features was performed, guided by the Attention-based mechanism learning different weights on sentence fragments through the Whale Optimization technique. The model achieved an accuracy of 94% and 92% on Kaggle [47] and FakeNewsNet [48] datasets, respectively, surpassing decision tree, logistic regression, K-NN, Random Forest, support vector machine, and naïve Bayes classifier.
Our experimental results highlight the superior performance of the WOABiLSTM model, outperforming both deep learning and traditional machine learning models. In conclusion, this study not only contributes a Novel Late Fusion framework but also provides a benchmark for future studies seeking to improve fake news detection methodologies. The comparison of our performance metrics with existing literature underscores the effectiveness and innovation of the proposed WOA-Attention BiLSTM model in addressing the challenges of fake news detection in social media datasets.
