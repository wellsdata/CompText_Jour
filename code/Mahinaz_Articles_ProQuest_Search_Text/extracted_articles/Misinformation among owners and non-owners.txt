The idea of ‘misinformation’ is often invoked, but it is not often that misinformation in any particular area gets carefully quantified. One area where there has been significant accusation of misinformation but little quantification is surrounding electric vehicles (EVs) 1 . Legitimate concerns about EVs are easy to find and important to address on their own merit, but concerns founded in misinformation can unfairly manipulate the image of EVs in people’s minds and disallow their decision processes from being based in reality. Writing in Nature Energy , Matthew Hornsey and colleagues, from University of Queensland, Leibniz-Institut for Psychology and University of Hohenheim, work to establish a baseline for the prevalence of misinformation about EVs, explore predictors of belief in misinformation, and test interventions for reducing endorsement of misinformation as factual 2 .
The paper presents insights from three survey-based studies. In the first two studies, the research team recruited 6,341 participants from Australia, Austria, Germany, and the United States to assess the prevalence of misinformation about EVs in these four countries and analyse what individual characteristics predict a tendency to endorse misinformation. These two samples are representative of each country’s demographics in age, gender, and education levels.
The researchers used Southwell and colleagues’ definition of misinformation: “publicly available information that is misleading or deceptive relative to the best available scientific evidence and that runs contrary to statements by actors or institutions who adhere to scientific principles” 3 . Operating from this definition and based on the best available information on EVs today, the authors generated an extensive set of true and false statements about EVs that were shared with participants. The results show that, on average, more than one third of respondents believe the false claims to be true, while another 41% are unsure or undecided.
Hornsey and colleagues then leverage various traits of the people in their sample to predict who has a stronger tendency to agree with this misinformation. A particularly notable result is that a conspiratorial mindset is one of the strongest predictors; namely, those who have a tendency to see conspiracies are more likely to agree with misinformation about EVs and less likely to want to purchase an EV than those without a conspiratorial mindset. The authors are also able to compare differences between EV owners and non-owners in their perceptions of misinformation. We might assume that believing negative misinformation about EVs would deter potential buyers, but Hornsey et al. surprisingly observe that EV owners are no less likely to reject misinformation statements than non-EV owners. The authors speculate that EV owners may be more sensitive to EV benefits, such as environmental impact, and thus less impacted by the misinformation itself. It is intriguing to think that owners and non-owners alike agree with misinformation about EVs to similar degrees.
Separately, the researchers also test a possible intervention for reducing the acceptance of misinformation. 1,500 participants either saw an EV fact sheet from the US Department of Energy, had a brief conversation with Chat GPT 4o-Turbo about EVs, or were part of a control condition. Immediately following the intervention and also after a ten-day interval, both the fact sheet and the conversation with artificial intelligence (AI) show reduced misinformation agreement for participants. Although there are no observed differences between the fact sheet and AI conversation, it is notable to see that these can have a lasting effect, enduring at least a full ten days.
With the amount of information available at our fingertips today, it is becoming increasingly difficult to ensure that the information in front of us is truthful. Although there are legitimate concerns surrounding EVs and a possible transition to them, a cloud of misinformation will only serve to muddy the waters. Like rose-coloured glasses skewing a person’s view of the world, misinformation can replace legitimate strengths and weaknesses of the EV market with illegitimate understanding of their pros and cons not based in fact.
Although Hornsey and colleagues focus on the negative misinformation about EVs, it is important to remember that positive misinformation may be an issue as well. For example, research on rebound effects suggests that people may overestimate the positive effects of environment-related technologies like residential solar panels, where consumers increase their consumption after obtaining what they believe to be a green source of energy 4,5 . Although the most current science indicates lower lifecycle emissions for EVs than combustion engines, the total carbon footprint is certainly not zero or negative, while EVs are often presented as helping the environment. These two ideas could suggest a belief that green technology exerts an actively positive influence on the environment, even though current science indicates the impact is still currently negative, albeit less so. It would be interesting to observe whether positively framed (but also false) ideas are also held among consumers regarding EVs, which could also serve as an explanation for the difference between EV owners and non-owners in the work from Hornsey and colleagues. Perhaps EV owners have a more inflated view of the positive side of EVs, even to the point of falsehood.
Hornsey and colleagues’ work on misinformation is an important step forward, both as a way of highlighting the degree to which consumers are agreeing with information that is false and also as a way of demonstrating the capacity of two different interventions to reduce this belief both immediately and over a period of time.
