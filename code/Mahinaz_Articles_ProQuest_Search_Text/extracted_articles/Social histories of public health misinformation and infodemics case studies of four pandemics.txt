Introduction During the COVID-19 pandemic, Robert Califf, the 25th Commissioner of the US Food and Drug Administration, stated his belief that “misinformation is now our leading cause of death” in the USA. 1 These words are similar to the sentiments of health authorities worldwide, who believe that the prevalence of misinformation has increased non-compliance with public health recommendations. 2 For example, refusal to wear face masks has been associated with the belief that masking might cause CO 2 toxicity. 3 Furthermore, ivermectin was inappropriately used by the US general public to prevent or treat serious illness with COVID-19. 4 72% of 806 physicians in the USA believed that misinformation negatively affected patient outcomes for COVID-19. 5
Public health authorities continue to experience challenges in mitigating misinformation during infodemics, defined by WHO as “an overabundance of information—some accurate and some not—occurring during an epidemic”. 6–8 A literature review of infodemic-related publications showed scholarly disagreement about how to define terms such as misinformation, disinformation, rumours, and conspiracy theories. 9 Having few standardised definitions complicates conversations about and intervention strategies for the management of infodemics. 9 Moreover, research that characterises the relationship between misinformation, health, and behaviour is scarce. 1 For example, social-media analyses can be useful in tracking the spread of misinformation, but the relationship between consuming specific media content and subsequent behaviour is poorly understood. 10
Although medical and public health communities have focused on creating intervention strategies to address current and future infodemics, the ways in which infodemics affected public health crises before COVID-19 are less widely understood. On March 13, 2023, the time of writing, we had identified one review that explored how information-related issues affected the influenza pandemic in 1918, the beginning of the HIV pandemic, and the 2003 severe acute respiratory syndrome pandemic. 11 This one review focused on providing policy considerations for mitigating infodemics in the future and detailed factors during outbreaks of these diseases that contributed to historical infodemics. 11 We also identified a systematic review that examined the demographic or economic effects of all pandemics occurring in the 200 years before COVID-19. 12 Although this systematic review did not focus on information-related issues, its findings highlighted the need to engage multidisciplinary scholars in the holistic analysis of multiple pandemics rather than assessing the effects of these pandemics in isolation, as is common in historical-pandemic literature. 12
Key messages
- • The spread of misinformation has been a persistent public health challenge throughout history
- • Evolving evidence during the process of scientific discovery might inadvertently provide opportunities for misinformation before scientific facts can be established
- • Poor relationships between public health institutions and local populations can reduce public trust in those institutions and in public health authorities, which can contribute to the proliferation of misinformation
- • Implementing a holistic approach that assesses the information ecosystem, defined as how people consume, produce, interact with, and behave around information, will benefit public health and medical practitioners in managing misinformation
- • Health-care systems should prioritise community engagement and transparency to improve trust between health-care providers and local populations



For infectious-disease practitioners, who are increasingly involved in addressing misinformation, understanding the historical perspective is crucial in informing practices both in the clinic and across institutions. In this Historical Review, we aimed to explore the history of health misinformation via a broad methodological approach to provide a basic historical understanding of misinformation during pandemics and to synthesise key insights from history to provide context and considerations for infectious-disease practitioners and other health-care providers.
Methods We chose to conduct a narrative review for this Historical Review, as having no standardised terminology precluded a systematic review. Scholars across disciplines have varied use and interpretations of terms such as misinformation, disinformation, and infodemics, and these terms are relatively novel concepts of study that would not have been used in historical sources. Narrative reviews are particularly appropriate when assessing infodemics due to the required breadth of literature across disciplines and flexibility in evolving terminologies across publications. Narrative reviews are also often used to synthesise information for medical and public health audiences in academic journals. 13,14
We focused on four infectious diseases associated with pandemics (ie, smallpox, 1918 influenza, cholera, and HIV), which were selected due to their historical significance, interest to both infectious-disease practitioners and historians, and subsequent abundance of scholarly historical discussion on information-related issues pertaining to these diseases.
We first identified widely cited, major historical monographs of smallpox, 1918 influenza, cholera, and HIV through a consultation with historians. We subsequently searched for articles using combinations of the terms “infodemic”, “misinformation”, “disinformation”, “health communication”, “fake news”, “pandemic”, “epidemic”, “smallpox”, “cholera”, “1918 influenza”, “spanish flu”, “HIV”, “AIDS”, and “COVID-19”. When articles of relevance were identified, we incorporated the additional search terms “trust”, “mistrust”, “distrust”, “public health authorities”, “public health institutions”, “science communication”, “media”, “social media”, “scientific authority”, “scientific consensus”, “knowledge gaps”, “information voids”, “information ecosystem”, “Edward Jenner”, “vaccine hesitancy”, “miasma”, “cholera denial”, “John Snow”, “dot map”, “myth”, “contingent contagionism”, “pseudo-influenza bacillus”, “Richard Pfeiffer”, “discrimination”, “stigma”, “medical harm”, and “medical ethics” on the basis of major points of interest that were discussed in the identified articles. Only US spellings were used for search terms. In some cases, we also identified and reviewed other articles that were referenced in publications from our initial search. This iterative approach allowed us to gain insight into the breadth of literature. All searches were conducted in Google Scholar for articles published between database inception and Jan 31, 2024. We only included secondary sources published in English.
Articles were reviewed by SLJ, JP, JK, and HC to identify discussions of information-related issues or events. When an event of historical significance was identified, referenced articles were reviewed by the same authors or an additional search was conducted via the same method to ensure that at least two references were identified pertaining to each event. The references of reviewed manuscripts or articles were reviewed if relevant. SLJ, HC, JK, and JP decided the relevance of articles on the basis of whether each article provided accurate information or scholarly discussion pertaining to understanding how historical events or contexts affected the spread of information or misinformation. After the initial synthesis, other authors (DAP, DP, CW, NA, KAB, and CV) suggested including other specific publications to improve the breadth of reviewed monographs and articles.
This review process was crucial to explore a wide range of sources, despite little consistency in how misinformation has been studied or discussed through time. 15 As is characteristic of narrative reviews, we ultimately relied on the perspectives of experts with backgrounds in history, anthropology, medicine, health communications, and public health to provide a narrative discussion of the literature. 16
Smallpox Smallpox is the only human disease that has been globally eradicated to date. One of the most substantial milestones occurred in 1796, when the English physician Edward Jenner discovered that previous cowpox infection could confer protection against smallpox. 17 These observations led him to develop an early prototype of modern-day vaccines by inoculating patients with cowpox scabs. 18 This monumental discovery was responded to with scepticism and distrust, and misinformation circulated both within and beyond the scientific community. 19 These challenges hindered eradication efforts, with smallpox continuing to negatively affect human health for almost 200 years after the invention of the vaccine. 20,21
Debates about the safety and efficacy of cowpox-scab inoculation rapidly emerged in the scientific community. Some health authorities were initially sceptical of the Jennerian vaccine because its mechanism of action was poorly understood among scientists at the time. 22 As vaccination campaigns progressed, physicians and scientists began to observe smallpox infections in individuals who had already received the vaccine several years before. 22 However, Jenner staunchly denied the possibility of waning vaccine efficacy and attributed subsequent infections to physician error, which led scientific opponents to retaliate by speaking publicly about their distrust of the vaccine. 22
Vaccine hesitancy was also common among the public. One notable factor in this hesitancy was the discomfort that many individuals felt regarding inoculation with cowpox scabs, as cowpox had originally been transmitted to humans from an animal source. 19 This use of an animal source contributed to the belief that miniature cow heads could grow from sites of vaccination ( figure ). 22 Furthermore, many clergymen argued that the Jennerian vaccine was unholy due to its animal origin, thus further contributing to vaccine hesitancy. 23 Moreover, some populations did not trust the safety of the Jennerian vaccine due to their previous experiences with insufflation or variolation, in which people were deliberately infected by inserting smallpox scabs or pustular fluid intranasally or through superficial punctures in the skin as another way of conferring immunity. 24 These methods were practised in parts of the world as early as the 16th century, yet rarely gained widespread popularity due to the risk of contracting severe disease. 25 As such, people who had negative experiences with insufflation or variolation struggled to distinguish these practices from the Jennerian vaccine, resulting in continued fears and vaccine hesitancy. 19
In some cases, vaccine hesitancy intersected with anti-imperialist sentiment, as populations from colonised territories distrusted the vaccination campaigns forced on them by overseas ruling authorities. For example, communities in India in the 19th and 20th centuries prophesied that British rule would be expelled through the birth of a “Black child with White blood”. 19 According to this belief, British authorities forced smallpox vaccinations on people who had been colonised to achieve mass genocide of Indigenous populations and prevent this prophecy from being fulfilled. 19 A similar occurrence was observed in Burma, another former British colony from the same period, in which local communities believed that the Queen of England dreamt about a Burmese child who would grow up to overthrow her rule. 19 Many people who had been colonised therefore believed that British vaccination programmes aimed to stop any possibility of this overthrow. 19 These examples show how political contexts have affected narratives about vaccines, contributing to vaccine hesitancy.
When reviewing the history of smallpox eradication, considering how technological advances affected the dissemination of health information about smallpox and vaccinations is also important. During the two centuries between the invention of the Jennerian vaccine and smallpox eradication, mechanisms of communication evolved from print journalism to include television-broadcast journalism. The increasing popularity of television inadvertently amplified fears of smallpox vaccination by broadcasting both descriptions and visual footage of the rare instances in which the smallpox vaccine produced severe side-effects. 26 These broadcasts resulted in skewed perceptions about the relative risks and benefits of the vaccine. 26
The history of smallpox shows how gaps in scientific knowledge, mistrust of government, and other social and political contexts have affected narratives of misinformation. 19 Furthermore, it shows how scientific knowledge of smallpox vaccination has increased and evolved over time, indicating the importance of encouraging scholarly debate as an integral mechanism of scientific progress. 22 Moreover, technological advances in mechanisms of health communication highlight the role of mass media in influencing perceptions of disease risk or vaccine safety. These changes emphasise the challenges experienced by health authorities when figuring out how to sensitively present accurate information without causing unnecessary anxieties. Altogether, the case study of smallpox shows the complexity and historical precedence for many of the challenges that health authorities experience in mitigating misinformation nowadays.
Cholera The global disease burden of cholera has put it at the forefront of scientific conversation, especially because there have been seven major cholera pandemics since 1817. 27 However, scientists had substantial difficulty in identifying the cause of the disease or deciding on diagnostic criteria due to the wide range of symptoms. A high mortality rate and little scientific consensus contributed to widespread panic that encouraged the spread of misinformation. In 1832, for example, political turmoil in Paris, France, nurtured the belief that government officials had poisoned drinking supplies with arsenic, thus producing cholera-like symptoms. 28 In Liverpool, many individuals who had cholera refused to enter hospitals because they believed inpatient treatment had a higher mortality risk than staying at home, 29,30 which was potentially understandable due to scarce knowledge about proper treatment. This perception, alongside the murders in Edinburgh, Scotland during 1827–28, in which two men killed people and sold their bodies to an anatomy school, caused individuals to believe that doctors deliberately killed people with cholera to harvest their organs for study. 29,31
There was substantial progress in 1854, when English physician John Snow recognised that cholera was spread through contaminated water. However, many scientists and sanitary reformers rejected Snow's hypothesis because it opposed prevailing scientific belief in the miasmatic theory, which posited that diseases were transmitted through foul air. 32 Debates ensued among the scientific community regarding whether cholera spread as a contagious disease. Even the discovery of Vibrio cholerae in 1854 did not clarify the mechanism of transmission for cholera because infection only occurred under specific circumstances. These observations led to the so-called contingent contagionist view of disease transmission, which explained how diseases could be both miasmatic and contagious on the basis of specific environmental conditions. 33 In response to this complex scientific debate, some medical journals sought to maintain public trust in the legitimacy of medical knowledge by limiting the publication of differing scientific opinions. 34 However, their attempts often backfired and inadvertently caused increased distrust in medical authorities. 34
Towards the end of the 19th century, scientific consensus eventually changed from miasmatic theory to the understanding that microorganisms spread infectious disease. Although medical institutions often praise John Snow for initiating this change, some aspects of Snow's work have been altered as part of public health mythology. According to popular renditions of his biography, Snow created a dot distribution map that connected patterns of cholera mortality with the Broad Street water pump used during the 1854 cholera epidemic in London, UK. 35 These observations allegedly led to the removal of the pump and halted the outbreak in the surrounding community. 36 However, close examination revealed that the cholera outbreak had already peaked before Snow's discovery, indicating that the reduction in mortality was not directly associated with removal of the pump. 37 Despite this inaccuracy, Snow's work—including its mythicised features—continues to be celebrated as an integral part of medical history. This continued celebration of historical falsehoods indicates the enduring existence of mythology and inaccuracies, even among medical and public health professionals.
Despite an increased understanding of cholera transmission, systemic issues (eg, living in poverty or having little access to clean water) continue to be barriers to disease eradication. 38 In this context, misinformation often takes the form of so-called cholera denial, which describes how authorities might deny the severity or origins of disease to avoid responsibility for providing aid. 39 One example of cholera denial occurred during the 1910 cholera epidemic in Naples, Italy. The Government of Naples had already allocated funding to improve sewer systems after a previous epidemic in 1885, but efforts were abandoned after the contracting company went bankrupt. 40 Many government officials and members of the public denied the severity of the second cholera epidemic to protect the reputations of the officials and the city, which is believed to have allowed the government to avoid the responsibility of funding further development. 40 The 2010 cholera epidemic in Haiti provided a more recent example, in which leaked reports revealed how the UN initially denied that UN health workers were responsible for bringing cholera back to Haiti. 41 Instead of acknowledging the responsibility of the UN, media reports attributed the cholera epidemic in Haiti to impoverished and unsanitary conditions. 42 These narratives, which were deliberately selected to portray a specific scenario, were criticised for supporting government agendas of denying responsibility for providing support to Haiti.
Examining changes in scientific debate concerning cholera transmission highlights how boundaries between truth and misinformation are often unclear due to emerging empirical evidence. 33 Examples of cholera denial also show how misinformation does not have to be based on the science of disease transmission or prevention to cause public health harm. 39 Misinformation narratives can be partisan, political, and strategically used by powerholders to achieve their underlying goals. This finding highlights the salience of political agendas or power dynamics in influencing narratives regarding the origins or severity of diseases, which ultimately affects disease elimination.
1918 influenza pandemic The H1N1 influenza A virus responsible for the 1918 influenza pandemic emerged and spread with unprecedented speed during World War 1, killing 50–100 million people in 2 years. 43 During this time, the scientific community struggled to agree on the cause of the disease. For example, leading scientists from Switzerland and Germany believed that the symptoms were caused by the return of the bubonic plague or by a severe resurgence of smallpox or cholera. 31 However, German bacteriologist Richard Pfeiffer argued that the disease was caused by “pseudo-influenza bacillus”, 44 a bacterium that he had isolated from patients with influenza during the previous influenza pandemic of 1889–90. His findings were shared and replicated during the 1918 influenza pandemic before additional evidence indicated that the bacterium caused a secondary infection rather than being the primary disease-causing pathogen. 44
No consensus and evolving evidence affected the ability of public health institutions to respond to the influenza pandemic. 44 Prevention and treatment strategies remained inconsistent as scientists hurriedly attempted to understand the mechanism of transmission of influenza. Discrepancies in the scientific evidence on the effectiveness of wearing a face mask also resulted in conflicting public health recommendations. For example, local governments instituted face-mask mandates across most of California, USA, despite an official announcement from the California State Board of Health that wearing a face mask was not necessary. 45 Some media sources also criticised face-mask mandates and claimed that they were only intended to “increase the scare” during already heightened tensions from World War 1. 45 This inconsistency increased panic and led to an increase in face-mask resistance groups across the USA. Moreover, the absence of an influenza vaccine allowed drug manufacturers in the USA to overcharge for vaccines that were originally designed to prevent colds or other infections. 44 British pharmaceutical advertisements used scaremongering tactics to increase demand for their products. 46
The wartime context caused governments in the USA to restrict media coverage of the influenza pandemic. 47 For some people, news restrictions increased suspicion and mistrust regarding government, media, and public health authorities. 45 The coinciding invention of the telegraph also allowed particular information to spread more quickly than before, which probably amplified mass hysteria about the influenza pandemic. 48 At the same time, wartime propaganda hid the severity of the pandemic and caused many people to believe that the disease was equivalent to seasonal influenza. 45 Government officials and military leaders in the USA also attempted to downplay the prevalence of influenza so as to avoid taking attention away from the war. 45 As a result, US military generals sometimes claimed that contracting influenza was weak or unpatriotic, which caused many soldiers who had influenza to hide their illness and continue on the battlefield. 47
The 1918 influenza pandemic ultimately shows the challenges in communicating disease risk when there is evolving scientific consensus regarding the causes of a disease. 49 Limitations in the speed of scientific progress can complicate how health authorities communicate with the public as they navigate the sensitive balance between encouraging preventive measures and avoiding contributing to widespread panic. 50 Furthermore, wartime restrictions on media coverage showed how information voids, defined as situations where there is little credible or accessible health information to address community concerns or questions, 51 might amplify panic, which can produce favourable conditions for the spread of misinformation. 45
HIV and AIDS The history of HIV reinforces how medical knowledge necessarily changes over time. Cases of autoimmune disease gained global attention when HIV began affecting the USA in 1981. 52 However, that these disease symptoms were caused by infection with HIV was initially unclear. Even after the association between HIV and AIDS was proposed, many scientists continued to argue that autoimmune disorders were triggered by multifactorial conditions that could not be explained by HIV infection alone. 53 After antiretroviral therapy became available in 1987, the roll-out of HIV interventions was affected by debates about the optimal time to start treatment, as the scientific evidence was often unclear or interpreted in different ways. 54 These disagreements extended waiting periods for treatment and ultimately hindered HIV control. 54
The history of HIV, however, also brings attention to the public health challenge of preventing and correcting disease stigma. When the first documented cases of this new autoimmune disease were concentrated among men who have sex with men, these observations led to the labelling of the mysterious disease as “gay-related immune deficiency”. 30 HIV was also widely miscommunicated to only infect the “4-Hs”, consisting of “homosexuals, hemophiliacs, heroin users, and Haitians”, 55 which became a salient and damaging categorisation. Although “gay-related immune deficiency” 30 was subsequently renamed to AIDS when evidence showed that the disease could spread to people other than men who have sex with men, activities and behaviours that were associated with men who have sex with men were often still blamed as the primary mode of HIV transmission. 56–58 Moreover, a 1983 publication in the Journal of the American Medical Association erroneously communicated that the disease could spread outside of direct “sexual contact, drug abuse or exposure to blood products”. 59 Although this statement was later corrected, it nevertheless increased public fear of being around men who have sex with men.
The consequences of this early association of HIV and AIDS with men who have sex with men resulted in substantial ramifications for the public understanding of the disease, including effects on disease control that persist today. For example, social stigma continues to deter individuals at risk of acquiring HIV from using HIV testing and pre-exposure prophylaxis (PrEP). 60,61 Furthermore, the stigma that associates HIV only with men who have sex with men might cause people who are not men who have sex with men to underestimate their own risk of acquiring HIV. 62 Moreover, due to the implicit association between HIV and sexual behaviour, some populations—such as older adults or people who inject drugs—often receive less targeted risk communication about HIV prevention. 63,64 As a result, they often have increased risk of developing severe disease without knowledge of their HIV status, which could prevent their use of available interventions, such as PrEP. 64
Social context around HIV stigma has also resulted in reduced trust in medical institutions. In the USA, Black men who have sex with men often experience the most social harm as HIV stigma can co-occur with anti-gay and anti-Black discrimination. 65 Communities in the USA who have been historically marginalised due to a longstanding history of institutional and interpersonal racism in health care have less trust in governments and medical authorities than communities who have not been historically marginalised. 66,67 This reduction of trust has been suggested to contribute to the fabrication of rumours and conspiracy theories related to HIV and its prevention and treatment in the USA. 68 For example, two US surveys have shown that some communities believe that US Government officials created HIV to achieve the mass genocide or sterilisation of Black men who have sex with men. 69,70 In Africa, individuals also experience disease stigma because of the perceived association between HIV and sexual promiscuity and uncleanliness. 71 These social realities can discourage individuals from disclosing their HIV-positive status and can hinder willingness to seek treatment. 72
The history of HIV and AIDS highlights the importance of communicating epidemiological realities without exacerbating social stigma, as well as the ongoing need to overcome the damaging effects of stigma on trust in public health authorities, governments, and health care. Although disease stigma is not typically considered to be misinformation, it is an important social factor that has led to the proliferation of rumours that continue to affect public health behaviours, intervention uptake, and associated outcomes. 68 This case study shows the complexity of communicating disease risk, as well as the potential longevity and influence of statements issued by public health authorities and their effects on stigma and misinformation.
Discussion Our Historical Review has shown that difficulty in identifying and defining misinformation is not a new phenomenon. Although definitions remain contested, one common definition is “information that is contrary to the epistemic consensus of the scientific community”. 9 However, the historical case studies of smallpox, cholera, influenza, and HIV included in this Historical Review are reminders that scientific consensus is rarely reached at the outset of a scientific discovery or during the early stages of an outbreak. Furthermore, majority scientific belief might not always equate to factually accurate information, as shown by the initial resistance to the discoveries of Edward Jenner and John Snow. Changes in scientific consensus are often an intrinsic part of scientific progress, which means differentiating between scientific opinions and misinformation is often difficult. 37
Despite being intrinsic to scientific progress, inconsistencies in the interpretation of data can damage the public's trust in scientific authorities and institutions. 73 For example, evolving evidence surrounding the mechanism of transmission of SARS-CoV-2 contributed to contradicting public health recommendations in South Korea. 74 Similar to during the 1918 influenza pandemic, these inconsistencies can lead to confusion, panic, and the spread of misinformation. 75 Therefore, health authorities should encourage scientific debate, be cautious when communicating emerging findings, and prioritise transparency about the dynamic nature of the scientific process. 73 As other scholars have argued, public health experts could be cautious not to “overestimate the certainty of our predictions”, 76 which can breach public trust in science and scientific authorities when predictions change or become unreliable over time.
A historical perspective highlights how the quality and amount of information circulated are also important. For example, information voids perpetuated by governments and health authorities contributed to mass hysteria and misinformation during the 1918 influenza pandemic. 45 This pandemic also foreshadowed the information voids that exist nowadays, even within oversaturated information ecosystems (defined as how people consume, produce, interact with, and behave around information) 51,77 as the inundation of content means identifying and accessing crucial information are difficult. 45,78 The COVID-19 pandemic exacerbated this issue by increasing the amount of time that people spent consuming online content. 79 During the current democratisation of information sharing on social media, sensationalised information is more likely to be spread than unembellished information as algorithms prioritise popular content irrespective of accuracy. 80 Simultaneously, health authorities that speak with credibility but do not sensationalise the presentation of science often struggle to compete for public attention. 81 These challenges necessitate innovative strategies to allow health authorities to fill information voids that emerge within the general overabundance of information.
Our Historical Review also brings attention to the importance of monitoring broad factors in an information ecosystem. Information ecosystems include “the complex, dynamic infrastructure, sources, and relationships through which information flows”, 51 which can be influenced by health systems and social dynamics that affect access to information. Our analysis highlights the importance of domains such as trust, social stigma, and sociopolitical or historical contexts within information ecosystems that can support the spread of misinformation. For example, the case study on cholera showed how political agendas can incentivise the understatement or overstatement of disease severity, which was similarly observed in media coverage of the COVID-19 pandemic in the USA. 82 Furthermore, the presented narratives of the malintent of governments or medical institutions—whether they are true or false—have shown the effects of institutional discrimination on public trust in those governments and health authorities. At worst, medical racism, stigma instigated by biased political leaders, and historical exploitation have left reasonable legacies of distrust among affected communities. 19,69,83 The history of HIV and AIDS further highlights the long-term consequences of statements issued by public health authorities, which have contributed to disproportionate disease stigma among vulnerable populations and complicated how health authorities communicate disease risk. Similar challenges continue to be observed nowadays with mpox, a zoonotic disease primarily spread through sexual contact that has had global outbreaks since May, 2022. 84 Similar to the initial description of AIDS as “gay-related immune deficiency” 30 is the common mislabelling of mpox as the “gay man disease” 84,85 due to the high proportion of men who have sex with men who have acquired it. Scientists have predicted that the resulting stigma will reduce adherence to contact tracing and other public health recommendations. 86–88
However, these challenges are not insurmountable. Although information is increasingly globally circulated through television, mass media, and social media, localised responses can help to reach specific populations, including communities that have been historically marginalised. One example of a localised response includes engaging local communities in the analysis, interpretation, and dissemination of research. 89 Furthermore, partnerships with community members can help to design communication materials that correct disease stigma. 90 Health authorities can also benefit from engaging social and behavioural scientists, as social-science expertise can provide value in understanding social, cultural, and political contexts and increasing or restoring trust from local communities. 91
The importance of health-care workers in managing information ecosystems should also be recognised. A 2005 survey suggested that individuals in the USA generally trust their health-care providers more than other health information channels, 92 so improving the relationship between health-care workers and their patients is likely to be an effective way of connecting the general public and health institutions. In particular, health-care providers can be important in correcting institutional and interpersonal racism in health care. 93 Prioritising health-communication training might also help health-care workers to effectively communicate accurate scientific evidence and increase trust by improving relationships with local populations, thus strengthening a crucial component of the information ecosystem.
Our Historical Review has limitations. First, historical references tend to focus on specific events or themes rather than a comprehensive discussion of the disease through time. Second, as is typical of narrative reviews, expert opinion was used to choose references, so there is potential for incomplete or biased interpretations of disease history. Third, no standardised terminology necessitated broad search terms and potentially resulted in an overstated emphasis on misinformation in our narrative review. Finally, our analysis primarily consisted of secondary historical sources, which might have hindered our ability to characterise stigma, discrimination, and other social realities that support the spread of misinformation.
Our narrative review shows the nuance and complexity that lead to the generation, proliferation, and reception of misinformation in the information ecosystem. Understanding how these dynamics have existed throughout history highlights the importance of anticipating information challenges before the outset of emerging public health crises. The historical perspective emphasises the importance of holistic approaches that strengthen information ecosystems before misinformation occurs, rather than solely addressing misinformation as in current approaches to the management of infodemics. As such, prioritising improving relationships between medical or public health authorities and local populations will probably strengthen the overall information ecosystem. We are hopeful that this historical perspective will help to develop ways to manage current infodemics and highlight the importance of a preventive approach for future infodemics and other information-related challenges.
