Introduction The rise of self-media has fundamentally transformed the information landscape, democratizing content creation while simultaneously creating unprecedented channels for the spread of disinformation 1, 2 . This proliferation of false information erodes public trust, distorts social discourse, and poses significant threats to social stability and national security 3 , making its effective governance a critical and urgent challenge.
Addressing this challenge requires an understanding of disinformation propagation as a complex, strategic interaction involving multiple autonomous actors. Evolutionary Game Theory (EGT) has emerged as a powerful analytical framework for modeling such dynamic, multi-agent systems where strategies evolve through imitation, learning, and adaptation 4, 5 . Unlike classical game theory, EGT does not assume hyper-rationality but rather explores how population-level behaviors emerge and stabilize over time, providing key insights into phenomena like cooperation and norm evolution in structured groups 6, 7 .
A substantial body of research has applied EGT to dissect online public opinion dynamics. Studies have built tripartite models involving regulators, media, and the public to reveal the interactive mechanisms of disinformation spread 8 . Analyses using Jacobian matrices have shown that increasing regulator penalties can curb false information, though a balance with regulatory costs is necessary 9 . Other work has integrated network effects and herd behavior into game-theoretic models to explain multidimensional opinion dissemination 10 , with findings corroborated in specific contexts like university settings 11 . Research has further elucidated the strategic interdependence between regulator regulation and media reporting tendencies 12 , modeled the acceleration of negative emotions via self-media “traffic” incentives 13 , and demonstrated the vicious cycles triggered by regulator inaction 14 . Collectively, these studies provide a strong foundation by quantifying stakeholder interests and simulating equilibrium outcomes 15, 16, 17, 18, 19 .
However, two significant gaps persist in this literature. First, a predominant assumption of perfect or bounded rationality in standard EGT often overlooks the descriptive insights of behavioral economics. Real-world decision-makers in high-uncertainty environments like disinformation governance are not purely rational optimizers. Their choices are heavily influenced by cognitive biases, particularly those related to risk perception under uncertainty. Prospect Theory (PT), the seminal descriptive model of decision-making under risk 24 , posits that individuals evaluate outcomes relative to a reference point, exhibit loss aversion (losses loom larger than equivalent gains), and demonstrate diminishing sensitivity to changes in value. While PT has been successfully integrated with EGT in other domains such as environmental governance and supply chain management 20, 21 , its application to model the risk-sensitive, psychologically-grounded strategic choices in the tripartite disinformation governance ecosystem remains notably underexplored.
Second, while the roles of regulators and content creators (self-media) are frequently examined, the pivotal and legally-increasingly-responsible role of social media platforms is often under-theorized or treated passively 22, 23 . Platforms are not neutral conduits but active governance actors with their own strategic incentives concerning content moderation, user engagement, and regulatory compliance. A comprehensive model must explicitly account for their strategic choice between active governance and passive response.
To bridge these gaps, this paper constructs a tripartite evolutionary game model that integrates the precepts of Prospect Theory. We focus on the strategic interactions between the regulator, social media platforms, and self-media operators. Our core contribution is to ground the agents’ payoff calculations in PT’s value function, thereby capturing how subjective perceptions of gains and losses, driven by loss aversion and diminishing sensitivity, fundamentally alter the evolutionary dynamics of the system compared to traditional utility-based models. Through stability analysis and systematic numerical simulation, we investigate how key parameters—including the intensity of regulator rewards and penalties, initial willingness to cooperate, and the agents’ psychological parameters (loss aversion λ, sensitivity coefficients α and β)—influence the trajectory and efficiency of the system’s convergence toward an ideal collaborative governance state. Notably, the strategic interaction between regulators and platforms is driven by legal obligations, reputational incentives, and content moderation costs, all of which have been systematically incorporated into the parameter settings of our proposed model.
The remainder of this paper is structured as follows: Sect. "Problem description and model construction" details the problem description and the construction of the PT-based tripartite evolutionary game model. Section "Stability analysis of equilibrium point" provides a stability analysis of the system’s equilibrium points. Section "Numerical simulation analysis" presents numerical simulations and sensitivity analyses. Section "Research conclusions" concludes with findings, policy implications, and directions for future research.
Problem description and model construction Problem description As the direct producer and disseminator of information, the behavior of self-media operators plays a decisive role in the formation and development of online public opinion. The regulator plays the role of regulator and standardizer in the field of public opinion, formulates perfect laws and regulations, strengthens the regulation of self-media behavior, standardizes the behavior of self-media accounts, and maintains the clarity of cyberspace. Social platforms, as content publishing and dissemination channels, need to establish a sound content review mechanism to deal with inaccurate information in a timely manner. Social platforms should also cooperate with the regulator to combat online rumors, improve the reach of disinformation, and take measures such as banning and closing down offending self-media accounts.
Among them, the regulator and social platforms need to strengthen the supervision of self-media operators, while self-media should consciously abide by laws and regulations. Through the mutual constraint game of the three main parties, a stable and healthy public opinion environment is gradually formed. It can not only enhance the clarity of the network environment, but also effectively prevent and reduce the phenomenon of rumor spreading and protect the legitimate rights and interests of citizens. Accordingly, this paper constructs a tripartite evolutionary game model of “regulator-social platform-self-media operators”.

Prospect theory, a cornerstone of behavioral economics developed by Kahneman and Tversky 24 , provides a psychologically realistic framework for decision-making under uncertainty. It posits that individuals do not evaluate outcomes in absolute terms but rather relative to a reference point (which is set to 0 in this model). The theory is characterized by two key features: loss aversion and diminishing sensitivity.
Loss Aversion: Individuals feel the pain of a loss more acutely than the pleasure of an equivalent gain. This is captured by the loss aversion coefficient in the value function. A λ greater than 1 indicates that a unit of loss has a greater impact on perceived value than a unit of gain.
Diminishing Sensitivity: Individuals are increasingly less sensitive to changes in value as they move further from the reference point. This is modeled by the risk preference coefficients and . As α and β decrease, the decision-maker becomes more risk-averse in gains and more risk-seeking in losses.
In the context of disinformation governance, all three actors—the regulator, social platforms, and self-media operators—operate in a highly uncertain environment. Their strategic choices (e.g., to publish, verify, or regulate) are influenced not by perfect cost-benefit calculations but by subjective perceptions of potential gains and losses. Therefore, prospect theory offers a more behaviorally grounded foundation for our evolutionary game model than traditional expected utility theory.
Stability analysis of equilibrium point It should be emphasized that although the replicator dynamics equations are formally separable, they are not mutually independent or decoupled. The expected payoffs for each player are calculated from the three-party joint payoff matrix, which fully embodies the strategic interactions and interdependence among the regulator, self-media, and platforms. In particular, the replicator equation F(z) explicitly contains x and y, which directly reflects the mutual influence of strategy choices between players. Although the replicator dynamics equations appear separable in form, the expected payoffs are derived from the jointly determined payoff matrix of the three-player game. Thus, the evolutionary forces implicitly reflect strategic interdependence among the regulator, self-media, and platforms. Numerical simulation analysis Parameter assignment basis Numerical simulations are conducted in MATLAB 2026a using the ode45 (Dormand-Prince) solver, with relative and absolute tolerances set to default values to balance computational efficiency and accuracy. The simulation duration is T = 5, and initial probabilities are set as [0,1]. The average runtime per simulation trial is less than 0.2 s. To ensure rigor, sensitivity analysis is performed over reasonable parameter ranges, and Monte Carlo simulations are adopted to test the robustness of evolutionary trajectories. The assignment of parameter values in this model is guided by the need to satisfy the stability conditions of the ideal equilibrium point (as derived in Sect. "Stability analysis of equilibrium point") and to reflect logically consistent relative relationships commonly found in the literature on evolutionary games in public opinion and governance 9, 10, 11, 14 . It is crucial to note that the primary objective of the subsequent numerical simulation is to provide a theoretical proof-of-concept and to observe the dynamic trends and sensitivity of the evolutionary system, rather than to produce precise quantitative predictions. Therefore, while the absolute values are assumed, we meticulously ensure the logical hierarchy among them.
Given the assumption of bounded rationality, the strategy selection logic of the regulator, self-media, and social platform remains driven by self-interest maximization. In the evolutionary game framework, dynamic changes in initial parameters will lead to heterogeneous evolutionary paths across participants. Accordingly, this study employs MATLAB simulation to dynamically track the developmental process of public opinion events. The evolutionary game system, characterized by the replication dynamics equations (F(x), F(y), F(z)), is numerically solved using the aforementioned ode45 solver. The simulation code is available upon request to ensure research reproducibility.

Analysis of stable points in system evolution The stabilization point (1,1,1) implies that the regulator, self-media, and social platforms each fulfill their respective roles. Specifically, the regulator actively releases authoritative information, quickly grasping the facts of public opinion events through official channels. This prevents public panic and misjudgment caused by rumors, enhances public trust, and maintains social stability. Self-media, leveraging their unique advantages, avoid the deterioration of events by strictly verifying acquired information and carefully screening news, rather than blindly following trends. Social platforms, through intelligent algorithms and artificial auditing technologies, implement active governance: promptly removing harmful content, recommending high-quality information, maintaining platform order, ensuring information authenticity and effectiveness, improving user experience, and fostering a positive online atmosphere. In this state, the three parties work in synergy to promote the sound development of public opinion and build a clear, orderly cyberspace.

Sensitivity analysis of incentives and disincentives In this section, other parameters are fixed; only the reward–penalty intensity β is adjusted to observe its effect on the system’s convergence speed and evolutionary direction. As the gain coefficient in prospect theory, β reflects the risk appetite and behavioral choices of individuals or organizations when facing potential rewards. The evolutionary results are shown in the following figures, and the key changes are as follows:
As shown in Fig. 3(a), when the intensity of rewards and penalties is low (β = 0.28), regulators and self-media quickly converge toward an active governance strategy, while social platforms remain in a stable state of passive governance. This result reveals the asymmetry in perceived gains and losses among the three parties in a low-reward-and-punishment environment: for regulators, the public trust gains from active governance and compliance pressures act as rigid constraints; therefore, even with limited reward and punishment intensity, the evolutionary path of their active strategy remains unaffected; For self-media creators, the compliance benefits and risk-aversion expectations associated with active governance are sufficient to offset the short-term temptation of traffic; however, for social platforms, the operational costs and potential user churn risks of active governance are amplified by the loss aversion effect of prospect theory, while positive incentives under low reward-punishment levels are insufficient. Consequently, passive governance becomes their dominant strategy. This comparison directly confirms that the governance inertia of platforms is essentially a rational risk-averse behavior in a low-incentive environment, rather than a simple lack of responsibility.
As shown in Fig. 3(b), when the intensity of rewards and penalties is increased to a moderate level (β = 0.68), the proactive governance strategies of regulators and self-media creators remain stable, while social platforms begin to transition from passive to proactive governance; however, their convergence rate lags significantly behind that of the other two parties. This lag reflects the platform’s “threshold effect in benefit perception”: although moderate-intensity rewards and penalties have altered the platform’s expected returns from active governance, as a “rule enforcer,” the platform’s decision-making adjustments are subject to path dependence and delays in cost transmission, causing its response to policy signals to be significantly slower than that of regulators (rule-makers) and self-media (content producers). At the same time, the asynchronous convergence of the three parties’ strategies during this phase also indicates that single-dimensional adjustments to rewards and penalties struggle to achieve a coordinated response among the three governance actors, and that moderate-intensity policy interventions suffer from significant “platform transmission losses.”
As shown in Fig. 3(c), when the intensity of rewards and penalties is further increased to a higher level (β = 0.88), the three parties achieve a rapid, synchronized equilibrium of proactive governance. At this point, high-intensity rewards and penalties completely transform the agents perceived utility functions: for regulators, the credibility gains from proactive governance are reinforced; for self-media creators, the long-term traffic and reputational benefits of compliant content outweigh short-term speculative gains; and for platforms, the reputational value, user trust, and compliance benefits of proactive governance fully offset operational costs and potential losses. This result validates a core assumption of prospect theory: when perceived gains exceed the loss aversion threshold, actors’ strategic choices shift from risk aversion to risk-seeking, enabling the emergence of an evolutionary equilibrium in tripartite collaborative governance. Simultaneously, this provides a key insight for public opinion governance: the intensity of rewards and penalties must reach a “perceived tipping point” to overcome platforms’ governance inertia and path dependence, thereby achieving synchronized responses among the three governing actors.

Sensitivity analysis of risk appetite coefficients In this section, other parameters are fixed; only the loss aversion coefficient λ is adjusted to observe the shift of the platform regulation strategy. In prospect theory, the loss aversion coefficient λ is a crucial parameter that quantifies an individual’s aversion to losses in relation to their preference for gains. A higher λ indicates stronger loss aversion: the discomfort from losses is felt more intensely than the pleasure from equivalent gains.
As the loss aversion coefficient increases from 1.45 to 1.75, social platforms tend to adopt passive regulation strategies. At these values, platforms are more sensitive to the immediate costs of active regulation (such as resource investment and legal risks) than to the long-term consequences of inaction. As λ rises within this low range, their aversion to incurring these direct costs grows, leading them to avoid the perceived loss of spending resources, and thus converge rapidly to the passive regulation equilibrium.
When λ increases further from 2.25 to 2.75, platforms instead shift toward proactive regulation. With stronger loss aversion, platforms become far more sensitive to the severe, irreversible losses that can result from inaction—such as legal penalties, reputational damage, and user churn. To mitigate these large perceived losses, they are increasingly willing to bear the costs of active regulation (e.g., enhancing content review and transparency). Moreover, as λ increases from 2.25 to 2.75, the speed of convergence to the proactive strategy equilibrium accelerates, reflecting the amplified impact of loss aversion on risk-averse decision-making.
As shown in Fig. 4, the numerical simulations clearly illustrate the non-monotonic sensitivity of the platform’s regulation strategy to changes in the loss aversion coefficient, revealing a critical threshold effect: below a certain λ threshold, loss aversion drives passive regulation, while above this threshold, the same psychological mechanism promotes proactive regulation.

Multi-dimensional visualization and robustness analysis of evolutionary equilibrium As a complement to the time-evolution trajectories presented earlier, this section addresses the limitation of one-dimensional visualizations by introducing phase portraits and parameter-space analysis, aiming to reveal the system’s global evolutionary properties and parameter-driven mechanisms. This section complements the numerical simulation from three perspectives: interactive relationship analysis between agents, parameter threshold effect analysis, and global stability analysis via phase diagrams, to enhance the completeness and persuasiveness of the simulation results.
First, a two-dimensional phase portrait is constructed to depict the co-evolution of strategies between the regulator and self-media. Based on fixed baseline parameters, multiple sets of differentiated initial strategy combinations are set to observe the co-evolution trend of their strategy variables. As shown in Fig. 5(a), regardless of initial willingness levels, the regulator’s supervision strategy and the self-media’s reporting strategy exhibit coordinated co-evolution, with all trajectories converging stably to the equilibrium point (1,1). This indicates that in the public opinion regulation game system, the government’s proactive supervision can positively guide self-media to operate in compliance, and the compliant transformation of self-media can in turn reduce government regulation costs. There is a stable positive interaction between the two subjects, and the system shows no multiple equilibrium branches or periodic fluctuations, demonstrating strong resilience of the cooperative equilibrium.
As shown in Fig. 5(b), by continuously adjusting the value of the loss aversion coefficient, the characteristics of the platform’s steady-state strategy under different parameter conditions are statistically analyzed. The analysis reveals a clear threshold effect: at low levels of λ, the platform is less sensitive to potential reputational losses and regulatory penalties, leading to a passive governance strategy. Once λ exceeds the critical threshold, the platform’s motivation to avoid losses increases significantly, triggering a strategic shift from passive to active governance. This threshold behavior aligns directly with the core logic of loss aversion in prospect theory, effectively explaining the differentiated choices of platform governance behavior under different risk preferences, and providing quantitative support for the previous multi-scenario sensitivity analysis.
Finally, a three-dimensional evolutionary phase portrait is constructed to demonstrate the global evolutionary law of the three-party game involving the regulator, self-media, and social platform. Evolutionary trajectories are generated in batches across the full range of initial strategy values, covering different combinations of participation willingness of the three parties. As shown in Fig. 5(c), the simulation results show that, regardless of whether the initial strategy tends to be proactive or passive, all three-dimensional evolutionary trajectories continuously converge to the unique equilibrium point (1,1,1), with no other local stable points, limit cycles, or divergent paths observed. This confirms the global asymptotic stability of the ideal cooperative equilibrium, where proactive governance, verified reporting, and platform control are the inevitable outcome of the system’s long-term evolution. The steady-state conclusion of the model is universal and not affected by initial state fluctuations. Parameter space analysis is introduced, focusing on the loss aversion coefficient, to explore the threshold effect of key prospect theory parameters on the platform’s governance equilibrium strategy.
In summary, the phase diagram analysis verifies the global stability of the tripartite game system and the synergy mechanism among agents, while the parameter space analysis reveals the threshold-driven role of key prospect theory variables. The multi-dimensional visualization results are mutually consistent with the time-evolution trajectories, forming a complete numerical simulation system that further supports the rationality of the theoretical derivation and game analysis conclusions of this paper.

Research conclusions This paper constructs a three-party evolutionary game model of “regulator–social platform–self-media” based on prospect theory, and explores the strategic decision-making mechanism of multiple agents in the network environmental regulation process. Numerical simulations implemented in MATLAB verify that game participants dynamically adjust their behavioral strategies under the combined influence of multiple factors and eventually reach evolutionary equilibrium under specific parameter constraints. The main conclusions are as follows:
- The evolutionary system converges to different equilibrium states under distinct parameter settings. With the improvement of the regulatory mechanism, the system asymptotically evolves toward the ideal cooperative equilibrium. In this optimal steady state, the corresponding behavioral strategies are proactive regulation by social platforms, active information release by the regulator, and verified content reporting by self-media.

- The initial willingness of game agents significantly affects system convergence speed. Higher initial participation enthusiasm accelerates the system’s evolution toward the ideal equilibrium. Incentive and punishment mechanisms effectively promote proactive regulatory strategies of platforms and self-media; stronger reward–punishment intensity further shortens the convergence time to the optimal evolutionary state.

- Within the prospect theory framework, both the gain coefficient and loss aversion coefficient in the perceived value function exert decisive influences on system evolution. The system stabilizes at the ideal equilibrium when the gain coefficient reaches a sufficient level and the loss aversion coefficient exceeds the threshold around 2.25.


From a methodological perspective, this study still has several limitations, which also suggest directions for future research. First, the current model simplifies the interactive relationship between the regulator and social platforms and does not fully incorporate the constraints of existing regulatory policies and reward–punishment mechanisms. In addition, model parameters are set based on theoretical reasoning and logical deduction rather than empirical calibration. Future research can adopt real-world public opinion events and measurable practical data to calibrate parameters and optimize model settings. Second, subsequent studies can further decompose the interactive mechanism between regulators and platforms by explicitly incorporating legal obligations, reputational incentives, and dynamic content moderation costs. Third, self-media can be divided into heterogeneous types (e.g., opportunistic information spreaders, opinion influencers, and automated bots) to better reflect the characteristics of real online information ecosystems. Fourth, the model can be expanded into a four-party evolutionary game by introducing the public and professional fact-checking institutions, so as to incorporate public supervision mechanisms and reconstruct a more complete regulatory ecosystem.
Future research will extend the current well-mixed evolutionary game to complex networks. According to the evolutionary dynamics framework for structured populations 7 , real social systems rely on heterogeneous topological interactions rather than uniform full mixing. Introducing scale-free or small-world networks can capture local learning and neighbor-based imitation, which will fundamentally alter stability conditions, convergence speed, and equilibrium reachability. Recent advances have shown that temporal activation effects and bursty switching dynamics can significantly influence networked evolutionary games 25, 26 , while self-interaction learning can further change strategy selection and cooperation thresholds 27 . As highlighted by networked evolutionary game and opinion dynamics studies 7, 19 , topological structure directly changes strategy diffusion paths and equilibrium domains. This extension will replace the full-mixing assumption with realistic interactions and improve the model’s applicability to real social-media ecosystems. The policy implications derived from this study are not generic suggestions; they reflect behaviorally informed guidance rooted in loss aversion, subjective perceived value, and coordinated governance dynamics among regulators, platforms, and self-media.
