Headnote ABSTRACT
This article presents the theoretical and conceptual foundations of the interdisciplinary didactic concept of Fictional Science (FiSci), which is positioned at the interface between media and language didactics. Based on reference concepts such as epistemic vigilance, inoculation, lateral reading, and media literacy in education, FiSci aims to promote secondary school students' skills in dealing with fake news. While course 1 of FiSci (Activating the Fake News Radar) sensitises students to false information, course 2 (Verification of Information) teaches strategies for identifying false information that are also used by professional fact-checkers, and course 3 (Refutation) focuses on refuting false information.
Keywords: verification of information, refutation, fake news, media literacy in language education.
INTRODUCTION
Digital transformation has given 21% century students more opportunities to access information quickly and easily than any generation before. However, especially on social media, the spread of misinformation and disinformation! is increasing rapidly (Surjatmodjo etal, 2024) and assessing the credibility of information and texts as sources of information poses a major challenge for (language) learners (e.g., Breakstone et al, 2022; Ireton & Posetti, 2018; McGrew et al., 2018). Оп the one hand, the widely discussed dangers of fake news? (European Commission, 2018; Huber et al., 2022; Treton € Posetti, 2018) have led to numerous calls for language classes to address this issue in order to enable learners to adopt a generally critical and evaluative attitude towards media content, enabling learners to recognise false information and refute it. On the other hand, language teachers themselves are becoming increasingly aware of the changing paradigms for language learning and teaching: Even though students spend considerable amount of time in digital environments (MPS, 2024), they often lack basic digital skills (Fraillon, 2024). These new challenges are already reflected in the formulation of competences in individual language curricula? (Раза & Ruokamo, 2015). However, there 15 still a desideratum in terms of theoretically well-founded and field-tested concepts and teaching materials that integrate concepts of Media Literacy Education (MLE) into language teaching and thus build a bridge between media and language education. Against this backdrop, the present paper addresses the following theoretical-conceptual research question: How must a pedagogical framework-and the instructional materials derived from it-be designed to meaningfully integrate concepts of language education and media literacy education in order to sensitise learners for false information, prepare them to identify and refute fake news?
The project Fictional Science - Promoting Literacy in Dealing Critically with Fake News aims to close this research gap. It develops a new pedagogical concept, Fictional Science (FiSci), for the German language classroom for secondary school students, which combines concepts from language and media education in order to support students in recognising and refuting fake news.
This paper presents the conceptual, theoretical, and empirical foundations of Fictional Science." The Section "Media literacy and language education" describes the challenges for language teaching in the digital age and outlines how these challenges have been addressed in the FiSci concept by combining MLE and language education. The next Section ("Theoretical framework for the pedagogical concept Fictional Science") discusses the theoretical foundations of FiSci from an interdisciplinary perspective and with a particular focus on the design of the texts used in FiSci. Based on this conceptual framework of FiSci, the three courses of the FiSci concept are then presented with reference to the specific theoretical implications of each course (Section "The pedagogical concept Fictional Science"). Finally, the paper highlights the pedagogical, social, and political dimensions of dealing with false information in an educational context.
Media literacy and language education
The following section focuses on contextualising the Fictional Science concept within the broader framework of media literacy (ML). It has always been one of the central tasks of language teaching to enable leamers to engage with media content not only as a means of acquiring knowledge, but also as an object of analysis and interpretation. As a result of increasing digitalisation, a new and challenging task has been added: teaching a critical approach to information in different media. In order to achieve these goals in language teaching, FiSci draws on existing concepts such as MLE, because, among other important concepts", MLE is, in particular, attributed an important role in combating fake news.
MLE is commonly described as an umbrella term for teaching learners to access, (critically) evaluate, and produce information in different media formats (Schofield et al., 2023, p. 125). Two key definitions in particular characterise a prevailing understanding of media literacy: First, in 1992 MLE was defined at the National Leadership Conference on Media Literacy as "the ability of a citizen to access, analyse, and produce information for specific outcomes" (Aufderheide, 1993, р. 6). The second important characteristic of MLE derives from the National Association of Media Literacy Education's (NAMLE) Six Core Principles for MLE. These principles were first developed in 2007 and have been continuously revised and expanded by NAMLE, most recently in 2023. Whereas the definition by the Leadership Conference on Media Literacy focuses more on individual empowerment and specific competences of learners, the Core Principles by NAMLE address broader educational practices and the process of engagement rather than specific outcomes (NAMLE, 2023).
Although these two reference documents offer a definition of MLE, many authors state that there is an inconsistent operationalisation of the concept of MLE (Ashley et al., 2012).5 As a consequence, Potter (2022, р. 40) called it a requirement for scholarly publications in the field of MLE that a clear and precise definition of media literacy should be provided. For the purposes of this publication, we therefore draw on the frequent definition of MLE used by Aufderheide (1993), which characterises MLE based on the key skills access, analyse, evaluate, and communicate. Each of these processes 15 addressed in a specific phase of the pedagogical concept Fictional Science (see Section "The pedagogical concept Fictional Science").
THEORETICAL FRAMEWORK FOR THE PEDAGOGICAL CONCEPT FICTIONAL SCIENCE
The prevalence of fake news is a critical challenge for educators. To meet the challenges of identifying and refuting false information, an interdisciplinary approach to language teaching is needed to prepare students to access, analyse, evaluate and refute information in a digital world. In this context, findings from psychology and text processing science on how we process and store (false) information need to be taken into account (Section "Cognitive biases and the correction of false information"). The interdisciplinary theory of epistemic vigilance from the field of social and cognitive sciences and philosophy, discussed in the Section "Epistemic vigilance", provides a theoretical basis for the changing attitudes and skills learners need to read (digital) texts. The theory of inoculation (Section "Inoculation™), originally developed in the field of social psychology, lays the theoretical foundation for strategies that can help to immunise learners against means of manipulation.
Cognitive biases and the correction of false information
Psychological and cognitive insights into how (false) information is processed form a key foundation for the FiSci concept introduced in this paper: In psychology, cognitive biases, defined as errors in thinking when people process information, explain why false information is initially given credence and why it is relatively difficult to 'correct' false information once it has been memorised. Based on the illusory truth effect, for example, the mere repetition of information can increase its credibility (Polage, 2012), that is statements that have been perceived repeatedly are usually considered more correct because people are more familiar with the information (Ecker et al., 2014, pp. 19- 22). This psychological effect is also known in text processing theory as the frequency effect: It refers to the fact that information is more firmly anchored in memory when it is received several times (Van Dijk & Kintsch, 1983; Kintsch, 2004).
Other psychological effects focus more on the fact that it is difficult to correct incorrect information once it has been acquired. The continued influence effect shows that when we receive conflicting information, our memory cannot simply replace the (incorrect) old information with the new information, but that the old information remains anchored in our memory. For example, if a narrative is coherent, it is difficult to replace only parts of it, because the 'gap' created by the correction must be plausibly filled by the new (correct) information (Ecker et al., 2014, р. 15; Lewandowsky et al, 2012; Sanderson & Ecker, 2020, pp. 463-465). Hence, Lewandowsky et al. (2012) outline that "once a coherent story has been formed, it is highly resistant to change: |... | each element is supported by the fit of other elements, and any alteration [...] may be made implausible by the downstream inconsistencies it would cause" (р. 112).
The backfire effect (Edwards & Smith, 1996) shows that contradictory evidence can even reinforce our previous beliefs, while confirmation bias (Nickerson, 1998) refers to the strong tendency to select information that corresponds to our pre-existing beliefs and to disregard evidence or facts that challenge our opinions. (Richter & Maier, 2017).
Revision models from text processing theory also confirm that once information has been acquired, regardless of whether it is reliable or incorrect, it is relatively robust to correction (= cognitive resistance). For example, Kendeou and O'Brien's (2014) Knowledge Revision Components framework (KReC model) describes that "previously acquired but no longer correct information still has the potential to compete with the revised knowledge for activation that can impact comprehension and memory processes [...]" (р. 357). This cognitive robustness of false information and misconceptions against correction has also been confirmed empirically (Shtulman & Valcarcel, 2012).
The previously described effects from psychology and text processing science show that once false information has become entrenched, it is difficult to correct (Kendeou & O'Brien, 2014, p. 357). In addition, these empirical findings also suggest that entrenched false information and an already adopted point of view can also subsequently hinder learning new (correct) information and acquiring skills to detect false information. This is because, when we learn new information (e.g. when reading a text), we build a mental representation of what we are reading (Van den Broek et al., 2005). These mental representations are built on existing prior knowledge, as we look for anchor points in our own realm of experience when reading new information. Thus, previously stored (false) knowledge and an already adopted viewpoint on a topic may hinder learning new (correct) information and acquiring strategies to detect incorrect information.
The FiSci concept therefore does not work with 'real' false information or fake news, but with false information constructed for educational purposes. This ensures that learners do not yet have any preconceived opinions or a 'coherent story' in their heads and can focus on acquiring skills in dealing with false information. It also prevents the spread of (harmful) false information, which can reinforce beliefs in learners that are difficult to correct later (frequency effect).
The texts that are used in the teaching materials have been written and designed in accordance with the following principles: Firstly, the fabricated 'fake news' texts, designed for educational purposes do not fuel prejudices or stereotypes.The topics have been chosen to ensure that the texts do not expose any social groups, do not cause harm or do not convey any hostility. Secondly, the topics of the texts were chosen in such a way that it can be assumed that the learners have no preconceived opinions about them.
The idea of using fictional texts to train strategies for dealing with fake news in science also gives rise to the name of the pedagogical concept Fictional Science, since the teaching materials use false (fictional) information created for educational purposes in texts. All texts address topics within the context of science.
Epistemic vigilance
"To have been literate yesterday, in a world defined primarily by relatively static book technologies, does not ensure that one is fully literate today" (Leu et al, 2013, p. 1150). These remarks emphasise that the requirements for reading texts have changed. In particular, the new challenges posed by digitalisation relate to the increased cognitive demands caused by the joint execution of many processes: These challenges range from navigating to critically evaluating texts (Rouet & Potocki, 2018), with a particular challenge being the unclear status of information whose factuality is not certain (Cook et al, 2017).
To meet these challenges, a new approach to reading is required: Epistemic vigilance describes a general attitude that readers (should) adopt towards texts. It refers to an awareness on the part of readers that they may be exposed to the risk of being "misinformed by others" when encountering information (Sperber et al., 2010, p. 359). This critical-evaluative reading attitude is therefore also described as a protective mechanism not to blindly accept everything as trustworthy, but rather to critically evaluate statements or their sources in cases of doubt.
Referring to the cognitive mechanisms that individuals use to assess the reliability of information and the credibility of its sources, Sperber et al. (2010) introduce two aspects that play a central role, namely "who to believe" and "what to believe" (p. 369). This concept therefore encompasses skills such as assessing the trustworthiness of an information provider, examining the coherence of a message, and crosschecking claims with prior knowledge or other sources (Sperber et al., 2010). These verification processes are also central to course 2 of FiSci, where students learn to verify information (see Section "Course 2: Verification (Evaluation)").
In the Fictional Science concept, this new reading attitude 1s initially prompted by an experience of deception. First, learners are presented with a seemingly trustworthy text. Without knowing that the teaching materials focus on fake news, they are asked to work through the text by answering comprehension questions. At first glance, the false information in the text appears credible as it is presented in a way that is consistent with conventional online media formats: The language used in the text corresponds to the register or style usually found in newspapers or magazines. Additionally, places of institutional research, such as universities, are mentioned. However, these references remain vague (e.g. neither the universities nor the researchers are mentioned by name). Only in the second step does the teacher reveal that the text contains false information and encourages the learners to reflect on it." Thus, the deceptive experience aims to foster a critical-evaluative reading attitude, as learners realise that they could easily be deceived.
Inoculation
In terms of its aims, inoculation theory can be related to MLE because it overlaps conceptually with Potter's (2004) cognitive processing model of media literacy: both frameworks address the development of resistance to (harmful) external influences, such as false information. 19
Inoculation theory (McGuire, 1964) was originally conceptualised within the fields of social psychology and communication studies. The aim was to promote cognitive skills that foster awareness of strategies and means of manipulation (Kozyreva et al., 2020, p. 103).
Cognitive inoculation is based on the biological analogy of vaccine immunisation [...]. It posits that the process of injecting a weakened dose of a virus to activate antibody production [...] can similarly be applied to the context of information processing. In other words, by warning and exposing people to severely weakened doses of attitudinal challenges, cognitive resistance or "mental antibodies" are generated against future persuasion [...] (Basol et al., 2020, p. 1).
Empirically, the effectiveness of inoculation in developing a critical attitude towards potentially false information has already been demonstrated in several studies (e.g. Cook et al., 2017; Roozenbeek & Van der Linden, 2019). The theoretical explanation from a cognitive perspective for the efficiency of inoculation is that it shifts attention from a heuristic surface level to a deeper level of analysis, allowing people to detect patterns of deception (Cook et al., 2017, р. 15).
The Fictional Science concept uses logic-based inoculation, where learners are made aware of the deceptive strategies used. This form of inoculation has the advantage of being generalisable: Research results (Cook et al, 2017, р. 15) suggest that, once an awareness of deceptive strategies has been raised for one topic, this can be applied to other topics where the same deceptive strategies are used. A popular framework for classifying and systematising these deceptive strategies is the FLICC taxonomy (Cook, 2020). The acronym stands for the five main techniques of science denial: fake expert, logical fallacies, impossible expectations, cherry picking and conspiracy theories (Hoofnagle & Hoofnagle, 2007). Building on this taxonomy, FiSci focuses on the following deceptive strategies, among others, selected for their clarity, frequency in real-world examples, and relevance in classroom contexts:
* Fake Experts: Presenting an unqualified person or institution as a source of credible information
* Quote Mining"": Taking quotes out of context
* Slothful Induction: Ignoring empirical evidence.
FiSci extends the concept of logic-based inoculation: In order to prepare learners for these deceptive strategies in 'real (digital) life', they first experience a 'basic inoculation' against these means of manipulation in a protected environment, that is in texts that are fictional and created for pedagogical purposes. As part of the instructional approach, the deceptive strategies in these texts are made transparent to the learners by means of parallel argumentation. These parallel arguments "use the same logical structure as the target argument, but instantiate it in such a way that the conclusion is obviously false" (Cook et al., 2018, pp. 4-5). Parallel argumentation can help learners to identify deceptive strategies in false information because the deceptive strategy is placed in a similar - but more exaggerated or absurd, sometimes humorous - situation than the initial situation of the actual false information. The exaggerated presentation of deceptive strategies in the context of parallel arguments highlights the flaw in the argumentation or logical structure of false information and thus enables learners to recognise them themselves in comparable situations. For example, in one of FiSci's teaching materials, Professor James Ulysses Forrington is a (fictional) illustrative example of the deceptive strategy fake expert, whereby an unqualified person or institution is presented as a source of credible information. Since Professor Forrington and his qualifications are presented in a humorous and quirky way, the deceptive strategy of the fake expert is highlighted.
First, students read a newspaper article by Professor Forrington in which he shares his opinion on a chemical process. At first glance, the reader is inclined to trust his perspective on chemical processes, simply because he is a professor. However, the metatexts about him reveal that he is actually a professor of Old English literature, not chemistry, and that his scant knowledge of agrochemistry consists only of introductory lectures in two semesters of chemistry. Consequently, no matter what topics outside his specialist field he writes about with his non-existent expertise: He always quotes Old English chroniclers, even if they are rather inappropriate in the context of the respective subject area (such as chemistry).
This specific example illustrates the humorous elements used in the FiSci teaching modules. In this case, the deceptive strategy of the fake expert is highlighted particularly vividly in exaggerated, sometimes absurd situations. In terms of instruction, this has the advantage that abstract concepts, such as the question of what constitutes (academic) expertise, can be explained in a concrete and humorous way using analogue situations (Juthe, 2009, p. 159).
Based on the results of Cook et al. (2017) and Van der Linden & Roozenbeck (2020), it can be assumed that strategies for identifying deception acquired in a fictional setting constructed for pedagogical purposes lead to learners being able to use these strategies when dealing with real false information on other topics as well. Therefore, once learners have been made aware of deceptive strategies such as the fake expert in this fictional setting, they are likely to apply their knowledge in the real world too.
THE PEDAGOGICAL CONCEPT FICTIONAL SCIENCE
The concept of Fictional Science was developed based on the theoretical principles described in the Section "Theoretical framework for the pedagogical concept Fictional Science". It consists of three sub courses. Course 1 sensitises learners to common characteristics of false information and aims to raise awareness of the fact that texts may contain false information. Course 2 focuses on verifying information and course 3 on refuting false information.
Based on the pedagogical concept of Fictional Science, two task arrangements were developed for the 8th school level (secondary level 1) and two task arrangements for the 10th and 11th school levels (secondary level 2) for the German language classroom.12 These four task arrangements have the same theoretical basis and structure and pursue the same learning objectives.13 In what follows, the course progression of these task arrangements will be presented in detail, focusing on their implementation and the design of the texts used.
Course 1: Activating the fake news radar (Access & analyse)
In course 1, learners are sensitised to the fact that false information can be identified by certain characteristics, such as features on the linguistic surface (e.g. emotional language) or by internal contradictions or means of manipulation in texts (logical fallacies). The individual features are based on empirical studies focusing on the language of fake news (Zhou et al., 2020; Giachanou et al., 2022; Grieve & Woodfield, 2023), theoretical models of argumentative fallacies (Walton, 2010), and findings from text processing theory (Rapp & Kendeou, 2009). These textual characteristics of false information are presented in the teaching material as red flags for learners to critically examine the information in the text.
From a pedagogical perspective, learners identify these characteristics of false information through discovery learning (Stiller, 2019). The tasks encourage learners to 'discover' principles and concepts inductively, for example when learners themselves develop and reflect on frequent (content-related or linguistic) features of false information by analysing texts in the teaching materials. This enables the independent induction of rules by analysing concrete examples, which promotes sustainable knowledge transfer and enhances the practical application of the acquired knowledge (Bruner, 1961).
The texts in course 1 are designed to facilitate the identification of linguistic features of fake news, internal contradictions, and logical fallacies by the learner. In order to 'discover' these features, learners are required to undertake a thorough examination of the texts, as these features are not immediately apparent. For example, one of these texts focuses on the logical fallacy of post-hoc. This fallacy, short for post hoc ergo propter hoc ('after this, therefore because of this'), occurs when someone wrongly assumes a causal relationship between two successive events. This text reports that Finnish researchers have discovered that one should speak Finnish to plants: they grow particularly well when you do so. The text also states that this was tested on one plant. At first glance, this may seem a plausible for learners - and the fallacy may not be noticeable. On closer inspection, however, it becomes clear that just because the growth of the plants happened to coincide with talking to the plants, talking to the plants cannot be the cause. As a learning objective, learners become aware of the fact that there may be a correlation, but that correlation provides no information about the cause.
Course 2: Verification (Evaluation)
Once learners are aware of the fact that there may be textual clues indicating false information in the text, these textual clues are the starting point for verifying information. To teach learners strategies for identifying false information in a profitable way, FiSci is inspired by how professional fact-checkers proceed when verifying information. In a study on Civic Online Reasoning by Wineburg and McGrew (2019), the approach of professional fact checkers was analysed in terms of the strategies they use to verify information. The successful approach of fact checkers when evaluating information in texts is called 'lateral reading' (Wineburg et al., 2022). When reading laterally, readers read with multiple open tabs/sources at the same time, not restricting themselves to solely one text, but verifying or checking information across multiple texts. Lateral reading essentially uses two verification strategies:
* Sourcing: At its core, sourcing refers to the identification, cognitive representation, evaluation and use of metadata, meaning data either about the source of specific pieces of information or about entire texts (Bråten et al., 2018). Other definitions of sourcing indicate that metadata for evaluating the credibility of information can represent very different types of information: Goldman and Scardamalia (2013), for example, define sourcing as "processes of identifying and representing metadata, including the author(s) or spokesperson(s) who created the information resource, their purpose in creating it, the intended audience, where and when it first appeared (i.e., where it was published - YouTube, NY Times, etc.), as well as subsequent publication" (p. 259).
* Corroboration: It is commonly defined as the intertextual comparison of documents or texts for the purpose of determining agreement or disagreement by specifically searching for confirming or contradictory information and perspectives (List & Alexander, 2019). This means that readers consult external sources of information with the intention of verifying intertextual coherence and thus obtaining an indication of the plausibility of statements (Philipp, 2021). Corroboration thus initially consists of integration processes that involve linking, combining, and organising information with the aim of creating coherence when dealing with different pieces of content information that can be related to each other through inference formation (Brante & Strømsø, 2018, p. 777).
The definitions of the two strategies make it clear that sourcing in particular presents one of key strategies in MLE, especially when it comes to evaluating the credibility of texts (Scheibe & Rogow, 2012).14 In addition, the concept of epistemic vigilance by Sperber et al. (2010) (Section "Epistemic vigilance") also refers to these two key strategies in terms of the verification questions 'who to believe' and 'what to believe'.
When looking at professional fact-checkers and how they evaluate information in texts, it becomes clear that the two strategies are often carried out together or simultaneously to support each other. For example, in the Stanford History Education Group's Civic Online Reasoning Curriculum (McGrew et al., 2018), both strategies are used to determine the factuality of information. Central to this are three key questions that learners are asked to answer when investigating untrustworthy information:
1. Who is behind this information? This question implies the execution of sourcing processes, as it involves the identification and use of metadata to determine who is behind the information.
2. What is the evidence? This question aims at an intertextual comparison of information (corroboration), as one has to look for evidence in other (reliable) sources.
3. What do other sources say? This question also focuses on carrying out corroboration processes, as the aim is to look specifically for confirming or contradictory information and perspectives in order to verify their factuality.
Several studies (Brodsky et al, 2021; McGrew, 2020; Wineburg et al, 2022) have shown that an instructional focus on sourcing and corroboration to promote lateral reading increases learners" ability to assess the credibility of information in a digital context. However, common models that aim to describe sourcing and corroboration processes (e.g. Braasch Bráten, 2017; Stadtler € Bromme, 2013) also indicate that learners do not automatically use these processes. Hence, Philipp (2020) concludes that "an obvious deficit with regard to the use of sourcing is that learners [...] do not use it" (p. 271). One reason for this is that most learners may find easier but less efficient ways to verify information, such as ignoring conflicting information, drawing on prior knowledge, or making plausibility checks based on internal text features (e.g., Braasch & Braten, 2017; Stadtler & Bromme, 2013).
In order to guide the mandatory reference to metadata or content-related facts for the verification of information, Fictional Science uses the Textwelten (text worlds) concept (Schicker & Ehrenmiiller, 2023), which refers to a fictional text corpus created for educational purposes. As these text worlds are self-contained fictional universes, it is not possible to verify information through a plausibility check based on internal aspects of a single text or prior knowledge. The factuality of information within the text worlds can only be assessed by using and analysing relevant metadata (sourcing) or content information in other documents (corroboration). Hence, the fictional setting of the text worlds and the corresponding tasks in the teaching material force learners to carry out sourcing or corroboration strategies to determine the factuality of information. Fictional text worlds serve as a 'vehicle' for initiating sourcing and corroboration skills, as learners can focus their cognitive resources on learning the strategies for verifying information rather than on the content itself.
An important guiding principle for the concept of text worlds in FiSci is epistemic trust, which combines expertise, integrity, and benevolence and is used to evaluate information within the fictional world (Bromme et al., 2018). Epistemic trust refers to the learner's willingness to consider information as trustworthy based on the perceived credibility and intentions of its source. Following the concept of epistemic trust, information in the text worlds should be classified as 'trustworthy' if
* it comes from people who have sufficient knowledge or skills in the relevant field (expertise),
* the knowledge someone relies on is considered to have been produced in accordance with the rules (integrity), and
* good intentions are assumed (benevolence).
Based on these principles, the text worlds in FiSci are constructed in a similar way: The starting point of each text world is a fictional (newsworthy) event that is reported in a (fictional) newspaper. One text world, for example, describes a new fertiliser made from a substance extracted from the mucus of the (fictional) Worchester snail. The newspaper reports that the fertiliser has been developed by researchers at Gerald Ford University in the USA and has already been tested in Idaho City. The (fictional) city was once important for its rich lignite deposits but suffered a decline during the steel industry crisis of the 1980s. As a result, the city is now plagued by high unemployment, and the area around the city resembles a lunar landscape due to opencast mining and the severe environmental damage caused by lignite mining. However, the Worchester snail only releases its secretion when its upper surface is touched. As this process requires many "snail-strokers" (a humorous element in the text), the new fertiliser would not only revitalise the landscape but would also create many jobs.
In the next step, learners are asked to read two more newspaper articles that contradict the first report. As a result, learners are presented with conflicting information about the effectiveness of the fertiliser made from snail slime. One of the other texts, a commentary in a local newspaper by the aforementioned Professor James Ulysses Forrington, strongly doubts the effectiveness of the new fertiliser.
In accordance with the text world concept, learners can only find out which texts contain reliable and which texts false information by using metadata or content information from other documents and discovery learning. The additional information to verify the first texts can be found in so-called wikipoints, which are articles in a fictional wiki created for the text worlds. They contain information about the media in which the texts were published and about the fictional characters who appear as authors or who are mentioned as experts in the texts. The (fictional) biographies and the information about the newspapers they publish in are also intentionally designed to be quirky and humorous to make deceptive strategies more transparent to learners (see Section "Inoculation™).
Similar to a detective game, learners have to find out who is spreading false information, whether intentionally or not, who is credible, and which information is correct. By analysing relevant metadata (sourcing) or content information of other documents in the wikipoints (corroboration), learners are able to identify which fictional characters in the text worlds have real expertise and which are fake and distinguish quality newspapers with high journalistic standards from those that do not meet these criteria. Hereby, learners also uncover deceptive strategies such as the fake expert or quote mining by comparing the source texts with further information.
Once learners have acquired strategies to verify information through sourcing and corroboration and are aware of deceptive strategies like the fake expert or quote mining, they can further apply their knowledge to real world discourses.
Course 3: Refutation (Communication)
There are two main reasons why learners need skills that enable them not only to identify false information but also to refute it. First, a well-informed public is a basic requirement for maintaining a functioning society and democracy (Lewandowsky et al., 2017). Second, refuting false information can also be justified by learning theory: Studies (Kendeou et al., 2013; Mason et al., 2020) show that causal justifications (and explanations) can correct false information and prevent interference from false information.
In a writing task in course 3, learners are asked to respond to a post by a former classmate containing false information from the text world of course 2, allowing them to practise correction strategies in a controlled setting that avoids the cognitive resistance described in Section "Cognitive biases and the correction of false information". They should write a direct message to their former classmate and refute the misinformation, which allows them to actively participate in the discourse of the text worlds of course 2. To support their writing process, learners are provided with a linguistic scaffold for formulating refutation texts based on Swales" (1990) moves concept: It identifies the different rhetorical steps or moves that serve specific communicative purposes and how these actions can be adequately expressed in a linguistically transparent way for learners. The moves in refutation texts, which can be defined as recurring patterns, such as indicating that a reader has been deceived or questioning a person's trustworthiness, are based on an empirical analysis of refutation texts (Schicker et al., 2024).
CONCLUSION
Adressing fake news in schools is not merely an educational challenge but a societal imperative. The Fictional Science concept exemplifies how MLE can be meaningfully implemented in the context of language education by combining interdisciplinary theoretical foundations with concrete evidence-based educational strategies for identifying and refuting false information.' It aims to help educators to empower students to navigate the complexities of the digital age with discernment and resilience.
Although the materials were designed for use in German language classrooms, the underlying principles and the conceptual framework of the Fictional Science concept offer potential for broader application. With suitable linguistic and cultural adaptations, the concept may be transferable to other educational contexts. We encourage future research to explore its relevance and impact across diverse learner groups and instructional settings.! Readers are encouraged to explore and examine the applicability of this concept in other educational settings and with other learners from other linguistic backgrounds.
This concept should ensure that future generations are equipped not only to recognise false information but also to contribute to an informed and democratic society. Further research is ongoing, with the objective of evaluating the teaching materials and effects of an intervention grounded in the FiSci concept's educational framework."
