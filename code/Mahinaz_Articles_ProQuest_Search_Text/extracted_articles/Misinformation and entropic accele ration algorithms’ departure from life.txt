The visual and literary outcomes from the combined capabilities of algorithms and large data sets have become so lifelike that they leave us both enthralled and anxious. The consequences of this development are enormously pervasive. We may ask what is intelligent about these machines, but it is perhaps more revealing to ask what life is when machines have become so lifelike. What is Life? is the title of a small and influential book by the Nobel laureate Austrian physicist of quantum theory Erwin Schrödinger (Schrödinger, 1944). Written in 1944 and anticipating the major breakthroughs of twentieth-century biology such as the discovery of the DNA structure, this book was particularly illuminating perhaps due to the fact that it was a physicist rather than a biologist who explains the broadest outlines of life. For this reason, it is instructive to re-visit it to understand the extraordinary lifelike characteristics of data- and algorithm-enabled artificial intelligence, and their potentials and challenges in the field of architectural design and construction.
The design and construction of architecture, still an area of human activities deployed with large armies of professionals producing immense amounts of instructions for material assemblies, are traditionally conceived and practiced as a series of interconnected but separate operations: firms that specialize in design, technical consultation, management, materials manufacture, and construction. The sheer amount of information each firm must process set some kind of limits to the scale of operation, which forms the basis of this multi-firm organization; they have in time developed optimal and maximal sizes beyond which it would be far too complex for humans to manage effectively. The largest design-construction conglomerates essentially replicate this condition by having separate inhouse units as they seek to minimize coordination and costs, some of the highest in life.
The rapid increase of information processing power today has meant that algorithms are able to not only assist humans to manage information, but also to approximate that human judgement to generate optimized original information from data sets. Technologies change rapidly, but some kind of milestone has been reached recently to enable a “machine autonomy” in the production of information. The Generative Adversarial Network (GAN) has already gained sufficient capacity to generate “unsupervised” original contents from data sets – uncannily demonstrated by the endless faces of non-existent humans – through a competitive game between the Generator and the Discriminator. The Generator creates artificial images from data sets, the Discriminator detects “fake” images and eliminate them, and the Generator responds to the elimination with an improved set of images. The end results are so realistic that the difference between the real and the fake is undetectable. A key mechanism in the production of convincing yet unreal information is the combination of batch normalization and randomized variation (noise), Adaptive Instance Normalization (AdaIN), which relies on modifying a parameter source and a content source through weightings. The result is endless variety of realistic but unreal information. Taking this to the world of design and construction, this is what awaits us in the world of design: processing power to generate original designs integrated with robotic fabrication and construction. What is tested out at the Fab-Union in Shanghai will, in time, deeply disrupt and transform the two-thousand-year tradition of design and construction of architecture defined by biological limits.
The two key developments in algorithms – normalization and randomized variation – come very close to two characteristics of biological life. Schrödinger vividly demonstrates that normalization of large amounts of atoms in biological cells ensure their stability in assemblage and continuity; this is what led to stable organisms. Without normalization, each single atom is far too easily affected by heat to become a building block of life. Before this crucial stage of microbial evolution on earth, cell assemblages were dissipative. Dissipative cells cannot organize themselves in a meaningful way; certainly, they cannot reproduce predictably. Randomized variation in evolutionary terms involves swapping sections of genetic materials in prokaryotic cells (cells without nuclei) starting from about four billion years ago, and in the exchange of half of the genetic information in eukaryotic cells (cells with nuclei) starting from 3,500 million years ago. Lynn Margulis and Dorian Sagan, in Microcosmos, Four Billion Years of Microbial Evolution (1986) describes this crucial transition from prokaryotic cells to eukaryotic cells in microbial evolution, which gave us life as it is current today (Margulis & Sagan, 1986).
While the shortest length of time in biological change takes a generation, algorithms can accomplish these in short periods of time. This changes the game significantly. In architecture, it can allow an integration of design and construction as a single process just like it is in biology, and it can do it with very high rates of evolutionary change. Architecture has used biology as a metaphor (morphology, typology, biomimicry); but now, architecture has the potential to become a form of life, an autonomous design intelligence and construction.
Yet, despite these lifelike characteristics in architecture and in other fields, algorithms depart from life in two significant aspects in relation to misinformation and entropic acceleration. It is worth noting that truthfulness has been fundamental to all human societies. Lies have been told worldwide, in general, only with the penalty of shame and punishment through social norms and legislation. Perhaps because humans are the only species that can willfully lie, there seems at the same time to be an innate repulsion against misinformation.
What Schrödinger has shown, in biology, is that exact and truthful copying of information carried out by nuclei of cells is a crucial innovation in evolution that led to the stability of organisms. Could this innateness against misinformation in humans a consequence of a structural protection against dissipation of life? If we think with Richard Dawkins to consider “memes” as an approximation in human culture to genes in biology, cultural memes carry a sufficient degree of truthfulness in copies that are recognizable and precise (Dawkins, 1976). This can be a melody or a decorative pattern that does not change over thousands of years. If the memes become dissipative, i.e., if misinformation takes over memes, cultural continuity becomes impossible. In the development of early science, intellectuals in late seventeenth- and early eighteenth-century Europe conceived a “moral sense” next to the newly emerging ideas of empiricist “corporeal senses” as the base of human knowledge. The “moral sense school”, among the leading proponents Shaftesbury and Kant, wanted to keep morality at the center of knowledge production in the context of a much more mechanical understanding of knowledge production promoted by Locke, Hobbes, and the scientists (Shaftesbury, 2000). The moral sense of Shaftesbury and Kant remains with us in modified formulations today as philosophical expressions of a common intellectual condition in cultures throughout the world. No human society can function with prevalent misinformation, just like no cells can be stable with dissipation. As algorithms take over more and more human work and creation, and as humans becoming more reliant on artificial intelligence, it seems that misinformation can increase at a massive scale; this is not only in the form of linguistic information, but also in the form of much more subtle non-verbal forms. Life’s success in overcoming dissipation through truthfulness thousands of million years ago is now challenged by a very new form of misinformation.
The ubiquity and speed of the power to process large amounts of information perhaps prompted Bernard Stiegler to reflect on a second foundational feature of life: its unstoppable progression towards entropy. Schrödinger reminds us that life does not follow the laws of physics but those of thermodynamics. The second law of thermodynamics is that of entropy: all life must end in a state of eventlessness like what it is on Mars and the Moon, and all life is a temporary reversal of that process, a negative entropy, a negentropy. Life is order out of disorder; this order depends on the absorption of matter from the environment, and produces heat as entropic waste. Life is a temporary suspension of the second law of thermodynamics even though it may appear to be permanent and indestructible. The world of life is one of long carbon molecules capable of carrying information, of the central archive of information in the nucleus, of energy production by mitochondria, of networking of organelles of different genetic origins, and of metabolism and endosymbiosis. Yet, this hive of endless and complex activities masks a reality that entropy is never far away. Bernard Stiegler wanted us to understand in his The Neganthropocene (2018) that algorithms produce uncannily lifelike assemblies, yet ironically, they rush life towards its entropic end on a massive scale (Stiegler, 2018).
The scaling up of entropy by artificial intelligence is not so much a feature of technical invention but one of capital accumulation. Stiegler reminds us that humans and technics have always been in each other’s coproduction; historically, no technological advancement has been reversed by human conservatism. The lifelike features of our new technologies may be enthralling, and they may have crossed a threshold of capabilities to generate original content, but they are part of human-technics evolution that is as old as language. Powered by artificial intelligence, capital accumulation is usurping biological metabolism in profound ways. Older forms of economy, partly due to biological limits in the number of transactions, were much closely mapped onto material limits: perhaps the pegging of currencies with precious metals – the supplies of which are limited – such as copper, silver, and gold was an indication of inherent limitedness of economic scales. Intellectual systems such as Daoism intuitively frames this human-material metabolism through a metaphysical mutual identification. This is no longer the case today; in the past few centuries, capital accumulation is only pegged with an abstract idea: that of endless growth. Lovelock’s and Margulis’s Gaia and the contemporary “degrowth economics” may descend from the Daoist instinct of human-material metabolism, but they drown in the heat and noise of capital accumulation today (Lovelock, 1979). The mainstream idea of endless growth is very much invested in the new capability of algorithms in approximating biology with high speed and without limitations of entropy. Ironically, in becoming so lifelike, artificial intelligence accelerates entropy.
If we are to reverse this rapid acceleration towards entropy, algorithms must be like biology in all its aspects: not just normalization and randomized variation, but also truthfulness and negentropy. The challenge to bring a version of this to reality will be enormous. One entry point to this intractable problem may be through the existing practice of introducing noise in the process of normalization. But noise as it is applied currently is far too simplistic. In the world of cellular reproduction, noise is exquisitely constructed through the exchange of the information systems in the nucleus – its exact mixing of two halves of parental DNAs – and in the complex process of matching parental pair that we refer to as love or match making. All of these are grounded the central qualities of truthfulness of information as a moral sense, and by the display of resource assembly within an understanding of inherent limits – intellectual and material, manners and estates – that would result in the eventual agreement and exchange of DNA information in reproduction. All of these take place against the background of mortality, the entropic process that is at the heart of life. In architecture, truthfulness of form can be understood as being grounded in a human-style metabolism – styling of buildings being biological markers to enable strategic DNA exchange – that facilitates life rather than undermine it through autonomous algorithmic operations; negentropy of material use can be understood as extraction of energy from the environment that has inherent limits and comes with proportionate entropic waste.
Our current economic framework would unfortunately consider this level of noise in algorithms, even if technically translatable to weightings, as unacceptable inefficiencies in economic production. The nature of today’s challenge is therefore not just technological, but techno-economic. If the twentieth century struggled with compensating for the alienation of the human condition resulting from global capitalist exploitation, the twentieth century will surely wrestle with the impending end of life resulting from its techno-economy. Our techno-economy, our algorithms, must return to life as a foundational framework. There are multiple pathways such as degrowth and a re-valuation of all planetary materials with the understanding of life and metabolism. It will take both the strength of the moral sense and the power of data processing to accomplish this new twenty-first century techno-economy based on life. In accomplishing this, biology, rather than mathematics, must be placed as the metaphysics of our time.
