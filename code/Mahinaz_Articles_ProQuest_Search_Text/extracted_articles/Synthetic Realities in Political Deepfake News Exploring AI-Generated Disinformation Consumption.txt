1. Introduction The advancement of artificial intelligence (AI) is transforming the creation, distribution, and consumption of information in the contemporary digital media environment. Although AI enables more efficient and accessible content production, it also facilitates the rapid creation and dissemination of hyper-realistic synthetic media that can be difficult for audiences to distinguish from authentic information. AI-generated content therefore adds a new layer of complexity to existing disinformation problems by enabling persuasive and sophisticated falsehoods to be produced at unprecedented speed, scale, and relatively low cost across multiple domains (Nanabala et al., 2024). Compared with conventional forms of human-generated disinformation, which may contain detectable inconsistencies or errors, AI-generated content can reproduce realistic visual, auditory, and linguistic cues, making its fabricated nature less apparent. This concern is particularly salient in political communication. For example, deepfake videos depicting prominent political leaders making fabricated statements have circulated widely across social media platforms, further blurring the boundary between truth and falsehood in an already polarized political environment (Barari et al., 2021).
The term deepfake, derived from “deep learning” and “fake,” refers to AI-powered synthetic media that manipulate or generate images, audio, or video to convincingly depict individuals, statements, or events that did not occur (Westerlund, 2019). When incorporated into political news, deepfakes create substantial challenges for audiences attempting to assess the authenticity and accuracy of online information. Exposure to such content may increase uncertainty, reduce confidence in one’s ability to identify reliable information, and undermine trust in online political communication more generally (Vaccari & Chadwick, 2020). News organizations and researchers have also emphasized the potential of deepfakes to intensify social conflict, manipulate public opinion, and disrupt public discourse (Di Sotto & Viviani, 2022; J. Zhou et al., 2023). If left unchallenged, political deepfakes may have broader consequences for journalism, informed citizenship, institutional trust, and democratic decision-making (Bennett & Livingston, 2018).
Despite growing scholarly attention to political deepfakes, important gaps remain in the literature. Existing research has largely focused on the technological detection of manipulated media, the perceived credibility of individual deepfake messages, or the general effects of exposure on trust, uncertainty, and political attitudes (Lu, 2026; Moyo et al., 2026; Shin & Lee, 2022). These studies provide valuable insights, but they often examine predictors, processing mechanisms, and outcomes separately. Consequently, the literature lacks an integrated, audience-centered explanation of how susceptibility to political deepfake news develops across the full consumption process. In particular, insufficient attention has been paid to how relatively stable individual characteristics and potentially modifiable factors jointly influence the ways audiences process and evaluate political deepfake news, as well as how these evaluations subsequently generate cognitive, attitudinal, and behavioral consequences. This fragmentation limits the field’s ability to explain not only whether political deepfakes are persuasive, but also why certain individuals are more susceptible than others, through what mechanisms susceptibility emerges, and what consequences follow.
To address this gap, the present study develops a comprehensive theoretical model of political deepfake news consumption in social media newsfeeds. Rather than treating susceptibility as a single perceptual judgment or examining isolated variables in parallel, the proposed model conceptualizes deepfake news consumption as a multistage process that connects antecedent conditions, information processing and evaluation, and subsequent outcomes. Specifically, the framework incorporates three categories of antecedents: (1) ideological antecedent, represented by political orientation, captures enduring political identities that shape how individuals interpret political information; (2) cognitive processing antecedent, represented by confirmation bias, reflects individuals’ tendency to process information that reinforces prior beliefs selectively; (3) information competency antecedent, represented by news media literacy, captures the knowledge and skills necessary to evaluate news content and recognize deceptive media critically. In addition, news issue familiarity serves as a contextual factor that influences individuals’ ability to evaluate political deepfake news by providing prior knowledge about the topic under consideration. These antecedent factors jointly shape the central information processing and evaluation stage, conceptualized as political deepfake news assessment. Rather than assuming that exposure to political deepfake news directly produces downstream effects, the framework posits that individuals actively evaluate the authenticity, plausibility, and credibility of deepfake political news through the interaction of their ideological predispositions, cognitive biases, media literacy, and issue familiarity. This evaluative process serves as the key mechanism linking antecedent characteristics to subsequent cognitive, attitudinal, and behavioral outcomes.
The framework further proposes that political deepfake news assessment influences three categories of consequences: (1) it shapes cognitive outcomes, reflected in accuracy-motivated news skepticism, which encourages individuals to critically evaluate news based on evidence and factual accuracy, and identity-motivated news skepticism, whereby skepticism toward news is guided primarily by partisan or identity-based considerations; (2) it influences attitudinal outcomes, represented by deepfake news credibility, or the extent to which individuals perceive political deepfake news as believable and trustworthy; (3) the framework examines behavioral outcomes, represented by news engagement intention, including individuals’ willingness to discuss, comment on, or share political deepfake news on social media platforms. By integrating ideological, cognitive, and informational antecedents with a central information evaluation process and multiple downstream consequences, the proposed framework provides a comprehensive explanation of how individuals assess political deepfake news and how these assessments shape subsequent beliefs, attitudes, and behaviors. In doing so, the study complements existing research by shifting attention from the narrow question of whether audiences can detect or believe a particular deepfake to the broader question of how political deepfake susceptibility is formed and how its effects unfold. The study’s originality therefore lies in integrating previously fragmented explanatory and outcome factors into a unified audience-centered framework, thereby offering a stronger theoretical basis for understanding political deepfake news consumption and identifying potential points of intervention. 2. Literature Reviews 2.1. AI-Generated Dis-/Misinformation and Deepfake News Disinformation is “intentional falsehoods spread as news stories or simulated documentary formats to advance political goals” (Bennett & Livingston, 2018, p. 124). AI-generated disinformation refers to false or misleading information created using AI technologies, including manipulated, hyper-realistic images, audio, or videos, sometimes making authentic political figures appear to express inauthentic viewpoints. This type of disinformation can be more sophisticated and convincing than traditional forms, deliberately intended to fabricate, decontextualize, or manipulate to provide a purposefully inaccurate, detrimental, or misleading picture of reality (Bennett & Livingston, 2018). Kreps et al. (2022) compared AI-generated and human-written news articles and found that participants could not distinguish between them, perceiving them as equally or more credible than human-written articles. Buchanan et al. (2021) demonstrated the large language model’s (LLM) ability to create moderate-to-high-quality misinformation messages with little human involvement. They also discovered that AI-generated misinformation could customize language for specific groups and deploy stereotypes and racist language on certain topics. Moreover, J. Zhou et al. (2023) found significant linguistic differences between human- and AI-generated misinformation, with AI-generated misinformation often enhancing details, expressing uncertainty, drawing conclusions, and adopting a personal tone.
Specific to deepfake-based fake news, prior research examined the effects of different disinformation modalities, revealing that political deepfakes are less credible than factual news on the same topic. However, deepfakes are more persuasive than textual disinformation (Hameleers et al., 2022). Barari et al. (2021) further showed that nearly 50% of a representative sample have been convinced by political misinformation through deepfakes or videos synthesized through deep learning. However, this effect is less than the misinformation conveyed by existing news formats, such as textual headlines or audio recordings. A recent study examined perceived source vividness as a psychological mechanism underlying the effects of deepfake news on news credibility and engagement intention. The research findings revealed that participants who watched deepfake news reported higher source vividness than those who saw fake information presented in other modalities, such as text-only or text–photo, suggesting that deepfake news appears more credible and more easily deceives news readers (Lee & Shin, 2022).
2.2. Uncertainty The primary societal concern surrounding political deepfake news is not only its potential to deceive individuals but also its capacity to generate epistemic uncertainty about the authenticity of political information. While deception occurs when individuals incorrectly accept false information as true, uncertainty arises when individuals become unsure whether the information they encounter is genuine or fabricated. These two outcomes are conceptually distinct. Ambivalence occurs when individuals hold conflicting evaluations of competing alternatives, such that additional information intensifies rather than resolves their internal conflict (Alvarez & Brehm, 1997). In contrast, uncertainty arises when individuals perceive that they lack sufficient or reliable information to make an informed judgment and can therefore be reduced through the acquisition of additional credible information (Alvarez & Brehm, 1997).
This distinction is particularly important in the context of political deepfake news. As Downs (1957) argued, political uncertainty emerges because citizens face costs in acquiring accurate information. The emergence of sophisticated AI-generated deepfakes further increases these costs by making authentic and manipulated political content increasingly difficult to distinguish. Rather than simply misleading audiences, political deepfakes can erode confidence in their ability to evaluate the authenticity of political information, thereby increasing uncertainty about what is true and false. Consistent with this argument, Vaccari and Chadwick (2020) found that individuals exposed to political deepfakes are more likely to experience uncertainty than to be directly deceived, and that this heightened uncertainty subsequently undermines trust in news encountered on social media.
Within the proposed theoretical framework, uncertainty provides an important rationale for examining political deepfake news assessment. As individuals encounter increasingly realistic AI-generated political content, they must actively evaluate its authenticity, credibility, and plausibility to reduce the uncertainty generated by deepfakes. Political deepfake news assessment therefore represents the central information processing and evaluation mechanism through which individuals attempt to distinguish authentic from manipulated political information. Understanding the antecedent factors that shape this evaluative process is essential for explaining why some individuals are better able than others to navigate uncertainty and resist the influence of political deepfake news.
2.3. Antecedents to Disinformation Exposure 2.3.1. Political Orientation Political orientation, often called political ideology or political affiliation, describes an individual’s beliefs, values, and preferences concerning political matters. It usually determines an individual’s stance on various issues, policy preferences, and which political parties or candidates they may support (Piurko et al., 2011). Kandler et al. (2012) conceptualized political orientation as a psychological trait that leads left-wing people to prefer change and equality, while right-wing people value stability and hierarchy. Political orientation is therefore commonly framed within a left–right spectrum, with the single-dimension model of political competition being the “left–right” or “liberal–conservative” dimension (Piurko et al., 2011). The main factors that differentiate political liberalism from conservatism are its preference for equality and social change. In contrast, conservatism is marked by acceptance of inequality and a preference for tradition (Thorisdottir et al., 2007). Furthermore, ideological conservatives are more sensitive to threats, which may help explain differences between liberals and conservatives on policy issues such as immigration (Aarøe et al., 2017) and gun control (Celinska, 2007). However, contemporary political orientations are increasingly multidimensional. Political divisions now extend beyond traditional left–right distinctions to include issues such as cultural identity, globalization, environmental sustainability, technological governance, and democratic values. Although these cross-cutting dimensions have increased the complexity of political identities, the liberal–conservative continuum remains one of the most widely used indicators of ideological predispositions in political communication research because it captures broad differences in political values and information processing (Piurko et al., 2011).
Political orientation also serves as an important ideological antecedent of political information processing. Research on motivated reasoning suggests that individuals evaluate political information through the lens of their pre-existing ideological beliefs, tending to perceive ideologically congruent information as more credible while scrutinizing incongruent information (Lockett, 2024). Consequently, political orientation influences not only political preferences but also judgments about the authenticity and credibility of political messages. This perspective is particularly relevant to political deepfake news. Sütterlin et al. (2023) identified a moderate but significant relationship between conservatism and deepfake recognition, revealing that more socially (but not economically) conservative political views are associated with greater susceptibility to believing deepfake videos are authentic. The link between trait conservatism and the ability to recognize deepfakes is mediated by the extent to which individuals agree with the political message conveyed in the video (Sütterlin et al., 2023). Accordingly, this study conceptualizes political orientation as an ideological antecedent of political deepfake news assessment. While acknowledging the multidimensional nature of contemporary political identities, the present study employs the liberal–conservative continuum as a theoretically grounded indicator of ideological predispositions that shape how individuals evaluate the authenticity and credibility of political deepfake news on social media.
2.3.2. Confirmation Bias Confirmation bias is an individual’s tendency to favor information supporting their beliefs or decisions, which can influence subsequent choices (Oswald & Grosjean, 2004). The bias can be seen as a subset of epistemic egocentrism, where people prioritize their knowledge or viewpoints over others’ (Royzman et al., 2003). Confirmation bias appears in several manifestations. One essential manifestation is selective exposure and attention, in which people gravitate toward and focus more on information that aligns with their existing beliefs (Frimer et al., 2017). Second, confirmation bias can manifest as biased weighting and selective utilization of information—individuals tend to process choice-consistent information more efficiently and give more weight to, or even misinterpret information that aligns with their preferences and attitudes when making decisions (Talluri et al., 2018). Third, confirmation bias can lead to biased perceptions, as information that aligns with one’s beliefs is typically seen as more credible and influential (Ahluwalia, 2000). In news consumption, studies on confirmation bias typically use indicators from news sources to measure selective exposure, meaning that people pay attention to messages they choose to focus on rather than spending equal time on all available messages (Westerwick et al., 2017). Y. Zhou and Shen (2022) further suggested that (1) the source of attitude-consistent messages was perceived as more credible (expertise and trustworthy) than their attitude-inconsistent counterpart; (2) attitude-consistent information was perceived as more factual than counter-attitudinal messages; (3) individuals reported a tremendous amount of empathy with attitude-consistent messages than attitude-inconsistent ones; and (4) attitude-consistent messages were perceived as more effective than counter-attitudinal ones. Confirmation bias is a crucial factor in propagating disinformation and polarizing attitudes, according to data-driven models trained on large social media datasets, which further contribute to its spread (Del Vicario et al., 2016). Confirmation bias tend to amplify public opinion polarization, make individuals more set in their beliefs, push their views to extreme ends, and intensify societal disagreements. Hence, this investigation postulates that people’s confirmation bias is related to their evaluation of political deepfake news.
2.3.3. News Literacy News literacy is “knowledge of the personal and social processes by which news is produced, distributed, and consumed, and skills that allow users some control over these processes” (Tully et al., 2022, p. 593). Potter (2010) examined various elements influencing comprehensive media literacy, including the essential knowledge required before engaging with media and the methods people use to interpret information once encountered. Five foundational “knowledge structures”—understanding of media content, media industries, media effects, the real world, and oneself—work in tandem with an individual’s mix of motivations, requirements, and cognitive skills to guide how they process information and derive meaning from it (Potter, 2010). Maksl et al. (2015) further developed a news literacy scale with three subscales. “Media Knowledge Structures” comprise knowledge items related to news media industries, content, and effects. “Need for Cognition” and “Media Locus of Control” indicate the motivations and need components of Potter’s model. Furthermore, Jones-Jang et al. (2021) found that those with greater news literacy are more likely to be skeptical of social media content but are less likely to critically assess its completeness, truthfulness, and potential biases in political deepfake news. The research also discovered that information literacy, rather than media literacy and news literacy, can significantly increase the likelihood of identifying fake news stories (Jones-Jang et al., 2021). Thus, this investigation posits that people’s news literacy knowledge and skills are associated with their ability to detect political deepfake news.
2.4. The Effects of Disinformation Exposure 2.4.1. Social Media Skepticism Media skepticism is the “subjective feeling of alienation and mistrust toward mainstream news media” (Tsfati & Cappella, 2003, p. 67) and the degree to which citizens doubt and distrust the information journalists and newsmakers present (Cozzens & Contractor, 1987). In the social media context, Li (2023) proposed distinguishing media skepticism into “accuracy-motivated skepticism” and “identity-motivated skepticism” regarding social media misinformation. Specifically, accuracy-driven skepticism is a form of skepticism rooted in concerns about how misinformation circulating on social media platforms affects the accuracy of one’s understanding and decision-making. Their skepticism and vigilance stem from acknowledging that personal biases, including their own, may sometimes make them susceptible to misinformation (Li, 2023). In contrast, identity-driven skepticism is a form of skepticism driven by apprehensions about how social media misinformation affects the perceived advantages or disadvantages of one’s political group and its opponents. This skepticism arises from the belief that social media often hosts biased content favoring one group over another and fails to accurately represent the individual’s perspectives (Li, 2023). Moreover, Ahmed (2023) found that exposure to and concerns about deepfakes are positively associated with social media news skepticism, whereas those who frequently rely on social media as a news source are less skeptical.
2.4.2. Perceived News Credibility News credibility is closely linked to trust in media, denoting how believable a message, source, or platform is (Metzger & Flanagin, 2013). Trust in news media can be divided into three categories: trust in the information presented (message credibility), trust in the provider of the information (source credibility), and trust in the medium conveying the message (media credibility) (Fisher, 2016; Metzger et al., 2003). Research on online credibility draws upon the literature on source credibility and interpersonal communication, incorporating elements from traditional media credibility studies, including the credibility, trustworthiness, and impartiality of websites and online information. Viviani and Pasi (2017) proposed that social media introduce a more complex scenario and suggested a framework with five layers, including “intermediary platform credibility” (e.g., Facebook) and “intermediary sender credibility.” Moreover, Shin and Lee (2022) suggested that educating people about the low cost of producing deepfakes can effectively reduce both the perceived credibility of deepfake news and the intention to share it, particularly among individuals whose issue attitudes are congruent with the message.
2.4.3. Engagement Intention This study delves into the behavioral effects of engaging with political deepfake news, measured in actual behavior or behavioral intention following exposure. Chadwick and Stanyer (2022) argued for including deceit as a crucial concept in the study of misinformation, disinformation, and misperceptions, as it can help connect actors’ intentions with the information they produce and the subsequent attitudinal or behavioral outcomes. Additionally, Shin and Lee (2022) examined behavioral intentions following deepfake news consumption, including intentions to share, like, or comment on online news embedded with deepfake images or videos. Their findings showed that people whose preexisting attitudes toward controversial issues are congruent with the advocated position in the news are more likely to believe and be willing to share deepfake news as much as real video news (Shin & Lee, 2022).
2.5. News Issue Familiarity as a Mediator News issue familiarity may mediate political deepfake news detection and evaluation, defined as individuals’ level of understanding or awareness about a particular news topic or current event (Han et al., 2009). It measures people’s familiarity with the details, context, and implications of a specific news story or issue. High news issue familiarity suggests that people are well-informed and knowledgeable about the topic, while low familiarity indicates a need for more understanding and awareness. When familiar with the topic, people tend to focus primarily on semantic features of the information (e.g., factual accuracy). In contrast, individuals unfamiliar with the subject will likely pay attention to surface features (e.g., text length) (Lucassen et al., 2013). Prior studies posited that people’s familiarity with news events and issues may influence the types of knowledge activated by news frames and how those frames are processed (Han et al., 2009). Familiarity with a news issue is associated with attitudes and opinions related to that issue, and overall familiarity with a topic may increase complex cognitions about news coverage of that area and enhance audiences’ evaluative ability (Han et al., 2009). Therefore, this study argues that news issue familiarity may mediate the relationships between antecedents and consequences of political deepfake news exposure. The following research questions are proposed: RQ1: What level of uncertainty do audiences have when exposed to political deepfake news through social media?
RQ2: How do audiences’ political orientation relate to their skepticism, perceived credibility, and engagement intention of political deepfake news on social media?
RQ3: How do audiences’ confirmation bias relate to their skepticism, perceived credibility, and engagement intention of political deepfake news on social media?
RQ4: How do audiences’ news literacy levels relate to their skepticism, perceived credibility, and engagement intention of political deepfake news on social media?
RQ5: How do audiences’ familiarity with news issues mediate the relationships between their political orientation, confirmation bias, and news literacy as well as skepticism, perceived credibility, and engagement intention of political deepfake news on social media?

3. Methods 3.1. Study Design and Procedure By employing online experiments, the study consisted of three steps: (1) an initial survey, (2) exposure to four social media newsfeeds with deepfake videos, and (3) a feedback survey. Specifically, the first step required participants to answer questions assessing their preexisting political orientation, confirmation bias, and news literacy. In the second step, participants engaged with a simulated newsfeed reminiscent of social media platforms like X ( Twitter ), showcasing posts on two political issues: immigration and the Israel–Hamas war, distributed by CNN and FOX media outlets. While participants could replay deepfake videos in the social media newsfeeds, there was a 2-min limit on this viewing. Following this, they moved on to the third step, responding to questions probing their thoughts, attitudes, and behavioral intentions after exposure to the fabricated political deepfake news. Qualtrics administered the pre- and post-session online surveys and study stimuli, and each participant was randomly assigned to one of four political deepfake videos. The between-subjects design allowed us to assess whether exposure to ideologically different media platforms affected how news readers evaluated deepfake video content and the levels of trust they placed in social media news.
An online experimental design was employed because it provides a rigorous yet ecologically valid approach for examining how individuals assess and respond to political deepfake news. Unlike cross-sectional surveys, experiments expose participants to standardized stimuli while controlling for extraneous factors that may influence their evaluations. This is particularly important for political deepfake news, where message characteristics and contextual cues can substantially shape perceptions. Conducting the experiment online further enhances ecological validity because political deepfake news is primarily encountered through digital platforms and social media newsfeeds, allowing participants to evaluate the stimulus in a realistic information environment.
The online experimental approach is well suited to the objectives of this study. The research examines how individual antecedents—including political orientation, confirmation bias, news media literacy, and issue familiarity—influence political deepfake news assessment, and how these assessments relate to subsequent cognitive, attitudinal, and behavioral outcomes. By exposing all participants to the same deepfake news stimulus, the design minimizes variation in message exposure and enables differences in news assessment to be more confidently attributed to individual characteristics rather than differences in the information encountered. Although the study does not establish definitive causal relationships among all measured constructs, the controlled experimental exposure strengthens the validity of testing the proposed theoretical framework.
3.2. Stimulus Materials By following a typical social media newsfeed design, this study created four social media newsfeeds with political deepfake videos as experiment stimuli, including a news organization brand name and logo, a headline (a line of text at the beginning of a piece of content designed to grab a reader’s attention and summarize the main idea of the news content), and a visual element (an image or video). The authors first used generative AI, ChatGPT-4, to generate video news transcripts and then modified them into newsfeed headlines. Two social media newsfeeds on the U.S. immigration issue focused on two politicians’ distinct attitudes toward the Supreme Court’s decision allowing the Border Patrol to cut razor wire installed by Texas. The first CNN deepfake video was about Vice President Kamala Harris with the news headline, “Vice President Harris visited the U.S.-Mexico border today and expressed support for the court’s decision allowing the Border Patrol agents to cut razor wire installed by Texas.” The corresponding newsfeed from FOX was about Texas Governor Greg Abbott’s negative reaction to the court’s decision, with the headline: “In a bold move, Texas Governor Greg Abbott has ignited a heated debate by suggesting the possibility of an independent Texas amid ongoing challenges at the border.” Regarding the Israel–Hamas war news, two social media newsfeeds centered on distinct attitudes expressed by two politicians. In the deepfake video of CNN ’s social media newsfeed, President Joe Biden stated, “He had asked Israeli Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu for a ceasefire in the war between Israel and Hamas in Gaza in a recent telephone call,” while FOX ’s deepfake video featured House Speaker Mike Johnson’s responses, with a headline “Speaker Mike Johnson calls for Israel-Hamas ceasefire is ‘outrageous.’”
The deepfake videos were produced using different AI systems trained on vast amounts of data, such as actual footage of a person, to generate content that closely resembles the original. An online program, Parrot.ai, was utilized to create audio recordings of each politician reading the provided news scripts. This program used the given transcript and a voice model for each politician to generate an audio file of each politician delivering the script. Next, this study focused on deepfakes generated by neural networks trained to mimic authentic facial expressions and movements. Using those techniques, genuine politician videos can be manipulated by integrating speeches from voice actors. The mouth movements and gestures of the depicted political actors were manipulated to match the speech. Lastly, those deepfake videos were deployed through the online platform, TweetGen , to generate the most realistic tweets that simulated social media newsfeeds from two mainstream news outlets, CNN and FOX . Two computational visual artists were hired to achieve the most realistic content before data collection. The screenshots of those four social media newsfeeds with political deepfakes are shown in Figure 2.
3.3. Participants To improve the representation of participants relative to the U.S. population, this investigation employed Dynata , the world’s largest first-party data firm, to recruit a national representative sample based on the age distribution reported in the 2022 U.S. Census. The nationally representative sample included 1001 qualified respondents. The age distribution was as follows: 11.5% were aged 18–24, 17.3% were aged 25–34, 16.9% were aged 35–44, 15.8% were aged 45–54, 16.6% were aged 55–64, and 22.2% were aged 65 and above. Men comprised 49.8% of the sample, women comprised 49.9%, and individuals identifying as other genders accounted for 0.4%. White Caucasians constituted the largest racial group at 69.3%, followed by African Americans at 16.0%, Latinos/Latinas/Hispanics at 6.6%, Asians at 4.8%, and others at 3.3%. Regarding educational attainment, 27.7% had completed a bachelor’s degree, while 24.9% had undertaken some college or university study without obtaining a degree. Approximately 40.9% were employed full-time, and 25.0% were retired. Regarding income levels, 22.6% of participants reported a yearly household income below $30,000, 21.6% reported incomes ranging from $50,000 to just under $75,000, and 21.5% reported incomes exceeding $100,000.
3.4. Measures Uncertainty. Adapted from Vaccari and Chadwick (2020), the present study asked participants to use their best judgment to evaluate whether the political deepfake news they viewed in social media newsfeeds was false, unsure, or true. The “TRUE” answer indicated that the political deepfake news deceived participants, while the “UNSURE” answer indicated that it caused uncertainty.
Political orientation. A five-item, five-point Likert scale was adapted from Mehrabian’s (1996) Conservatism–Liberalism Scale to measure people’s political orientation (Cronbach’s Alpha = 0.863, M = 3.07, SD = 1.13). This scale was nearly balanced for response bias, composed of two positively and three negatively worded items. Higher scores indicated people’s right-leaning political orientation (conservatism).
Confirmation bias. A five-item, five-point Likert scale was adapted from Ardi and Pradiri’s (2021) confirmation bias scale to measure individuals’ tendency to favor information supporting their beliefs or decisions (Cronbach’s Alpha = 0.859, M = 2.79, SD = 0.91). The more significant the scores, the higher the tendency individuals have to process or interpret information according to their preexisting beliefs.
News literacy. Adopting a multi-dimensional approach to measuring news literacy (Vraga & Tully, 2021), this study used a ten-item, five-point Likert scale to assess people’s news literacy knowledge and skills from three perspectives: authors and audiences, messages and meanings, and representations and realities (Cronbach’s Alpha = 0.895, M = 4.02, SD = 0.64).
Social media accuracy-motivated and identify-motivated skepticism. Adapted from Li (2023), accuracy-motivated skepticism was measured by how much respondents agreed with three statements through a five-point Likert scale (“I worry that I will believe in stories on social media that turn out to be inaccurate,” “I am worried that false information on social media will negatively affect my own decisions,” and “It is possible that I get tricked by false stories on social media due to my own biases”) (Cronbach’s Alpha = 0.859, M = 3.10, SD = 1.10). Identity-motivated skepticism was measured by the extent to which respondents agreed with five items on a 5-point Likert scale (Cronbach’s Alpha = 0.835, M = 3.88, SD = 0.76).
Perceived news credibility. This study defined perceived news credibility as an evaluative judgment of trust in the media, reflecting how believable a message, source, or platform is (Metzger & Flanagin, 2013). Adapted from prior studies on online news credibility (Chung et al., 2012) and fake news credibility (Lee & Shin, 2022), eight items with a five-point semantic differential scale were constructed to measure source, message, and media dimensions (Cronbach’s Alpha = 0.950, M = 3.30, SD = 1.15).
Engagement intention. This study was adapted from the fake news engagement intention scale, which included multiple items (Lee & Shin, 2022). The five-item, five-point Likert scale measured people’s social media sharing intention and other engagement activities, such as commenting on or liking social media news (Cronbach’s Alpha = 0.939, M = 2.54, SD = 1.16).
News issue familiarity. This study defined news issue familiarity as a global evaluative summary of individuals’ understanding or awareness about a particular news topic or current event. The respondents marked how familiar they are with a specific news issue, immigration or the Israel–Hamas war, delivered in political deepfake news. One single item using a five-point Likert scale was constructed, asking the participants to agree on the item “Are you familiar with the news event that you just viewed?” (1 = Not at all, 5 = Extremely familiar).
4. Results This study first tested to what extent respondents exposed to each of the four political deepfake videos considered it “True” (deceived audiences), “Unsure” (uncertain), or “False” (not deceived). Of the total 1001 respondents, 256 (25.6%) viewed Biden’s deepfake video, 246 (24.6%) saw Harris’s video, 246 (24.6%) watched Abbott’s video, and 253 (25.3%) viewed Johnson’s video. The four political deepfake news videos deceived 48.85% of the participants, 35.67% were uncertain about the news content, and only 15.48% detected the fakeness. In particular, the immigration news with Texas Governor Greg Abbott’s deepfake video led almost 62% of subjects exposed to this newsfeed to believe it was true. A Chi-square test of independence was performed to examine the relationship between these four pieces of deepfake content and people’s authentic assessments. The relation between these two variables was significant, Pearson Chi-square χ 2 (6, N = 1001) = 27.575, p < 0.001, suggesting a significant association between different responses and their exposure to distinct political deepfake video content (Table 1).
To address RQ2 to RQ4, this study performed Structural Equation Modeling (SEM) using SPSS AMOS 29.0 statistics software, including measurement model fit and path analysis. First, Confirmatory Factor Analysis (CFA) was computed using SPSS AMOS to test the measurement models. The model-fit measures were used to assess the model’s overall goodness of fit (CMIN/ df , GFI, CFI, TLI, SRMR, and RMSEA), and all values were within their respective expected acceptance levels. The seven-factor model (political orientation, confirmation bias, news literacy, accuracy-motivated skepticism, identify-motivated skepticism, perceived news credibility, and engagement intention) yielded good fit for the data: Chi-square χ 2 was 1842.430 ( df = 749, p = 0.000). The goodness of fit indices (CMIN/ df = 2.46, GFI = 0.916, CFI = 0.957, TLI = 0.953, SRMR = 0.076, and RMSEA = 0.038) indicated that the measurement model fit the data adequately. Additionally, construct reliability was also assessed using Cronbach’s Alpha. Cronbach’s coefficient alpha values of all multi-item scales ranged from 0.835 to 0.950, indicating that all constructs in the measurement model were reliable.
To examine the causal relationship proposed in the structural model for the antecedent and consequence tests, SEM with SPSS AMOS 29.0 was performed to test the proposed model. The minimum fit function Chi-square χ 2 for the structural model was 2011.592 ( df = 755, p = 0.000). The goodness of fit indices (CMIN/ df = 2.66, GFI = 0.909, CFI = 0.951; TLI = 0.946; RMSEA = 0.041; SRMR = 0.069) indicated that the structural model fit the data adequately. Specifically, RQ2 examined the relationships between political orientation and cognitive, attitudinal, and behavioral effects after exposure to political deepfake newsfeeds. Political orientation (conservative tendency) impacted all outcomes but exhibited distinct effects. The individual’s conservatism tendency had negatively influenced accuracy-motivated social media skepticism ( γ = −0.078, p = 0.025), perceived deepfake news credibility ( γ = −0.098, p < 0.004), and engagement intention ( γ = −0.098, p = 0.002). However, political orientation (conservative tendency) positively impacted identity-motivated social media skepticism ( γ = 0.081, p = 0.008). The results suggested that people who are politically liberal are more likely to suspect social media news/information accuracy, perceive deepfake news as authentic and believable, and show higher engagement intention with those deepfake newsfeeds. However, political conservatives tend to be skeptical of social media information because they feel there is much false information that runs counter to their political identities and affiliations.
When examining the relationships between confirmation bias and the consequences of disinformation exposure (RQ3), the current study showed positively significant effects of confirmation bias on accuracy-motived social media skepticism ( γ = 0.242, p < 0.001), deepfake news credibility ( γ = 0.146, p < 0.001), and engagement intention ( γ = 0.460, p < 0.001), but not on identify-motivated social media skepticism. The results indicated that individuals with a higher confirmation bias are more likely to be deceived by political deepfake news. They tend to rate these newsfeeds as authentic, accurate, truthful, and believable, stimulating them to share, comment on, or recommend them to others. However, individuals’ tendency to favor information that supports their beliefs or decisions did not motivate their skepticism about how social media misinformation affects the perceived advantage or disadvantage of their political group and its opponents. Moreover, there were positively significant impacts of news literacy on accuracy-motived social media skepticism ( γ = 0.226, p < 0.001), identify-motivated social media skepticism ( γ = 0.650, p < 0.001), and deepfake news credibility ( γ = 0.108, p = 0.001), rather than on engagement intention. The research findings demonstrated that people with higher news literacy knowledge and skills tend to suspect social media news, no matter whether it is driven by information accuracy or identity motivations. However, their level of news literacy knowledge does not appear to influence their intentions to engage with social media news, such as liking, sharing, or commenting on news posts (Table 2).
Regarding RQ5, this study assessed the mediating role of news issue familiarity on the relationship between antecedents and consequences. The minimum fit function Chi-square χ 2 for the structural model was 2098.414 ( df = 789, p = 0.000). The goodness of fit indices (CMIN/ df = 2.66, GFI = 0.908, CFI = 0.949; TLI = 0.944; RMSEA = 0.041; SRMR = 0.069) indicated that the structural model fit the data adequately. The research results revealed a significant indirect effect of news issue familiarity: confirmation bias versus deepfake news credibility ( b = 0.038, p = 0.000) as well as confirmation bias versus behavioral intention ( b = 0.035, p = 0.000). There was also a significant indirect effect of news familiarity: news literacy versus deepfake news credibility ( b = 0.052, p = 0.000) as well as news literacy versus behavioral intention ( b = 0.048, p = 0.000). Moreover, the direct effects of confirmation bias on deepfake news credibility ( b = 0.119, p = 0.002) and behavioral intention ( b = 0.482, p = 0.000), as well as news literacy on deepfake news credibility ( b = 0.127, p = 0.024) were also significant. However, the direct effect between news literacy and engagement intention ( b = −0.103, p = 0.053) was insignificant. Hence, news issue familiarity fully mediated the relationship between people’s news literacy and news-sharing intentions, while partially mediating the relationship between confirmation bias and media literacy and perceived news credibility (Table 3).
5. Discussion By adopting an Antecedents–Disinformation–Outcomes framework, this study investigates the predictors and consequences of exposure to political deepfake news via social media newsfeeds and the mediating role of news issue familiarity between those drivers and outcomes. The current study reveals that embedding political deepfake videos in social media newsfeeds can easily deceive or cause uncertainty among news audiences. Nearly half (48.85%) were misled by the news message, 35.67% were uncertain, and only 15.48% detected the fakeness. The findings contribute to growing research on deepfakes or manipulated AI-generated disinformation (Barari et al., 2021; Hameleers et al., 2022; Vaccari & Chadwick, 2020). Although several prior studies cast doubt on the claim that deepfakes directly deceive the public on a mass scale, this study offers evidence that deepfakes leverage the “realism heuristic,” in which social media users tend to trust images and videos as more reliable depictions of the real world. As technology progresses, synthesized reality in deepfake videos could be more convincing, amplifying the negative impacts of social media disinformation. As noted by Vaccari and Chadwick (2020), “deepfakes may foster the belief among citizens that a fundamental truth cannot be established” (p. 3). Moreover, experts warn that deepfakes could predispose citizens to become overly skeptical and distrust authentic information, particularly during electoral periods (Chesney & Citron, 2019). Therefore, it is imperative for communication scholars to closely monitor this emerging form of disinformation, especially AI-powered synthetic media.
All negative correlations between conservatism and the three outcomes of deepfake video exposure indicate that individuals with more liberal tendencies are more likely to perceive deepfake news as credible and show higher engagement intentions, such as commenting on, sharing, or recommending these news stories to others. Likewise, they are more skeptical about social media information, mainly driven by motives for accuracy. The findings contrast with much of the threat perception literature; conservatives are generally more sensitive to a range of threats, such as immigration. The exceptions to this tendency occur when the media portray an issue in a partisan manner, as observed in climate change cases (Carmichael et al., 2017) and COVID-19 (Calvillo et al., 2020). Similarly, the current analysis of political deepfake news on immigration and the Israel–Hamas war demonstrates that obtaining news from highly partisan sources (e.g., FOX and CNN ) can influence participants’ thoughts, attitudes, and behavioral intentions about those issues. Prior research reveals that a significant portion of disinformation spread online today targets conservative partisans and is often amplified by mainstream, highly partisan media outlets (Benkler et al., 2018). While conservatives tend to perceive threats, this inclination can be counteracted through significant intergroup pressure, effective political leadership, or influential media messaging (Calvillo et al., 2020). The current study suggests that the association between trait conservatism and deepfake recognition performance is mediated by the extent to which individuals agree with the political statement conveyed in the video, aligning with their political ideology and partisan affiliation. Therefore, politically conservative individuals motivated by their identity tend to exhibit greater skepticism toward social media information. This skepticism arises from their perception that much of the information on social media opposes their political identities and affiliations.
Regarding political news consumption, the study shows confirmation bias’s positive impacts on people’s social media news accuracy-motivated skepticism, deepfake news credibility, and engagement intention. Although there is ongoing discussion regarding the strength of the inclination to avoid messages conflicting with one’s attitudes compared to the preference for messages aligning with them (Garrett, 2009) and how confirmation bias may contribute to political polarization (Stroud, 2010), the prevailing perspective suggests that individuals typically approach political messages with a confirmation bias. This study affirms that individuals who exhibit higher levels of confirmation bias while consuming political news are more inclined to be skeptical about accurate stories on social media, consider deepfake news more trustworthy and authentic, and even yield higher engagement intention. Y. Zhou and Shen (2022) further suggested the presence of confirmation bias in source and message perception and processing: the source of attitude-consistent messages was perceived as more credible (expertise and trustworthiness) than their attitude-inconsistent counterpart; attitude-consistent information was perceived as more factual than counter-attitudinal messages. Since this study utilizes two highly partisan media platforms (i.e., CNN and FOX ) to simulate their social media newsfeeds, the positive effects of confirmation bias imply that cognitive, attitudinal, and behavioral outcomes might be determined by individuals’ biased responses to political news sources and messages.
As expected, the present study finds that individuals with higher levels of news literacy are more likely to exhibit skepticism toward political deepfake news, although this skepticism does not necessarily translate into more accurate assessments of its authenticity. This finding suggests that understanding how news is produced and circulated alone may be insufficient for identifying increasingly sophisticated AI-generated misinformation. Previous research similarly found that information literacy, rather than media or news literacy, more strongly predicts individuals’ ability to identify fake news online (Jones-Jang et al., 2021). While media literacy emphasizes understanding media institutions, production processes, and message construction, and news literacy focuses on evaluating journalistic practices and news credibility, information literacy equips individuals with the skills to locate, verify, compare, and critically evaluate information across multiple sources. In rapidly evolving digital information environments, these competencies become particularly important for distinguishing authentic from manipulated content.
From a broader theoretical perspective, these findings also highlight the importance of critical media literacy. Hall’s (2014) encoding/decoding model argues that media messages are not passively received or judged solely on their factual accuracy; rather, they are encoded within particular ideological, cultural, and institutional contexts and actively interpreted by audiences according to their own social positions and prior beliefs. Applied to political deepfake news, this perspective suggests that audiences evaluate synthetic political content not only by assessing whether it is factually accurate but also by interpreting its meanings through existing political identities, cultural values, and media experiences. Consequently, susceptibility to political deepfake news depends not only on technical detection skills but also on individuals’ capacity to critically reflect on how media messages are produced, framed, and circulated within broader systems of political communication and power.
The present findings therefore suggest that strengthening resilience against political deepfakes requires a more comprehensive approach than improving news literacy alone. Given the increasingly realistic and systematically disseminated nature of AI-generated political content, effective evaluation depends on the integration of news literacy, information literacy, and critical media literacy. Beyond understanding journalistic norms or acquiring technical verification skills, individuals must also develop the ability to interrogate the social, political, and technological contexts in which media messages are produced and interpreted. Educational and policy interventions should therefore promote not only information verification strategies—such as cross-checking claims, consulting multiple credible sources, and utilizing AI-assisted verification tools—but also critical reflection on the ideological assumptions, framing practices, and power relations embedded in contemporary digital media environments. Such a multidimensional literacy framework is likely to better prepare citizens to navigate the challenges posed by increasingly sophisticated political deepfakes.
This study further examines the mediating role of news issue familiarity between the antecedents and consequences after consuming social media newsfeeds featuring political deepfake videos. Notably, familiarity with news issues fully mediates people’s news literacy and deepfake news engagement activities. As discussed above, those with greater news literacy, such as understanding the processes involved in news production, the characteristics of news, and the subjective aspects of news consumption, do not automatically translate it to their enhanced evaluations of political deepfakes. However, suppose they are more familiar with such news issues as immigration or the Israel–Hamas war. In that case, they are more likely to share, comment, like, or even recommend those deepfake news stories across their social media networks. Prior research has shown that familiarity with a specific issue is linked to one’s opinions, attitudes, and behaviors. This correlation is reinforced by comprehension of the topic and perceptions of personal relevance, leading to more deliberate and controlled processing of news content (Han et al., 2009). Enhanced cognition regarding those political issues, their potential impacts, and personal relevance can significantly boost individuals’ inclination to engage with such social media news content virtually.
6. Conclusions This study develops and empirically examines a comprehensive theoretical framework of the antecedents and consequences of political deepfake news consumption on social media. By conceptualizing political deepfake news assessment as the central information processing and evaluation mechanism, the study demonstrates how ideological predispositions, cognitive biases, and informational competencies jointly shape individuals’ evaluations of political deepfake news and subsequently influence cognitive, attitudinal, and behavioral outcomes. In doing so, the study extends previous research that has primarily focused on isolated predictors or consequences by integrating these factors into a unified, audience-centered framework.
The findings contribute to the growing literature on political dis/misinformation and AI-generated content by highlighting that susceptibility to political deepfake news is not determined solely by technological sophistication or message characteristics but also by the interaction between individuals’ political orientations, confirmation bias, news media literacy, and issue familiarity. The results further suggest that improving resilience to political deepfakes requires a multidimensional approach that combines news literacy, information literacy, and critical media literacy to strengthen audiences’ ability to evaluate increasingly sophisticated AI-generated political content.
The study also offers practical implications for educators, journalists, policymakers, and social media platforms. Interventions designed to combat political deepfakes should extend beyond technological detection tools to include educational initiatives that foster critical evaluation, information verification, and awareness of how political identities and cognitive biases shape the interpretation of online information. Such efforts may help reduce the societal harms associated with political deepfakes and strengthen public trust in digital information environments.
Several limitations should be acknowledged. The findings are based on a U.S. online panel sample and an online experimental design using a specific deepfake stimulus, which may limit the generalizability of the results to other populations, media environments, and forms of political deepfake content. In addition, the study examines behavioral intentions rather than observed behaviors. Future research should validate the proposed framework using nationally representative and cross-cultural samples, longitudinal and behavioral research designs, and a wider range of AI-generated political content to further advance understanding of political deepfake news consumption.
