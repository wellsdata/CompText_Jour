Introduction
In crisis situations, the immediate transmission and timely accurate exchange of information are essential for addressing urgent needs (Palen and Liu, 2007; Chauhan and Hughes, 2017). Effective communication facilitates rescue and recovery efforts and plays a critical role in mitigating public anxiety and fear (Fearn-Banks, 2016). Social media platforms have emerged as vital conduits for information dissemination during crises, offering unparalleled reach and immediacy (Xiong and Liu, 2014; Avvenuti et al., 2018; Chou, Oh, and Klein, 2018). During periods of heightened uncertainty, such as the early stages of the COVID-19 pandemic, misinformation on social media proliferates rapidly. Users often share unverified information to fill informational voids and cope with uncertainty (Huang et al., 2015; Zubiaga et al., 2018). The velocity at which misinformation spreads surpasses that of any other health-related topic (Dhawan et al., 2021), eroding trust in health advisories, accelerating virus transmission (Krause et al., 2020), and undermining global disease control efforts (Lee et al., 2020).
Misinformation is defined as “incorrect or misleading content presented as factual statements” (Green and Donahue, 2018, p. 109). Unlike disinformation, which is deliberately created to deceive, misinformation is characterized by the unintentional dissemination of false information (Wardle, 2018; Wardle and Derakhshan, 2017). It is typically shared without the intent to cause harm, often as a result of individuals unknowingly spreading inaccurate information on social media in an attempt to inform or assist others. While misinformation is not always disseminated with malicious intent, its unintended consequences can pose serious threats to public health (Wardle and Derakhshan, 2017; Vosoughi et al., 2018; King and Wang, 2023), thereby presenting a significant challenge for individuals in urgent need of reliable information (Huang, Lei, and Ni, 2022). Research has shown that misinformation and resistance to containment measures significantly influence disease transmission and mortality patterns during pandemics (Forati and Ghose, 2021). The spread of misinformation is influenced by an individual’s emotional state. Negative affect—such as fear, sadness, depression, isolation, and anxiety—commonly experienced during pandemics stimulates people’s desire to share information, thereby promoting misinformation (Wang and Chen, 2019; Duan and Zhu, 2020; Fleming, 2020; Moghanibashi-Mansourieh, 2020; Lwin et al., 2023; Huang et al., 2022).
Since January 23, 2020, Wuhan, identified as the initial epicenter of the COVID-19 pandemic, implemented stringent quarantine measures (Zhang et al., 2020; Xu et al., 2021), demonstrating positive outcomes in epidemic control (Feng et al. (2020a)). During subsequent waves, digital technologies were leveraged for precise containment strategies (Xu et al., 2023). These restrictions altered physical activity patterns and increased online interactions, creating an environment conducive to misinformation dissemination (Chen et al., 2022).
Despite changes in social interaction due to the information technology revolution, geographical proximity remains influential, even more so in certain contexts (Li et al., 2021a; Liu, Zhang and Huang, 2020; Blauza et al., 2023). A negative correlation was observed between physical distance from the epicenter and misinformation sharing during the Zika virus outbreak (Valecha et al., 2020a, 2020b), underscoring the importance of analyzing both cyberspace and geographical perspectives in public health crises.
Understanding how geographical distance, emotional states, and social media usage collectively shape misinformation dissemination is essential for developing a comprehensive framework of crisis-related information behavior. While previous studies have examined these factors individually (Luo et al., 2021; Slovic, 1987; Vosoughi et al., 2018), there remains a notable gap in understanding how these elements interact to influence misinformation dynamics in crisis contexts. This study addresses this gap by investigating the interplay among geographical distance, emotional responses, and social media usage habits, thereby contributing to a more integrative perspective on misinformation spread. Specifically, it examines how physical distance from the crisis epicenter moderates emotional reactions and, in turn, affects misinformation dissemination patterns.
By situating this investigation within the broader framework of risk communication and digital misinformation theories, this study extends existing research by identifying how spatial and emotional dimensions jointly shape crisis-related misinformation behaviors. Furthermore, by examining these relationships in diverse geographical contexts, it enhances our understanding of misinformation dynamics across different crisis scenarios, providing theoretical insights that inform context-sensitive information management strategies (Sun and Xie, 2024). This approach not only refines existing misinformation models but also offers practical implications for designing geographically adaptive misinformation interventions.
Although the immediate impact of the COVID-19 pandemic has diminished, understanding the intrinsic mechanisms driving individual misinformation sharing during the early stages remains crucial. This study aims to uncover the patterns and dynamics of misinformation dissemination during major public health events, offering empirical evidence and policy recommendations for future crisis scenarios. By revealing underlying mechanisms, this study contributes valuable insights to the ongoing discourse on effective crisis communication and misinformation control.
Literature review and research hypotheses
The influence of physical distance from the epicenter on online misinformation dissemination
In disaster events, physical distance plays a crucial role in shaping individual risk perception and information-sharing behavior. Geographic proximity has been recognized as a key factor influencing misinformation dissemination, as individuals closer to the epicenter often perceive a higher sense of urgency and personal relevance, leading them to engage more actively in information exchange (Trilling et al., 2017). Information originating from geographically proximate locations tends to be perceived as more relevant and credible, increasing the likelihood of it being shared, even when its accuracy is uncertain (Huang et al., 2015).
Within the pandemic context, physical distance from the epicenter directly influences individuals’ risk assessments and emotional responses. It serves as a proxy for perceived exposure risk, meaning that individuals residing closer to the outbreak center experience higher perceived threat levels, which in turn shape their behaviors (Zheng et al., 2022). Research on past health crises suggests that individuals in close proximity to an outbreak are more likely to engage in rapid and frequent information-sharing behaviors, often as a coping strategy to manage uncertainty and seek social validation (Duffy et al., 2019; Valecha et al., 2020a, 2020b). During the COVID-19 pandemic, misinformation sharing was particularly prevalent in areas near the epicenter, where heightened anxiety and uncertainty compelled individuals to spread unverified information to warn others and reinforce collective awareness (Feng et al., 2020b; Huang et al., 2022).
Although psychological distance theory (Trope and Liberman, 2010) has been widely applied in misinformation research, it primarily focuses on cognitive and subjective perceptions of distance, including social, temporal, and hypothetical dimensions. However, this study emphasizes physical distance as a distinct and measurable factor that directly influences misinformation-sharing tendencies. While psychological distance may encompass physical distance as one of its components, it is important to recognize that physical proximity to a crisis center produces unique behavioral effects that extend beyond cognitive construal. Specifically, individuals near the epicenter are more likely to experience firsthand exposure to crisis-related stimuli, leading to stronger emotional reactions and an increased likelihood of engaging in misinformation dissemination.
Based on this theoretical framework and empirical evidence, this study posits:
H1: Physical distance from the epicenter is negatively correlated with online misinformation sharing behavior. Specifically, individuals closer to the epicenter are more inclined to disseminate misinformation.
The mediating role of negative affect
Emotions, defined as transient psychological responses to external stimuli (Reeve, 2008), serve as motivational states that prompt individuals to act (Bagozzi et al., 1999) and significantly influence communication behaviors (McDermott, 2019). As integral components of the information processing system, emotions rapidly guide value judgments about information and behavioral choices (Slovic et al., 2007), reflecting dynamic reactions to environmental changes (Scherer, 1982). During crises such as the early stages of the COVID-19 pandemic, the public experienced notably diminished positive affect and heightened negative affect (Li et al., 2022). Negative affect refers to the experience of aversive emotional states, which are typically associated with perceptions of threat or loss (Watson and Clark, 1994). When perceived demands exceed coping resources, individuals may face various adverse psychological states, including isolation, depression, anxiety, and worry (Cao et al., 2020; Duan and Zhu, 2020). Due to the uncertainty and unpredictability associated with the pandemic, these negative emotional states were particularly pronounced.
Empirical evidence underscores that physical distance from the epicenter substantially impacts individual negative affect. According to the “ripple effect” theory, the repercussions of major crises emanate from the center and diminish with increasing geographic distance (Slovic, 1987). Individuals near the outbreak epicenter, such as those in Wuhan, may endure greater psychological stress due to stigmatization (Lee et al., 2005). Empirical studies corroborate this phenomenon, revealing that public emotional levels correlate positively with event severity but negatively with distance and duration (Liu and Fu, 2024; Shi et al., 2022; Yang et al., 2022). Therefore, proximity to the epicenter is associated with increased negative affect (Wen et al., 2020; Liu, Zhang and Huang, 2020).
An individual’s evaluation of an event critically shapes their emotional response (Lazarus, 1991; Scherer et al., 2001), which in turn molds behavioral intentions (Chiu and Oh, 2021). Emotions can drive information-sharing behavior, serving as potent motivators for disseminating information, even if unverified (Brown et al., 2017), particularly during crises when individuals seek validation and support (Zhou et al., 2022). This phenomenon is especially pronounced in public health emergencies (Moghanibashi-Mansourieh, 2020; Huang, Lei, and Ni, 2022), where the prolonged impact of the COVID-19 pandemic on individual emotions has been documented (Gruber et al., 2021). Empirical research indicates that individuals experiencing negative affect are more likely to believe and share misinformation (Martel et al., 2020; Lai et al., 2020; Weeks, 2015). For instance, intense emotions like anger can expedite and broaden the dissemination of misinformation (Han et al. (2020); Vosoughi et al., 2018; Lee et al. (2024)). Moreover, sharing misinformation can exacerbate individual anxiety, perpetuating a vicious cycle (Verma et al., 2022).
Synthesizing the above analysis, physical distance from the epicenter not only directly influences individual negative affect but also affects online misinformation sharing through this channel. Firstly, as physical distance from the epicenter decreases, perceptions of threat intensify, resulting in higher levels of negative affect. Secondly, negative affect functions as an emotional drive mechanism, prompting individuals to alleviate unease by sharing information. Lastly, given the high uncertainty during crises, individuals are more inclined to accept and propagate unverified information in pursuit of security and support. Based on this discussion, the following hypothesis is proposed:
H2: Negative affect mediates the relationship between physical distance from the epicenter and misinformation sharing.
The moderating role of social media usage
During the COVID-19 pandemic, physical distancing measures significantly increased reliance on social media for acquiring, sharing, and seeking information about the virus (Limaye et al., 2020; Farooq et al., 2020). As a multifunctional and multimodal platform, social media enables the global dissemination of information while transcending many limitations of traditional media (Cho et al., 2024; Scaglione et al., 2015). Compared to conventional mass media, social media is particularly adept at providing timely updates on events and promoting two-way communication (Zhai et al., 2020). By reducing communication costs, social media enhances connectivity, transforming physical space into virtual space and diminishing the “distance” between users with similar characteristics (Zhai et al., 2024). Consequently, social media significantly broadens the reach of news, allowing people to access articles through links shared on these platforms (Allcott and Gentzkow, 2017; Thompson et al., 2020; Ku et al., 2019). Notably, the COVID-19 pandemic was not the first major outbreak to witness significant social media activity; previous pandemics such as H1N1, Ebola, and Zika viruses also had substantial impacts on social media (Brindha et al., 2022). Social media has become a crucial hub for real-time content creation and dissemination by individuals and organizations, surpassing their social networks and personal locations (Oh et al., 2013).
Research indicates a strong association between individual emotions and social media usage (Turel and Qahri-Saremi, 2016). Platforms like Twitter and Weibo have emerged as important channels for expressing emotions and attitudes (Wang et al., 2020; Sheldon and Bryant, 2016). Therefore, considering social media use is essential when examining the relationship between physical distance from the epicenter and negative affect. During the pandemic, there was an urgent desire to understand the consequences of the outbreak, which social media satisfied through content streams such as Weibo’s “Hot Search” and WeChat’s “Moments” (Bayer et al., 2020). This may have facilitated emotional contagion, as users could observe and perceive others’ expressions of negative affect on social media. The constant influx of disease-related information can provoke discomfort among users. Moreover, to maximize engagement, social media platforms tend to prioritize appealing content over accuracy (Van Bavel et al., 2024), potentially leading to misinformation and exacerbating negative affect (Jones et al., 2017; Dong and Zheng, 2020). A survey in China found that over 80% of respondents reported frequent social media use during the pandemic, with higher usage correlating with increased experiences of negative affect (Gao et al., 2020).
In light of this analysis, social media usage may moderate the relationship between physical distance from the epicenter and negative affect. Specifically, individuals who frequently use social media might perceive greater pandemic risk based on physical distance from the epicenter, thereby increasing their experience of negative affect and strengthening the relationship between physical distance from the epicenter and negative affect. Conversely, less frequent social media users receive fewer related stimuli and are less influenced by environmental cues from the proximity to the epicenter, resulting in weaker experiences of negative affect and thus weakening the relationship between physical distance from the epicenter and negative affect.
Therefore, this study proposes the following hypothesis:
H3: Social media usage moderates the relationship between physical distance from the epicenter and negative affect, such that higher social media usage strengthens the relationship between physical distance from the epicenter and negative affect, while lower usage weakens it.

Research methodology
Data collection
This study aimed to explore the misinformation-sharing behaviors of the Chinese public during the COVID-19 pandemic, a critical period marked by government-imposed stay-at-home policies that restricted face-to-face interactions. To ensure a diverse and representative sample, the survey design took into account several factors: participants were recruited from multiple provinces to include individuals experiencing different levels of pandemic risk, and only residents within China were recruited due to the sensitivity of pandemic-related information. The questionnaire was administered through WJX (https://www.wjx.cn), an online survey platform extensively used in China for its efficiency in managing large-scale surveys. The survey commenced on February 22, 2021, at a time when China had generally achieved good control over the pandemic but still faced sporadic localized outbreaks, making online surveys the most suitable choice.
A three-day soft launch was conducted to monitor response times and set completion time thresholds. Based on pilot testing, responses under 5 min were excluded as too hasty, while those over 30 min were flagged as inattentive or abandoned. To ensure data quality, an IP address check was used to detect and remove duplicate submissions, preventing multiple responses from the same participant. Additionally, attention-check questions (e.g., “Select ‘Strongly Agree’ for this question”) were embedded to identify inattentive respondents. Ethical guidelines were strictly followed, ensuring voluntary participation and anonymity. After removing invalid responses, 1094 valid questionnaires were obtained from 27 provinces and autonomous regions in China, covering areas with varying pandemic severity.
Measurement
In this study, Model 7 of the PROCESS macro (v3.5) was employed to examine the moderated mediation model. A Bootstrap sample size of 5000 iterations with a 95% confidence interval was used to enhance the robustness of the estimates. Physical distance from the epicenter was specified as the independent variable ( X ), while misinformation sharing served as the dependent variable ( Y ). Negative affect was included as the mediator ( M ), and social media usage was incorporated as the moderator ( W ). In addition, gender, age, education level, monthly income and internet usage duration were controlled for in the analysis.
Physical distance from the epicenter
During the early stages of the COVID-19 outbreak (January–February 2020), the risk of infection exhibited an approximately linear attenuation trend with increasing geographic distance. The epidemic initially surged in Wuhan, the capital of Hubei Province, and rapidly spread to surrounding cities and other regions of China during the Spring Festival period (Wu et al., 2020). A spatiotemporal study by Feng et al. (2020a) demonstrated that Wuhan, along with nearby cities exhibited significantly higher case densities compared to provinces outside Hubei. The stringency of containment measures, including transportation restrictions and stay-at-home orders, also varied spatially, forming a gradient of control intensity (Feng et al., 2020a; Feng et al., 2020b).
To measure the spatial distance between individuals and the outbreak epicenter—Wuhan, Hubei Province—Geographic Information System technology was employed based on respondents’ provided address information (Li et al., 2021b). Specifically, we collected participants’ specific locations during the COVID-19 outbreak in February 2020 through a questionnaire survey. This study treated physical distance (Wuhan/other cities in Hubei Province/outside Hubei Province) as an ordinal continuous variable, assigning numerical values (Wuhan = 1, other cities in Hubei Province = 2, cities outside Hubei Province = 3), where a higher value indicates a greater distance from the outbreak epicenter. This measurement effectively captures individuals’ relative spatial positioning, providing a reliable variable for subsequent analysis. Such classification simplifies data analysis while reflecting the gradient differences in distance from the epicenter, which is crucial for understanding the impact of geographic proximity on pandemic spread and individual behavior. Grounded in spatial analysis theory, this method aims to reveal differences in individual behavior across varying levels of geographic distance (Zhou et al., 2022).
Misinformation sharing
Building on existing research, an adapted question item was designed to assess participants’ involvement in misinformation sharing (Chen et al., 2015). Specifically, the following question was asked: “During the pandemic, have you ever shared information or messages about COVID-19 online (including on WeChat Moments/WeChat groups) that you later found to be incorrect?” Responses were coded as 1 for “Yes” and 0 for “No.” This approach facilitates binary analysis of misinformation-sharing tendencies (d’Andrea et al., 2022), enabling exploration of influencing factors through logistic regression models.
Negative affect
To evaluate negative affect, we employed a validated Chinese version of the emotion scale (Han et al., 2021; Watson et al., 1988), which assesses six emotional states: anxiety, boredom, depression, tension, fatigue, and loneliness. Participants rated each emotion on a five-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (never) to 5 (very frequently). A composite negative affect score was calculated by averaging the ratings across these emotions, with higher scores indicating greater negative affect intensity. The scale exhibited excellent internal consistency in this study (Cronbach’s α  = 0.89), confirming its reliability. Grounded in affective psychology theory, this measure effectively captures individuals’ emotional responses during crisis situations (Scherer, 1982).
Social media usage
Based on Drouin et al. (2020), a question item was developed to assess the importance of social media as an information source during the pandemic. Participants were asked: “During the outbreak of COVID-19 (February 2020), did you primarily rely on social media platforms (such as QQ groups, WeChat groups) for pandemic-related information?” Those who answered “Yes” were marked as 1, indicating high reliance on social media, while those who answered “No” were marked as 0, signifying that social media was not their primary information channel. This method integrates media dependency theory and social network analysis, aiding in understanding the role of social media in crisis information dissemination (Rogers, 1995).
Control variables
Considering that demographic characteristics may influence misinformation-sharing behavior (Sun and Xie, 2024), gender, age, education level, and monthly income were included as control variables. Prior research suggests that age and education affect critical thinking and digital literacy, influencing misinformation susceptibility (Guess et al., 2019). Income level relates to media consumption patterns and trust in information sources (Allcott et al., 2019). Additionally, internet exposure frequency impacts information-sharing behavior (He et al., 2019). Thus, these variables were controlled to account for potential confounding effects, aligning with research on cognitive biases, information processing, and digital literacy in social psychology and communication studies (Cohen (2013)).
Demographic characteristics of respondents
The demographic characteristics of the respondents are as follows: males comprised 45.2% ( n  = 495) of the sample, while females accounted for 54.8% ( n  = 599). In terms of age distribution, the majority of respondents were between 18 and 49 years old (83.7%, n  = 916). Smaller proportions included those under 18 (2.7%, n  = 30) and over 50 years old (13.6%, n  = 148). Educational attainment ranged widely, from elementary school or below (1%, n  = 11) to postgraduate education or above (5.5%, n  = 60), with university bachelor’s degree holders forming the largest group (46.3%, n  = 506). Concerning pre-tax monthly income, respondents’ earnings predominantly fell within the lower-middle income range, such as less than 3000 Chinese yuan (RMB) (31.1%, n  = 340) and between 3001 and 5000 RMB (36.1%, n  = 395). High-income groups (monthly income exceeding 10,000 RMB) constituted a smaller proportion (6.4%, n  = 70).
These sample characteristics closely mirror the general profile of Chinese internet users (China Internet Network Information Center (2020)), although this study highlights a higher proportion of individuals with a bachelor’s degree or above and a slightly greater percentage of female participants. This sample structure provides a robust foundation for the study, enhancing the external validity and generalizability of the research findings.

The descriptive statistics indicate that the mean score for misinformation sharing was 0.21 (SD = 0.41), with 21.4% of respondents reporting that they had shared COVID-19-related news or information on online platforms (e.g., WeChat Moments/WeChat groups) that was later found to be false. The mean score for social media usage was 0.31 (SD = 0.46), and 31% of respondents relied on social media as their primary source of pandemic-related information. The mean score for negative affect was 2.19 (SD = 1.01), reflecting relatively high distress levels, while the mean score for physical distance from the epicenter was 1.88 (SD = 0.42), suggesting that the sample covered diverse geographic regions.
Correlation analysis revealed a negative correlation between physical distance from the epicenter and misinformation sharing ( r  = −0.11, p  < 0.001), indicating that individuals closer to the outbreak center were more likely to share misinformation. A similar negative correlation was observed between physical distance and negative affect ( r  = −0.11, p  < 0.01), suggesting that individuals in closer proximity to the epicenter experienced higher distress levels. Additionally, negative affect was positively correlated with misinformation sharing ( r  = 0.18, p  < 0.001), implying that individuals with heightened distress were more likely to disseminate misinformation.
For social media usage, a positive correlation was observed with negative affect ( r  = 0.10, p  < 0.01), indicating that frequent social media users experienced higher distress levels. However, social media usage was negatively correlated with misinformation sharing ( r  = −0.08, p  < 0.01), suggesting that despite increased exposure to misinformation, social media users were less likely to spread it. This counterintuitive finding may be attributed to factors such as selective exposure to credible sources or heightened information scrutiny among social media users (Van Bavel et al., 2024). Further analysis examined the relationship between physical distance and social media reliance, revealing no significant correlation ( r  = −0.04, p  > 0.05), suggesting that individuals closer to the epicenter were not necessarily more reliant on social media for pandemic-related information.

Testing of mediation and moderation effects
To explore the mechanisms underlying the effect of physical distance from the epicenter on misinformation sharing behavior, this study employed Hayes’s (2013) Bootstrap method. This method constructs an empirical sampling distribution through repeated resampling (typically set at 5000 times), overcoming the limitations of the traditional stepwise regression method, which relies on the assumption of normal distribution and has insufficient statistical power.
Table 3 presents the regression results for both mediation and moderation effects. In Model 1, physical distance from the epicenter significantly predicted negative affect ( β  = −0.195, p  < 0.01, 95% CI = [−0.338, −0.052]), consistent with prior research on the psychological impact of proximity to crisis events. In Model 2, negative affect significantly predicted misinformation sharing ( β  = 0.389, p  < 0.001, 95% CI = [0.249, 0.528]). According to Zhao et al. (2010), these results indicate that negative affect partially mediates the relationship between physical distance from the epicenter and misinformation sharing, supporting Hypothesis H2. Discussion
This study empirically examines the relationship between physical distance from the epicenter and misinformation sharing, with negative affect acting as a mediating factor. The findings provide critical insights into the mechanisms underlying crisis-related misinformation dissemination, highlighting the interplay between geographic proximity, emotional responses, and digital media engagement.
The results indicate a negative association between physical distance and misinformation sharing, suggesting that individuals in closer proximity to the crisis center are more likely to engage in the dissemination of misinformation. This finding aligns with risk communication theories, which posit that heightened personal relevance and perceived urgency in high-risk environments lead to increased information-seeking and sharing behaviors (Slovic, 1987). While prior research has predominantly focused on cognitive biases (Pennycook and Rand, 2020) or motivated reasoning (Lewandowsky et al., 2012) as primary explanatory factors for misinformation propagation, this study introduces a geospatial perspective, demonstrating that physical proximity serves as a contextual factor that intensifies emotional responses, thereby influencing information-sharing behaviors. This perspective extends existing models of misinformation dissemination, which have often overlooked the role of spatial and situational determinants in shaping crisis-related information dynamics.
The identification of negative affect as a mediating mechanism complements existing research on emotion-driven rumor propagation (Bordia and DiFonzo, 2004) and affective influences on misinformation sharing (Weeks, 2015; Martel et al., 2020). Previous studies have established that heightened emotional states—particularly fear and anxiety—can lead to impulsive information-sharing behaviors. Expanding on this body of work, the current study provides empirical evidence that geographic proximity amplifies these emotional responses, thereby exacerbating misinformation dissemination. This finding underscores the need to consider not only individual psychological predispositions but also contextual and environmental factors when examining the spread of misinformation during crises. Consequently, misinformation mitigation strategies should incorporate geographically tailored emotional regulation interventions, addressing the heightened affective states that contribute to misinformation susceptibility in crisis-affected regions.
Beyond individual-level factors, this study also examines the moderating role of social media usage. Prior research has established that algorithm-driven content exposure can amplify emotional responses and facilitate the spread of misinformation (Vosoughi et al., 2018; Cinelli et al., 2020). However, the findings of this study contribute a more nuanced understanding by demonstrating that this amplification effect is contingent upon geographic proximity. Specifically, individuals closer to the crisis center—who are already experiencing heightened emotional arousal—may be disproportionately affected by algorithmic reinforcement of fear-inducing content, further exacerbating misinformation dissemination. This insight advances discussions on algorithmic bias in crisis misinformation dynamics, illustrating that the effects of digital information environments are not uniform but rather spatially and emotionally differentiated.
These findings have important theoretical implications for misinformation research. By integrating geospatial, emotional, and digital media perspectives, this study offers a comprehensive model of crisis-related misinformation spread. Existing misinformation frameworks have primarily emphasized individual cognitive heuristics (Pennycook and Rand, 2020) and partisan-driven information processing (Lewandowsky et al., 2012), while relatively less attention has been given to how contextual factors—such as geographic proximity—shape emotional and behavioral responses to misinformation. This study addresses this gap by demonstrating that misinformation susceptibility is not solely a function of individual characteristics but is also influenced by the spatial and affective context in which information is received and processed.
From a practical standpoint, these findings suggest that misinformation regulation strategies should be adapted based on geographic risk levels. Social media platforms and public health agencies should implement localized fact-checking interventions and algorithmic modifications to mitigate misinformation spread in high-risk regions. Additionally, crisis communication strategies should integrate both cognitive and emotional dimensions, ensuring that factual information is not only accessible but also emotionally resonant with the target audience. Addressing misinformation effectively requires a multidimensional approach that accounts for the psychological, spatial, and technological factors influencing information dissemination.
Conclusion
This study offers a novel perspective on the role of physical distance in misinformation dissemination, emphasizing the interplay between geographic proximity, emotional responses, and digital media dynamics. The findings highlight that individuals closer to the crisis epicenter experience higher levels of negative affect, which in turn increases their likelihood of sharing misinformation. Furthermore, social media usage amplifies this effect, reinforcing the emotional and behavioral responses of geographically proximate individuals. As misinformation continues to pose challenges in crisis communication, these insights provide a valuable foundation for designing more effective regulatory and mitigation strategies in the digital age.
From a policy perspective, misinformation regulation strategies should be tailored based on geographic risk levels. Social media platforms and public health agencies should implement localized fact-checking interventions and algorithmic adjustments to reduce misinformation spread in high-risk regions. Additionally, crisis communication strategies should integrate both cognitive and emotional dimensions, ensuring that factual information is not only accessible but also emotionally resonant with the target audience. Addressing misinformation effectively requires a multidimensional approach that considers not only the accuracy of shared content but also the psychological and contextual factors driving information dissemination.
Despite its contributions, this study has several limitations. The cross-sectional design restricts the ability to establish causal relationships, and future research should adopt longitudinal approaches to track changes in misinformation-sharing behaviors over time. Additionally, reliance on self-reported data may introduce recall bias or social desirability effects, highlighting the need for behavioral tracking methodologies to obtain more objective insights. Future studies should also explore how different social media engagement patterns—such as passive consumption versus active content creation—mediate the relationship between emotional responses and misinformation sharing.
By addressing these gaps, future research can provide more refined recommendations for platform interventions and public information campaigns. As misinformation remains a critical challenge in crisis management, a deeper understanding of its psychological and geographic determinants can inform more effective mitigation strategies, ultimately contributing to more resilient and informed digital societies.
