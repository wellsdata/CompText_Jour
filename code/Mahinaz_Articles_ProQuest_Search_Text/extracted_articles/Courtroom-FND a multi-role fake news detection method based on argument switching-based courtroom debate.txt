Introduction
The rapid growth of social media and online platforms has amplified the dissemination of fake news, posing serious risks to public trust and societal harmony (Sharma et al. 2019; Zhou and Zafarani 2020; Hu et al. 2022). As false information spreads at unprecedented speeds, it influences public opinion, disrupts democratic processes, and exacerbates societal polarization. This challenge calls for robust and effective fake news detection (FND) techniques that go beyond surface-level analysis, delving into the linguistic, contextual, and psychological aspects of misinformation (Phan et al. 2023; Comito et al. 2023).
To address these challenges, researchers are leveraging advancements in natural language processing (NLP) and machine learning to develop systems capable of identifying deceptive content and reducing its harmful effects on individuals and communities (Shu et al. 2017). For example, various FND approaches have been proposed (Hu et al. 2022; Comito et al. 2023), focusing on analyzing the dynamics of information propagation and utilizing network structures, such as the processes of information dissemination or the underlying social structures that influence the spread of misinformation (Nguyen et al. 2020; Wu et al. 2023; Wu and Hooi 2023; Mehta et al. 2022; Jin et al. 2025). Despite their promising results, these methods substantially face limitations when social contextual information is incomplete or inaccessible due to evolving network dynamics and stringent privacy regulations (Ma et al. 2024).
In response to the challenges posed by incomplete or inaccessible social contextual information, text-mining-based fake news detection methods have emerged as a viable alternative (Jin et al. 2024). These approaches focus on analyzing the textual characteristics of news articles, delving into sentence- and document-level semantics to classify content without relying on external social data (Nan et al. 2021; Sheng et al. 2022; Wu et al. 2019). While such methods offer a promising solution to circumvent the limitations of social context dependency, their effectiveness is often constrained by the complexity and subtlety of fake news, which require more nuanced and comprehensive analysis (Ma et al. 2024).
Traditional fake news detection methods, including machine learning and deep learning models (Choras et al. 2020; Goldani et al. 2021; Iceland 2023), have primarily relied on handcrafted features or shallow textual representations. Although these methods have shown potential in specific scenarios, they struggle to capture the intricate linguistic and contextual patterns inherent in deceptive content (Sharma et al. 2019). Moreover, their reliance on large labeled datasets for training creates additional barriers, particularly in the face of the rapidly evolving nature of fake news (Ma et al. 2024). These limitations underscore the need for scalable, sophisticated solutions capable of adapting to the dynamic and multifaceted challenges in FND tasks (Shu et al. 2017).
The emergence of large language models (LLMs), such as GPT-3, Llama, Claude, and Gemini, has revolutionized the field by providing exceptional capabilities in understanding context, logical reasoning, and generating coherent narratives (OpenAI 2023; Brown et al. 2020; Touvron et al. 2023; Anthropic 2023; Team 2024). LLMs’ extensive pretraining on diverse datasets enables them to identify intricate patterns in language, positioning them as powerful tools for tackling tasks requiring deep semantic comprehension. Recent innovations in prompt engineering-such as Chain-of-Thought (CoT) (Wei et al. 2024; Kojima et al. 2022; Xu et al. 2024; Lyu et al. 2023), Self-Reflect (Shinn et al. 2023; Madaan et al. 2023), and In-Context Learning (ICL) (Dong et al. 2024; Brown et al. 2020)-further enhance LLMs’ ability to handle complex reasoning. These methods allow LLMs to iteratively refine their outputs, better understand contextual cues, and provide actionable insights, making them particularly suited for the dynamic and multifaceted nature of FND.
Despite advancements in LLMs, challenges persist in effectively leveraging them for fake news detection. One significant issue is their tendency to hallucinate, generating plausible but inaccurate arguments that can undermine the credibility of their conclusions. More specifically, CoT, which involves prompting LLMs to articulate their reasoning steps, performs well on tasks requiring logical deduction and multi-step reasoning. However, its reliance on static reasoning pathways can sometimes lead to incomplete or biased outputs. For FND task, the structured nature of CoT can limit the model’s ability to adjust dynamically to the fluid and unpredictable nature of fake news, where the context evolves over time. ICL, which allows the model to adapt its responses based on provided examples or prior context, offers greater flexibility compared to traditional prompting methods. However, its final performance is highly sensitive to the quality and relevance of the examples used in the prompt, leading to unstable results with significant fluctuations. If the examples are poorly chosen or do not adequately represent the complexity of the news content, ICL can produce unreliable or skewed results.
Moreover, existing FND approaches often lack mechanisms to thoroughly interrogate competing viewpoints, leading to unbalanced or overly confident outputs. Traditional methods also struggle to adapt dynamically to the nuanced and evolving nature of fake news content, as they typically rely on static prompts or single-pass evaluations.
These limitations highlight the need for frameworks that actively promote critical thinking, robust reasoning, and adaptability. Addressing these challenges requires a shift from static evaluations to interactive, iterative processes where models are required to rigorously defend or contest claims. Debate-inspired frameworks (Liang et al. 2024; Feng et al. 2024) provide an ideal foundation for tackling these issues, as they enable LLMs to engage in structured argumentation, exposing and resolving biases, inconsistencies, and errors through controlled adversarial interactions (Aryan 2024; Motwani et al. 2024).
To address these gaps (Hu et al. 2022; Shu et al. 2017; Ma et al. 2024; Liang et al. 2024), we propose a novel multi-role detection framework, Courtroom-FND, inspired by courtroom debates. This framework introduces a multi-role approach in which LLMs take on the roles of prosecution, defense, and judge to simulate a structured debate on the authenticity of news, which harnesses the strengths of argumentation and introspection to facilitate a more comprehensive evaluation of news authenticity. By engaging in this process:
- The prosecution argues that the news is false, while the defense counters with arguments supporting its truthfulness;

- The judge evaluates the evidence and arguments presented by both sides to reach an initial verdict;

- Roles are then switched, forcing the prosecution and defense to argue from the opposite standpoint, which helps uncover potential biases or gaps in the initial arguments;

- The judge reviews the outcomes of both rounds to deliver a balanced and robust final decision.


This debate-based framework addresses key challenges by encouraging divergent thinking, ensuring critical examination of multiple perspectives, and leveraging the reasoning capabilities of LLMs in a dynamic, role-driven setting. By incorporating argument switching and iterative evaluations, Courtroom-FND promotes more accurate and comprehensive assessments of news authenticity, offering a significant improvement over existing FND methods.
Our contributions in this work can be summarized as follows:
- We introduce a courtroom debate-based approach to FND namely Courtroom-FND, incorporating multi-role interactions to enhance reasoning and decision-making processes. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first work to systematically integrate the principles of courtroom debates into the domain of fake news detection;

- Courtroom-FND incorporates a novel argument-switching mechanism, forcing opposing roles to argue from alternative perspectives, which reduces bias, exposes hidden inconsistencies, and enhances the robustness of conclusions, further improving adaptability to the nuanced and evolving nature of fake news;

- Extensive experiments validate the effectiveness of Courtroom-FND on multiple challenging datasets, including cases of controversial and misleading news. The results highlight its superior performance in both accuracy and reliability, surpassing existing state-of-the-art methods, including advanced LLMs such as GPT-4;

- Courtroom-FND demonstrates that the structured debate process effectively balances accuracy and scalability in FND tasks, confirming the positive impact of multi-perspective argumentation on fake news detection. Moreover, Courtroom-FND enhances the explainability of FND tasks by simulating human-like reasoning processes in structured debates.


Our proposed Courtroom-FND represents a significant step toward building more reliable, scalable, and adaptive solutions for fake news detection, leveraging the strengths of LLMs and debate-inspired methodologies to navigate the complexities of modern information ecosystems.

Fake news detection: task overview and mainstream approaches
The FND task (Sharma et al. 2019; Hu et al. 2022) has become increasingly critical with the proliferation of misinformation on digital platforms. With the success of deep learning (DL) (Devlin et al. 2019; Liu et al. 2019), DL-based FND methods have gained significant attention (Hu et al. 2022). These methods can be categorized into the following categories: Knowledge-based Approaches which rely on factual verification by comparing claims in news articles with external sources (Nguyen et al. 2020; Wang 2017; Shu et al. 2019; Mitra and Gilbert 2015); Style-based Methods which focus on distinguishing linguistic, syntactic, and stylistic differences between fake and real news (Liu and Wu 2018; Shu et al. 2019; Goldani et al. 2021); and Social Context Propagation-based Models which leverage network structures (Wu et al. 2023; Jin et al. 2025), such as dissemination patterns in social media, to detect fake news, like graph convolutional networks (GCNs) (Kipf and Welling 2017) and heterogeneous graphs (Nguyen et al. 2020; Wu and Hooi 2023; Mehta et al. 2022).
Moreover, the small pre-trained language models (SLMs)-based FND methods leverage powerful PLMs to enhance the classification performance of fake news detection tasks. These methods include BERT-FT (Devlin et al. 2019), RoBERTa-FT (Liu et al. 2019), PET (Schick and Schütze 2021), KPT (Hu et al. 2022). These PLM-based methods have demonstrated strong capabilities in tackling the challenges of fake news detection by leveraging the knowledge encoded in pre-trained language models. For example, Kaliyar et al. (2021) propose FakeBERT, a fake news detection method based on BERT, which integrates parallel blocks of a single-layer CNN with BERT to address linguistic ambiguity in fake news. However, these methods typically necessitate fine-tuning the LMs on specialized datasets, which may not completely leverage the models’ vast prior knowledge. The advent of LLMs has significantly transformed NLP tasks by enabling models to learn from extensive datasets and grasp complex language patterns. Notable examples include OpenAI’s GPTs, Google’s Gemini, and Meta AI’s Llama (OpenAI 2023; Touvron et al. 2023; Team 2024). These LLMs, particularly the GPT series (OpenAI 2023; Brown et al. 2020), have shown remarkable capabilities in tasks that require a deep understanding of semantics, positioning them as promising tools for FND. Recent research has examined the potential of LLMs in fake news detection, with notable works like DAAD (Su et al. 2024) and LLM-GAN (Wang et al. 2024). Large language models and prompt engineering
The rapid advancement of NLP has been significantly driven by the development of LLMs and innovative prompt engineering techniques (Liu et al. 2022; Sanh et al. 2022). Models (Shu et al. 2017; Brown et al. 2020; Team 2024) such as Generative Pre-trained Transformer (GPT) (OpenAI 2023), Llama (Touvron et al. 2023), and Claude (Anthropic 2023) have set new benchmarks in understanding and generating human-like text, revolutionizing applications from machine translation to content creation. LLMs has revolutionized NLP tasks, offering powerful capabilities for semantic understanding, reasoning, and decision-making (Aryan 2024; Wang et al. 2024; Lai et al. 2024; Li et al. 2024). Unlike traditional methods, which often focus on static feature extraction or shallow models, LLMs (OpenAI 2023; Mao et al. 2024) excel in dynamically adapting to diverse contexts, reasoning through complex scenarios, and incorporating external knowledge seamlessly.
Simultaneously, prompt engineering (Liu et al. 2022; Kang et al. 2024) has emerged as a crucial technique to maximize the potential of LLMs without extensive fine-tuning. By carefully crafting input prompts, users can guide LLMs to perform specific tasks more effectively. For example, In-Context Learning (ICL) (Brown et al. 2020; Dong et al. 2024), involves embedding task-specific examples directly within the input prompt, allowing LLMs to infer desired behaviors from limited demonstrations, making it efficient for tasks with scarce labeled data. Chain-of-Thought (CoT) prompting (Wei et al. 2024; Kojima et al. 2022; Xu et al. 2024; Lyu et al. 2023) encourages LLMs to generate intermediate reasoning steps before arriving at a final answer, enhancing their capability to handle complex, multi-step problems. Variants like Zero-shot CoT (Kojima et al. 2022) and Self-Consistency CoT (Chen et al. 2024; Zhang et al. 2023; Wang et al. 2023) have further refined this approach, improving reasoning accuracy and transparency. Retrieval-Augmented Prompting (RAP) (Kang et al. 2024; Han et al. 2024; Hu et al. 2024; Chen et al. 2024) integrates external information retrieval systems with LLMs to enrich the context provided to the model, ensuring access to up-to-date and pertinent information. Yao et al. (2023) proposed ReAct , in which LLMs generate reasoning traces and task-specific actions in an interleaved manner. The reasoning traces assist the model in guiding, tracking, and updating its action plans, as well as handling exceptions, while the actions enable it to interface with external sources, such as knowledge bases or environments, to gather additional information. Instruction tuning (Ouyang et al. 2022; Khattab et al. 2023; Singhvi et al. 2024; Gururangan et al. 2020) trains LLMs on a variety of instructions to better follow complex and nuanced directives, enhancing generalizability and responsiveness to diverse user intents. LLMs in fake news detection
The emergence of LLMs has revolutionized NLP tasks by enabling advanced semantic understanding and contextual reasoning (OpenAI 2023; Anthropic 2023; Touvron et al. 2023). Especially, LLMs have shown exceptional potential in addressing the limitations of traditional FND methods (Brown et al. 2020; Zhou and Zafarani 2020; Hu et al. 2022). Many notable works have explored the use of LLMs for fake news detection task. Li et al. (2024) proposed STEEL , the first framework leveraging LLMs for automated information retrieval to detect fake news. This approach improves the accuracy of fake news detection through a multi-round Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) mechanism. Qi et al. (2024) developed Sniffer , a multimodal large language model (MLLM) designed to detect out-of-context (OOC) misinformation by assessing image-text consistency and providing interpretable explanations. Through two-stage instruction tuning and integration with external tools, Sniffer significantly improves detection accuracy and outperforms state-of-the-art methods. FactAgent (Li et al. 2024) is an automated fake news detection system that utilizes LLMs in an agentic manner, breaking down complex verification tasks into manageable sub-steps. LLM-GAN (Wang et al. 2024) utilizes prompting mechanisms to enable an LLM to function as both a generator and detector for realistic fake news generation and detection. The approach demonstrates effectiveness in both prediction performance and explanation quality. RumorLLM (Lai et al. 2024) is fine-tuned with rumor writing styles and content to address the challenges of fake news detection. It introduces a data-augmentation method for small categories, effectively mitigating the issue of category imbalance in real-world fake-news datasets. A novel retrieval-augmented LLM framework, called Re-Search for The Truth (Li et al. 2024), automatically and strategically extracts key evidence from web sources for claim verification.
These advancements illustrate the versatility of LLMs in tackling the evolving challenges of fake news detection (Hu et al. 2022; Goldani et al. 2021; Raza et al. 2024). Beyond improving accuracy and scalability, they also enhance interpretability, which is crucial for building trust in automated systems (Hu et al. 2022; Shu et al. 2017; Li et al. 2024). As misinformation continues to evolve, the adaptability and reasoning capabilities of LLMs will undoubtedly play a central role in mitigating its societal impact.
Preliminaries
In this section, we present the foundational concepts that inform our Courtroom-FND framework. We first formalize the FND task and examine its challenges (Hu et al. 2022; Shu et al. 2017). Next, we introduce LLMs (OpenAI 2023; Touvron et al. 2023) and their prompting paradigms (Brown et al. 2020; Wei et al. 2024), emphasizing how prompt engineering can help build more reliable FND solutions.
Fake News Detection (FND)
The FND seeks to classify a given news piece or claim as real (authentic) or fake (misleading) (Zhou and Zafarani 2020; Hu et al. 2022; Shu et al. 2017). Let a news article be represented by x , containing textual content such as headlines, main body text, or quotes. We assign a label y ^ ∈ { 0 , 1 } , where y ^ = 1 indicates fake content, and y ^ = 0 indicates authentic content. Let F( · ) represent the fake news detection algorithm or the information processing framework. Formally, a detection function can be defined as: y ^ ← F ( x ) = 1 , if x is judged to be fake , 0 , otherwise . Despite the apparent simplicity of a binary classification task, detecting fake news is complicated by several issues. First, linguistic ambiguity —articles sometimes appear legitimate while subtly embedding distortions or sensational claims. Second, limited labeled data —collecting and verifying large amounts of training data is time-consuming and prone to subjective biases. Third, rapid content evolution —misinformation sources adapt their tactics, rendering static features (e.g., fixed keywords or style cues) less effective over time.
Earlier FND approaches often relied on external structures (e.g., social graphs or knowledge bases) to bolster textual analysis (Nguyen et al. 2020; Wu et al. 2023; Wu and Hooi 2023; Jin et al. 2025). While valuable, these methods are hindered if the social or factual context is missing or intentionally obfuscated. Consequently, leveraging more adaptive and semantically robust models—such as LLMs—has become a natural progression for tackling FND with fewer domain constraints (Ma et al. 2024; OpenAI 2023; Lai et al. 2024).
LLM-based prompt engineering
LLMs like GPT (OpenAI 2023; Brown et al. 2020) or Llama (2023) have reshaped natural language processing by capturing complex language patterns and contextual cues through massive pretraining. However, their outputs can still be erroneous or misleading if prompts are too vague or biased. Prompt engineering addresses this limitation by guiding the model’s responses through carefully designed instructions or examples, with the goal of eliciting more accurate, relevant, and interpretable outputs. We categorize the main prompting techniques commonly utilized in LLM-based systems as follows:
- Zero-Shot Prompting. (Kojima et al. 2022; Dubois et al. 2024; Zhang et al. 2024; Hussain et al. 2024) In zero-shot prompting, the model receives only a task description and the text x . The expectation is that the LLM will use its pretraining to infer the appropriate label y ^ . Although straightforward, zero-shot methods can miss crucial context if the prompt is poorly phrased or if the model’s training data lacks similar examples.

- Few-Shot Prompting. (Jin et al. 2025; Brown et al. 2020; Jiang et al. 2025) In few-shot prompting, a small set of labeled examples { ( x i , y i ) } is prepended to the query, showing the LLM how to map similar content to the desired labels. This often leads to better generalization—particularly when the examples highlight deceptive language patterns relevant to FND. Yet, if the examples are unrepresentative, the model’s performance may degrade.

- Multi-Step (Chain-of-Thought) Prompting. (Wei et al. 2024; Kojima et al. 2022; Xu et al. 2024; Lyu et al. 2023) Instead of directly demanding an answer, multi-step or chain-of-thought prompts encourage the model to show intermediate reasoning. These intermediate steps can mitigate hasty conclusions, but they also risk propagating flawed logic if the model’s internal reasoning is incorrect.

- Other Prompting Techniques. Beyond these three categories, there exist additional prompting strategies-such as Retrieval-Augmented Prompting (Han et al. 2024; Hu et al. 2024; Kang et al. 2024; Chen et al. 2024), Self-Consistency Prompting (Chen et al. 2024; Zhang et al. 2023; Wang et al. 2023), Meta-Prompting (OpenAI 2024), and Instruction Tuning (Ouyang et al. 2022; Khattab et al. 2023; Singh et al. 2024)-that further expand the design space for eliciting specific reasoning paths, contextual nuances, or structured outputs from LLMs.



Our approach: courtroom-FND
Our proposed Courtroom-FND is a multi-role framework inspired by courtroom-style debates, designed to enhance fake news detection by addressing challenges such as cognitive biases, hallucinations, and incomplete logic, while also improving reasoning depth and ensuring transparency (Aryan 2024; Li et al. 2024). As shown in Fig. 1, this framework leverages large language model (LLM) agents, assigning them distinct roles as the Prosecution, Defense, and Judge. Through role switching and iterative debate, the framework systematically uncovers hidden inconsistencies, produces comprehensive analyses, thereby improving overall reasoning explainability and arrives at more reliable conclusions. Framework overview
Courtroom-FND consists of three principal components: (1) Prosecution - Argues that the input news is fake, presenting reasons such as inconsistencies, unreliable sources, or contradictory evidence; (2) Defense - Argues that the input news is genuine, citing verifiable data, contextual information, and logical consistency; (3) Judge - Evaluates both sides’ arguments and issues a verdict. After the first round, roles are switched (Prosecution ↔ Defense) to ensure a more rigorous and balanced analysis.
Correspondingly, as outlined in Fig. 1, which illustrates the multi-role debate process in Courtroom-FND, the entire process unfolds in two rounds of debate:
- Round 1: Prosecution argues the news is fake, while Defense argues it is genuine.

- Round 2: Roles are switched to ensure each side tackles the opposing perspective.


A Judge evaluates both rounds of arguments to deliver a final verdict. This multi-round, adversarial mechanism helps mitigate superficial or biased reasoning that may arise from single-pass evaluations.
By design, Courtroom-FND fosters divergent thinking -each side must construct or refute arguments from different angles, effectively simulating a courtroom debate. The iterative, adversarial nature of the framework counteracts common pitfalls in single-pass classification models, such as cognitive biases , hallucinations , and overconfidence in outputs.
Detailed courtroom debate process
In this section, we provide a more detailed explanation of the principles and overall operational process of Courtroom-FND, along with the specific construction of its prompts. As depicted in Fig. 1, the courtroom debate process begins with the input of a suspicious news article or statement. At this stage, meta prompts are carefully crafted to guide each role.
For instance, in Round 1, the Prosecution is instructed to argue that the news is fake by examining the content for logical inconsistencies, deceptive claims, or questionable sources, while the Defense is prompted to defend the news by presenting counter-evidence, reliable references, and contextual clarification. The Judge’s role is to neutrally evaluate the strength of arguments from both sides and deliver an interim verdict that may identify unresolved contradictions or suggest areas requiring further exploration.
Afterwards, in Round 2, role-switching compels the new Prosecution to critique the news from a fresh perspective, leveraging insights from the first round to strengthen its arguments. Simultaneously, the new Defense builds a robust case for the news’s credibility, addressing critiques raised earlier and offering additional explanations or evidence. The Judge evaluates these renewed arguments and issues a revised verdict, incorporating new insights and cross-referencing inconsistencies or confirmations between the two rounds.
Finally, the Judge synthesizes the outcomes of both rounds to provide a conclusive verdict, stating whether the news is fake or genuine and explaining the rationale with reference to critical evidence and arguments.
Systematically, the detailed courtroom debate process can be divided into the following procedures. - Initial News Input
- A suspicious news article or statement is given as the debate topic.

- Meta prompts initialize roles and instruct how each role should respond, as shown below.




General Meta Prompt - Round 1: Debate Start. Before the debate begins, the system prepares by assigning roles ( Prosecution , Defense , and Judge ) to the LLM agents and generating meta prompts to guide their responsibilities, as shown below.
- (a) Prosecution’s Argument: The Prosecution agent reads the news and attempts to prove its falsehood. It scrutinizes the text for contradictions, logical gaps, misleading claims, or unreliable sources.

- (b) Defense’s Counterargument: The Defense agent responds by defending the news item as genuine. It offers supporting context, verifiable sources, and coherent explanations, aiming to counter the Prosecution’s accusations.

- (c) Judge’s Interim Verdict: The Judge summarizes both perspectives, weighing the validity of evidence. The Judge then issues an interim decision, explaining which arguments seem stronger. If a clear winner emerges (fake vs. genuine), the Judge may state it. Otherwise, the verdict highlights unresolved issues.

The new Prosecution (previously Defense) now asserts the news is fake, while the new Defense (previously Prosecution) defends it as true. This reversal compels each side to explore alternative viewpoints and helps reveal hidden biases or overlooked details. Similarly, we have designed meta prompts to guide their responsibilities in this stage, as illustrated below.
- (a) New Revised Prosecution’s Argument: The new Prosecution (previously Defense) shifts its focus to discrediting the news, but each approaches it from an entirely fresh and distinct vantage point. Uses the knowledge and insights gained from the first round to formulate additional counterclaims.

- (b) New Revised Defense’s Counterargument: The newly assigned Prosecution critiques the news from the opposing perspective, highlighting potential biases, inaccuracies, or omissions, while the newly assigned Defense responds by reinforcing the credibility of the news, presenting new evidence or offering clarifications to address the misinterpretations or criticisms raised by the Prosecution.

- (c) Judge’s Revised Verdict: The Judge again evaluates both sides in light of the new arguments. This second verdict may confirm the initial verdict or contradict it, depending on how compelling the arguments are in the second round.




- Final Decision
- The Judge reviews the outcomes from both rounds comprehensively.

- By comparing contradictions and consistencies across arguments, the Judge delivers a final determination on whether the news is fake or genuine.

- The final output includes a verdict (“Fake” or “Genuine”) and a concise explanation summarizing the most decisive arguments and evidence.

In summary, Courtroom-FND offers a structured, role-based mechanism to tackle the complexities of modern fake news detection. By leveraging carefully crafted meta prompts and iterative debates—complete with role switching—it systematically uncovers hidden contradictions, thereby improving reliability, transparency, and overall performance in tasks requiring nuanced reasoning. Courtroom-FND methodology flow
In this section, we provide a detailed explanation of the Courtroom-FND Methodology Flow in Fig. 2 to guide the reader through the step-by-step process of the Courtroom-FND framework. The figure visually summarizes the methodology, illustrating the key phases and role assignments within the framework.
- Initial News Input & Meta Prompts Generation: The process begins with the introduction of a suspicious news article or statement. At this stage, meta prompts are generated to assign roles to the participants: Prosecution, Defense, and Judge;

- Round 1: Argument and Interim Verdict: In this round, the Prosecution argues that the news is fake, pointing out inconsistencies, unreliable sources, and contradictory evidence. Conversely, the Defense argues that the news is genuine, presenting verifiable data and logical reasoning;

- Round 2 (Role Switching): To ensure a more balanced and rigorous analysis, roles are switched in this round. The Prosecution now argues that the news is genuine, while the Defense argues that it is fake;

- Final Prediction: The final phase of the process involves the Judge synthesizing the findings from both rounds. By comparing the arguments and evidence from both rounds, the Judge delivers a conclusive ruling, determining whether the news is Fake or Genuine.


Overall, this figure illustrates the iterative, adversarial nature of the Courtroom-FND methodology, ensuring a more thorough, transparent, and reliable evaluation of news credibility.
LLM selection for prosecution, defense, and judge roles
To reduce cognitive biases and enhance the fairness of courtroom debates, the framework employs diverse LLMs for the roles of prosecution, defense, and judge. Using different models helps mitigate the risk of single-model biases and ensures a more balanced evaluation.
For first Prosecution role and second Defense role, we choose DeepSeek-V2 (DeepSeek-AI et al. 2024). DeepSeek-V2 is an advanced Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) language model with 236 billion parameters, of which only 21 billion are activated per token for efficient inference. Featuring innovative architectures like Multi-head Latent Attention (MLA) and DeepSeekMoE, it achieves high performance with reduced training costs, efficient memory usage, and enhanced generation throughput, making it a strong candidate for the debate role, which requires critical scrutiny of the news content.
For first round’s Defense role and second round’s Prosecution role, we choose Llama3-70B (Grattafiori et al. 2024; Touvron et al. 2023). Llama3-70B is a cutting-edge foundation model developed by Meta AI (Grattafiori et al. 2024) as part of the Llama 3 series. With 70 billion parameters, it supports multilinguality, advanced reasoning, and programming tasks. This model is designed for high versatility and robust performance across diverse AI applications while benefiting from extensive training on a high-quality, multi-source corpus. With its strong contextual understanding and nuanced reasoning abilities, Llama3-70B adopts a balanced approach to information analysis, allowing it to deliver well-founded counterarguments that align perfectly with the demands of the defense role.
For the Judge role, we choose GPT-4 (OpenAI 2023; Brown et al. 2020). GPT-4 is an advanced large language model that excels in logical reasoning, critical evaluation, and complex argument synthesis. With extensive pretraining on diverse and high-quality datasets, it demonstrates superior capabilities in assessing intricate debates and providing well-reasoned judgments. Its ability to integrate and analyze multifaceted arguments while remaining impartial makes it an ideal candidate for the Judge role, ensuring fair and transparent evaluations of the evidence and arguments presented by other LLM courtroom debaters.
The rationale for selecting distinct LLM agents for each role in the Courtroom-FND framework lies in the need to ensure diversified perspectives and balanced adjudication. By assigning different LLMs to the prosecution and defense roles, the framework incorporates diverse viewpoints, reducing the risk of systemic biases that could arise from relying on a single model. This strategic selection of models not only enhances the reliability and robustness of the debate process but also ensures transparency and minimizes potential biases in the assessment of fake news, ultimately improving the overall credibility of the Courtroom-FND framework.

Compared methods
Generally, we compare our Courtroom-FND framework with multiple competitive baselines. Specifically, we incorporate LLM-based prompting engineering methods such as Direct Prediction, ICL, CoT, Self-Consistency, Self-Reflect, and Analogical Prompting. To broaden the experimental comparisons and strengthen the persuasiveness of our findings, in addition to the LLM-based prompting methods, we also incorporate traditional PLM-based approaches (both fine-tuning and zero-shot). Below, we provide detailed descriptions of these compared methods.
- Direct Prediction with GPT-4.0, DeepSeek-V2, and Llama3-70B (OpenAI 2023; DeepSeek-AI et al. 2024; Touvron et al. 2023): These comparative baselines harness the advanced reasoning and contextual understanding capabilities of GPT-4.0, DeepSeek-V2, and Llama3-70B to make direct predictions. GPT-4.0 excels in handling nuanced queries and complex information processing. DeepSeek-V2 employs mixture-of-experts mechanisms for efficient and precise outputs, while Llama3-70B combines multilingual and context-sensitive reasoning capabilities for robust performance across tasks.

- Chain-of-thought (CoT) (Kojima et al. 2022; Wei et al. 2024; Xu et al. 2024): LLM-based Chain-of-thought implements chain-of-thought (CoT) prompting by adding the phrase “Let’s think step by step” to guide reasoning. This technique helps models break down complex problems into intermediate logical steps, significantly improving their performance on reasoning-intensive tasks;

- In-Context Learning (ICL) (Dong et al. 2024; Brown et al. 2020): LLM-based In-Context Learning provides example input-label pairs during inference, allowing LLMs to draw direct parallels and apply learned patterns to similar tasks. In experiments, we randomly selected three examples from the training set to serve as references, ensuring that LLMs could effectively utilize contextual information to improve accuracy;

- Self-Consistency (Wang et al. 2023; Chen et al. 2024; Zhang et al. 2023): LLM-based Self-consistency introduces a mechanism to sample multiple plausible answers and determine the final prediction through majority voting, which reduces noise in reasoning processes and increases robustness by exploring diverse reasoning paths;

- Self-Reflect (Shinn et al. 2023; Madaan et al. 2023): LLM-based Self-reflect requires the language model to iteratively refine its outputs until achieving a self-evaluated satisfactory result. This introspective approach enhances the overall quality of predictions by allowing the model to identify and correct its own errors;

- Analogical Prompting (Yasunaga et al. 2024; Webb et al. 2023): LLM-based Analogical prompting introduces analogical reasoning, enabling the model to generate its own task-relevant exemplars based on past patterns. Inspired by human reasoning experience, this method enhances adaptability and mitigates the reliance on labeled data;


Moreover, we also introduce traditional PLM-based fine-tuning approaches. Specifically, we incorporate three variations of the Bidirectional Encoder Representations from Transformers (BERT) model1: BERT B a s e - U n c a s e d , DistilBERT B a s e - U n c a s e d , and RoBERTa B a s e - U n c a s e d .
- BERT B a s e - U n c a s e d (Devlin et al. 2019): We implement a fine-tuned variant and a zero-shot prompting variant of BERT B a s e - U n c a s e d . In the fine-tuning scenario, the model parameters are updated using labeled examples from the training set. In the zero-shot prompting scenario, we utilize natural-language prompts (without parameter updates) to directly infer predictions.

- DistilBERT B a s e - U n c a s e d (Sanh et al. 2020): Following the same procedure, we train a fine-tuned DistilBERT model on the training data and compare it against a zero-shot prompting version. Although DistilBERT has fewer parameters and is more computationally efficient, including both its fine-tuned and zero-shot variants provides valuable insights into performance trade-offs between model size and training/fine-tuning strategies.

- RoBERTa B a s e - U n c a s e d (Liu et al. 2019): We similarly evaluate a fine-tuned RoBERTa-Base model alongside a zero-shot prompting variant. RoBERTa tends to leverage more extensive pre-training optimizations and tokenization strategies. By comparing its fine-tuned and zero-shot prompting outcomes, we obtain a more holistic perspective on how different pre-trained language models perform under various experimental conditions.


It is worth to note that our experimental setup-covering these LLM prompting methods-assumes no information from the training set (i.e., zero-shot usage). Therefore, it is expected that zero-shot results for smaller PLMs would generally be outperformed by their fine-tuned counterparts. Nevertheless, to ensure fairness, we also adopt a prompting-based zero-shot approach (Liu et al. 2022) for the three PLMs described below. In our experiments, we denote their zero-shot learning variants as BERT Uncased (zero), DistilBERT Uncased (zero), and RoBERTa Uncased (zero), allowing us to ensure that each baseline is evaluated under comparable conditions.
These additions allow us to gauge the performance disparities between traditional PLM-based methods (with and without fine-tuning) and the LLM-based prompting methods discussed earlier, thus enhancing the overall comprehensiveness and fairness of our experimental comparisons.

Evaluation metrics
In this study, Accuracy and Macro-F1-score both are chosen as the primary evaluation metrics to align with previous research in fake news detection. As widely accepted standards for classification tasks, these metrics effectively assess the model’s capability to predict labels correctly while ensuring a balanced evaluation of precision and recall.
Accuracy is determined by dividing the number of accurate predictions ( Count T ) by the total predictions made ( Count N ), as expressed in the following formula: 1
Accuracy = Count T Count N A prediction is deemed accurate when the label assigned by the model matches the true label. Higher Accuracy values indicate a more reliable model, particularly in classification tasks where the goal is to maximize the ratio of correct predictions to the total dataset. This metric is commonly utilized as a benchmark for evaluating model performance in fake news detection tasks.
F1-score, on the other hand, is incorporated to provide a more comprehensive assessment by balancing precision and recall, particularly in scenarios where class distribution may be imbalanced. It is defined as the harmonic mean of precision and recall: 2
F1-score = 2 × Precision × Recall Precision + Recall Where precision measures the proportion of correctly predicted positive instances among all predicted positives; recall measures the proportion of correctly predicted positive instances among all actual positives.
Macro-F1 is obtained by calculating the F1-score for each class separately and then taking the average across all classes: 3
Macro-F1 = 1 C ∑ i = 1 C F1-score i where C is the number of classes, and F1-score i represents the F1-score of class i .
Macro-F1-score is especially valuable in fake news detection, where both false positives and false negatives can have significant consequences. By averaging the F1-score across classes, this metric ensures a balanced evaluation even when the dataset is imbalanced2.
By considering both Accuracy and Macro-F1-score, this study ensures a robust evaluation of model performance, accounting for both overall correctness and the balance between precision and recall.
Adopted datasets
We conduct experiments on four challenging FND benchmarks, namely, FakeNewsClassification 3, NELA-GT-2022 4, LIAR 5, and Counter-Intuitive AR v1 6. The statistical details of the five datasets are summarized in Table 4.
- FakeNewsClassification (Raza et al. 2024): The FakeNewsClassification dataset is a meticulously curated resource for binary classification of fake and real news, comprising 10,000 political news articles annotated using a hybrid approach of GPT-4 Turbo and human expert review. The integration of AI-driven annotations with rigorous human verification ensures both efficiency and reliability. This dataset is designed to support advanced research in fake news detection, with a well-structured 80-10-10 split for training, validation, and testing.

- NELA-GT-2022 (Gruppi et al. 2023): The NELA-GT-2022 dataset is a large-scale, multi-labeled benchmark corpus for the study of misinformation in news articles. It contains 1,778,361 articles collected from 361 outlets between January 1, 2022, and December 31, 2022. The dataset uses distant supervision for labeling, inferring article veracity from source reliability ratings provided by the Media Bias/Fact Check (MBFC) database. Furthermore, following previous work (Raza et al. 2024), we extracted a sample of 10,00 articles from NELA-GT-2022, carefully maintaining a balance between fake and real labels and ensuring that each article contained at least 50 words to guarantee sufficient content for a meaningful analysis.

- Counter-Intuitive AR v1 (CIAR v1) (Kong et al. 2024): The Counter-Intuitive Arithmetic Reasoning (CIAR) dataset is designed to evaluate the deep reasoning capabilities of LLMs. It comprises 200 questions sourced from elicitation questions, web data, and hand-generated examples. The questions in CIAR are crafted with hidden traps that prompt intuitive yet incorrect answers, assessing an LLM’s ability to resist superficial cues. Each question necessitates a rigorous, multi-step reasoning process, testing the model’s capacity for complex problem-solving. Building upon CIAR, Counter-Intuitive AR v1 (CIAR v1) (Kong et al. 2024; Wu et al. 2019) is a specialized binary classification dataset tailored for determining the veracity of propositions that require intricate reasoning. This dataset challenges models to engage in deep logical analysis to accurately classify statements as true or false.

- LIAR (Wang 2017): The LIAR dataset is a benchmark for fake news detection, consisting of 12,836 short statements labeled for truthfulness, subject, context, speaker, and party affiliation, collected over a decade from PolitiFact.com. It features six fine-grained truthfulness labels and includes rich metadata for each statement, making it suitable for both linguistic and metadata-based machine learning approaches in misinformation research.

Hyperparameter settings and LLM backbones
In Courtroom-FND, the underlying mechanism is based on LLMs engaging in a structured dialogue to solve the problem of fake news detection. These models, acting in distinct roles ( Prosecution, Defense , and Judge ), rely on a set of key hyperparameters to guide their performance during the debate process. As this framework is primarily focused on LLM-driven question-answering and reasoning tasks, the critical hyperparameters involve settings that govern the behavior of these models during inference. The most important parameters include Temperature, Top-k, Top-p, Maximum Token Length, and Maximum Debate Length, which control the creativity, diversity, and coherence of the model outputs, as well as the debate length and the final verdict constraints.
(1) Temperature , for instance, determines the level of randomness in the model’s output. A lower temperature (e.g., 0.3) leads to more deterministic, consistent responses, making the arguments more structured and focused. Conversely, a higher temperature (e.g., 0.8) increases the diversity of responses, encouraging more varied but less predictable reasoning.
(2) Top-k Sampling is used to control the diversity of generated responses by restricting the model’s choices to the top k most likely words. By adjusting this, we balance between generating highly plausible, coherent arguments and fostering a more creative exploration of counterarguments.
(3) Top-p (Nucleus Sampling): Top-p randomly selects the next word from the set of words where the cumulative probability reaches p . The value of Top-p is dynamic and adapts to each context, as opposed to Top-k , which uses a fixed number of top candidates. We followed a typical setting of 0.95 for Prosecution/Defense and 0.8 for Judge to balance creativity with coherence in the debate process.
(4) Maximum Token Length: Given the nature of the debate and the necessity for a structured evaluation of arguments, the Maximum Token Length is set to 2048 tokens per round to ensure each side can present detailed and thorough arguments. This is particularly important as each round in the debate can involve complex reasoning and citation of multiple pieces of evidence.
(5) Maximum Debate Length: To further ensure rigorous and balanced reasoning, the framework enforces a Maximum Debate Length of 10 rounds, after which the Judge is mandated to issue a final verdict, synthesizing the arguments from both rounds. This ensures that the debate process is comprehensive and mitigates any over-reliance on a single round of reasoning.
In Courtroom-FND, we utilize three LLM agents for distinct roles: two debaters (Prosecution and Defense) and a Judge. The first Prosecution (and second Defense) role employs DeepSeek-V27 (DeepSeek-AI et al. 2024), a 236B Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) model activating 21B parameters per token for efficient inference. The first Defense (and second Prosecution) role uses Llama3-70B8 (Touvron et al. 2023), a highly versatile foundation model supporting multilinguality and advanced reasoning. For the Judge role, we leverage GPT-49 (OpenAI 2023), known for its superior logical reasoning and impartial evaluations. These models are selected to ensure diverse perspectives and minimize systemic biases in fake news detection. In the ablation study, we additionally incorporate GPT-3.5 Turbo10 (Brown et al. 2020; OpenAI 2023) and Qwen2-72B11 (Qwen et al. 2025) to compare their performance across different roles on the testbed.

From the performance of baseline methods, we find that in Direct Prediction with LLMs , among the direct prediction methods, GPT-4.0 performs the best, achieving 71.3% acc [70.8% macro-f1] on FakeNewsClassification and 66.7% acc [66.6 % macro-f1] on LIAR, surpassing DeepSeek-V2 (66.1% acc [65.6% macro-f1], 62.3% acc [59.5% macro-f1]) and Llama3-70B (60.5% acc [60.2% macro-f1], 56.9% acc [55.5% macro-f1]). However, even GPT-4.0 struggles with datasets requiring advanced reasoning, such as CIAR v1 (51.0% acc [46.5% macro-f1]), reflecting the inherent limitations of direct prediction without iterative evaluation or argument switching. In various advanced Prompting Strategies , self-consistency and self-reflect deliver the highest performance among advanced prompting techniques. Specifically, self-consistency achieves strong results on FakeNewsClassification (75.1% acc [75.1% macro-f1]) and LIAR (68.5% acc [68.0% macro-f1]), demonstrating the robustness of sampling-based reasoning. And self-reflect achieves the second-highest accuracy on NELA-GT-2022 (68.1% acc [68.0% macro-f1]) and CIAR v1 (57.0% acc [48.3% macro-f1]) by refining outputs iteratively. Other methods, such as In-Context Learning (ICL) (73.4% acc [71.8% macro-f1] on FakeNewsClassification) and Chain-of-Thought (CoT) (68.7% acc [67.9% macro-f1] on FakeNewsClassification), also perform well but are limited by their dependency on prompt quality and error propagation.
As for the performance of our Courtroom-FND framework, it achieves the highest performance across all four datasets, consistently outperforming all baseline methods. On FakeNewsClassification, it achieves an accuracy of 78.6% [77.5% macro-f1], surpassing the best baseline, Self-Consistency (75.1% acc [75.1% macro-f1]), by increasing ↑ 3.5% acc [2.4% macro-f1]. For LIAR, Courtroom FND achieves 74. 3% acc [74. 3% macro-f1], marking a notable improvement of ↑ 4.1% acc [4.4% macro-f1] over Self-Reflect (70.2% acc [69.9% macro-f1]). On NELA-GT-2022, the framework records 75.6% [74.6% macro-f1], outperforming Self-Reflect (68.1% acc [68.0% macro-f1]) by an impressive increasing of ↑ 7.5% acc [6.6% macro-f1]. Finally, on the most challenging dataset, CIAR v1, Courtroom-FND demonstrates a significant advantage, achieving 67.0% acc [64.9% macro-f1], which is about acc ↑ 10.0% [macro-f1 11.6%] higher than the next-best method, Self-Reflect (57.0% acc [53.3% macro-f1]). Especially, Courtroom-FND achieves significant improvements over GPT-4.0, DeepSeek-V2, and Llama3-70B, demonstrating the limitations of direct prediction and the necessity of structured frameworks for handling complex datasets like CIAR v1.
Moreover, we also compare the performance of PLM-based fine-tuning approaches ( BERT B a s e - U n c a s e d , RoBERTa B a s e - U n c a s e d , and DistilBERT B a s e - U n c a s e d ) against their zero-shot prompting counterparts and our Courtroom-FND. The results highlight a critical trade-off between training-set dependency and generalization capability, demonstrating the distinct advantages of Courtroom-FND in handling complex fake news detection tasks.
Firstly, PLM-based fine-tuned models show strong performance across all datasets, outperforming zero-shot learning methods. For example, RoBERTa B a s e - U n c a s e d follows a similar trend, achieving 89.6% accuracy on FakeNewsClassification and 83.5% accuracy on NELA-GT-2022, while its zero-shot version only scores 63.6% and 58.5% accuracy respectively. This superior performance, however, comes with a major drawback-it benefits from the training set’s knowledge. In contrast, zero-shot learning methods (BERT Uncased ( zero ) , RoBERTa Uncased ( zero ) , DistilBERT Uncased ( zero ) ) operate without exposure to the training set. Since training and test sets come from same distribution, the fine-tuned models absorb dataset-specific biases, leading to an advantage that does not necessarily translate well to unseen, real-world data.
Our Courtroom-FND outperforms both fine-tuned and zero-shot PLM-based approaches across all datasets, demonstrating its strength in balancing structured reasoning, iterative evaluation, and adaptability. On FakeNewsClassification , Courtroom-FND achieves 78.6% accuracy, significantly surpassing zero-shot PLM models (which range between 57%-64% ) and even outperforming advanced LLM prompting strategies (e.g., self-consistency at acc 75.1% ). Most notably, on CIAR v1 , Courtroom-FND achieves 67.0% accuracy, which is 10.0% higher than the best advanced prompting strategy (Self-Reflect at acc 57.0% ) and vastly superior to all zero-shot PLM baselines.
This underscores that while fine-tuned PLMs perform well due to training-set exposure, they lack generalization, whereas zero-shot PLMs are more generalizable but less competitive in performance. Courtroom-FND successfully bridges this gap, offering higher generalization, adaptability, and superior reasoning capabilities, making it the most effective solution for real-world fake news detection.
Overall, the results unequivocally demonstrate the superiority of the Courtroom-FND framework, which achieves state-of-the-art performance by combining multi-round debates, role switching, and iterative evaluations. These findings highlight the importance of structured, interactive methodologies in enhancing both the accuracy and reliability of fake news.

To help readers better understand the framework’s operation and reasoning details, we selected a classic case from the LIAR12 dataset, as shown in Fig. 4. This LLM reasoning case followed a structured, multi-role debate mechanism to assess the authenticity of news articles.
Initially, a suspicious news claim is presented as input, and roles are assigned: the Prosecution argues that the news is fake, while the Defense contends that it is genuine. Both sides present their arguments, scrutinizing the claim for inconsistencies, misleading statements, or corroborative evidence. [See PDF for image]
Fig. 4 A typical case from LIAR for illustrating Courtroom-FND’s multi-round debate process with role switching and final verdict


In Round 1 , the Prosecution and Defense present their respective cases, after which the Judge evaluates both sides and issues an interim verdict. However, the case remains inconclusive, the roles are switched in Round 2 , where the previous Prosecution now defends the news, and the previous Defense argues against it. This role-switching ensures a more balanced analysis and exposes potential biases or overlooked points. The Judge evaluates the new arguments and synthesizes the information from both rounds to deliver a Final Verdict , determining whether the news is genuine or fake.
This iterative process improves the robustness and transparency of fake news detection by encouraging critical thinking, revealing hidden contradictions, and ensuring that all perspectives are rigorously examined. Overall, this dynamic, adversarial approach ensures a comprehensive evaluation of the news’ authenticity, making the Courtroom-FND method a powerful tool in tackling fake news detection.
Discussion
The Courtroom-FND framework presents a novel approach to fake news detection by emulating the dynamics of courtroom debates, thereby leveraging the strengths of LLMs in a structured and interactive manner. This discussion elucidates the key findings from our experiments, contextualizes them within the existing body of research, explores the broader implications of our approach, and acknowledges the limitations and potential avenues for future investigation.
Interpretation of experimental results
Our extensive experiments across diverse and challenging datasets have consistently demonstrated that Courtroom-FND outperforms traditional fake news detection methods, including state-of-the-art LLM-based approaches like GPT-4. The superior performance can be attributed to several factors inherent in the courtroom debate framework:
1. Enhanced Reasoning through Role Switching : By alternating the roles of prosecution and defense, Courtroom-FND facilitates a comprehensive exploration of both sides of a claim. This bidirectional argumentation mitigates the risk of model bias towards a single perspective, ensuring a more balanced and thorough evaluation of the news content.
2. Robustness in Complex Scenarios : The framework’s ability to handle controversial and misleading media posts highlights its robustness. The iterative debate process allows for the dissection of nuanced and multifaceted misinformation, which static models might overlook or misinterpret.
3. Improved Explainability : The structured debate provides clear and interpretable reasoning paths for the final verdict. This transparency is crucial for building trust in automated fake news detection systems, as stakeholders can trace the logical progression of arguments leading to the conclusion.
Comparison with existing methods
Traditional fake news detection approaches predominantly rely on static feature extraction, shallow textual analysis (Choras et al. 2020; Goldani et al. 2021; Jin et al. 2024), or propagation-based models (Nguyen et al. 2020; Wu et al. 2023; Wu and Hooi 2023; Mehta et al. 2022; Jin et al. 2025) that depend on social context. While these methods have achieved commendable results, they often fall short in scenarios where social contextual information is limited or rapidly evolving. In contrast, Courtroom-FND’s interactive debate mechanism circumvents the dependency on external social data, focusing instead on the intrinsic linguistic and semantic properties of the news content.
Moreover, existing LLM-based methods such as Chain-of-Thought (CoT) (Wei et al. 2024; Kojima et al. 2022; Xu et al. 2024; Lyu et al. 2023) and In-Context Learning (ICL) (Dong et al. 2024; Brown et al. 2020) paradigms, despite their advanced reasoning capabilities, encounter challenges like error propagation and dependency on prompt quality. Courtroom-FND addresses these issues by incorporating multi-role interactions and argument switching, which enhance the depth and reliability of the reasoning process.
Mitigating LLM hallucinations through structured role-based debates
One of the known limitations of LLMs is their tendency to generate hallucinations-statements that are syntactically plausible but factually inaccurate or unverifiable. This presents a critical challenge for tasks such as fake news detection, which inherently demand high factual accuracy and interpretability. Our proposed Courtroom-FND incorporates several built-in mechanisms that collectively serve to mitigate the effects of LLM hallucinations.
1. Structured Debate with Role-based Constraints : The core of our Courtroom-FND is a structured, multi-role debate setting in which different LLM agents take on the roles of prosecution, defense, and judge. Each role is guided by dedicated prompts with specific objectives-either to prove the news is fake, defend its authenticity, or provide an impartial assessment. This process ensures that each claim is critically examined from opposing perspectives. Consequently, hallucinated claims are more likely to be identified and rebutted, reducing the risk of unverified information influencing the final outcome.
2. Unique Role-Switching Mechanism : To further reinforce robustness, we implement a role-switching mechanism in Courtroom-FND: the prosecution and defense roles are reversed after the first round of debate. This encourages each LLM agent to re-evaluate the same content from a completely new standpoint. By compelling the models to argue both for and against the same piece of news, Courtroom-FND promotes deeper reasoning and exposes potential inconsistencies, contradictions, or hallucinations that may have gone unnoticed in a single-pass evaluation.
3. Multi-Model Diversity for Redundancy and Bias Reduction : To prevent hallucinations from being reinforced by a single model’s biases, we deliberately assign different LLMs to each role in the debate. For example, DeepSeek-V2 (DeepSeek-AI et al. 2024) is used for the Prosecution, Llama3-70B (Touvron et al. 2023; Grattafiori et al. 2024) for the Defense, and GPT-4 (OpenAI 2023; Mao et al. 2024) for the Judge. This model heterogeneity ensures that no single model’s generative tendencies dominate the decision-making process. This diversity acts as an additional safeguard against hallucinated information shaping the final verdict unchallenged.
Through the combination of structured role-playing, adversarial debate, role switching, and model diversity, Courtroom-FND effectively reduces the likelihood and impact of hallucinated content in LLM-based fake news detection.
Limitations
Despite its promising advancements, Courtroom-FND is not without limitations:
1. Computational Complexity : The multi-role and iterative nature of the framework inherently demands greater computational resources compared to single-pass detection models. This could pose challenges in real-time applications or deployment in resource-constrained environments. Specifically, the performance of CourtRoom-FND scales with the dataset volume, meaning that larger datasets will increase processing time and costs. Since CourtRoom-FND employs multiple rounds of debates ( prosecution, defense, and judge ), each fake-news instance processed adds to the computational load. Therefore, in the future, we will utilize parallelization or distributed computing to handle very large datasets in successive practical deployments for online real-time news detection applications with high frequency.
2. Dependency on LLMs (Reasoning Time & Fee Cost) : The effectiveness of Courtroom-FND is closely tied to the capabilities of the underlying LLMs. Any limitations or biases present in the LLMs can propagate through the debate process, potentially affecting the detection outcomes. Simultaneously, each "round" of debate consumes computational resources, including time spent on the outputs and token consumption of LLMs. So, the computational demands of some "expensive" LLMs such as Llama3.1-405B (Touvron et al. 2023), DeepSeek-R1 (DeepSeek-AI et al. 2024), and GPT-4o (OpenAI 2023; Mao et al. 2024) can significantly increase processing time and (token fee) costs. Thus, in the future, we will consider techniques like model distillation (Sanh et al. 2020), more efficient LLMs, or limiting the maximum number of debate rounds to enhance detection efficiency without significantly sacrificing predictive quality.
3. Role Allocation and Management : While role switching enhances argument diversity, it also introduces complexity in managing consistent and coherent role transitions. As each role of prosecution, defense, and judge, has its own specific objectives and argumentative style, ensuring that these roles maintain their argumentative integrity throughout the process is a challenge. This requires careful coordination to ensure that each role continues to fulfill its designated function, even as the agents switch positions. Managing the continuity of arguments and preventing any confusion or contradictions during the transitions between roles is crucial for maintaining the reliability of the overall reasoning process.
4. Scalability to Multimodal Content : Currently, Courtroom-FND is primarily text-based, focusing on analyzing written content to detect fake news. However, to handle multimodal misinformation, which may involve a combination of text, images, videos, or audio content, would require significant methodological adaptations. In the future, we plan to extend Courtroom-FND to handle multimodal misinformation involving images, videos, or audio content requires significant methodological adaptations.
Future work
Building upon the foundations of Courtroom-FND, several directions merit further exploration:
1. Multilingual and Multimodal Extensions : In the furute, we plan to expand Courtroom-FND to accommodate multiple languages and integrate non-textual data sources (Comito et al. 2023; Feng et al. 2024; Qi et al. 2024), which we believe will enhance its applicability across diverse and global contexts. Specifically, expanding Courtroom-FND to accommodate multiple languages will require addressing linguistic nuances and cultural context. We could achieve this by leveraging multilingual LLMs (MLLMs), such as Multilingual BERT (M-BERT) (Pires et al. 2019) or XLM-R (Conneau et al. 2020), which have been trained on diverse linguistic datasets. Developing a unified variant of Courtroom-FND that processes both textual and non-textual data could improve performance in scenarios where visual or audio misinformation is prevalent, such as deepfakes (Abbas and Taeihagh 2024) or manipulated images.
2. Automated Role Allocation : Developing mechanisms for dynamic and context-aware role assignment could streamline the debate process, ensuring optimal argumentation strategies tailored to specific scenarios. Inspired by this idea, we aim to develop dynamic mechanisms that allocate roles based on the complexity of the debate topic or the expertise required for each role. Specifically, we plan to involve using topic modeling techniques to categorize news content and assigning the prosecution and defense roles to agents specialized in those topics. Integrating this adaptive role switching mechanism could allow agents to switch based on evolving arguments within the debate, further enhancing the framework’s robustness.
3. Integration with Reinforcement Learning : We plan to incorporate reinforcement learning (RL) techniques with our Courtroom-FND’s LLM agents. This can enable the framework to adapt and refine its debate strategies based on feedback, thereby improving its resilience against evolving misinformation tactics. Specifically, we aim to create a robust RL-related reward function, drawing inspiration from DeepSeek’s Group Relative Policy Optimization (GPRO) (Shao et al. 2024) and OpenAI GPT’s Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF). This reward function will evaluates the effectiveness of arguments based on both the quality and persuasiveness of the claims made. Through this, agents could receive feedback that allows them to adapt their strategies in real time, improving their performance in future debates.
4. Ethical Considerations and Bias Mitigation : In the further research, we also needed to systematically identify and mitigate any inherent biases within the debate process, ensuring fairness and impartiality in detection outcomes. Adversarial training techniques or bias correction algorithms could be integrated into the Courtroom-FND framework to actively mitigate any identified biases. These efforts could ensure that LLM agents used do not propagate harmful stereotypes or misinformation, particularly in sensitive topics like politics or public health.
Conclusions
In this paper, we introduced Courtroom-FND, a novel multi-role fake news detection framework inspired by courtroom debate principles. By assigning roles of prosecution, defense, and judge-and enabling role switching-our approach systematically uncovers potential biases and fosters comprehensive scrutiny of news authenticity. Through extensive experiments across diverse and challenging datasets, Courtroom-FND demonstrated substantial improvements over conventional FND methods and even advanced large language models such as GPT-4. Several key factors underpin its success. First, structured debate promotes divergent thinking and balances arguments, leading to deeper exploration of evidence and reasoning pathways. Second, argument switching forces opposing roles to contest each other’s claims from multiple standpoints, mitigating cognitive bias and refining the final verdict. Third, the multi-role design integrates the strengths of LLMs while maintaining transparency and explainability, as the judge must base its decisions on verifiable arguments presented by both sides. Overall, Courtroom-FND takes a significant stride toward more reliable, interpretable, and adaptive FND solutions. By leveraging debate-inspired methodologies and the powerful language understanding capabilities of LLMs, our approach offers a promising new avenue for addressing the persistent and ever-changing challenges posed by fake news.
