This article records how Dickens's celebrity and the print culture of the time caused a trivial item of disinformation about him to achieve brief but widespread currency.
A Letter
In November 1868, Charles Pope, a printer and collector of subscriptions for the London-based Printers' Pension, Almshouse and Orphan Asylum Corporation, received a letter:

Bedford Hotel, Brighton,
Nov. 7, 1868.
Sir,—Referring to the appeal in aid of the Printers' Almshouses, I beg to say I shall be happy to devote the gross amount of my next reading in London to the fund. What should I have been without printers? I am grateful, and wish to show it.
Yours faithfully,
CHARLES DICKENS.
Mr. C. Pope. 1

The letter was evidently written in response to a notice which had appeared recently in London newspapers:

PRINTERS' ALMSHOUSES.—At the council meeting held at the London Tavern on Tuesday [3 November] … a letter was read … announcing that the late Henry Wright, Esq., of Kingston, had bequeathed 2000 l . to build the second wing of the above most worthy institution. The collector was requested to make use of his most strenuous exertions to obtain the remainder of the 1000 guineas (now being collected) required for erecting the first wing, so that the bequest of the liberal donor may become available for the completion of these most excellent almshouses. Subscriptions will be most thankfully received by the collector, Mr. C. Pope, 14, Derby-street, Argyle-square, W.C. 2

Context
A Printers' Pension Society had been established in 1827, and its amalgamation with other, related charitable initiatives in the 1860s gave rise to the Printers' Pension, Almshouse, and Orphan Asylum Corporation. 3 This body operated a handsome twelve-apartment almshouse building at Wood Green, then a rural area north of central London. The charity regularly solicited donations, requests for which, perhaps because of the particular goodwill of newspapers to its beneficiaries, often appeared not as regular, paid-for advertisements but as short paragraphs between other minor "news" items. Here is the comic journal Punch in 1857, good-humoredly teasing the charity about its frequent solicitations (while still broadcasting its appeal):

THE OLD PRINTER'S HAVEN.
THE enjoyment of the intellectual pleasure afforded by these and other pages is greatly enhanced by the beauty and clearness of the type which is the vehicle for the conveyance of our profound, poetical, and facetious ideas … [However,] the gratification afforded by nice typography is purchased by the amaurosis of pressmen, and the cataract of compositors. Some working Printers, moreover, as well as some other people, live to be old and infirm, and few who do … have been able to provide for that contingency. …
What fate, then, awaits the poor old Printer, who is too much a Christian to commit suicide, and who probably cannot see his way to do so if he would? … There exists a charitable … receptacle for them … called the PRINTERS' ALMSHOUSES. …
The … Annual Report … has lately been published. … We will now quote as much … as it is necessary to quote–the portion of a sentence: –
"Our list of Annual Subscribers is not so large as could be wished."
All persons addicted to the practice of charity are invited to consider whether the above brief statement may not suggest to them a way for indulging their besetting propensity. To any wealthy individual who has never tried the luxury of feeding the hungry, and clothing the naked, the PRINTERS' ALMSHOUSES may be recommended as affording a good case for a first experiment. … any amount of money … will be received with rapture by … the Collector, MR. C. POPE, 14, Derby Street, King's Cross, London. 4

For many years Dickens had been a supporter of the Printers' Pension Society, acting as its annually appointed president in 1843 and 1864. At the Society's anniversary festival on 6 April 1864, in particular, he had spoken warmly of the debt owed by society to the printing profession:

The printer is a faithful servant, not only of those connected with the business, but of the public at large, and has, therefore, when labouring under infirmity or disease, an especial claim on all for support. Without claiming for him the whole merit of the work produced by his skill, labour, endurance, and intelligence; without it what would be the state of the world at large? Why, tyrants and humbugs in all countries would have everything their own way!
… The printer is the friend of intelligence, of thought; he is the friend of liberty, of freedom, of law; indeed, the printer is the friend of every man who is the friend of order; the friend of every man who can read. Of all inventions, of all the discoveries in science or art, of all the great results in the wonderful progress of mechanical energy and skill, the printer is the only product of civilization necessary to the existence of free man. 5

The 1868 Appeal
By 1868 the printers' almshouse building was fully occupied, and the Corporation sought funds to construct a wing to increase its capacity. Wright's bequest to enable a second wing once funds for the first had accumulated led the charity to redouble its efforts.
On the afternoon of Saturday 7 November, the date appended to Pope's letter, their celebrated former president had read in public as part of his professional "Farewell Series" tour of London and the provinces. The reading had been at Brighton, where he habitually stayed at the seafront Bedford Hotel (the address appended to the letter)–as he did on this occasion, together with his touring manager George Dolby. 6 The letter mentioned "my next reading in London." Dickens was due to read there on 17 November. The venue was to be the two-thousand-capacity St. James's Hall in Piccadilly and tickets at 1, 3, 5 or 7 shillings were already selling briskly. Such a handsome intervention as the letter suggested would raise the profile of the printers' charitable campaign and contribute to its fund enormously. Mr. Pope lost no time in communicating the good news to the press.
The News Spreads
By 10 November, a paragraph appeared in London newspapers, beginning "In answer to an appeal made by Mr. Pope, collector of funds for the Printers' Almshouses, Mr. Charles Dickens has sent the following reply," and quoting from the letter. The following day the piece was reprinted in newspapers of large provincial towns: Birmingham, Bristol, Glasgow and Liverpool. Two days later the letter was being published in Manchester, Edinburgh and smaller towns like Bolton, Bradford and Dundee. On Saturday 14 November provincial week-end newspapers picked it up. Eventually the news filtered through to smaller concerns like the Leighton Buzzard Observer , Soulby's Ulverston Advertiser and the Whitstable Times . 7
The news itself, of course, was of only minor general interest, and its propagation was due in part to the custom of publications to reprint as column-fillers material taken from contemporaries. Its celebrity angle was also an attractant, however, and its message positive. Some publications simply reprinted the original paragraph, but others added a cheerful gloss:

ONE of the greatest charms of CHARLES DICKENS is his generous frankness in speaking of himself. The latest instance of this is found in the great novelist's reply to an appeal made to him for help towards a fund for erecting a new wing to the Printers' Almshouses.
Mr. DICKENS, in more ways than one, is a good friend to the printers.
Mr. Charles Dickens, in a graceful note, acknowledges how much he is indebted to printers.
Mr. Charles Dickens has done a generous thing which deserves to be made known, both on account of the thing itself and the manner in which he has done it. 8

Denial
Very shortly after the dissemination of the news about the donation began, a startling announcement appeared in a London journal; it was signed by the promoters of the reading tour on which Dickens was then engaged:

We beg to inform you that the letter published in your paper of this day, purporting to be addressed by Mr Charles Dickens to Mr Pope, the secretary [ sic ] of the Printers' Almshouses, is a stupid forgery.
Chappell & Co. 9

This unwelcome turn of events was, in turn, rapidly and widely communicated in the press:

We are sorry to read this, for more reasons than one.
Mr. DICKENS's sympathy with the printers is no doubt as warm as the letter averred, but it turns out that the letter itself is "a stupid forgery."
Sundry journalists have been lavish of their approbation of [Dickens's] "handsome conduct," considering it a specially neat way adopted by the great novelist to do a little for the printers, who have done so much for him. In the midst of their admiration [comes the news] … "that the letter … is a stupid forgery."
If meant for a joke [the letter] is an extremely pointless one, and if for a hint it is utterly undeserved. Mr. Dickens has never forgotten those who have been the means of communicating his thoughts to the world, and requires no such reminder. Mr. Dickens's hand is rather a remarkable one, and it must have been a clever imitation to deceive our contemporary, or else the letter did not receive a sufficient scrutiny.
The facetious gentlemen who employ their leisure time in getting up hoaxes for the journals have had … unusual fortune … They have obtained circulation for a mock letter from Mr. C. Dickens to the … Printers' Pension Society … stating that the entire proceeds of the next of his readings would go to the funds of the charity … These persons appear to have a double object in obtaining publicity … the first that of indicating what should be by a fanciful account of what is; the second, that of pure amusement. We have no doubt theirs will be the experience of most knaves. They will for a time play upon the public with impunity, but in the long run they will be found out and punished as they deserve. 10

For a time in November 1868, while some newspapers carried news of the donation, others simultaneously printed its denial.
The embarrassed Printers' charity sought to make its position clear:

THE RECENT HOAX.
To the Editor of the CLERKENWELL NEWS.
SIR,—Will you allow me space, on the part of the Council of the Printers' Pension, Almshouse, and Orphan Asylum Corporation, and also myself as secretary, to enter a most positive disclaimer of any knowledge whatever of the "stupid forgery," as Messrs. Chappell properly describe the letter purporting to have been written by Mr. Charles Dickens to Mr. Pope, the collector. I have ascertained that not any one connected with the institution (save Mr. Pope) has up to this hour seen the letter, and I desire, on the part of the executive of the corporation, publicly to express the sincere regret they feel at the annoyance which it must have caused Mr. Dickens, and also at the injury which it is calculated to inflict upon the interests of the corporation. Any assistance that can be offered towards the discovery of the perpetrator of the malicious hoax will be cheerfully rendered by the corporation—I am, &c.,
J. S. HODSON, Secretary.
34, Falkland-road, Kentish Town, Nov. 13. 11

And the newspaper printed a sympathetic response:

Mr HODSON … writes to express his great regret that such an impudent hoax should have been committed … It may be the writer had some vague notion that he would benefit the printers' institution … by thus covertly pointing out that which he would like to see done … Mr. CHARLES DICKENS is too much a man of spirit and discernment to be worked upon by any such an absurd plan, and it is quite clear … that if the great novelist had been seriously appealed to he would readily have lent such aid … as he considered due … 12

Persistence of the Story
Long after its denial, the fake news of Dickens's generosity continued to spread. By late November notice of the original letter was being published in America; by mid-February 1869 the news had reached New Zealand; and by late March it appeared in Australian newspapers. 13 In most cases the item attracted little editorial comment, but here is the Dallas Herald in March 1869 echoing, somewhat less elegantly, the kind of sentiments which Dickens himself had expressed when addressing the company at the Printers' Anniversary Dinner four years before:

Fewest persons deserve more respect and more thanks than the printers. It is to their indomitable energy and midnight labor that the literary aspirant owes his fame, the ambitious statesman his renown and the successful general his notoriety.
By depriving themselves of many pleasures, the printers thus devote a long life to the benefit of others, and often after spending their best years in the service of the public, are on their retirement, reminded by upstarts that they were printers. Printers–as if any scorn could be attached to a vocation among whose followers can be classed many pure and eminent men.
Hear what the immortal Dickens said of the profession, when called upon to subscribe for the proposed erection, in England, of printers' almshouses: [Thereafter follows the text of the letter.] 14

Dickens's purported offer was still further broadcast in 2002, when text from the letter, gathered from an 1868 newspaper, was included as genuine in the Pilgrim Edition of his collected correspondence ( Letters 12: 219).
With the Benefit of Hindsight
In retrospect, it is possible to detect lack of authenticity in the letter Pope received. Dickens had certainly read, in the past, for charitable causes, usually by invitation, and often in connection with welfare initiatives concerned with education and encouragement of literary pursuits. And his name frequently appeared in published lists of donors to charitable causes. But since taking up professional reading in 1858, he had read for charity very rarely. Would he, at short notice, having received no direct application from a supplicant, and without reference to its promoters, have turned over the proceeds of a previously scheduled, highly lucrative professional public reading? And, although eulogising the work of their profession, would he really have written something so self-deprecating as "What should I have been without printers?" Or so ingenuous as "I am grateful, and wish to show it"? More prosaically, even the letter's "Yours faithfully" was not his customary valediction.
Dickens, Gossip and Misinformation
There are many instances of news items about Dickens which rapidly spread through the international press. Famously, in 1858–59, accounts of London gossip alleging third-party involvement in the breakdown of his marriage, although largely suppressed in the capital's press, appeared rapidly in provincial British, Irish, American and Australian newspapers. 15 Such stories were often partially (and in some cases totally) inaccurate; some were no doubt recounted maliciously; but they do not appear to have involved much if any deliberate invention and propagation of information known to be false.
Dickens, as a public figure, was also commonly the subject of newspaper stories of doubtful accuracy about less sensational matters. Here are two examples among many, where Dickens felt constrained to correct–reasonably good-humoredly in these cases–misinformation about himself in the press:

[From Boulogne, 18 September 1853:] I observe two statements from a country paper, copied into your columns [of The Times ] of Saturday last, and therefore made important. They represent me as having availed myself of the experiences of that excellent police-officer, Mr. Inspector Field, in Bleak House , and also as having undertaken to write the said excellent officer's biography. Allow me to assure you that, amid all the news in the Times , I found nothing more entirely and completely new to me than these two pieces of intelligence.
Your faithful servant,
CHARLES DICKENS. 16


[From Gads Hill Place, 3 September 1867:] In a number of letters that this morning's post brings me, your journal [the London Sunday Gazette ] is quoted as authority for my being in "a critical state of health," and also for certain recommendations supposed to have been applied to my dismal case by "eminent surgeons." You will not think me … ungrateful for your interest … if I beg you to contradict these statements as absurdly unfounded. I am perfectly well …
Faithfully yours,
CHARLES DICKENS.
P.S.–It has been suggested by a sagacious farmer and member of my village club here, that a "critical" state of health may originally have been a misprint for "cricketing," in consequence of our having lately won a match.
( Letters 11: 420) 17

He also observed, in connection with reports about his supposed illness, how widespread such stories were apt to become:

I notice that about once in every seven years, I become the victim of a paragraph-disease. It breaks out in England, travels to India by the Overland Route, gets to America per Cunard Line, strikes the base of the Rocky Mountains, and, rebounding back to Europe, mostly perishes on the steppes of Russia from inanition and extreme cold.
( Letters 11: 419)

And, in another context, he commented on the origin of the inaccuracy of at least some of such "news" items: "I think 'our London Correspondent' one of the greatest nuisances of this time, in as much as our London Correspondent, seldom knowing anything, feels bound to know everything, and becomes in consequence a very reckless gentleman in respect of the truthfulness of his intelligence" ( Letters 5: 678).
The misinformation in many such stories no doubt did arise from a journalistic over-eagerness to supply newspaper readers with items about the doings of a popular celebrity. But it is unlikely that many were composed deliberately to deceive. Very different were some other news items, about a variety of matters, which were prevalent in newspapers of the time.
Fake News in 1860s Newspapers
"Fake news," a neologism currently used in a variety of contexts, is most commonly understood to refer to the dissemination of disinformation; that is, information known by its author to be false. Its transmission may be associated with an intention to deceive, confuse, divert the attention of, or influence the opinions, beliefs and actions of recipients. There may also be an intention to discredit, embarrass and generally cause mischief for the subject(s) of the "news," or others. A common (but not universal) feature of fake news is its inclusion of some content either already known by its recipients to be true, or which is readily credible to them and reinforces their prejudices. In many cases, the origin and intention of the disinformation remain obscure.
In the late 1860s, the British press was full of news of doubtful authenticity: rumors, false alarms, hoaxes, statements represented as fact followed by retractions abounded. False reports of impending Fenian outrages were common, 18 but many other dubious stories also circulated. "Hoaxing the Papers," seemed, for a time, to become a commonplace, and concomitant forgery, for some people, a favorite pastime. 19 A London newspaper noted that a European contemporary intended to publish a daily " Bulletin des Mensonges ," so as to keep canards distinct from authentic news, and, although commending the effort, considered that it would be difficult to implement in London, "in days when inventors of hoaxes 'lie like truth,' and forge people's names without scruple, while editors neither attempt to verify signatures nor apologise for errors." 20
Among such deceptions were two which unfolded shortly before the printers' charity affair and perhaps deserve particular attention.
The Crédit Foncier of England Limited
In summer 1868, this London finance house, previously represented as flourishing, and having awarded its directors large commissions, was abruptly announced to be in difficulties. Shareholder losses were considerable. The Times , in a powerful leader, urged the directors to refund their commissions. 21 This elicited a letter:

THE CREDIT FONCIER OF ENGLAND
TO THE EDITOR OF THE TIMES.
Sir,–I have read your article … and, without going into the causes which induced me to resign … my seat at the board of direction, I cannot but express my regret that I was ever connected with the company in any shape or way, and feeling that there is great justice in your remarks, and that the principle upon which myself and others appropriated a very large sum of money was not a fair one … I intend to make restitution; and shall, as soon as my pecuniary arrangements can be made for such a purpose, pay into the hands of the company's bankers, to an account which I shall open as "A Restitution Fund Account," the sum I consider I have wrongfully received on account of imaginary or unrealized profits. I think the amount will be something over 18,000 l .
I am, Sir, your obedient servant,
J. S. WORTLEY.
Royal Hotel, Lowestoft, Aug. 21. 22

The announcement was received in the press with much satisfaction, 23 but was almost immediately followed by the following letter:

TO THE EDITOR OF THE TIMES.
Sir,—In The Times of this day I have read with astonishment a letter addressed to you and apparently bearing my signature. I have written no such letter … I was never at "Lowestoft" in my life … and I … have never received such a sum as "18,000 l ." in connexion with the Crédit Foncier, or any sum in any degree approaching to that amount.
I am, Sir, your obedient servant,
J. STUART WORTLEY.
Wortley Hall, Sheffield, Aug. 24. 24

This left The Times reflecting ruefully on having been gulled and previously complimentary contemporaries spitefully enjoying its discomfiture. 25 The culprit was not identified.
The Victoria and Albert Convalescent Hospital
On 1 June 1867 The Times carried a news item previously supplied by a London correspondent to a west-country provincial newspaper:

ROYAL MUNIFICENCE.–The Queen is about to perform an act of great munificence. During the five years that she has remained in retirement 26 she has necessarily accumulated a large portion of her privy purse allowance, which, under happier circumstances, would have been spent in Royal hospitality both to her own subjects and to foreign Princes. Her Majesty, desiring it should not be supposed that she had saved this money for parsimonious reasons, has resolved to give no less a sum than half a million sterling for the erection of a convalescent hospital. The matter hitherto has been kept very quiet, but it is believed that the money has been already made over to trustees, and the Royal bounty will before long be made known officially. The new institution will be built more especially in connexion with St. Bartholomew's Hospital, and the treasurer and other officials of that renowned charity will be among the trustees. It is proposed to spend about 200,000 l . in purchasing the site and erecting the building, and to keep the remainder of the sum in hand for the present. Of course, the Convalescent Hospital will be built at a considerable distance from London, so as to give the inmates the advantage of pure air. 27

The news was very widely broadcast and soon elaborated. Independent sources, it was said, had confirmed its accuracy. The name of the facility had been decided: it was to be the "Victoria and Albert Convalescent Hospital." 28
Four days after breaking the news, The Times printed, without strapline or other editorial gloss, a short statement: "We are authorized to contradict the reports which have been in circulation for the last few days as to the intention of the Queen to endow a Convalescent Hospital." 29 A heated press discussion followed. The Times , presumably reluctant to participate more directly in what was now an embarrassing situation, simply reprinted the views of a contemporary. These were (unsurprisingly) that the object of the fraud was to cause annoyance and vexation, and (reassuringly) that blame attached not to those who propagated the falsehood but to its anonymous creator. Of more interest, in the light of present-day considerations of the relationship between fake news and the expansion of social media, was the observation that such deceptions, "are not … becoming less common in this country as newspapers increase in number and in circulation." 30
Two deceptions then, not wholly dissimilar to the one involving Dickens and the printers' charity.
Conclusions
As we have seen, newspapers struggled to understand the motive for the printer's charity hoax, suggesting either a hope (surely always forlorn) of shaming Dickens into making a donation, or a simple mindless wish (if so, surely fulfilled) to make mischief.
When considering the two additional deceptions discussed here, it is hard to avoid regarding them as malicious attempts to discomfort a target perceived to be unjustly privileged; that is, respectively, a businessman self-rewarded while his shareholders suffered, and a Queen receiving public monies while failing to perform public duties. It seems unlikely that the perpetrators expected their actions to be effective in righting the perceived "wrongs." It is perhaps more probable that safely anonymous, highly public swipes were being aimed at targets who seemed otherwise impervious. Could the printers' charity letter be viewed in the same light? At the time it was written newspapers were printing outrageously large estimates of the sum Dickens had purportedly amassed by professionally reading in America–between £40,000 and £50,000 was being quoted. 31 Perhaps juxtaposition of such reports with the newspaper appeals for charity seemed to a disinterested observer inconsonant. 32
Outcomes
Dickens continued his schedule of readings. The Printers' Corporation continued to collect funds for its Almshouse extension, which eventually opened in August 1871. The charitable organisation itself now flourishes as the Printing Charity, 33 and has a welfare and education focus which extends across the printing, publishing, packaging and graphic arts sectors. The author of the forged letter from Brighton remained unidentified. 34

William F. Long
William F. Long is Emeritus Professor in Biochemistry at the University of Aberdeen. He has published numerous articles for The Dickensian and Dickens Quarterly and contributed to the Oxford Readers' Companion to Dickens .
