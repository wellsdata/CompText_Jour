Introduction
Trust in science is a key determinant for public acceptance of human-caused climate change 1 . However, conveying scientific information is complex and multidirectional in the contemporary digital media ecology 2,3 . In most cases, science news is filtered through several media channels and platforms before it reaches its intended publics. Each of these is a place where a narrative can be distorted or changed: often intentionally framed in a particular way to achieve certain outcomes 4 . Such filtering and framing 5 is consequential for the science messages that people receive, transform and pass on. This process, therefore, requires explicit examination if we are to better disentangle its potential to influence public understanding of science issues and, relatedly, attitudes to climate change risk and mitigation 6 . Given the critical urgency for action on climate change and biodiversity loss, tracing the origin and complex trajectory of science messages into and throughout the public domain is critical to understanding how narratives about climate change and its impacts evolve. Examining these processes can serve to inform strategies and tactics for communicating science—climate science in particular—in a way that supports public trust and acceptance and impels evidence-based policy making.
The role of the media in disseminating information, and misinformation, about science has been extensively researched 7, 8–9 . The literature shows that science information is typically a small portion of overall news, that it is usually written by non-subject specialists 10 and that it risks being over-simplified in its media ‘translation’ 11 . Science messages can also become politicised through behind-the-scenes production factors and in the process of their distribution across media landscapes 12, 13–14 . Driven by media political economy and long-held news values, of which ‘conflict’ is key 15 , journalists and other media professionals may pursue ‘splashy’ or ‘clickbait’ headlines by extrapolating, overstating or ‘cherry picking’ the findings or scientific reports 16 . In the context of reporting on climate change in particular, this may include sometimes emphasising ‘climate contrarianism’, by minimising climate impacts in reporting or suggesting climate science is unreliable 17 .
At the same time, much social science scholarship has also emphasised how communicating about climate change is more profound and nuanced than simply being about ‘the science’. For example, Hulme 18 and other scholars 19,20 have argued that the social meaning of climate change—the ways that we think, disagree, frame and deliberate about climate change—and the competing discourses that we build around the issue, are an essential site of study if we are to define and pursue a broadly supported course of action. In this context, there have been calls for better examination of the ‘missing human dimension’ 21 of global environmental change research, and an impetus towards a wider interdisciplinary dialogue in the study of climate change. In this paper, as a group of both natural scientists and social scientists who study climate change, we aim to contribute to the nuance and interdisciplinarity called for, to bridge this gap.
Examinations of media coverage of Australia’s Great Barrier Reef (GBR) in particular, have shown that conservation science, and the human dimensions of the climate story, embodied both in politics, and in activism to protect the reef, have been intertwined here for decades 22 . Research has also found that media reporting about the health of the GBR has tended to be sensationalised and may undermine certainty in climate science, risk perceptions of climate change, and trust in science 23, 24–25 . In this context, the way that government science reports about the GBR are framed and conveyed to publics is an essential site for examination if we are to understand how science news is mediated in contemporary media ecologies 26 . The point at which science findings are initially collated, framed and distributed to media by government science organisations, is a crucial—and understudied—location for science message framing.
In this paper, we focus on a single case of science message framing about the climate-impacted GBR. The Australian Institute of Marine Science (AIMS), a government science agency, releases a survey report on the health of the GBR annually 27 . In 2022, an AIMS report found some parts of the GBR had experienced an increase in average coral coverage compared to previous years 28,29 . This finding was showcased with a media release titled “Highest coral cover in central, northern reef in 36 years” 30 , that foregrounded ‘reef recovery’. In our analysis below, we follow the trajectory this news about the GBR took as it travelled from the report’s accompanying media release, through global digital news and social media. When discussing activity prior to rebranding, we refer to ‘Twitter’, ‘tweet’, and ‘retweet’ instead of ‘X’, ‘post’, and ‘repost’, respectively), we found that the ‘reef recovery’ framing of the report’s accompanying media release was replicated and amplified in online media coverage that disputed the poor state of health of the GBR. Furthermore, we found content in both social and mainstream media used AIMS’ report to question the legitimacy of climate science and the threat of climate change, despite the irrefutable and critical threat of climate change to the GBR 31,32 .
This case is significant because inaccurate information about climate change in the media undermines public climate risk perceptions and justifies delays in now urgent policy making to mitigate the climate problem. Recognising that the nuanced findings of a scientific report may be lost or cherry-picked in social and mainstream media, we use this case to suggest how government science organisations, and media more broadly, can anticipate and prepare for the ways their messages about the impacts of climate change are mutated and transformed as they traverse contemporary mediaspheres.
Coral reefs worldwide are under severe threat, with climate change causing more frequent marine heatwaves and increased sea surface temperatures resulting in reef-damaging coral bleaching 33 . On Australia’s GBR, repeated bleaching events combined with land-based water pollution, cyclones, and crown-of-thorns starfish outbreaks led the Great Barrier Reef Marine Park Authority to declare in 2019 that the 5–25 year outlook for the reef was “very poor” 34,35 . Long-term climate projections are bleak for reefs globally. Even at the Paris Agreement goal of no more than 1.5 °C of heating above pre-industrial levels, the vast majority (70–90%) of tropical coral reefs will be lost 36 . With warming of over 2 °C, more than 99% of tropical coral reefs will cease to exist 36 .
The urgent need to mitigate climate change and other drivers of reef degradation is the overriding challenge for the various actors involved in the governance of the GBR 37 . These actors include the United Nations Educational, Scientific and Cultural Organization (UNESCO) World Heritage Committee, governments, industry bodies, environmental advocacy organisations, activists, science agencies, Indigenous traditional owners, and community groups. These actors can differ in their perspectives on GBR interventions and governance 38 . These divergent perspectives have historically, but not exclusively, been enacted in contemporary hybrid media systems: interconnected online and offline communication spaces where older and newer media logics interact 39,40 .
Several studies have examined media coverage of the GBR and climate change 22,24,41 , finding complex interdependencies and interactions between media and the politics of reef protection, across transnational spaces 42 and over time. For example, Konkes and Foxwell-Norton have considered how mediatised GBR science information has been deployed in politicised debates, rendering science a social field in its own right, and a contributor to the “vexed polarisation of debate” 43 . Media are indeed actors in this debate and a site for Hutchins and Lester’s mediatised environmental conflict over the reef 44 . Understood as a process in which “mutually constitutive interactions between activism, journalism, formal politics, and industry” coalesce 44 , mediatised environmental conflict extends also to the communication of science knowledge, making science information a constitutive component of politicised debates 43 . Lubicz-Zaorski and colleagues 45 explored the mediatised environmental conflict that surrounded UNESCO’s 2021 appraisal of the reef’s health. During this time, findings from the 2021 AIMS reporting on GBR coral coverage were ‘cherry-picked’ and used by a network of partisan and ideologically-aligned actors in the media, across then-Twitter, YouTube, and Facebook 45 . This information was used to argue that the GBR had recovered, and the threat of climate change was exaggerated. This study found that some media were central cogs in a well-established ‘denial machine’ 46 seeking to discount or discredit anthropogenic climate change and its impacts in Australia, as well as globally.
Historically, Australian events have been used to promote (‘export’) climate scepticism globally 47 . During Australia’s catastrophic 2019–2020 Black Summer bushfires, for example, misinformation about the cause of these unprecedented 48 fires originated on Twitter and travelled into mainstream media in Australia and internationally 49 . During the fires, tweets with the hashtag ‘#ArsonEmergency’ claimed, spuriously, that arson was the cause of the bushfires, downplaying the fires’ links to climate change 50 . This misinformation was part of a sustained campaign of “online influence operations” of “coordinated inauthentic behaviour” on social media, with the aim of undermining the perceived threat of climate change and the need for action 50,51 . Australian articles amplifying denialist discourses about the fires’ connection to climate change were shared by foreign political figures, such as by Donald Trump Jr. on Twitter, to millions of followers 52 . This event demonstrates how interaction between Australian online news media and social media amplified misinformation about climate change, underscoring that ‘homegrown’ climate change denial shapes media discourse on climate change well beyond national boundaries.
Communication about Australia’s GBR is, therefore, embedded in a local and global media system that is predisposed to spreading misinformation and sowing doubt about climate change. This system contributes to eroding public acceptance and trust in climate science, which may quell a sense of urgency around climate change mitigation policy. This context is particularly consequential for government science organisations, which must work symbiotically with the media to disseminate their research. There is, therefore, a pressing need to better understand and anticipate how climate contrarianism in media and social media might use information distributed by government science agencies.
In August 2022, the Australian government science organisation AIMS released its Annual summary report of coral reef condition 2021/2022 29 —hereafter, the ‘coral coverage report’. According to this report, the period between August 2021 and May 2022 brought few acute stressors to the GBR. With no major cyclones and an absence of prolonged high temperatures, given La Niña conditions, coral coverage increased on the northern and central reefs of the GBR. This increase was largely driven by the fast-growing Acropora genus of corals, which is particularly susceptible to wave damage from tropical cyclones and bleaching from marine heatwaves and is also the preferred prey for the invasive crown-of-thorns starfish. Indeed, outbreaks of crown-of-thorns starfish reduced coral coverage in the southern reefs of the GBR over the same period. Despite some increased coral growth, the reef as a whole was by no means recovering and remained highly vulnerable.
The coral coverage report was accompanied by a media release 30 . The media release foregrounded recovery the reef, with the headline and first three paragraphs of the media release emphasising a historic increase in coral coverage. The fourth paragraph mentioned the small decrease in coral coverage of the southern reefs of the GBR. Following an image of a thriving part of the reef, AIMS staff were quoted and the release explained that the new coral growth remained vulnerable to future stressors, and thus the outlook for the GBR remained pessimistic.
By foregrounding the recovery of the reef, the media release accompanying the coral coverage report adopted a particular framing 5 . This ‘reef recovery’ framing in the headline and initial paragraphs provided an incomplete picture of threats to the GBR in a world with climate change. Headlines and an initial paragraph may be the first, and sometimes the only, part of a text encountered by readers, particularly when the content is shared on social media. In the time-pressured 24/7 media cycle, media releases are often a key source of stories for journalists. Fact-checking of media releases may be scant, and releases may even be reproduced verbatim 53 . Consequently, the strategic framing of a communicating organisation may be reproduced uncritically in media outputs derived from media releases. Here, the ‘reef recovery’ framing of the headline immediately orientated media consumers to a ‘good news’ story of reef resilience, and may have led readers to doubt the existence, or impact, of climate change.
In the following analysis, we explore the media and social media response to the coral coverage report’s release, posing the following research questions. For our media analysis, we asked whether, and if so, how media articles perpetuated a ‘reef recovery’ framing (RQ1). We analysed articles referring to the coral coverage report from online news outlets. We included articles of all types, as media audiences typically do not distinguish between news, opinion, and editorials 54 , especially on social media. For our social media analysis, we sought to identify whether content related to the AIMS report release expressed a climate change contrarianism (RQ2). We analysed content from Twitter due to its prominence, at the time, as a central communication space for media, politicians and activists—key actors in mediatised environmental conflict. Twitter was also selected for its role in news proliferation and agenda setting, and for its accessibility for data collection at the time of the coral coverage report’s release 55,56 .

Most headlines emphasised recovery without caveat ( n  = 20, 71.43%). Beyond headlines, the majority of articles foregrounded ‘reef recovery’ ( n  = 21, 75.00%). Although many articles went on to qualify the increase of coral coverage, such as by acknowledging the specific regions of the reef that had exhibited growth ( n  = 20, 71.43%). Nearly half of the articles ( n  = 13, 46.33%) failed to accurately represent established climate science, for example, by not mentioning the GBR’s vulnerability to climate change. Over a third of the articles ( n  = 10, 35.71%) used the coral coverage report as a news hook to overtly challenge climate science and actively express climate sceptic views. All climate change sceptical articles exclusively used a ‘reef recovery’ framing. In comparison, all articles that eschewed a ‘reef recovery’ framing reported the GBR’s state of health and the ongoing threat of climate change to the GBR in a manner broadly consistent with established science about the reef. Thus, the ‘reef recovery’ framing of the media release appeared to be used in the media coverage as a basis for challenging established climate science and the well-documented threat of climate change to the GBR.
RQ2. Social media analysis
We also collected 59,586 tweets discussing the GBR in the twenty-nine days following the coral coverage report’s publication (Fig. 2). To explore Twitter content, we used topic modelling—a natural language processing technique—to identify unique discussion topics (see “Method” section). We excluded tweets ( n  = 707, 1.19%) with no words related to semantic meaning (e.g., see “Methods” section). From the remaining 58,879 tweets, we found fourteen topics, nine of which reflected coral coverage discussion. On average, the proportion of tweets associated with coral coverage topics was 78.56%. Thus, most GBR discussions in the weeks following the coral coverage report, particularly in the first week, discussed changes in coral coverage. Summary
Our results indicate the ‘reef recovery’ framing adopted from the AIMS media release that accompanied its 2022 coral coverage report was clearly associated with climate sceptical discourses in both online media outlets (RQ1) and in social media narratives (RQ2). In the media corpus, we found that every article that included a climate scepticism component also adopted the ‘reef recovery’ framing. That is, the ‘reef recovery’ narrative and climate scepticism went hand in hand in our media sample. In the social media data, we likewise saw the proliferation of the ‘reef recovery’ framing. Climate sceptic websites were widely shared, and climate change scepticism endured for weeks in Twitter discussions of the Great Barrier Reef.
Discussion
Our case study documents the informational ‘long tail’ in online news and social media of the 2022 AIMS coral coverage report on the health of the GBR. This is a very specific case of one report released by one government science organisation about one threatened environment—though certainly an environment of global significance. However, given that this information travelled globally and was used by climate contrarians online to challenge the legitimacy of climate science, we contend that this case offers critical learnings. This includes insights for other research agencies elsewhere, when releasing such science information, and for media organisations when reporting it—particularly when the information relates to climate change. In our discussion below, we offer insights from the present case on communicational strategies and tactics to mitigate the risk of science information about climate change being similarly subverted. We emphasise that science communication within the hybrid media system is by nature complex and multidirectional. In this contemporary media ecology, information is vulnerable to intentional—as well as unintentional—transformation and mutation as it passes between disseminators, users and platforms. There is no simple ‘quick fix’, which is precisely why misinformation continues to be a crucial global issue 57 . Structural and radical transformation are needed to address the climate crisis 58 , requiring approaches beyond the remit of this article. We offer, however, targeted suggestions to address some aspects of the critical, informational component of the problem across the interconnected domains of strategic organisational communication, digital news, and social media.
Scientific research is generally regarded to be information that is ‘gold standard’ and credible 59,60 . However, as in the case examined here, science information from governments can be misinterpreted and misused when translated for broader audiences, with certain framings emphasised over others. The process of packaging science information for media audiences, then, must be carefully understood and strategically planned at the source. This means communications staff who write media releases accompanying government science reports must be well-versed not only in the science itself, but also in the potential ways such information could be transformed and redeployed in the interests of science denialism in the broader media landscape. Communication staff could leverage media logics, including the power of headlines, the ‘stickiness’ of the soundbite, the news value of the novel, and indeed the power of sensational science findings to attract both news media and social media attention. However, in the current moment, hybrid media logics and the specific affordances of online platforms (for example, the ease of on-sharing content) mean established attention-getting tactics warrant closer consideration. The tendency to highlight the most ‘sensational’ aspect of science news must be resisted when translating and conveying science around a politicised issue, such as climate change and its impacts.
Furthermore, noting the proliferation of problematic information, the ongoing use of tactics to undermine trust in science, and the hybrid nature of media systems, communication is now much more labour-intensive than in the past, particularly in the context of mediatised environmental conflicts and on politicised topics like climate change. As such, communications professionals need to be equipped with adequate resourcing, which includes staffing and training to bolster skills for communicating in hybrid media systems. For example, skills to counter online misinformation could be supported by appropriate, strategic and research-informed message construction, social media training and misinformation expertise for in-house science communicators 61,62 .
In regard to our case study, we note AIMS has modified its approach to releasing information about the GBR in subsequent years. In 2023, when the agency posted about the next edition of their report, they highlighted complexity and pointed audiences to a detailed explainer in The Conversation 63 . Likewise, in 2024, the media release headline was nuanced: “High coral cover amid intense heatwaves and bleaching? Here’s how both can be true on the Great Barrier Reef” 64 . Interviews with AIMS staff would enable further insights into the science agency’s experience (this was outside the scope of the current research). However, a recent book authored by the former CEO of AIMS (CEO at the time of the 2022 coral report release) indeed acknowledged that the response to the ‘good news’ framing, the subject of this study, was unintended:
“While the record coral cover levels was great news, and showed how resilient the ecosystem still was, it did not in any way mean that the reef was somehow okay… of course, this was not how the contrarians and deniers saw it” 65
It is clear, then, that in this case study, GBR science communication itself, as well as the response to it, has played a constitutive role in mediatised environmental conflict over the reef, and broader power struggles about the reality of climate change. This case highlights the importance of scientific actors considering how certain information framing could be leveraged for unintended purposes within the hybrid media system 43 . Instead, communicators could rely on frames that emphasise the complexity and vulnerability of the GBR, such as those used by AIMS in subsequent years.
There are also lessons in the case analysed here for journalistic practice in relation to covering climate. Research has clearly established that journalists often rely on media releases for writing the news 66 . Such ‘information subsidy’ crucially influences media agendas and story content. Those media organisations with the least financial resources tend to rely on media releases more, sometimes to the extent of doing ‘copy-paste’ journalism, where media releases are reproduced wholesale with the original organisational framing intact 67 . For organisations seeking to relay messages to their publics, such replication of framing clearly suits organisational objectives. However, when framing emphasises one aspect of science over a more complex and nuanced story, this can come at the expense of accurate media coverage, resulting in poorer public understanding of the science issue at hand. Journalists and editors must be aware of the consequences of ‘soundbite’ stories that emphasise an issue’s most sensational aspects. Such problems in the ‘translation’ of science information, particularly in relation to climate change, call for better training of journalists who cover climate change and the environment 68,69 .
Our case study also underscores that social media companies have failed to stem the global flow of misinformation. We found climate change scepticism strongly evident in social media discussions around this news event. This finding reflects established literature showing misinformation is generally shared more quickly and to more users than accurate, factual information 70 . Historically, social media companies have removed, or added warnings to, some misinformation flagged by algorithmic systems or professional fact checkers. More recently, X 71 and Meta 72 have discarded fact-checking programmes for a crowdsourcing model (‘community notes’), where users create warnings for misinformation. This ‘wisdom of the crowd’ approach may be ineffective for complex scientific topics or subtle forms of misinformation, such as arguments justifying climate inaction or ‘discourses of delay’ 47,73,74 . These conditions were present in our case study, where a novel event of perceived ecological optimism (‘reef recovery’) was used to seed doubt, sometimes indirectly, about climate science. Furthermore, corrections via community notes may be ineffective at countering widely-circulated misinformation, sometimes by media and political elites. Research indicates that the more misinformation is repeated, the more it seems credible, even to those who endorse the consensus of climate science 62,75 .
At the time of writing, climate change misinformation is increasingly prolific on X, despite the company’s claims 76 . X has reinstated and monetised disseminators of climate change misinformation, such as the Gateway Pundit 77 . Meanwhile, pro-environmental users, including climate scientists, have disproportionately abandoned the platform 78 , and opaque algorithms promote the visibility of hashtags like ‘#ClimateScam’ over more common hashtags, such as ‘#ClimateEmergency’ 73 . Similar trends may emerge on Meta, given that the company has more recently abandoned fact-checking programmes. Both X and Meta restrict researchers’ access to data, preventing oversight from independent investigators 79 . Given the prominence of social media platforms in discourse over key public issues like climate change, the current moment is a pivotal one for the enforcement of regulation on social media platforms, where self-regulation has failed.
To conclude, our study underscores how information about climate change moves quickly and prolifically from ‘official’ sources, through mainstream news, and into the long tail of the internet, changing and mutating as it travels. Online news media and social media are communicationally intertwined, making it difficult for user-producers in these hybrid media systems to determine the source of the information or misinformation 80 —whether a strategically framed media release, incomplete news reporting, or social media commentary that is blatantly contrarian. The nexus reported here between online news about GBR coral cover, social media, and climate scepticism is also enduring: the case we have examined is no isolated happening. For example, Danish economist Bjørn Lomborg—who called climate change a “False Alarm” 81 —has cited AIMS’ coral coverage reports and data since 2022 to repeatedly claim that the data “doesn’t fit the narrative” of climate science 82, 83–84 .
The contemporary global media ecology in which climate change and responses to it are deliberated—and also denied—is a highly consequential one. In this landscape, information that fails to emphasise the magnitude and urgency of the threat of climate change can quickly evolve into misinformation that serves the purposes of climate scepticism, denial and delay. Such obfuscation of science information as traced here may erode public trust in the institutions of science, diminish public understanding of the climate crisis, and curb support for climate change policy, making meaningful climate policy action less politically viable. Communicators of all kinds who create messages about climate change in this landscape have an immense responsibility: to decisively convey the acute risks that climate change poses to all human and ecological systems, particularly to environments of Outstanding Universal Value 85 , like Australia’s Great Barrier Reef.
Methods
This project was approved by the Human Research Ethics Committee of the Commonwealth Scientific and Industrial Research Organisation, application 141/22. Analysis and social media data collection were conducted with code written in the R programming language 86 . Analysis scripts are available online at: https://github.com/matt-lab/great-barrier-reef-coral-coverage-discussions. Data has not been made available due to ethical and legal implications.
Media study
Data collection
We collected media articles that referred to the coral coverage report. Data was identified using top-down (determined by expertise knowledge) and bottom-up (determined by public association) searches for English news articles. For the top-down search, one author (GM) collected articles from online news outlets, published within two weeks of the coral coverage report’s publication date. Regarding the bottom-up search, we collected news articles and blog posts that were shared widely on Twitter (Twitter data collection is described in the “Social media study” section), such as an article from the Breitbart News Network. Via these two searches, we collected 28 articles (presented in Table 1).
Coding
Two authors (Redacted for review) coded the media data. The coding dichotomously (yes or no) categorised articles for five characteristics: (1) foregrounded recovery (emphasised coral growth); (2) qualified recovery (e.g., emphasised the limited region of growth); (3) accurately reflected science (e.g., states an ongoing threat to the Great Barrier Reef); (4) engaged in climate scepticism (e.g., suggested coral growth casts doubt on the existence of climate change) and (5) had a headline that emphasised recovery (e.g., emphasised coral growth without qualification).
Social media study
Data collection
From Twitter, now X, we collected ‘tweets’ using the R academictwitteR package 87 . We extracted all tweets that contained the text “Great Barrier Reef” or the hashtag “#GreatBarrierReef” and uploaded between the 4 th of August (when the coral coverage report was published) and 31st of August, 2022. This generated 59,586 tweets, of which 49,419 were retweets (tweets re-shared by other users without alterations).
Topic assignment
To analyse tweet content, we used the natural language processing technique of topic modelling. Topic modelling is an automated process that examines the words in common between texts, such as tweets 88,89 . If two tweets share words, they likely also belong to the same abstract topic. Topic content can be understood by inspecting a topic’s representative tweets and keywords (words unique to a topic). Prior to topic modelling, we converted all words to lowercase and used the R tidytext package 90 to remove common non-diagnostic words, such as stop words (such as “the”), common words related to our study topic (e.g., “great”, “barrier”, “reef”, “#greatbarrierreef”, “gbr”, “australian”, “marine”, “science”, “institute”, “aims”, “australia”), line breaks, punctuation, emojis, digits, and words which occurred less than three times in the dataset. As topics might be closely related to specific users or websites, we did not alter hyperlinks or user mentions (beginning with “@”, users can directly cite other users in tweets). We grouped words with similar meanings by converting all words to lowercase, then stemming words to their common root (e.g., both “corals” and “coral” were replaced with “coral”) using the R SnowballC package 91 . Following this process, 707 (1.19%) tweets had no words remaining and were excluded from the topic modelling. Duplicated tweets were excluded when identifying a topic model to prevent topics from clustering around highly prolific retweets.
Topic modelling was conducted using the R stm package 92,93 . This modelling requires the analyst to specify the number of topics to extract. If the analyst specifies too few topics, then the topics may be too broad, grouping tweets of heterogeneous content. If the analyst specifies too many topics, then the topics may be too specific, grouping tweets of narrowly defined content. To determine the most useful number of topics, we ran the topic modelling process 29 times, and each time we extracted a different number of topics, from 2 topics through to 30 topics. We found a model with fourteen topics performed best, with a balance between topic homogeneity, parsimony, and prediction of text in tweets.
To explore the meaning of topics, we identified the ten keywords (Table 3). Keywords were common words in the dataset that were also particularly exclusive to a topic, known as the FREX measure 92 . Using the keywords and exemplar tweets (tweets most closely associated with a topic), we identified: (1) whether a topic was related to the coral coverage report, and if so, then (2) whether a topic was expressly related to climate change scepticism. Hyperlinks
From tweets, we used the R rvest package 94 to extract hyperlinks that point towards non-Twitter websites. Three websites were unrelated to coral recovery and were removed from the analysis.
