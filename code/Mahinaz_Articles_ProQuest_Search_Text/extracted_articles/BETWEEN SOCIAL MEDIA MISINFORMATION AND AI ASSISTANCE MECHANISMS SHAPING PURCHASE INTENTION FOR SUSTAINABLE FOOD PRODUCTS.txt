Abstract
This paper proposes an extended Theory of Planned Behaviour model to investigate Romanian consumers' purchase intention for sustainable food products in the digital information context. The model integrates perceived food-related misinformation on social media and the use of artificial intelligence for information verification and decision support, examining the roles of perceived nutritional value and trust in food-related information and products as mechanisms through which digital influences translate into intention. Data were collected via an online survey and analysed using variance-based structural equation modelling. The findings suggest that AI use strengthens both nutritional benefit evaluation and trust, whereas food-related misinformation undermines these evaluative mechanisms. Purchase intention is explained primarily through perceived nutritional benefit evaluation, while trust plays a complementary role by reducing uncertainty. The study highlights implications for brands, digital platforms, AI solution developers, and public policies aimed at reducing misinformation and improving the quality of online food-related information.
Keywords: social media, food misinformation, AI influence, perceived nutritional value, trust in food information, sustainable purchase intention, sustainable food products.
JEL classification: M31, D83, Q01, Q13, Q18.
Introduction
Globally, the concern for sustainable consumption and the transition to diets compatible with the sustainable development goals is growing (Food and Agriculture Organization of the United Nations [FAO] & World Health Organization [WHO], 2019; Willett et al., 2019), and the sustainable food space is becoming a strategic area for both population health and the resilience of agri-food economic systems. The contemporary digital informational context fundamentally changes the way consumers form their perceptions of food products: decisions are increasingly made in contexts dominated by social networks, viral content, and persuasive messages, where information about nutrition and sustainability is often difficult to evaluate. Social media users receive large amounts of information, share content, even if the accuracy of the information is questionable (Appel et.al, 2020). Online nutritional information can be inaccurate or of low quality, and content popularised on social media can amplify confusion and the difficulty of distinguishing between accurate and misleading information (Denniss et al., 2023; Zeng et al., 2025). Thus, understanding sustainable consumption behaviour can no longer be separated from digital information risk.
The motivation for this study is given by two major developments that coexist and interact in the online environment: (1) the prevalence of food misinformation (MISINFO) in social media and (2) the emergence and popularisation of artificial intelligence tools used for information verification and decision support (AAI). On the one hand, the perception that social media contains numerous exaggerated, incomplete, or incorrect claims about food can erode the credibility of messages and distort the assessment of the real benefits of products (Denniss et al., 2023). On the other hand, the use of AI as a mechanism for information verification and filtering is becoming an emerging strategy for reducing information asymmetry, with the potential to strengthen trust and support more stable judgments about products (Dwivedi et al., 2023), although sometimes the information is not entirely reliable.
In this context, two evaluative mechanisms become central to explaining the intention to purchase sustainable products: perceived nutritional value (PNV) and trust (TRUST). The literature on sustainable food consumption highlights that one of the major reasons for the preference for sustainable products over conventional ones is the consumer's health and perceived benefits, including the association with reduced exposure to contaminants and a superior nutritional profile (Brantsæ ter et al., 2017; Hurtado-Barroso et al., 2019; Gundala & Singh, 2021; Çakmakçı & Çakmakçı, 2023). In parallel, trust in the food system and product information is essential for reducing uncertainty and for making decisions in contexts where consumers cannot directly verify the validity of claims at the time of purchase (Wu et al., 2021). Therefore, PNV and TRUST can be understood as mechanisms by which digital environmental influences are converted into behavioural predispositions.
The aim of this study is to expand knowledge on the formation of purchase intention for sustainable food products by simultaneously investigating the effects of MISINFO and AAI on PNV and TRUST and, subsequently, on INTENT. Specifically, the research objectives are to: (1) test the direct influences of AAI and MISINFO on PNV and TRUST; (2) assess the effects of PNV and TRUST on purchase intention; (3) analyse the indirect (mediation) effects through which AAI and MISINFO are transmitted to INTENT through PNV and TRUST.
The present article contributes to the literature by developing and validating a conceptual model that explicitly addresses the tension between informational risk (MISINFO) and digital decision support (DSA) in the formation of sustainable purchase intention. Furthermore, the study highlights the role of the evaluative mechanisms PNV and TRUST as "bridges" through which digital influences are converted into intention, providing a useful perspective for both research and practice (brands, retailers, platforms and AI solution developers), in a period in which the quality of online food information and verification tools are becoming increasingly important determinants of consumer behaviour.
This paper is structured as follows: the first section presents a literature review on food misinformation in social media and the role of AI, as well as the theoretical underpinnings of TRUST and PNV as evaluative mechanisms and INTENT as a dependent variable (anchored in TPB). The next section describes the research methodology and instruments used. Subsequently, empirical results and related discussions are presented, and finally, conclusions, implications, and future research directions are formulated.
1. Literature Review
1.1. Food misinformation in social media and the influence of AI
Social networks favour the chain propagation of information, the immediate and uncontrolled dissemination of informational content (Vese, 2022), contributing to the increasing prevalence of disinformation, fake news and misleading information about food, aspects that significantly influence consumer perceptions of food and consumption behaviour (Melios et al., 2025; Duong et al. 2025). In the context of SDG 12, the imperative "people must have relevant information", academic studies show that online information (websites and social networks) related to food and nutrition is often inaccurate and of low quality (Denniss et al., 2023; Denniss et al., 2024) and Internet users seeking information online are susceptible to the risk of manipulation and misinformation, due to the quality, validity and reliability of the information distributed on these platforms.
It is known that one of the major reasons for choosing sustainable foods over conventional ones is consumer health (Brantsæ ter et al., 2017; Gundala & Singh, 2021), associated with lower exposure to contaminants (Hurtado-Barroso et al., 2019), higher nutritional value (Brantsæ ter et al., 2017) and, in some cases, higher content of micronutrients and antioxidants (Çakmakçı S. & Çakmakçı R., 2023). The integration of these foods into diets, in accordance with current nutritional guidelines (increasing the consumption of whole grains, fruits and vegetables, while reducing the consumption of red meat), can contribute to the goal - responsible consumption and production (SDG 12), which amplifies the importance of informational content about sustainable food products and sustainable diets in the online space and on social networks. However, the literature suggests that the information ecosystem in social media is often deficient in content with educational value and scientific basis.
Specifically, Boerman et al. (2026)'s analysis of 84,338 posts by human influencers on Instagram, TikTok, and YouTube indicates a preponderance of commercial content (brand promotion and purchase incentives) and a much lower presence of content aimed at promoting a sustainable lifestyle. In addition, Raffoul et al. (2024) show that an overwhelming proportion of TikTok videos promoting dietary supplements do not provide scientific evidence for health and appearance claims, which can generate substantial risks for followers, including the adoption of dietary practices and supplements with unvalidated benefits. In this context, popular nutritional advice online can amplify consumer confusion, skepticism, and even avoidance of dietary recommendations (Ramachandran et al., 2018).
Furthermore, nutritional confusion has been associated with negative reactions to nutritional information and decreased engagement in fruit and vegetable consumption (Lee et al., 2018), suggesting that an ambiguous information climate can affect both consumers' cognitive evaluations and food choices.
In addition to the issue of information quality, the literature shows that the digital environment amplifies misinformation through platform-specific mechanisms (recommendation algorithms, engagement-based ranking, short and emotional content), which can favour viral content at the expense of accuracy. In the agri-food field, this dynamic has been discussed in terms of "information disorder", in which the source of the content, the form of the message and the speed of distribution increase the likelihood of repeated exposure to unverified claims, with effects on perceptions of nutrition and on consumption decisions (Chowdhury, 2023). Furthermore, consumers may end up internalising distorted nutritional assessments, which influences the perception of the real benefits of promoted products and ingredients (Denniss et al., 2023; Zeng et al., 2025). In particular, a systematic synthesis focused on the agri-food area highlights that effective interventions must aim at both identifying and limiting the flows of disinformation and at correction mechanisms, as simple point corrections cannot fully counterbalance the viral dynamics of platforms (Chowdhury, 2023).
In this context, artificial intelligence can function as a force with an ambivalent role. On the one hand, AI tools can support information processing (summarising, comparing, sorting sources), reducing the cognitive cost of verification and facilitating the detection of exaggerated or inconsistent claims (Dwivedi et al., 2023). On the other hand, recent literature warns that AI systems (including language models) can be vulnerable to information contamination and targeted manipulation, especially in contexts where the input is biased, incomplete, or constructed to appear credible (Han et al., 2024). In these situations, AI can produce convincingly formulated but inaccurate answers, which can increase the risk that users will give legitimacy to misinformation. Thus, the effect of AI on consumer perceptions depends on the quality of the sources used, the digital skills/AI literacy of the users, and the extent to which verification is carried out through triangulation and consultation of primary sources (Chowdhury, 2023; Han et al., 2024).
Consequently, the literature justifies treating the use of AI for verification and decision support as a relevant mechanism in the food information ecosystem: when used responsibly, AI can reduce uncertainty and support consumer evaluations; however, in the absence of verification and calibration of trust, it can amplify the effects of a contaminated information environment. This perspective theoretically supports the simultaneous testing of the influence of MISINFO and the influence of AI on evaluative mechanisms and behavioural intentions (Dwivedi et al., 2023; Han et al., 2024).
1.2. Trust and Perceived Value: The Impact of MISINFO and AAI on TRUST and PNV
In the literature on food consumption and sustainability, trust is treated as a central mechanism that reduces the perception of risk and facilitates the adoption of nutritional information and benefit claims, especially when the consumer cannot directly verify their quality or veracity at the time of decision (Wu et al., 2021). In this sense, trust operates as a "cognitive shortcut" necessary in complex information environments, where consumers depend on indirect sources (platforms, labels, influencers, brands, experts). However, it is precisely this dependence on digitally mediated sources that makes trust vulnerable to information contamination: when the ecosystem is perceived as saturated with exaggerated or misleading information, consumers may develop generalised skepticism and reduce both the acceptance of claims and the willingness to integrate them into their own decisions (Ruani et al., 2023).
In particular, MISINFO can erode trust through two complementary channels. The first is the degradation of the credibility of sources: repeated exposure to questionable content can lead to a decrease in trust in communicators and, by extension, in information about food products and their attributes (Ruani et al., 2023). The second channel is the induction of evaluative uncertainty, where consumers come to perceive nutritional claims as difficult to verify and potentially manipulated, which reduces trust in the "healthy/sustainable" promises of products (Wu et al., 2021). In this context, misinformation not only affects the credibility of the message, but also the consumer's predisposition to consider the reported benefits "plausible", which becomes relevant for how PNV is constructed. Specifically, when nutritional messages are perceived as exaggerated or incomplete, consumers may interpret "nutritional value" as image/marketing rather than real benefit, leading to diminished perceived value and decreased trust (Wu et al., 2021).
In contrast, AAI (the use of AI for food information verification and decision support) can act as an uncertainty reduction mechanism, as it facilitates filtering, comparison, and clarification of nutritional claims, which can strengthen trust and stabilise assessments of perceived benefits (Dwivedi et al., 2023). However, recent literature also highlights a possible "trust cost" associated with interacting with AI in sensitive contexts: for example, some experimental studies indicate that when consumers perceive interacting with AI as associated with greater exposure of personal information or a degree of impersonality, trust may decrease (Lefkeli et al., 2024). This observation supports the conceptual need to treat AI not as an "automatic guarantee" against MISINFO, but as a tool whose effectiveness depends on users' perceptions of reliability, transparency, and usability. Overall, the literature suggests that AI can increase TRUST and PNV when used as a verification and clarification support, but the effects may vary depending on the context and how AI is integrated into the decision-making process (Dwivedi et al., 2023; Lefkeli et al., 2024).
In addition to the TRUST dimension, a second central evaluative mechanism in the model is PNV, which reflects the extent to which consumers interpret the product as superior in terms of nutritional quality and actual health benefits. The literature on nutrition information shows that consumer evaluations are often constructed based on cues and interpretations of product claims, especially in contexts where objective verification is difficult at the time of decision (Grunert & Wills, 2007). Consequently, PNV becomes sensitive to how messages are formulated and distributed in the digital environment, as consumers can easily confuse the "healthy product image" with actual nutritional benefits, especially when the information is incomplete or presented persuasively.
In contrast, AAI can support PNV by reducing ambiguity and clarifying claims. When AI is used to compare sources, summarise evidence, and highlight inconsistencies, it can contribute to a "cleaner" assessment of nutritional benefits, reinforcing perceptions of value (Dwivedi et al., 2023). However, the positive effect is not automatic: if the use of AI is based on contaminated input or if users overestimate the ability of AI to "validate the truth" without further verification, there is a risk that AI will reproduce errors or provide apparent validation of questionable claims, which can destabilise PNV over time (Han et al., 2024). Therefore, the literature suggests that the impact of AI on PNV depends on verification practices (triangulation, primary sources) and how the user integrates AI recommendations into the decision-making process (Dwivedi et al., 2023; Han et al., 2024).
1.3. Purchase intention (INTENT) for sustainable products: theoretical anchoring (TPB / consumer behaviour)
The Theory of Planned Behaviour (TPB) has the ability to explain and predict human behaviour in a multitude of contexts (Ajzen, 1991; Ajzen & Fishbein, 1980), including sustainable agri-food behaviour (Alam et al., 2020; Ahmed et al., 2021). In the recent literature on sustainable agri-food consumption, TPB is frequently extended by including context-specific variables, precisely to better explain intentions in situations where benefits and risks are perceived through mediated information (e.g., health benefits, sustainability, perceived price, availability, or trust).
In this framework, the proposal to treat INTENT as the result of cognitive and affective evaluations is congruent with the logic of TPB: consumer evaluations of behaviour (attitude) are constructed based on beliefs about consequences, and these beliefs are, in the digital environment, strongly dependent on the quality of available information. Thus, PNV can be interpreted as an expression of beliefs about consequences and benefits (especially nutritional and health benefits), while TRUST reflects the credibility of the informational framework from which these beliefs are formed. In practical terms, when consumers believe that sustainable products (or with alternative ingredients) have real nutritional benefits, purchase intention increases; and when the credibility of information about these benefits is high, uncertainty is reduced and the willingness to act on the intention increases (Vermeir & Verbeke, 2006; Dorce et al., 2021; Jakubowska et al., 2024).
The explicit inclusion of digital informational influences in the TPB is also supported by the literature showing that social media shapes beliefs and attitudes through repeated exposures and social validation mechanisms, but at the same time misinformation raises issues of trust and credibility, affecting consumer evaluations and predisposition to adoption (Rini et al., 2024). In this sense, the proposed model offers a coherent extension of TPB for the current context: sustainable purchase intention is explained through an informational-evaluative chain, in which MISINFO and AAI influence the formation of beliefs and evaluations (PNV, TRUST), which subsequently support (or inhibit) INTENT.
2. Methodology
2.1. Research hypotheses and conceptual model design
To understand the formation of purchase intention for sustainable food products, in the current digital context, in which consumers form their evaluations of products under conditions of information asymmetry (Denniss et al., 2023; Zeng et al., 2025), the use of AI can facilitate information filtering, source comparison and clarification of product claims, contributing to the strengthening of evaluations of perceived benefits (Dwivedi et al., 2023). Consequently, the use of AI can support the formation of a higher perceived nutritional value by reinforcing beliefs about the positive consequences of the purchase (health benefits and quality). Thus, based on the literature, we formulate the following hypothesis:
H1: The use of AI for food information verification and decision support (AAI) has a positive and significant effect on perceived nutritional value (PNV).
At the same time, AI can influence trust through its role as a verification tool: if users perceive that AI helps them distinguish between accurate and misleading information, then the sense of informational control increases and, implicitly, the trust in the information used in the decision-making process increases. In a digital landscape where the credibility of sources is variable, such mechanisms can stabilise consumers' judgments of product claims (Ruani et al., 2023; Dwivedi et al., 2023). Therefore, we postulate:
H2: The use of AI for food information verification and decision support (AAI) has a positive and significant effect on trust (TRUST).
Second, the perception of MISINFO constitutes an informational risk that can distort consumers' evaluations. When the information environment is perceived as saturated with exaggerated or misleading claims, consumers may end up penalising the credibility of messages and diminishing the evaluation of real benefits, interpreting nutritional claims as persuasive "images" rather than substantiated advantages. The literature highlights that online nutritional information is often inaccurate, and viral content can exacerbate confusion, affecting how consumers evaluate benefits (Denniss et al., 2023; Zeng et al., 2025). In this context, MISINFO is expected to reduce PNV, which is why we formulate:
H3: Perception of food misinformation in social media (MISINFO) has a significant and negative effect on perceived nutritional value (PNV).
MISINFO can also erode TRUST by degrading the credibility of the information framework. The literature on trust in the food system shows that, in contexts of uncertainty and perceived risk, consumers can develop skepticism towards claims and reduce trust in information and products (Wu et al., 2021). Furthermore, when social media is perceived as a channel prone to misinformation, consumers may generalise this perception to product messages and reduce acceptance of nutritional and sustainability claims (Chowdhury, 2023). Thus, we state:
H4: Perception of food misinformation in social media (MISINFO) has a negative and significant effect on trust (TRUST).
The literature on sustainable food consumption indicates that health motivations and perceived benefits are often central to sustainable purchasing decisions. Sustainable products are associated with health benefits, lower exposure to contaminants, and a perceived superior nutritional profile (Brantsæ ter et al., 2017; Hurtado-Barroso et al., 2019; Gundala & Singh, 2021; Çakmakçı S. & Çakmakçı R., 2023). From this perspective, PNV expresses the consumer's beliefs about the positive consequences of the purchase. In the logic of TPB, such beliefs support the formation of behavioural intention (Ajzen, 1991). Accordingly, we formulate:
H5: Perceived nutritional value (PNV) has a positive and significant effect on purchase intention (INTENT).
In addition to benefit evaluation, trust remains an essential mechanism for the adoption of sustainable products, as it reduces uncertainty and facilitates the acceptance of benefit messages. In particular, the literature on trust in the food system suggests that TRUST facilitates decision-making when consumers cannot directly verify the validity of product claims at the time of purchase (Wu et al., 2021). Therefore, we hypothesise that higher levels of trust in food and product information are reflected in higher purchase intention:
H6: Trust (TRUST) has a positive and significant effect on purchase intention (INTENT).
Based on the above, the conceptual model assumes that informational antecedents (AAI and MISINFO) influence intention not only directly, but also through evaluative mechanisms. First, if the use of AI helps consumers clarify and verify information, this may lead to more favourable benefit evaluations (PNV), and these evaluations will transfer to intention, in line with the TPB logic of intention formation through beliefs about consequences (Ajzen, 1991; Dwivedi et al., 2023). Thus, we formulate:
H7: Perceived nutritional value (PNV) positively and significantly mediates the relationship between AI use (AAI) and purchase intention (INTENT).
AAI can also contribute to intention formation by increasing trust (TRUST): when consumers perceive that AI helps them distinguish correct from misleading information, the credibility of the information used increases and the uncertainty associated with the decision is reduced, which favours intention (Ruani et al., 2023; Wu et al., 2021). Thus, we postulate:
H8: Trust (TRUST) positively and significantly mediates the relationship between AI use (AAI) and purchase intention (INTENT).
Conversely, if social media is perceived as contaminated by misinformation, then the evaluation of benefits may become more negative, decreasing the perceived nutritional value (PNV). A decrease in PNV will reduce purchase intention, because consumers no longer perceive clear positive consequences of the purchase, in accordance with the TPB mechanism (Ajzen, 1991; Denniss et al., 2023; Zeng et al., 2025). Based on this reasoning, we formulate:
H9: Perceived nutritional value (PNV) negatively and significantly mediates the relationship between misinformation perception (MISINFO) and purchase intention (INTENT).
Finally, MISINFO is also intended to reduce intention by eroding trust (TRUST). The literature on trust in the food system indicates that lack of credibility and uncertainty can reduce message acceptance and purchase intention (Wu et al., 2021), and under conditions of heightened misinformation perception, consumers may be skeptical of product information and claimed benefits, which reduces intention (Chowdhury, 2023). Accordingly, we formulate:
H10: Trust (TRUST) negatively and significantly mediates the relationship between misinformation perception (MISINFO) and purchase intention (INTENT).
Based on the above, we developed a conceptual research model that captures the relationships between the use of AI for food information verification (AAI), the perception of food misinformation in social media (MISINFO), consumer evaluative mechanisms - perceived nutritional value (PNV) and trust (TRUST) - and purchase intention for sustainable products (INTENT). The model starts from the premise that AAI acts as a mechanism to reduce information asymmetry and support decision-making (H1-H2), while MISINFO acts as an information risk that degrades the evaluative framework (H3-H4). In turn, PNV and TRUST contribute to the formation of intention (H5-H6) and mediate the relationships between informational antecedents and intention (H7-H10), providing a contextual extension of TPB for understanding INTENT in the contemporary digital era.
In line with the TPB logic, contextual antecedents influence intention primarily through beliefs and evaluations that shape the attitudinal component and behavioural predisposition (Ajzen, 1991). In the present study, informational influences from the digital ecosystem are operationalized through two antecedents (AAI and MISINFO), while the evaluative mechanisms through which these influences translate into intention are explicitly captured by perceived nutritional value (PNV) and trust (TRUST). Therefore, the model was specified as a mediation model (AAI/MISINFO → PNV/TRUST → INTENT), because purchase intention is conceptualised as the outcome of perceived benefit evaluations and information credibility, rather than as a direct reaction to informational antecedents. Moreover, this specification aims for parsimony and clearer mechanism identification (the roles of PNV and TRUST), avoiding the estimation of residual direct effects in the absence of a distinct theoretical justification
2.2. Description of the data collection and analysis methods
A quantitative research approach was used to collect primary data, obtained through a structured questionnaire-based survey. In total, 638 fully completed questionnaires were collected from respondents in Romania between December 2025 and February 2026. The questionnaire was administered online via Google Forms, and all questions were set as mandatory to prevent missing data. For practical reasons and due to access to the target population, a non-probabilistic convenience sampling method was employed, which is typical for online self-selection studies; therefore, the sample offers acceptable relevance for the investigated phenomenon but entails limitations regarding strict statistical generalisation to the entire population. Because the questionnaire was distributed as an open online link (without a fixed invitee list), the response rate cannot be rigorously estimated in the classical sense (invited/responded ratio). To ensure data quality, all questions were mandatory in Google Forms, resulting in no missing values and no incomplete questionnaires. During preprocessing, the validity of response ranges (Likert 1-5) and the consistency of coding for demographic variables were verified.
The sample is predominantly female (63.5%) and mainly urban (71.6%). The largest share of respondents falls within the 18-25 age group (37.3%), followed by 36-50 (17.1%), under 18 (16.3%), over 50 (15.7%), and 26-35 (13.6%). Education is dominated by high school (41.4%) and bachelor's degree holders (36.5%). Most respondents identify as omnivores (75.9%), followed by flexitarians (16.3%).
Respondents' digital profile was assessed using intensity indices: Social Media Intensity (SMI; M = 3.085; SD = 1.034) and Agentic AI Influence (AAI; M = 3.004; SD = 1.058), computed as the mean of the corresponding Likert items (1-5). For descriptive purposes, scores were classified into three levels (low/moderate/high).
The distributions indicate moderate-to-high social media use (43.1% at a high level) and moderate-to-high use of AI for information verification (35.3% moderate; 36.1% high), supporting the sample's relevance for investigating the digital mechanisms in the proposed model.
The research instrument included multi-item scales adopted and adapted from the prior literature to ensure content validity and comparability of results. Five reflective latent constructs were operationalised: Perceived Food-related Misinformation (MISINFO), Perceived Nutritional Value (PNV), Trust in Food-related Information and Products (TRUST), Agentic AI Influence (AAI), and Sustainable Food Purchase Intention (INTENT). MISINFO was measured with four items adapted from established work on misinformation and information quality assessment (e.g., Lewandowsky et al., 2012; van der Linden et al., 2020), capturing dimensions such as exaggerated claims, difficulty distinguishing accurate from misleading information, and the spread of incomplete/incorrect information on social media. PNV was measured with four items adapted from the literature on nutritional perceptions and the interpretation of food attributes (e.g., Grunert et al., 2010; Grunert & Wills, 2007), explicitly referring to products with alternative ingredients. Trust (TRUST) was measured with three items adapted from research on trust in food-related information and the credibility of nutritional/sustainability claims (e.g., Grunert & Wills, 2007; Thøgersen & Nohlen, 2022; Murphy et al., 2022). AAI was operationally defined as AI use for verifying food-related information and decision support, and was measured with four items capturing AI tool use for verification, discrimination between accurate and misleading information, increased confidence in decision-making, and support for healthier and more sustainable choices (adaptations informed by work on AI-based decision support and interaction with AI systems; e.g., Dwivedi et al., 2023; Rzepka & Berger, 2018). INTENT was measured with three items adapted from foundational research on behavioural intentions and sustainable consumption (e.g., Ajzen, 1991; Vermeir & Verbeke, 2006), focusing on the intention to purchase products with alternative ingredients. All items were assessed on a five-point Likert scale (1 = strongly disagree; 5 = strongly agree).
In this study, AAI is not measured as the objective frequency of AI use, but rather as selfreported engagement and the perceived influence of AI tools on verifying food-related information and supporting decision-making. The AAI items capture both a declared use component (e.g., "I use AI-based tools to verify food-related information") and an evaluative component reflecting the perceived utility/impact of AI in this process (e.g., "AI helps me distinguish accurate from misleading food information," "AI-based recommendations increase my confidence in food-related decisions"). Therefore, AAI should be interpreted as a use-and-perceived-support construct, rather than a measure of interaction frequency. The questionnaire did not restrict respondents to a specific tool; participants could refer to commonly used AI tools for information seeking/verification (e.g., conversational chatbots, AI-assisted search, recommendation systems).
The primary objective of the analysis was to test the research hypotheses and the direct and indirect relationships proposed in the conceptual model (AAI and MISINFO as antecedents; PNV and TRUST as mediators; INTENT as dependent variable). The analysis was conducted using variance-based structural equation modelling (PLS-SEM) with SmartPLS 4, given the suitability of this approach for predictive models with latent constructs and for the simultaneous assessment of direct and mediating effects.
Because all constructs were self-reported and collected simultaneously, we assessed the risk of common method variance. The questionnaire was administered anonymously and did not request any personal identifiers, and respondents were informed that there were no right or wrong answers, thereby reducing response pressure and social desirability bias. In SmartPLS, we examined collinearity using VIF statistics. Inner VIF values for the predictors of endogenous constructs were very low (AAI → PNV = 1.0118; MISINFO → PNV = 1.0118; AAI → TRUST = 1.0118; MISINFO → TRUST = 1.0118; PNV → INTENT = 1.2788; TRUST → INTENT = 1.2788), with VIF_max = 1.2788, well below commonly used thresholds (3.3/5). In addition, outer VIF values at the item level did not indicate problematic collinearity (VIF_max = 3.9281). Therefore, it is unlikely that the estimated structural relationships are inflated due to a dominant common method bias.
3. Results and discussion
The scales used to measure the constructs are reliable (Table no. 2). Cronbach's α and composite reliability (CR/ρc) values exceed the 0.70 threshold, confirming internal consistency (Fornell & Larcker, 1981). In addition, AVE values are above 0.50 for all constructs, supporting convergent validity (Fornell & Larcker, 1981).
Discriminant validity was assessed using HTMT, and all values are below the 0.90 threshold (Table no. 3), indicating distinct constructs (Henseler et al., 2015). The highest value is observed between PNV and INTENT (HTMT = 0.730), suggesting a theoretically plausible association without conceptual overlap.
The model fit indicators for the proposed conceptual model are reported in Table no. 4. The model shows a good fit to the empirical data: SRMR is 0.045 for the saturated model and 0.079 for the estimated model, both below the 0.08 threshold, and NFI is approximately 0.91, confirming an overall good model fit.
Finally, the hypotheses were tested using path coefficients (β) and bootstrapping with 5,000 resamples (Table no. 5). The results confirm all direct relationships with the expected signs: AAI has positive effects on PNV (β = 0.308; p < 0.001) and TRUST (β = 0.442; p < 0.001), whereas MISINFO has negative effects on PNV (β = -0.365; p < 0.001) and TRUST (β = -0.161; p < 0.001). In addition, PNV strongly influences INTENT (β = 0.562; p < 0.001), and TRUST positively influences INTENT (β = 0.230; p < 0.001). Therefore, hypotheses H1-H6 are supported.
Regarding mediation, the results confirm the indirect effects: the influence of AAI on intention is transmitted through PNV (β = 0.173; p < 0.001) and through TRUST (β = 0.102; p < 0.001), while the influence of MISINFO on intention is transmitted negatively through PNV (β = -0.205; p < 0.001) and through TRUST (β = -0.037; p = 0.004). Thus, hypotheses H7-H10 are supported, and PNV emerges as the dominant mechanism through which effects are transmitted to purchase intention.
The model's explanatory power is adequate: for the endogenous variables, R2 = 0.252 for PNV and R2 = 0.237 for TRUST (moderate explanatory power), and R2 = 0.489 for INTENT (high explanatory power). The results indicate a clear contribution for the extension of TPB in the digital environment: informational antecedents (AAI/MISINFO) act on intention mainly through beliefs about consequences (PNV) and TRUST remains a support mechanism.
Although the model explains a substantial share of the variance in purchase intention (INTENT, R2 = 0.489), the R2 values for the mediators PNV (0.252) and TRUST (0.237) are moderate, suggesting that a significant portion of these constructs is driven by additional factors beyond the informational antecedents included (AAI and MISINFO). This is plausible because both perceived nutritional value and trust are complex constructs shaped simultaneously by personal factors (knowledge, experience, predispositions), product factors (price, taste, labeling), and social/contextual factors (norms, information sources, and the credibility of brands and institutions). Therefore, the proposed model captures a relevant explanatory core (informational risk and AI support), yet future extensions could increase the explanatory power for PNV and TRUST by including additional realistic determinants.
The results indicate that purchase intention (INTENT) in the digital environment is built through an information - evaluation chain in which MISINFO erodes, while AAI strengthens, evaluative mechanisms - especially perceived nutritional value. Accordingly, effective interventions should primarily target the quality of benefit evaluation (PNV), supported by credible verification tools and by strengthening trust in information.
The results of this study provide a coherent view of the way informational antecedents in the digital ecosystem - the use of AI for information verification and decision support (AAI) and the perception of food misinformation in social media (MISINFO) - propagate to purchase intention for sustainable food products (INTENT) through two evaluative mechanisms: perceived nutritional value (PNV) and trust (TRUST). Overall, the model supports the idea that intention is not formed "directly" by informational exposure, but through evaluations: (i) benefit evaluation (PNV) and (ii) credibility/certainty evaluation (TRUST), which is compatible with the logic of TPB (Ajzen, 1991) regarding the role of beliefs about consequences in the formation of intentional predisposition.
In our model, AAI has significant positive effects on TRUST (β = 0.442) and on PNV (β = 0.308). This pattern aligns with broader literature on AI acceptance/AI agents, which suggests that perceived benefit and experience quality can foster trust formation and, subsequently, behavioural responses. For example, in a general AI-agent acceptance framework (PLS-SEM, N = 632), Zhao et al. (2025) report that perceived benefit strongly affects cognitive trust (β = 0.651), while overall trust has a major impact on acceptance (β = 0.543). These values exceed AAI→TRUST in our study (0.442), which is plausible because their study treats trust in AI agents as the central object, whereas we operationalise AI as a verification tool and TRUST as trust in information/product; moreover, effects may be stronger when AI is the primary decision agent rather than support for verification.
In addition, in a study on "AI food" (PLS-SEM), Xia et al. (2024) report positive effects of trust on intention (cognitive trust → purchase intention: β = 0.311; affective trust → purchase intention: β = 0.535). Compared with these results, TRUST→INTENT in our model is smaller (β = 0.230), suggesting that when the decision object is a sustainable product with alternative ingredients, intention may depend less on an affective trust component and more on concrete benefit evaluation. This difference is also theoretically coherent: in Xia et al. (2024), trust is tied directly to a novel, potentially risk-laden technology/food concept where trust becomes dominant; in our case, TRUST is more general (information/product), and differentiation shifts toward "how nutritious the product truly is."
The most important finding is the dominant role of PNV: PNV→INTENT has β = 0.562 and a large effect size (f2 = 0.483). This magnitude is comparable to strong effects reported in the literature for trust/benefit-related constructs driving intention. For instance, in Xia et al. (2024), affective trust → purchase intention is β = 0.535, very close to PNV→INTENT in our model. The interpretation is that, in the product category analysed, PNV functions as a proxy for perceived personal benefit (especially health/value), which becomes the main engine of intention, while TRUST remains a supportive mechanism.
The fact that PNV dominates TRUST is not necessarily surprising: in food purchases, when consumers perceive a genuine nutritional advantage (beyond a mere "health halo"), intention can increase even if general trust in online information is not maximal. In practice, PNV reflects a high-stakes evaluation (personal benefit), whereas TRUST reduces uncertainty but does not replace benefit evaluation. This result is theoretically useful because it suggests that in TPB extensions to digital ecosystems, "benefit evaluation" mechanisms may play a larger role than "credibility evaluation" mechanisms, depending on product category and claim type.
In the present study, the asymmetry between MISINFO→PNV (β = -0.365) and MISINFO→TRUST (β = -0.161) is among the most theoretically interesting contributions. One way to interpret this pattern is to distinguish two levels of consumer evaluation. First, benefit evaluation (PNV) is detail-dependent (fibre, refined ingredients, nutritional profile). In an environment perceived as full of exaggerations and misleading claims, consumers become "epistemically cautious": they not only trust less, but-crucially-cannot calibrate which benefits are real. Hence the stronger negative effect of MISINFO on PNV. Second, general trust (TRUST) may be more stable because people can retain credibility anchors (preferred brands, labels, "trusted" sources), even if they perceive social media as problematic. Thus, MISINFO degrades TRUST more modestly, consistent with the smaller effect (and small f2 for MISINFO→TRUST).
This explanation is congruent with the literature on "unverified news" in the food domain, where unverified information affects trust and intention via intermediate mechanisms (e.g., image/credibility), and effect magnitudes can differ between specific evaluations and general attitudes. In our study, MISINFO appears to operate primarily as a "shock" to benefitevaluation capacity (PNV) rather than as a collapse of general trust (TRUST). This implies that anti-misinformation interventions should not only aim to "increase trust," but also to clarify and standardise benefit-related information (explanations, sources, comparable criteria).
The indirect effects confirm that AAI and MISINFO transmit to INTENT through both mechanisms, but the PNV channel is stronger (AAI→PNV→INTENT = 0.173; MISINFO→PNV→INTENT = -0.205) than the TRUST channel (AAI→TRUST→INTENT = 0.102; MISINFO→TRUST→INTENT = -0.037). In terms of contribution, this specifies where intention formation occurs less at the level of "I trust the information," and more at the level of "given the information, I believe the product has real nutritional benefits." This is important for extending TPB in the digital ecosystem: informational antecedents act mainly on outcome beliefs (PNV), while TRUST functions more as an uncertainty-reduction mechanism.
Against this background, the study provides two theoretically relevant clarifications: (1) it shows that AI (as verification/decision support) can strengthen evaluative mechanisms in a food context, consistent with work emphasising the role of trust/benefit in AI-agent acceptance; and (2) it highlights that misinformation does not operate uniformly-it affects benefit evaluation more than general trust, a nuanced theoretical result that can guide future research.
Conclusions
The study confirms the relevance of TPB-type behavioural approaches for explaining purchase intention toward sustainable food products in today's digital ecosystem and contributes to a deeper understanding of how consumer evaluations are formed under the simultaneous influence of social media misinformation and the use of AI for information verification and decision support. In a context where online food information is often perceived as inconsistent, incomplete, or misleading, this research provides conceptual and empirical clarification regarding the role of evaluative mechanisms-perceived nutritional value and trust-in translating digital influences into intention.
The paper develops and validates a conceptual model that simultaneously captures a digital "erosion" factor (MISINFO) and an emerging "protective" mechanism (AAI), showing that sustainable purchase intention is shaped primarily through benefit evaluation, reflected in PNV, and complementarily through TRUST. The findings suggest that, for the analysed sustainable products, consumers calibrate their intention mainly through a "concrete benefit" logic (perceived nutritional value), indicating that health-related motives and perceived utility remain central to the adoption of sustainable food consumption. At the same time, TRUST functions as an uncertainty-reduction mechanism, strengthening purchase predisposition when the credibility of product-related information is perceived as high.
From an applied perspective, the study's conclusions carry relevant implications for companies, platforms, and institutions seeking to promote sustainable consumption and reduce the effects of food-related misinformation. First, producers and retailers should prioritise clear, verifiable, and comparable communication of real nutritional benefits, avoiding exaggerations and ambiguous messages that can fuel skepticism and weaken PNV. Second, digital platforms and public actors can use these findings to design interventions aimed at reducing MISINFO, such as signalling information quality, guiding users toward credible sources, and implementing nutrition and digital literacy programs. Third, developers and implementers of AI solutions can leverage the role of AAI highlighted in the model by integrating transparent verification features (citable sources, brief explanations of reasoning, and uncertainty warnings), so that AI acts as a credible decision-support mechanism rather than an amplifier of informational confusion. Beyond statistical significance, examining effect sizes (f2) provides insight into the practical relevance of the relationships. The results show that the relationship PNV → INTENT has by far the largest effect (f2 = 0.483), indicating that perceived nutritional benefit evaluation is the primary "driver" of purchase intention for the products analysed (products with alternative ingredients). This finding aligns with the sustainable food consumption literature, which highlights the dominant role of health-related motives and perceived benefits in shaping preferences and purchase intentions (e.g., pro-health motivations and the perception of a superior nutritional profile). In TPB- consistent terms, PNV can be interpreted as an expression of "beliefs about consequences/outcomes" that directly fuels intentional predisposition: when consumers believe a product provides real nutritional benefits (rather than merely a "healthy image"), intention increases substantially.
By contrast, the relationship TRUST → INTENT is positive and significant but exhibits a relatively small effect (f2 = 0.081). This suggests that, in the present context, consumers respond primarily to perceived benefits (what concrete gain they obtain) rather than to the general credibility of information (how much they trust the message). A critical interpretation is that trust functions more as an uncertainty-reduction and decision-"stabilisation" mechanism, rather than becoming the main determinant when benefit evaluation is clear. In other contexts (e.g., highly novel/controversial products or food technologies with elevated perceived risk), literature often reports a more pronounced role of trust; therefore, the smaller TRUST effect here may reflect the specific product category and respondents' reliance on perceived utility criteria (nutritional profile).
The managerial implications follow directly. First, brands and retailers should prioritise communicating and demonstrating real nutritional benefits: clear, comparable messages, avoidance of exaggerations and "health halo" framing, and arguments that translate benefits into easy-to-understand terms (e.g., fiber, fewer refined ingredients, suitability for regular consumption). Second, because TRUST plays a complementary role, credibility should be built through transparency (sources, certifications, evidence), but it should be viewed as a "hygiene factor" that supports decisions rather than the primary accelerator of intention. Third, given the effects of informational antecedents, interventions that reduce MISINFO and those that facilitate verification (including AI-based tools) can increase intention primarily by improving PNV evaluation, that is, by clarifying the perceived benefit.
The results are also relevant for public policy, as they indicate that sustainable purchase intention is shaped mainly by perceived nutritional benefit evaluation (PNV), while foodrelated misinformation on social media erodes this evaluation more strongly than it affects general trust. Accordingly, public interventions should target not only general "trustbuilding" but, more importantly, improving the quality and comparability of nutrition information in the digital space. A realistic direction is the development of nutrition and digital literacy programs (including recognising misleading messages), integrated into education and public campaigns to reduce vulnerability to MISINFO. In parallel, guidance or standardisation of nutrition-related claims and communication about sustainable products (including countering "health halo" effects) could help ensure benefits are expressed transparently and verifiably. For digital platforms, the findings support the need for credibility-signalling mechanisms (e.g., content labeling, directing users to validated sources, reducing the visibility of demonstrably false claims) in the food domain, where consequences can affect public health. Finally, the positive role of AAI suggests opportunities to support AI-based verification and explanation tools, but under public transparency principles (source disclosure, uncertainty signalling, hallucination avoidance) so that AI functions as infrastructure for reducing informational asymmetry rather than as an amplifier of misinformation.
Although the study provides valuable insights, it also has limitations that should be considered. First, the research relies on convenience sampling, which may limit the generalisability of the results to the broader population and does not allow the capture of changes in perceptions and intentions over time. Second, construct measurements are based on self-reports, which may introduce biases related to subjective perceptions of MISINFO and the reporting of AI use behaviours. Third, although the model captures relevant evaluative mechanisms, it does not explicitly include classical TPB variables such as subjective norms and perceived behavioural control, which could enrich the explanation of intention in social contexts strongly shaped by social media.
These limitations can be addressed in future research by using more diverse samples, integrating mixed methods (interviews/focus groups to deepen understanding of how consumers evaluate credibility and benefits), and conducting longitudinal or experimental designs. In particular, future studies could test controlled exposure models contrasting MISINFO content versus AI-verified content to more clearly observe causal mechanisms. Moreover, including moderators such as AI literacy, nutritional literacy, education level, or social media use frequently could clarify the conditions under which AAI truly becomes a protective mechanism. Finally, extending the analysis to other categories of sustainable products would allow an assessment of the generalisability of PNV's dominant role and TRUST's complementary role in shaping intention.
Additionally, future research may incorporate nutritional literacy and sustainability knowledge (which can directly influence benefit evaluations and the ability to interpret claims), health consciousness and pro-health motivations (which may strengthen PNV regardless of the informational climate), perceived quality of labeling and certifications (as indicators of credibility/clarity of on-pack information), source credibility (trust in institutions, experts, and brands) and general trust in social media, prior experience with sustainable products and satisfaction (which may stabilise both PNV and TRUST), as well as classical TPB variables such as subjective norms and perceived behavioural control (e.g., product accessibility, price, and availability), which can influence both evaluations and intention formation.
In conclusion, the results reflect the specific features of today's digital context, in which consumers are simultaneously exposed to informational risks and benefit from new verification tools. The study highlights the importance of the quality of perceived-benefit evaluations and information credibility in shaping sustainable purchase intention, and it offers a replicable model for investigating consumer behaviour in emerging markets, including Romania. By integrating MISINFO and AAI as antecedents and by emphasising the mediating roles of PNV and TRUST, the study represents a relevant step toward understanding sustainable food consumption behaviour in the digital era, providing useful insights for the academic community as well as for practitioners and policymakers.
