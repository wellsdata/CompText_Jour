Headnote This brief essay outlines the forms of fake news that arose in the journalism world in recent decades and shows how they eroded the credibility of mainstream news sources and created an opening for alternative news, including, ironically, fake news-which also takes many forms. In an attempt to distinguish fake news that is meant to deceive from fake news that is meant to amuse, the term "foke" news is proposed.
Keywords
AFS ethnographic thesaurus: Journalism, hoaxes, humor, satire, parody
there is a quip that journalism instructors become accustomed to hearing when we say that we teach a class in news media ethics: "Isn't that an oxymoron?" Meaning, aren't journalists-with their penchant for lurid tales of sex and violence, their disrespect for the privacy of the dramatis personae in such tales, and their eagerness to rush such stories into circulation before they have been verified-utterly devoid of ethics?
The image is a caricature, but its currency bespeaks growing skepticism about the reliability of the mainstream news media.1 That skepticism has information seekers exploring all manner of alternative news sources, from those that more or less adhere to traditional journalistic standards, to those that are openly partisan, to those that either spoof journalistic conventions or use them to perpetrate hoaxes.
This essay outlines the various forms of fake news that have arisen in the journalism world in the past couple of decades and tries to show how those in-house lapses, coincident with the rise of computer-mediated communications, may have contributed to the present overheated information environment in which new, extra-journalistic forms of fake news, some benign and some malignant, compete for our attention.
I will also offer some thoughts on how to distinguish folkloric fake news-perhaps we should call it "foke" news!-which is meant to amuse, from propagandistic fake news, which is meant to deceive.
Journalistic Fake News
I taught my first news media ethics class in 1998. I don't recall using the term "fake news" back then, but the slide carousels I inherited from my retired predecessor included examples of material that could be considered fake news. One set of slides consisted of newspaper copy that had all the trappings of editorial content-headline, byline, captioned photograph(s), multi-column layout, and, in some cases, the writer's mug shot-but which was, on closer inspection, an advertisement.2 Hence, the portmanteau term "advertorial."3 One such ad that appeared in the automotive section of a Pennsylvania newspaper a few years after I moved from the newsroom to the classroom began thus:
The Ford Explorer, which has virtually defined the compact sports utility vehicle segment since its introduction in 1990, gets an all-new platform for 2002, designed to offer performance and convenience in a robust yet comfortable package. ("2002 Ford Explorer" 2001)
The piece continued in this laudatory vein for almost 1,000 words. A cynic might say the Ford Motor Company had obviously greased the writer's palm, and, indeed, one expects even a rave review to pick at least a few nits. But keep in mind that the piece looked like editorial content and, just as importantly, did not look like most ads, and that genuine rave reviews are not unheard of. What is unheard of, or is at least exceedingly rare, is for a rave review to end the way this one did, with an exhortation to "test-drive the 2002 Ford Explorer today," followed by the address of the local Ford dealer. At that point, it finally became clear that the purpose of the piece was not to evaluate, but to promote.
There can be only one reason to design an ad to resemble news: to make it more believable. To be an ad in news clothing is to seemingly have your product touted by an independent, knowledgeable source. If a Ford Explorer ad tells you the Explorer is the best car in its class, you take it with a grain of salt: of course the ad is going to say that. If a piece of writing looks for all the world like it was written by a staff or syndicated automotive columnist, you might just be that much more inclined to buy the Explorer rather than one of the other cars in its class.
Paradoxically, by "hitchhiking" on the credibility of news,4 advertorials began to undermine the credibility of real news. Once you find out that a story you thought was legitimate news is actually an ad, you naturally begin to wonder if other stories are also ads in disguise. To fulfill an assignment I call Project Watchdog, which requires members of my class to be on the lookout for unethical news content, some students mistakenly submit items that they think are advertorials but that are, in fact, favorable reviews by reputable critics.
A second form of fake news in my slide carousels (which I later converted to transparencies and then to PowerPoint presentations5) was the digitally altered photograph. Folklorists know their precursors as tall-tale postcards: images of outsized fruits or vegetables on hay wagons or railroad flatcars accompanied by a caption that read something like "We grow em big here in [place name]," or pictures of mythic beasts like the jackalope (Welsch 1974). The development of software applications like Adobe Photoshop enabled computer jockeys to use mouse and keyboard to create the same effects that had formerly been produced using scissors and glue. While professional photojournalists resized and combined images to improve the composition or heighten the drama of news photos, wise guys used the technology to create bizarre or comical tableaus that, like urban legends, tended to address one or another aspect of the human predicament. A now-classic example combined the image of a leaping shark with the image of a Navy diver climbing a rope ladder to a helicopter to make it seem as if the diver was in mortal danger.
I sometimes tacked such "folk" productions, or "photoshops" (or more wittily, "fauxtography"), onto my classroom presentations for their entertainment value, but the focus of those presentations was on deceptive practices in the news business. Once viewers learn that they were duped into thinking a fake photograph was real-as with advertorials-they understandably begin to suspect that the real photos they encounter might be fake.6
The third kind of fake news I covered in my ethics class is usually referred to as "fabrication": instances where a reporter or columnist has invented a source or a quote or an entire scenario. The cautionary tale was Washington Post reporter Janet Cooke's 1980 portrait of an 8-year-old heroin addict. "Jimmy's World" was so convincing and disturbing that cooke was awarded the Pulitzer Prize-which then had to be returned when "Jimmy" was found to be a fictitious character (Maraniss 1981). Boston Globe columnist Patricia Smith and New Republic writer Stephen Glass provided ample fodder for my class when each was found to have invented sources in 1998 (Pogrebin 1998; Bissinger 2007).
Then came Jayson Blair, who both fabricated and plagiarized at the New York Times in 2003 (Barry et al. 2003). This was the worst, because the Times was the best. Tipof-the-iceberg thinking was inescapable: if a reporter was inventing news at the most respected paper in the land, it seemed reasonable to assume that the problem was widespread. That view was only reinforced when USA TODAY reporter Jack Kelley was found to have fabricated a story a few months later (Morrison 2004). But what made matters infinitely worse for the mainstream news media was that these fabrication scandals coincided with the proliferation of online alternatives.
The web slashed the cost of reaching an audience and thereby broke the monopoly of the old reliables of the news media. The fabrication scandals, coupled with relentless attacks from the right on the supposed liberal bias of the old reliables (with the exception of the Wall Street Journal), led disgruntled readers to seek-and find- alternative news sources, often ones that accorded with their own biases. One of the ways this played out was that the absence of a news story from a source like the New York Times no longer signaled that the story lacked legitimacy. It could mean that organizations like the Times were either suppressing news that challenged the status quo or that they were too slow and too wedded to outdated journalistic norms to get news that nimbler and perhaps less scrupulous organizations hustled onto their websites. The proliferation of upstart news sources and the public's greater openness to sources with an overt political bias or a warmer embrace of rumor and gossip made for a more complex media landscape, one in which it could be hard to tell legitimate sources from fraudulent ones, especially once "prosumers"7 began designing websites as platforms for fraudulent content that looked like the websites of legitimate news organizations (see Frank 2015).
Folkloric Fake News
In the early 2000s, many large universities experimented with courses for first-year students that would offset the alienation and anonymity of the lecture hall with the intimacy of the seminar room. In such a setting, new students would, it was hoped, develop relationships with faculty members and with their peers. In my college, perhaps to sweeten the pot, faculty members were given carte blanche as to subject matter. This was my chance, as a folklorist hired by a journalism department on the strength of a dozen years of newspaper experience, to teach a class in the discipline in which I was trained.
For the first few go-rounds, I called my first-year seminar "The Communication Underground: Jokes, Legends, and Personal Experience Narratives." The premise of the course was that there were "above-ground" channels of communication through which formal or quasi-official information emanating from news organizations, government agencies, and public relations practitioners reached the public, and there were underground channels of communication through which members of the public-prosumers-shared informal, decidedly unofficial jokes, legends, and stories with each other.
My thinking at the time was that the two forms of communication ran on parallel tracks that did not intersect, except insofar as the underground communication-the folklore-functioned as a sort of running commentary on the above-ground communication. In other words, much contemporary folklore is topical, mocking or mulling the official pronouncements, often in the style of the announcements themselves. Eventually I began calling this material "newslore," and I wrote a book about the various forms that newslore might take and what these texts might reveal about the vernacular response to the absurdities of contemporary life in the years just before and just after the beginning of the twenty-first century (Frank 2011).
Some newslore took the form of fake news. That is, if it was a story, it was written in the "disinterested prose style" (see Brodie 2018) of a news report. If it was a photograph, it was composed of (at least) two news photographs, digitally combined. Prior to the advent of Facebook and other social media, there was a sort of firewall that mostly separated fake news from real news: fake news was confined to email and overtly satirical websites. Real news was to be found on the websites of the so-called legacy media-the newspapers, radio stations, and television stations that had long been regarded as providers of reliable information. One had only to consider the source.
True, the more expertly crafted stories or images might sound or look enough like real news to fool some recipients, but as long as the primary intent was to satirize, the joke, at least in the stories, would usually become obvious eventually, if not right away (well-executed digitally altered photos might be harder to detect). For example:
Scientists Discover New Element
The heaviest element known to science was recently discovered by investigators at a major U.S. research university.
The element, tentatively named administratium, has no protons or electrons and thus has an atomic number of 0. However, it does have one neutron, 125 assistant neutrons, 75 vice neutrons and 111 assistant vice neutrons, which gives it an atomic mass of 312.
These 312 particles are held together by a force that involves the continuous exchange of meson-like particles called morons. Since it has no electrons, administratium is inert. (DeBuvitz 1989)
The first sentence (or "lede," in newsroom parlance) of this mock news story is entirely plausible. The name "administratium" may sound unlikely, but perhaps no more so than dubnium, dysprosium, erbium, and many of the other more recent additions to the periodic table. A reader who isn't all that conversant with chemistry might even skip past the lack of protons and electrons and not realize his leg is being pulled until he gets to the assistant and vice neutrons. As with all stories of this type, the deadpan news style adds to the humor of the message, which obviously mocks the burgeoning of bureaucracy as well as the news media's appetite for tales of scientific discovery.
Indeed, the administratium story was one of many from that period that dealt with science and technology. Also worth recalling are the following stories: news that Microsoft was poised to buy the US government; a snickering report on a study concluding that "women who perform the act of fellatio and swallow semen on a regular basis" reduce their risk of breast cancer; and a story about research discovering that after 5 years of marriage, most men stop having sex with women and spend their free time shopping for furniture and, therefore, researchers concluded, "turn gay." And so on.
The other popular subtype of fake news story from that era, still going strong, is the celebrity death story, which I have written about elsewhere (Frank 2011; see also Winick 2018). Then came the 9/11 attacks. Fear and anger found their expression in a flood of internet folklore that included fake news photographs (the most famous of which showed a man, soon to be known as "Tourist Guy," posing on the observation deck of the World Trade Center just before the first plane hit) and fake news stories warning of terrorist attacks at shopping malls or snail mail laced with anthrax (Frank 2004, 2011). I had no end of material for that first-year seminar.
Fake News vs. "Foke" News: Propaganda vs. Satire
Bit by bit and byte by byte, the boundary between real news and fake news has been blurred by the proliferation of real news websites and of fake news websites that look real; the steady erosion of credibility of traditional news organizations; the opportunity to turn a profit from page views of fake news (see Kitta 2018); and the sharing on social media, especially Facebook, of content that may have originated on some dubious platform. As a result, fake news has come to be regarded as a scourge rather than a tonic.
It's tempting for a folklorist to argue that there is good fake news and bad fake news, much as we now differentiate between good cholesterol and bad cholesterol. By that, I don't mean that good fake news skewers people we don't like and buttresses people we do like, while bad fake news does the opposite. Salutary fake news is primarily intended to amuse, spoofing both the style and the substance of news. Malign fake news is primarily intended to misinform and/or generate income. Putting it another way, the good stuff is witty. The bad stuff is not.
Consider two photoshops that circulated within a year of each other. The first, which went viral in 2004, shows John Kerry, the Democratic Party's presidential nominee, on a speaker's platform with Jane Fonda. It was no secret that Kerry became an antiwar activist after he served, with valor, in Vietnam, but linking him to Hanoi Jane, widely reviled for visiting North Vietnam and denouncing the American war effort, was clearly meant to impugn his patriotism. The photo was made more convincing by the inclusion of a headline, a caption, and an Associated Press photo credit.8
The second photo, which went viral after Hurricane Katrina made landfall in 2005, shows President George W Bush and his father, former President George H. W Bush, proudly displaying a fish they had caught from a boat cruising the flooded streets of New Orleans. Like a good political cartoon, the photo is both funny and deadly earnest. The message: these guys are so out of touch with the suffering of ordinary Americans that they see the catastrophe as nothing more than opportunity to do some fishin. True, the photo fooled some people, but its intent, clearly, was more to mock than to inflict political damage. Bush had, after all, recently begun his second term.
At its best, folkloric fake news functions as folk political commentary, skewering the pomposity and hypocrisy of powerful people and institutions, much as editorial cartoons and satirical political commentary do. At a time when the president of the United States is condemning as fake news any reporting that falls short of glorifying him, while ignoring the pernicious fake news that may have contributed to his election,9 it may trouble people to see folklorists defending, even celebrating, satirical fake news. Hence, my half-facetious suggestion that we distinguish the satirical material from the propaganda by calling it "foke" news.10 It isn't necessarily the job of the folklorist to defend or promote this material, but to do what we have always done, which is to try to account for the artistry of folklore and to understand how it expresses the ethos of the group that shares it.
The line between fake and foke, admittedly, is a blurry one. Right after the November 2016 election, one of my colleagues had his students read and respond to a story published in the Boston Tribune. The headline: "Obama Considers Executive Order That Could Affect 'Fake' News Outlets." The lede, uncorrected:
In wake of the controversial 2016 presidential election, President Obama has signed what may be one of his final executive orders. On Monday morning, President Obama signed Executive Order 13749 banning all fake news websites and ensuring penalties ranging from fines/fees to criminal prosecution for those that own and maintain such websites. (quoted in "Execu-Fib Order" 2016)
There are plenty of grammatical and stylistic nits to pick here (as is often the case with fake news stories, this one deteriorates as it goes along11); nevertheless, of the 26 media studies students who read the article, only four realized it was fake news. As my colleague discussed these results in class, one student protested that an authority figure (the instructor himself) required them to read the story as an assignment, so that created a presumption of legitimacy. But that was exactly the point. A well-designed website of a news organization that calls itself the Boston Tribune also creates a presumption of legitimacy (see McNeill 2018). What makes this bit of foke news so brilliant is that it further buttresses its legitimacy by borrowing from the playbook of those who debunk fake news, pointing out how one could tell that a fake news story was fake:
According to multiple sources, the fake news article that was the apparent "last straw" leading-up to President Obama's decision to sign Executive Order 13749 was an article run on a fake news website with the misleading domain-www.thebostontribune.com.co. The website was designed to trick readers into thinking that they were reading information from our publication (The Boston Tribune), however-the almost unnoticeable distinction between our website and this fake website was the ".co" added to the end of the domain. (quoted in "Execu-Fib Order" 2016)
Folklore has long been regarded as synonymous with antiquated cultural practices, but in cyberspace, the folk appear to be ahead of the curve. While the humorless propagandists generate fake news, the clever folk have moved on to fake news about fake news.
Sidebar Russell Frank is both a folklorist and a journalist. He earned his PhD in Folklore from the University of Pennsylvania and worked as a reporter and editor at newspapers in California and Pennsylvania for 13 years.
