Headnote Reacting to the recent political importance given to "fake news" folklorists should make use of concepts and methods generated in the past generation to discuss contemporary legends. Instead of using "legend" (or "fake news") as a tag indicating skepticism, we should see legendry as a communal fact-finding process found among all classes, including this nation's presidents. And we should frankly and objectively examine how high political officials engage in legendry, knowing that both promoting and casting doubt on constructions of the news have a political agenda.
Keywords
AFS ethnographic thesaurus: Contemporary legends, politics, propaganda, mass media
in the interests of advancing the study of "fake news," I begin by addressing the substance of that emic term. As Tom Mould comments in his introduction to this special issue of JAF, this concept has a wide range of connotations rather than a single meaning (2018b). Merriam-Webster lexicographers observe that the combination of common words has been used as a synonym for "hoax" at least since the 1890s, when it began to appear alongside the older phrase "false news" (Merriam-Webster 2017). The recent interest in this particular phrase began in mid-November 2016, according to Google Trends ("Fake News" 2017), driven by its use by Donald Trump to claim that the mainstream media has maliciously distorted his actions. This president has since used the term in a loose, derogatory way simply to deny the truth of a breaking news story, and folklorists should concede that he is on firm ground in doing so: political bias is and always has been a factor in journalism. One person's "fact" is an editor's "delete," an old reporters' saw holds, and Trump's argument that his media critics cherry-pick quotes and details to reinforce a negative stereotype of his administration deserves respect.
For this reason, our examination of the claims that form part of the "fake news" and "alternative facts" phenomenon must take care to use our existing intellectual tools carefully and dispassionately. But we also need to move ahead with the courage of our disciplinary knowledge, which is far more than a vast compilation of quaint beliefs, stories, and crafts. That is the core argument of the paper that I circulated to the contributors to this issue before our presentations at the annual conference of the American Folklore Society (Ellis 2018). Folklore is a universal human imperative, and we must study its influence and significance wherever we find it. Yet in the past, artificial classist stereotypes have hindered this study by claiming that political elites communicate in ways intrinsically different from those used by the general public in everyday use. This perpetuated old stereotypes of "the folk" as an ignorant, backward subculture while academic and political elites are smart enough and educated enough not to need folklore. And yet, as my paper documents, two sitting American presidents used legendry to justify acts of war, while a third politician, on his way to being elected to that position, likewise used legendry to propose and justify changes in immigration policy. Folklore is folklore, I argue, and we lose our credibility in the academy and in the eyes of the educated lay public when we choose to study marginalized classes and close our eyes to parallel actions by prominent government officials.
Now I turn to the three important questions raised by "fake news" panel convener Dorothy Noyes.
1.What challenges are posed to established folkloristic approaches? I think the challenge is to our timidity in applying our tools to mass culture dynamics, particularly those of political importance. In previous situations dealing with small-town politics such as the Hazleton immigration controversy (Fine and Ellis 2010:95ff), I found folkloristic concepts helpful in interpreting the reasons for emergent rumor-panics. These were often therapeutic for the communities affected, even though they had the side effect of projecting negative stereotypes onto individuals and groups seen as deviant or foreign (see also Ellis 2002). As such events gained momentum, group violence, even ritual murder, became a real possibility, and they spread the seeds of future violence in the same area.
So one challenge is to our traditional methodological focus on ethnography, which privileges oral discourse and face-to-face contact in an age where the dynamics found in such situations are at work in web-based and video-based communication. With due respect to Linda Dégh, who famously insisted that the theories and methods used to study legend "cannot be borrowed from another discipline [but] must use the unique resources of folkloristics" (2001:203), I believe we should be familiar with the methodologies used by other disciplines, particularly sociology and group communication, to observe, data-mine, and conceptualize political events such as these when folklore is a motivating factor.1
Another challenge is to the presumption that folklore is predominantly or basically good in nature, speaking to cherished values that we attribute to past golden ages. This assumption, admittedly, is more often popular than academic, though it is implicit in many of the romantic characterizations of folklore collected up to the mid-twentieth century. These often held up tradition as intrinsically positive, resisting the allegedly dehumanizing influence of mass media and modern economics. "Values hold people together in groups," Henry Glassie argued, and when individuals function as "artistic communicators in ongoing associations," they "use personal power for collective, moral ends." It follows, then, that folklore, both as academic subject and as academic discipline, "stands against amorality" (1983:131-2).2
However, if the concepts we have developed are valid for describing all human behavior, we should expect them to be used as often for immoral agendas. We might even characterize such folk processes as evil in nature-certainly sociopathic at the very least, functioning in a "wedge-driving" way to reflect and justify one's hatred of others (see Fine and Turner 2001:64 and passim). One thing that holds back the development of the discipline is the way that other people, even fellow academics, see folklorists as studying things they like rather than confronting dynamics and functions of folk discourse that have troublesome consequences for society.3 The discipline risks seeming like a pastime for bored researchers, a discretionary night job rather than a socially necessary day job.
2.What tools do we have for assessing bad faith in communication? As noted, the term "fake news" as used by Trump and his supporters is similar to the call of "urban legend" or "urban myth" in a previous generation; that is, it is used to refer to a claim that is maliciously or ignorantly circulated as truth. Folklorists, too, have at times used such terms in a similarly loose way, implying that such stories are hoaxes, by nature intentionally misinformed. Such an assumption was built into the concept of "fabulate," the dominant class of legendry in twentieth-century folkloristics, by C. W von Sydow, who argued that it described events that "could not have actually happened in the form that they take in the telling" (quoted in Pentikäinen 1973:221). The matter of whether truth claims fall into different categories depending on whether we can demonstrate that they are objectively true or false continues to bedevil folk narrative research; indeed, the hallmark of legendry appears to be such claims' liminal status, making it impossible to determine their ultimate truth at the moment of performance. To suggest that this involves "bad faith in communication," I think, blurs this important aspect of legendry: one may advance a claim without having secured standards of evidence for it, but this is not "bad faith," just part of the human condition.
Folklorists have access to more nuanced and objective methods of observing and analyzing the political implications of legendry, whether made in good or bad faith. The ideas in the paper that I circulated were developed as part of my work on smalltown rumors and panics concerning satanic cults (Ellis 2000:202ff, 2001:199ff). But perhaps we should develop a longer sense of disciplinary history, looking back to the response of African ethnographers to the end of colonialism (see Ellis 2007) and to the work of labor activist and (later) sociologist William H. Friedland, one of the first scholars to use the term "urban myth" in the same sense as "urban legend." Writing in 1960, well before the folkloristic boom in "urban legend" research, Friedland was cautious not to define the traditions he encountered in Tanganyika in terms of literal truth or untruth. "Myth is not being used here in the sense of a 'false belief,'" he explained, "but as a set of ideas which can be utilized as a basis for action" (1960:85n; emphasis added). In the nearly 60 years that have passed since Friedlands paper, I don't think that American folklorists have come close to a definition of legendry that is nearly as concise or as incisive. One that comes close is Dégh and Vázsonyi's dictum: "The legend is neither always untrue nor always true; it is always right" (1973:16). That is, I would paraphrase, the legend is always fundamentally political in its impact.
In any case, Africa-based ethnography deserves credit for being well ahead of European and American folkloristics in recognizing and documenting the political nature of legendry, as seen in Luise White's probing analysis of the legends and panics generated by organ-theft legends (2000). And the effort of FOAFTale News to collect and document such ephemeral traditions at the time of this country's Desert Storm incursion of 1992 prodded South African journalist Arthur Goldstuck to engage in a similar project during the first months of 1994, as his nation moved toward democracy with Nelson Mandela as its first president. The resulting book, Ink in the Porridge (Goldstuck 1994), remains an invaluable document of how the diverse factions of this troubled country responded to unprecedented change with the help of legendry.
"Fake news," in sum, is often unverifiable, implausible, sensational, and internally inconsistent. But as more objective observers have noted, even debunked urban legends contain elements that are verifiably part of contemporary realities (child abduction, violence against females in mall parking lots, home invasions, etc.). And, as at least one sociologist (Kapferer 1993) has documented, they are circulated more often in a critical or skeptical context than in one that presumes committed belief by the narrator and his/her audience. They are, however, efforts to characterize a changing reality, and they include or imply calls for political action.
So the task of the folklorist is to grasp what is being claimed by the cry of "fake news" and the extent to which that claim does identify legitimate concerns about media conduits. Particularly we should look at the media's rush to circulate sensational or damaging claims on the basis of sources that (like the "friend of a friend") cannot be identified publicly and so cannot be either verified or discredited. In the essay Mould contributes to this issue, he argues that "fake news" is distinct from legend when it refers to a news story that is consciously fabricated, "because there is no doubt about its truth" (i.e., "I know that I'm lying to you") (Mould 2018a:414). Significantly, though, he concedes that if the story is then passed on by others who find it credible, even when they doubt its truth, it becomes a type of legend. Thus, "fake news" becomes part of a cultural debate process, a claim that cannot be confirmed on the basis of secure standards of evidence. In other words, it becomes a form of legendry.
Presidents may likewise assert claims that are unconfirmable in the same way, or indeed assert that they are true without even referencing a source, even secret intelligence. However, such heads of state are not subject to institutional checking (though the media certainly appropriates this action), as they are hired and fired through the electoral process-and, hypothetically, through the impeachment process, though the Constitution's requirement of "high crimes and misdemeanors" has not so far been taken to include passing on a dubious claim that the president has heard through the grapevine.
For this reason, "fake news" means something different for presidents and for media professionals, who are, as Russell Frank aptly observes in this issue, subject to internal checking for the imprudent use of false information. When revealed by internal fact-checking, this lack of secured sources of evidence is often grounds for terminating the reporter responsible. And, when such professional carelessness is revealed by rival news services, it compromises the credibility of the network. By contrast, when presidents claim a media story is "fake news," they are not obliged to back that charge with verifiable "alternative facts." Rather, that statement is analogous to an ordinary skeptic's statement of disbelief in a story that may (or may not) be an "urban legend" in the popular sense.
Presidents are subject instead to what Gary Alan Fine has described as "politics of plausibility" (1995). In this dynamic, claims are provisionally accepted so long as they make "intuitive and cultural sense" to most Americans and are "at least potentially true" (Fine and Ellis 2010:5; emphasis in original). In cases where fact-checking is indeed lacking and such stories are misleading by institutional intent, we can legitimately term them "propaganda" or "disinformation." Those terms, I agree, would be a valid characterization for the 1990 Hill & Knowlton Capitol Hill media show that I discuss in my longer essay, which included false testimony about babies being evicted from incubators in the wake of the Iraqi occupation of Kuwait (2017). But when such testimony is picked up by others and passed on as both plausible and credible, then, even by Mould's and Frank's sense of narrative genres, they become legendry.
For when such stories enter the conduits in which they actively circulate, as Dégh and Vázsonyi have noted (1995), they circulate among groups of people who know each other well and share common standards of interest and plausibility. Thus, most parties in "fake news" debates are not subject to institutional fact-checking processes, even when such resources might be available to them. The senior President Bush, for example, was electrified by the looted incubators story and used it and other such stories to justify his actions in approving the Desert Storm action (see Ellis 2018). No evidence has emerged since then to indicate that he or his advisors acted from a conscious effort to mislead others. They may well have been misled, just as intelligence officials in the junior President Bush's administration were misled concerning the "weapons of mass destruction" claim.
But being misled is not the same as intending to mislead. In situations where truth is impossible to verify, such claims may stand on their own, and high political officials may not have verifiable "alternative facts" that would demonstrate the falsity of these claims and the self-serving agenda for which they were concocted. This is the situation in which humans resort to legendry, and there is no reason to believe that high-ranking, putatively intelligent officials are superhuman.
Further, the discovery that alleged "alternative facts" may turn out to be untrue or fabricated may have little or no consequences on some sides. A third of Americans, when polled a year after the occupation of Iraq, claimed that weapons of mass destruction had in fact been found (Lewandowski et al. 2005). Similarly, as Patricia A. Turner notes in this issue of JAF, fact-based efforts to counter the "Birther" rumors that Barack Obama was actually born in Kenya were in fact ineffective among a wide range of Americans (Turner 2018). A poll conducted after Trump's election showed that 57 percent of the winning side thought these claims were "probably" or "definitely" true (Glum 2017). With due respect to Mould's plea for "doubt-based" study of legend (2018a:415), the surprisingly strong power of belief in the face of authoritative fact remains a valid focus for folkloristic research.4
3.What intellectual resources does our field offer for combating social mistrust and beginning to re-establish shared social facts? I would ask whether our field has such intellectual resources, or needs them. In general, our integral skills include the ability to see communicative events clearly and to comprehend the roles played by traditional values and verbal skills in these events. This can provide an alternative to becoming emotionally caught up in the arguments being made or taking sides in a personal rather than an expert way. So a reasoned engagement with the "fake news" dynamic can give folklorists credibility as expert observers among other professional fields, as Diane E. Goldstein found in the Newfoundland AIDS outbreak (2004). And the objective distinctions we make on the basis of our knowledge of how legendry works can make us partners with other disciplines in accomplishing cultural needs such as containing disease outbreaks or integrating newly arrived immigrants into small-town societies.
But "social mistrust" and the sense that we need to privilege certain "shared social facts" will eternally be part of the ongoing debate that is part of human experience. If priests and saints can do little to change this, though armed with the threats of divine judgment and eternal damnation, I don't know that we academic folklorists can expect to do more.
But what we can do, we should have the courage to carry through.
