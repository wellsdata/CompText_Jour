Although the spread of misinformation has escalated during the Covid-19 pandemic, the propagation of deceptive medical claims is as old as the health care profession itself. The ease with which misinformation and disinformation (inaccurate information that is purposefully misleading) are spread by means of social media, however, presents new and complex challenges. A recent survey found that 95% of people in the United States perceived misinformation to be a problem and 41% were very or extremely worried that they were personally exposed to misinformation. 1
In response to these challenges, the U.S. surgeon general recently issued an urgent advisory on misinformation as a public health threat. The report calls on health care organizations to proactively train their staff to fight misinformation. It recommends that health care professionals tackle misinformation using both technology (i.e., social media) and traditional media platforms to share accurate information. But clinicians who use social media to respond to misinformation face important obstacles: the lack of accountability for users (including health care professionals) who willfully spread disinformation and for the companies that provide them with a platform, the frequent online attacks and threats targeting clinicians who attempt to correct information, and insufficient clinician training in strategies for addressing misinformation. Overcoming these barriers is critical to engaging clinicians in responding to misinformation online, which is vital for improving trust in the medical profession more broadly.
Attacking disinformation at its source is essential. Clinicians have advocated for social-media companies to be held accountable for the spread of medical disinformation on their platforms. The Center for Countering Digital Hate has identified what it calls the “disinformation dozen” — 12 accounts (operated by individual users and one couple) that are responsible for the bulk (65%) of antivaccine disinformation on Facebook and Twitter. As of December 2020, the 425 most-followed antivaccine social-media accounts had an estimated combined reach of 59.2 million users. Some of these accounts are “verified,” which provides them with a stamp of credibility, despite the false information they spread. Although company executives have promised to take action against the disinformation dozen, little has been done. At the very least, we believe these users should lose their verified status; ideally, they should face sanctions for spreading false information, including the loss of their account privileges.
Some of the users perpetuating the spread of disinformation are clinicians, which undermines trust in the medical profession. We are encouraged by announcements that the Federation of State Medical Boards and various medical and nursing boards plan to take action against clinicians who purposely generate or spread disinformation, including by suspending their licenses to practice. Starting with the disinformation dozen will be an important test of this process. Nonpartisan grassroots groups consisting of health care professionals and members of the public, such as No License for Disinformation, have also been created to advocate that medical boards and governing bodies step in to protect the public from harm caused by medical disinformation.
Health care professionals who want to dispel harmful myths cannot be expected to publicly combat misinformation without protection from attacks and harassment. Our research, conducted before the Covid-19 pandemic, revealed that one in four physicians had been attacked on social media. The most common reason was that they had posted advocacy-related content, particularly about vaccines. 2 One in six female physicians reported being sexually harassed on social media — and the rate is probably higher among women who are also members of minority racial or ethnic groups or other marginalized groups. A survey conducted during the pandemic found that up to two thirds of physicians and scientists who had been interviewed by media outlets about Covid-19 reported being attacked (often online, but in several cases physically) because of their social-media posts or media appearances. 3 Alarmingly, 22% of respondents reported that they had received threats of physical or sexual violence, and 15% had received death threats. When they are harassed, physicians and scientists often don’t alert their employers because they don’t believe the employer will take action. Experts who reported being attacked said they will be less likely to participate in media interviews in the future — a finding that highlights the effectiveness of the attacks.
Strategies are needed for helping health care professionals who are attacked and for preventing attacks. One approach is to adopt a group identity. For example, at the beginning of the pandemic, Illinois health care professionals who are active on social-media platforms (including us) formed the Illinois Medical Professionals Action Collaborative Team (IMPACT) to create infographics to dispel misinformation. When one group member was attacked online, other members came to the person’s aid, both online and in person. Similar national-level organizations, such as This Is Our Shot, have been formed to support the Covid-19 vaccination effort.
It is also important for employers and other organizations to counsel and support health care professionals who face attacks and harassment on social media. Groups such as Hollaback!, Shots Heard Round the World, and PEN America offer training in responding to online attacks, including by preventing aggressive moves such as “doxxing,” or the online release of a target’s personal information. Institutions, employee assistance programs, and medical associations can help disseminate such training to health care professionals. Finally, we believe people who abuse social-media platforms to harm or threaten clinicians who dispute misinformation should face sanctions, such as the removal of their verified status and limitations on their ability to post.
Misinformation interferes with the doctor–patient relationship and causes harm. 4 The urgent need to train health care professionals in using scientific communication to combat misinformation is clear, but strategies for providing such training aren’t well described. Encouraging health care professionals to dispel misinformation without providing formal training could exacerbate the spread of misinformation online and in clinical interactions. For example, one popular messaging tactic involves presenting a myth first, followed by the relevant facts. Yet studies show that this approach can backfire by increasing the retention of misinformation, since cognitive biases favor the recall of the first content presented. 5 Instead, clinicians can debunk misinformation by means of the three-step process used by climate scientists, which involves first stating a fact, then stating the related myth, and finally explaining the fallacy — or why the myth is false. Outlining why misinformation is false can help change people’s minds. It’s equally important to consider when to debunk misinformation. Debunking misinformation before it is commonplace can actually increase its spread. On the other hand, “prebunking,” or providing general science education before myths appear, can be particularly effective.
We believe medical schools and universities should invest in teaching students strategies for effectively communicating scientific information, approaches to identifying and debunking misinformation, and the ways in which cognitive biases predispose people to believing misinformation. At the University of Chicago, biologic science majors are encouraged to participate in courses taught by science-communication experts on explaining their work to the public. We have taught a medical-misinformation course for students at the Pritzker School of Medicine. Methods such as SIFT (stop, investigate the source, find trusted coverage, and trace to the original context), which was developed by librarians, can be used to train clinicians to identify trusted sources of information, and health care professionals can then share these strategies with their patients. Empowering future clinicians to address misinformation and share accurate information with patients in an empathetic way is particularly important, given widespread distrust of the health care system. In spite of this general distrust, patients typically report trusting their own clinicians, and nurses consistently rank among the most-trusted professionals in the United States, with physicians not far behind.
To help health care professionals successfully tackle medical misinformation online, it’s imperative not only to instill in future clinicians a recognition of the importance of science communication and train them in dispelling medical misinformation, but also to support professionals who are attacked online and to ensure that various stakeholders, including social-media companies and licensing boards, hold people who spread disinformation accountable for the harm they cause.
