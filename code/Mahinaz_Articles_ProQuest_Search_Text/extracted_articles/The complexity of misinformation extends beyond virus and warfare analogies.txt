Main text
In an age of generative AI and global crises, the impact of mis- and strategic disinformation has become a growing concern, with the Global Economic Forum rating it as the most significant global risk for 2025–2026 1 and others calling it a substantial threat for democracies 2 . Yet, others argue that the threats of misinformation are misunderstood 3 , and that dramatic warnings might have more severe consequences than misinformation itself 4 . To move forward in a space marked by such controversies, we must account for the complexity of misinformation: the different actors involved, their relations to each other within and across different analytical levels, and the links and feedback loops between them, shaping how actors adapt to each other 5 . However, this complexity can be systematically disguised by our attempts to grasp it.
Misinformation is often described using analogies such as “information warfare” 6 , “information pollution” 7 , or “misinformation infecting our minds” 8 . By identifying similarities between unrelated concepts, such analogies make complex or abstract ideas more accessible through comparison with other familiar or concrete phenomena. In the misinformation context, analogies are, for example, used to stress the phenomenon’s harmfulness and the urgency for countermeasures.
Analogies also raise specific expectations (e.g., for a defense shield or a cure). However, countermeasures inspired by these analogies often fail to account for the phenomenon’s full complexity: the multiple levels, from individual to society, through which misinformation propagates, the different actors involved and their relations to each other, the dynamics that benefit disseminating misleading content, or the nonlinear interactions and feedback loops that characterize this complex system 9 . For example, psychological inoculation, a technique inspired by understanding misinformation as a virus, is an effective tool to strengthen resilience on the micro level of individual recipients 8 . However, it fails to account for higher-level variables such as weak media environments 10 or intergroup dynamics that motivate people to engage voluntarily with misleading content 11 . More concerning, interventions that fail to account for the phenomenon’s complexity—such as oversimplified recommendations not to trust online content—can backfire, increasing susceptibility to misinformation over time 12 .
The limitations of single analogies have been noted before in the broader field of belief dynamics , which models how beliefs form, spread, and change in individuals, groups, and societies. Research in this field often uses analogies to other well-understood processes (like epidemics or ferromagnetism) to gain analytical traction. However, while such analogies provide crucial “conceptual mileage,” they also carry significant “conceptual baggage” 13 . Specifically, they often entail simplified assumptions obscuring the socio-cognitive complexities of how humans adopt and share beliefs.
In the following, we will first discuss the definition of misinformation before introducing our new integrative multilevel framework for understanding the complex system through which misinformation propagates. Our approach adapts recent integrative models of belief dynamics 14,15 and applies them to the specific problem of misinformation. Afterwards, we will review prominent analogies in light of this framework, showing each analogy’s conceptual mileage and baggage 13 . We will demonstrate that while popular analogies have successfully been deployed to raise public awareness and ideate countermeasures, their limitations obscure salient aspects and limit the gamut of effective countermeasures. Finally, we will discuss the implications for policy and further research.
Misinformation as a complex concept
In this article, we use Freiling et al.’s 16 definition of misinformation as “claims—well-intentioned or not—that are at odds with the best available empirical evidence” (p. 143). This umbrella definition includes unintentional falsehoods (misinformation in a narrower sense) and strategic deception (disinformation). It acknowledges that our evaluation of misinformation’s credibility is embedded in a specific socio-historical context. Yet, acknowleding changing evidence does not imply that there are no evidence-based claims. For example, converging evidence demonstrates anthropogenic climate change 17 and the effectiveness and safety of vaccines 18 . Deviations from evidence can emerge on multiple dimensions: the core content of a message (e.g., a lie), a message’s meta-information (e.g., a false source attributed to a claim), or the biased presentation of content 19 (e.g., cherry-picking scientific findings to fit one’s partisan narrative). Misinformation exists in different modalities (e.g., text, image, video) and can include inauthentic dissemination patterns (e.g., through coordinated link sharing or fake accounts 20 ).
Further complicating the handling of misinformation, human beliefs are shaped not only by evidence but also by factors on the micro level of the individual and higher-order pressures. For example, people process information to be consistent with their beliefs, leading to several well-documented biases regarding how they see themselves (“I see the world as it is”, naive realism 21 ), their social groups (“we are the good ones”, ingroup bias 22 ) and the larger societal systems in which they operate (“the status-quo is fair”, system justification 23 ). These biases shape how information (true, false, or non-verifiable opinions) aligns with, promotes, or challenges the specific values, political goals, identities, and desired social orders of individuals, groups or political systems 24 . Furthermore, different contexts offer more or less misleading content 25 . For example, political misinformation originates more often in authoritarian than democratic regimes 26 and alternative counter media in many democracies tolerate more blatant misinformation than their “mainstream” counterparts e.g., 27 .
Misinformation often also opposes evidence-based belief formation. Examples include promoting logical fallacies 28 , relying on social proof instead of more reliable evidence 29 , or cultivating distrust toward verification methods and societal institutions 30 . Understanding misinformation requires accounting for these complex, multilevel forces.
Misinformation’s complex multilevel system
Figure 1 presents our framework for visualizing the structure through which misinformation propagates, is consumed, and evaluated. This structure is best understood as a complex multilevel network of different prototypical actors and their relations to each other. It can create pathways for rapid cascades 31 and homogenous communication spaces 32 , demonstrating sensitivity to network topologies 33,34 . Understanding such typologies requires considering the scales of interaction and organization, whether small friend groups or large-scale social media platforms. Fig. 1 Visualization of the complex multilevel system in which misinformation propagates. [Images not available. See PDF.]
The solid lines symbolize stronger ties; dashed lines symbolize weaker ties between actors 88 along which (mis-) information can propagate. Individual agents can create, spread, receive, ignore, or counter misinformation on the micro level, and their misperceptions can vary momentarily and over time. The response of (dis-) belief varies by audience characteristics (e.g., their cognitive style 89 ) and characteristics of the content and the source (e.g., their credibility), which are influenced by high-order factors. On the meso-level I , individuals are embedded in different social groups and part of broader social categories (e.g., nations, religions). Sociologically, these groups differ in their level of homogeneity but also in the strength of the ties between their members (e.g., primary groups such as families vs. secondary groups such as co-workers 90,91 ). Individuals furthermore identify themselves to varying degrees with multiple groups (their ingroups) and distinguish these from other groups (their outgroups 92 ). These relations shape how people process misinformation. For example, content benefiting one’s ingroup (vs. an outgroup) is evaluated more favorably 93 , particularly under conditions of intergroup conflict 94 and polarization 44 . On the meso-level II , different “types” or “genres” of social media can serve as venues for interaction and misinformation. While not mutually exclusive, these types bundle different social media elements 95 (e.g., a profile, feed, and chat) and technical affordances 96 (e.g., anonymity, visibility), shaping adverse effects 97 and misinformation dynamics. Social media platforms are also actors themselves. For example, they can be more or less oriented towards the broader societal mainstream versus counter-publics and alternative ideations and can have different buisiness models. This can shape their stance towards misinformation 98 . Finally, on the macro level , across countries, societal subsystems, such as legal regulations, the media system, or the political landscape, are intertwined differently in democratic and autocratic states, shaping misinformation dynamics across the globe 25,80 .

The suggested network structure unfolds on four levels. Three are typically distinguished in the social sciences as levels of analysis 35 : the individual micro level, the aggregate of individuals on the meso-level I, and the macro level of the whole system. The fourth level (meso-level II) accounts for the specific context of digital platforms. Building on prior theorizing in communication science, we consider the micro level of the individual and the macro level of the entire system as analytical anchors and allow for multiple meso levels between them 36 . On the micro level of individuals, misinformation is first and foremost a communication process in which prototypical actors create, spread, and receive misinformation. On the meso-level I, these actors are embedded in social groups whose relationships (e.g., ingroup/outgroup dynamics) shape the flow of (mis-) information. On the meso-level II, interactions take place across different social media platforms with diverging technological set-ups and proprietary interests (e.g., competing for advertisement revenues). Finally, all prior levels are embedded in the macro level of societies with their socio-political and legal systems. Across all levels, actors can contribute to misinformation dynamics due to various motivations (e.g., political 37 , economic 38,39 or social 40 ) and dissemination is subject to selective pressures, including biases, and ideological dynamics, which often do not prioritize factuality or truthfulness equally.
Crucially, these levels are porous and dynamically coupled within a complex adaptive system . The complexity of misinformation emerges from these dense, multi-directional interactions and the constant adaptation of actors across scales, not from simple linear aggregation. Micro-level behaviors, such as the decision to share content, are not isolated from higher-order pressures. Platform design choices (meso-level II) can distract or focus user attention on evaluating the credibility of the content they are sharing 41 , versus, for example, the concerns of one’s social group 42,43 . This dynamically interacts with factors on the meso-level I, where high affective polarization can create selective pressures towards in-group based identity-affirming content irrespective of its epistemic value 44 . Engagement-based algorithmic curation on platforms may then amplify resonant content (meso II). Simultaneously, user communities actively adapt to platform affordances, leading to a continuous co-evolution: platforms adapting their features and policies in response to user behavior (linking micro, meso I, and meso II) as well as regulatory pressure (macro level e.g., 45 ), while users and communities creatively appropriate the characteristics of their digital environment or migrate to platforms with different features or platform policies 46 . The result is a recursive entanglement of psychological, social, technical, and political factors.
We submit that, like all analytical aggregations, our framework may inevitably fail to capture the full nuances of human experiences. We propose this framework as an analytical heuristic or scaffolding designed to make these complex interactions tractable by providing a systematic way to map and understand the structure and potential feedback loops that define the complexity of misinformation (Fig. 1).
Review of key analogies
In public and scholarly discourse, the complexity of misinformation is often simplified through the use of metaphors, a figure of speech that directly states one thing is another, even though it is not literally true. People use metaphors to highlight a shared quality between two different things or to encourage a fresh perspective. Metaphors are frequently used by journalists, civic organizations, or politicians to illustrate the (alleged) risks of misinformation, for example, when the US-republican news diet before the January 6th storming of the Capitol is called a “deadly disease” or “foxitis” 47 . Three of these metaphors have become particularly prominent: misinformation as an “infodemic”, “information warfare,” and “information pollution,” rooted in medicine, warfare, and environmental topics, respectively.
Another prominent, albeit contested term is “fake news”, a description of misleading content mimicking professional news 48 that is also used to discredit unpopular coverage by political actors 49 . Global search trends show the popularity of these terms and provide an initial insight into how they shape how people understand the complexity of misinformation. An analogy often serves an explanatory purpose. It is a comparison between two systems or structures used to explain or reason about one of them. Analogies highlight structural similarities or functional correspondences between two distinct domains. Vivid metaphors like ‘infodemic’ or ‘information warfare’ are powerful rhetorical tools and valuable starting points for discussion, yet relying on them as analytical frameworks requires a structured comparison—an analogy—between the source domain (e.g., epidemics, warfare) and the target (misinformation). Scholarly work has frequently built upon metaphors of misinformation, treating them essentially as analogies, as a virus 8 , a weapon 50 , or a toxin in the environment 51 .
Following a search for each analogy using the respective keyword on the 22nd of May 2025 in the Web of Science, a central interdisciplinary academic database that offers access to scholarly journals and academic conference proceedings, 1275 scholarly contributions addressed the term “infodemic”, another 1258 examined “information war*”, and 103 looked at “information pollution” in their title or abstract. However, each of these analogies brings its conceptual baggage, potentially obscuring the phenomenon it tries to explain.
To illustrate each analogy’s strengths and inherent perceptual gaps, we systematically mapped its core implications onto the components of our multilevel framework (Fig. 1). This structured mapping identifies which aspects of the complex misinformation system each analogy illuminates and which it obscures. The codebook for the mapping (see Supplementary Material S.1) was co-created with the entire author team spanning multiple disciplines, academia, public institutions, and the tech industry. We refined our framework by first mapping the core components of ‘fake news’ as a genre of misinformation mimicking journalistic news (see S.2). We then applied this framework to systematically evaluate the three most prominent analogies of misinformation: infodemic, information warfare, and information pollution (see S.3). Consolidated versions of all coded analogies were circulated to all authors and iteratively refined till consensus was reached (see S.4). Based on the final consensual codings, the mileage and baggage of each analogy were mapped onto our framework, visualising each analogies conceptual strenghts and gaps (Fig. 3). Infodemic: misinformation as a virus
The metaphor of the “infodemic” became popular during the COVID-19 pandemic through the World Health Organisation (WHO) 52 . The WHO used it to describe the parallel spread of reliable and misleading content during COVID-19. On the 12th of August 2025, Google Scholar identified approximately 41,900 hits via a search for the term “infodemic” in quotation marks (Google Scholar reports results always as approximations). The epidemic analogy is one of belief dynamics’ most frequently used analogies, with the core assumption that people infect each other with beliefs just like they infect each other with diseases 13 . Misinformation here acts as a virus 53 infecting people’s minds 8 , and calling for measures to build immunity (e.g., through cognitive inoculation 54 ). Despite its popularity, the analogy has been criticized for equating exposure to persuasion, and as it “conflates multiple forms of social behavior, oversimplifies a complex situation and helps constitute a phenomenon for which concrete evidence remains patchy” 55 .
Our mapping shows that the central “mileage” of the medical analogy is its description of misinformation transmission on the micro level. The analogy emphasizes contact patterns, viral load, and individual susceptibilities, naturally highlighting cognitive inoculation as an effective countermeasure. However, it fails to capture the other levels of our framework. For example, misinformation as a virus fails to capture the social embeddedness of misinformation on the meso-level I, as social relations are mostly reduced to mere nodes in a transmission network. The role of different social media environments on the meso-level II is ignored, as are macro-level contexts, such as different political systems (democratic vs. autocratic states). The analogy also overlooks the goal-directed nature of some creators and spreaders (e.g., political or financial motives for disinformation campaigns). Further, the complexity of misinforming content, such as different deviations from evidence, cannot be intuitively mapped. Finally, contrary to misinformation, viruses care little about ingroup dynamics, political supplies, or the larger media context in which they spread.
Information warfare: misinformation as a weapon
Understanding information as a weapon in intergroup conflicts is as old as war itself. According to “On the Art of War” from the 6th century, “all warfare is based on deception” 56 . While the modern usage of the term “information warfare” is broad and includes, for example, attacks on communication systems 57 , misinformation (or rather strategic disinformation) is a central element. The literature on “information warfare” is quite broad. On the 12th of August 2025, Google Scholar identified approximately 69,300 hits via a search using the term “information warfare” in quotation marks. Rooted in military thinking, “information warfare” focuses on (mis-)information to “take the lead over competitors and enemies” 58 . Such adversarial thinking also coins terms such as “ memetic warfare ” 59 , “ cognitive warfare ” 60 , or “ troll armies ” 61,62 . This has been criticized for contributing to the securitization of digital practices and expanding military rights to the broader information space 63 . Furthermore, the analogy centers on antagonists 64 , thus underestimating the pivotal role of citizens 65 and non-political motivations for the spread of misinformation.
Our mapping shows that the central “mileage” of understanding misinformation as a weapon is the analogies’ focus on the macro level, where states use campaigns within geopolitical power contests. The analogy illuminates states’ intentional, strategic, and hierarchical use of misinformation to accomplish their objectives in adversarial situations. However, essential elements of misinformation dynamics remain unaddressed. For example, individual differences of micro-level actors operating outside state command are not included. Also missing are feedback loops within the network, such as individual actions influencing social norms or political decision makers. Society is treated as a monolithic entity manipulated to advance state objectives rather than a complex system. Factuality is subordinate to strategic aims, being of tactical value only to increase the believability of the strategically launched content.
Information pollution: misinformation as a toxin
Information pollution describes the “contamination of information supply with incomplete, inconsistent, or irrelevant information” 66 in “quantities large enough to produce significantly adverse effects on human activities and social life” 67 . The term’s popularity rose after the US presidential election in 2016 7 . On the 22nd of May 2025, Google Scholar identified approximately 6280 hits via a search for “information pollution” in quotation marks. The analogy of “information pollution” with misinformation as a toxin was most comprehensively applied by Phillips and Milner 68 to describe a “network climate change” (p. 36) driven not only by misinformation and “active polluters” but also, involuntarily, by citizens and journalists. Environmental metaphors have been criticized for their dramatic undertone 69 , rewarding “speed and epistemic homogeneity in misinformation research, ultimately discouraging in-depth inquiry”. Most notably, they equate human-made digital information systems to the natural environment, yet humans have evolved to live in nature, while they have created the digital environment themselves 69 .
Our mapping shows that the central “mileage” of the ecological analogy on misinformation is its complexity-informed system view. It illustrates the roles of those spreading misinformation and how misinformation affects not only the initial waste site but also those living “downstream”. It illuminates that humans are embedded in a larger system formed by political decisions and environmental conditions. Thus, the analogy can map the different levels of the complex system through which misinformation travels quite well. It also allows for conceptualizing tipping points (e.g., authoritarian shifts). However, the analogy fails to account for recipients’ agency in ignoring and countering misinformation and obscures the central role of intergroup dynamics and social media platforms for misinformation flows.
Policy implications
Despite their limitations, all three analogies are frequently used to draw policy implications and direct research funding. For example, current European Union president Ursula von der Leyen called for a Democracy Shield 70 against foreign interferences through information warfare, and the United Nations Development Program aims to combat the “crisis of information pollution” 71 . Yet, adopting only a single analogy risks systematically overlooking certain aspects and, in the worst case, investing in countermeasures that increase the susceptibility to misinformation. Simplified media literacy training teaches skeptical reading but can also foster distrust of mediated communication, per se 11 . Inoculation can be abused to meta-inoculate recipients against debunkings of conspiracy theories 72 . Individual-level interventions fail to account for economic interests driving the dissemination of misinformation 73 and focusing on “information warfare” risks overlooking internal actors.
Our integrative framework’s practical value is to help guide a more robust, complexity-aware intervention portfolio. For example, psychological inoculation is a well-established micro-level intervention for strengthening individual resilience against misinformation 8 . However, the efficacy of such micro-level interventions is shaped by the health of the broader information ecosystem, for example, whether individuals are faced with an information environment where misinformation is systematically produced by political or institutional actors and amplified by platform architectures 10,74 that use the same techniques to “meta-inoculate” 72 individuals against, for example, fact-checks. As another example, demanding personalized registration for social media platforms to defend against information warfare 75 ignores misinformation from internal actors but also fails to acknowledge, for example, the spreading of misinformation as a form of populist protest 76 .
A comprehensive portfolio approach—similar to policy approaches proposed for other complex challenges, such as climate change 77 — reflecting the different levels in our framework, would aim to address systemic risks more holistically. To illustrate, combating climate change misinformation using a coordinated portfolio approach based on our framework could mean: strengthening individual resilience among recipients through cognitive inoculation 8 (micro), deploying community-based interventions that leverage social norms to encourage accuracy evaluations before sharing content 41 (meso I), optimizing algorithmic recommendations so that they serve peoples’ fundamental rights and civic values 78 instead of “clickbait” 79 (meso II), and ensuring trustworthy independent media systems 25,80 . The evaluation of the effectiveness of interventions tackling complex phenomena should thereby follow exemplars from climate change research, complementing traditional causal inference with iterative learning mechanisms to avoid systematically underestimating emergent dynamics 81 . Our framework could also help to reflect on potential side effects on other levels when specific interventions are planned (e.g., how does a ban on political advertisement on large-scale social media platforms affect individual citizens’ access to misinformation versus official political campaigning?), or reflect upon multilevel influences on misinformation beliefs versus misinformation dissemination activities.
Future research directions
A critical direction for future research is to model the meta-dynamics of the framework itself, where the system’s structure co-evolves with the events it contains. The COVID-19 pandemic provides a clear case study, where the crisis did more than introduce new information—it altered the underlying parameters of the information ecosystem through reducing institutional trust 82 , rising affective polarization 83 , and fostering identity-based reasoning, where alignment with one’s social group often overrode other considerations 84 . Understanding these higher-order feedback loops and system transformations is a crucial next step for complexity science in understanding misinformation dynamics. Understanding the complexity of misinformation thereby requires interdisciplinary cooperation. Often, authors from different disciplines come to widely different conclusions about the risks of misinformation 2,85 while focusing on single levels or aspects, ignoring misinformation’s complex dynamics.
Our framework also echoes prior calls for the urgent need to study misinformation effects beyond the global North 17 and in different political systems 80 . We also need a better understanding of misinformation dynamics within and between various social groups and identities, and within and between digital platforms 86 , their affordances, and economic and ideological interests. Further, reciprocal, non-linear effects are best understood over time, requiring adequate funding and reliable access to social media data. Finally, all aggregation bears the risk of confounding aggregate observations with individual experiences (so-called ecological fallacy 87 ), thus further qualitative research into individual perceptions in specific contexts is needed to provide deeper insights.
Conclusion
Addressing misinformation requires more than simple analogies. Terms like “infodemic,” “information warfare,” and “information pollution” help initiate discussions but often miss the full scope of the phenomenon. Understanding misinformation as a complex, multilevel system allows for more effective interventions and underscores the value of interdisciplinary collaboration.
Our framework provides a valuable starting point to reflect on individual behaviors and broader societal factors and develop risk-aware interventions accounting for the multiple levels on which misinformation dynamics unfold. It can help to illustrate perceptual gaps, ultimately supporting the development of practical and more comprehensive policies, inspiring future research, and building more resilient societies. The flexibility of the suggested abstract framework allows for a plurality of approaches while providing a basis for common comparison.
