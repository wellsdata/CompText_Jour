1. Introduction False information and fake news have become synonymous in recent years, referring to a wide range of content including but not limited to clickbait, satire, rumors, hoaxes, propaganda, disinformation, misinformation, and satire. The main difference between misinformation, disinformation, rumors, hoaxes, clickbait, propaganda, and satire is the intention behind each term. Misinformation is false information that is spread without the intention to deceive. Disinformation is also false information, but it is spread intentionally to mislead people. Rumors are unverified information that may or may not be true. Hoaxes are deliberate attempts to deceive people by spreading false information. Clickbait is sensationalized or exaggerated headlines or content designed to attract clicks. Propaganda is information that is spread by a government or organization to promote a particular viewpoint or agenda. Satire is a type of humor that uses irony or exaggeration to comment on current events or social issues. Understanding the differences between these terms is important in evaluating the accuracy and credibility of information (Ruffo et al., 2023). The term “fake news” can also refer to reports that are fabricated or written with the goal to mislead the public. According to sources (Meel and Vishwakarma, 2019; Bondielli and Marcelloni, 2019), fake news is similar to real news in form but not in goals or structure. The proliferation of social media platforms provides a fertile environment for the propagation of false information. Journalists are facing new challenges as readers shift their attention from print to online and social media. Widespread use of multimedia technologies, particularly manipulative or frequently unrelated and out-of-context appealing images, is employed to entice and confuse readers for the quick dissemination of falsified informational content.
In the studies (Kaufhold et al., 2020; Zahra et al., 2020; Li et al., 2022), the authors examined the value of social media posts for crisis management and sentiment analysis (Ahmed et al., 2023). The dark side of web platforms, which today dominate our lives, envelops human society as they are exploited to disseminate deceptive, fake news for extremely damaging purposes (Nadeem et al., 2023a; Nadeem et al., 2023b). Building a system to identify fake news presents two major difficulties. First, fake news is crafted to trick readers and mimic legitimate news outlets, making it difficult to tell the difference between fact and fiction. Second, the rate and volume at which false information is generated outweigh the possibility of thoroughly verifying the credibility of news by experts. This is due to the fact that the volume of misleading news outweighs the possibility (Nadeem et al., 2022). The studies (Jin et al., 2017; Qi et al., 2019) demonstrated that intentional and verifiable fake news articles typically include both textual and visual information. In order to achieve rapid distribution, publishers of fake news often rely on text and images that either invent or misrepresent facts. Socially charged topics and conflicts often inspire detailed written descriptions of the emotional impact and visual impact of accompanying images. There are many different types of media that could be used to disseminate false information. Since it is a multimedia compilation, identifying false information requires more than just looking at a single type of content. There is a varying degree to which each mode of information might contribute to the spread of rumors. However, fake news frequently employs the utilization of content like photographs and phrases with sentiment-driven, fast-spreading ideas. However, few studies have examined the impact of these channels on the news. Therefore, to identify fake news, it is important to incorporate multiple facets of the news.
At present, fake news detection algorithms either rely on unimodal data or combine many data sources (Ma et al., 2016; Ma et al., 2019). While some models do consider the similarities between different modalities and their potential for effective modeling of multimodal data (Giachanou et al., 2020a; Giachanou et al., 2020b), there is a need for an updated and improved approach to better understand the context and related similarities (Wang et al., 2018; Yang et al., 2018; Zhou et al., 2020). An in-depth examination of hoax-typical characteristics such as picture manipulation, and inconsistent photos define a fraudulent news text. Some fake news uses dramatic, humorous, or enticing visuals to grab readers’ attention at the expense of the actual news’s linguistic content. Initial publication (Ma et al., 2016) includes multimodal content from social networks that can be used with deep neural networks to spot bogus news. The study (Wang et al., 2018) proposed a multimodal feature-based adversarial neural network for end-to-end event detection of new forms of fake news. However, the majority of these attempts are focused on text rather than the effective modeling of visual material. Those visible characteristics are acquired by pre-trained convolutional neural networks like VGG19, which makes it difficult to demonstrate the intrinsic value lacking task-relevant information, and elements of a fake news image (Antol et al., 2015; Lin et al., 2009). Meanwhile, these techniques combined elements from many modalities while ignoring the overlap in new modalities. The SAFE (Zhou et al., 2020) approach considers how closely an image and its accompanying text match up. To finish the image-to-text conversion (Vinyals et al., 2017), the similarity comparison module employs a pre-trained model called image2sequence. The purpose of image2sequence is better alignment with the objective declaration of image content than it is with the emotional text. It lacks the emotive elements present in photos, which are crucial for detecting fake news.
Experiments using LSTM and hybrid LSTM-CNN architectures have been conducted by Ajao et al. (2018). However, they also noted that the hybrid model will likely perform better only with a more extensive data for training. Agarwalla et al. (2019) used Support Vector Machine (SVM), Logistic Regression (LR) and Naïve Bayes (NB) with Lidstone smoothing on headline and body of news text for fake news classification. Perhaps the vector characteristics are reliant on the relevant dataset and are not suitable for a standard model for detecting fake news. Vishwakarma et al. (2019) proposed scraping and validating online searches as a means of analyzing the trustworthiness of fake news. This technique is largely dependent on the whitelist created by human interactions for dependable links. Image forensics methods were used by Lago et al. (2019) to detect altered photographs, and a verification procedure was used to ensure that the images corresponded appropriately with the news article. Meel and Vishwakarma (2021) demonstrated that a hierarchical attention network for text and a combination of image tagging and forensic analysis for visual aspects might be used to detect fake news. The aim of the proposed Stylometric and Semantic similarity oriented multimodal fake news detection (SSM) is to overcome the shortcomings of aforementioned approaches for improving the performance of fake news detection. For effective fake news detection, the proposed SSM is made up of five individual modules: Textual Stylometric Feature Learning Module, Headline and news summary similarity measuring Module, Image Semantic and News Summary similarity measuring Module, Image Forgery Detection module, and Feature Fusion and Classification Module. The real news data can be measured directly using the similarity measurement module. In contrast to existing methods, our approach is better able to capture the similarity of diverse modal data in multimodal news data, making it more effective at detecting fake news in complicated circumstances by analyzing the semantic features of texts and images. This article’s key contributions are as follow: - The deep architecture of the Hyperbolic Hierarchical Attention Network (Hype-HAN) is used to identify stylometric and content-specific hidden patterns of words and sentences in the fake news dataset. The patterns were found by analyzing the text for both style and content.

- We have summarized the news body to highlight the most important features of text and compute the similarity between headline & news body content.

- By developing a branching network for extracting visual data, we are able to better capture the semantic aspects in the visual expression of fake news.

- The similarity of visual semantic features and news content summary is computed to determine the relatability of the image with text.

- With the error level analysis (ELA) method mentioned in the image forgery detection module, pre-trained EfficientNetB7 is used to detect the physical validity of fake news images.

2. Literature review There are various tasks connected to the detection of fake news, such as rumor detection (Cao et al., 2019; Imran et al., 2020; Ma et al., 2019) and spam detection (Kaghazgaran et al., 2018; Wang et al., 2017). The biggest challenge with detecting fake news is distinguishing between news stories using characteristics. To this end, we review the current work in the following three areas: fake news detection based on hand-crafted features; fake news detection based on single-modal data; and fake news detection based on multimodal data. These characteristics are obtainable via online discussion groups, social networks, and even some illustrative visuals.
2.1. Machine learning based methods To identify fake news, this technique relied heavily on human-created attributes. The similarity of dissemination structure, geographic location, user influence, and emotional polarity was extracted from data related to the events using feature engineering. These attributes were then used to train classifiers such as decision trees (DT), support vector machine (SVM), and others to distinguish between phony and authentic news stories (Castillo et al., 2011; Jin et al., 2016; Reis et al., 2019; Wu et al., 2015). The researchers (Castillo et al., 2011) have trained a DT algorithm to identify rumors using sentiment scores based on a variety of criteria, such as the number of URLs posted on Weibo and the length of time a user has been registered. To detect rumors, Wu et al. (2015) has employed a support vector machine classifier trained on features such as the location where the microblog was released, the client that issued the microblog, the emotional polarity of text symbols, etc. The new set of features proposed by Reis et al. (2019) was based on an evaluation of 141 textual features previously proposed for identifying fake news. However, as stated by other authors (Castillo et al., 2011; Mikolov et al., 2013; Popat et al., 2016), building the successful hand-crafted features necessitates knowledge of the highly relevant area and specific events. However, the technique relies on hand-crafted features that aren’t very robust, and the feature vectors that are gleaned from them aren’t either, because the method doesn’t have any background expertise in detecting fake news. Furthermore, identifying bogus news with custom-built features is a challenging task.
2.2. Deep learning based single-modal methods Numerous researchers have attempted to automate the process of constructing deep features using deep learning models in order to spot bogus news. The purpose of the study (Ma et al., 2016) was to investigate the feasibility of presenting tweets with deep neural networks by collecting temporal-linguistic data. In order to focus on different temporal and linguistic details, Chen et al. (2018) tweaked recurrent neural networks (RNNs) with attentional processes. Labeled data is essential for the development of deep learning models and gathering such data has long been the primary challenge in the study of detecting disingenuous content. The largest obstacle to rumor identification using deep learning models is the difficulty of data annotation. To find internet rumors, several researchers tried to avoid data labeling and instead applied the concept of unsupervised learning. Adding a multi-layer recurrent neural network (RNN) to the autoencoder’s front end, as suggested by Chen et al. (2018), greatly improved the model’s performance when testing it to spot rumors. The study (Raza and Ding, 2022) has introduced a transformer-based model for fake news detection that uses an encoder for learning and a decoder for prediction. While the unsupervised learning approach does not have to deal with the labeling issue, the model’s inherent instability does. Fake news can be detected more accurately using deep learning’s unimodal approach, but this approach disregards news as a compilation of multimedia data. Fake news is useless because it contains misleading content and images (Ma et al., 2016; Liu and Wu, 2018; Ma et al., 2018).
2.3. Deep learning based Multimodality methods Recent years have seen an increase in the number of researchers interested in deep learning approaches that make use of multimodal data (Imran et al., 2020; Truong and Lauw, 2019). The authors (Zhao et al., 2019) describe a multimodal approach to gauging the tone of social media posts that is driven by the consistency of images and texts. Toward the goal of creating dynamically interpretable recommendations, Liu et al. (2020) introduced a novel Attentive Recurrent Neural Network (Ante-RNN) that fuses text and images. The study (Kumar et al., 2020) introduces a hybrid deep learning model for accurate, real-time sentiment prediction across many channels. The current studies use pre-trained deep CNNs models, such as VGG19, to extract image features and blend the resulting visual performance with text information for data from various modalities in news (Jin et al., 2017; Wang et al., 2018; Yang et al., 2018; Khattar et al., 2019). To address the issue of identifying false information, Jin et al. (2017) incorporated multimodal content from social networks into their Deep Neural Network solution. Using multimodal characteristics, Wang et al. (2018) suggested an end-to-end event-based anti-neural network to detect new instances of fake news. A study (Khattar et al., 2019) proposed a unique strategy for detecting fake news based on learning shared representations for multimodal data. However, these sections focus primarily on information integration and pay little attention to the efficient modeling of visual content. They rely on visual cues that are so broad that they obscure important details in the fake news image and make it hard to identify the internal features that would improve the visual content’s effectiveness in the detection of fake news. Nonetheless, the aforementioned models, such as TextCNN or LSTM, are not able to effectively unearth the connection between text and context, which severely limits their capacity for detecting fake news in the textual component (Jin et al., 2017; Zhou et al., 2020). Another method for detecting fake news know as SAFE (Zhou et al., 2020), which compares text and images for similarities. But this model uses a pre-trained model for generating image descriptions and can’t figure out how similar two different kinds of data are, which limits the scene.
In their experiments, Ajao et al. (2018) found that adding a 1D CNN right after the LSTM model’s word embedding layer improved accuracy for predicting fake tweets. However, the authors emphasize the need of using a large enough data set while training a hybrid model. Python’s Natural Language Toolkit (NLTK) was used by Agarwalla et al. (2019) with models including LR, SVM, and NB with Lidstone Smoothing. Using only vector-based methods to pull out certain features and train the classifiers might not be the best way for generic fake news detection because these methods are fixed to the particular dataset. A fake news authentication method was presented by Vishwakarma et al. (2019), with the goal of evaluating the reliability of visual content regardless of platform. The model first detects entities in the text retrieved from photos, and then it crawls the web to find more information that fits the entities’ categorization criteria. It employs a whitelist of trustworthy websites that people have determined to be either fake news or real news. Consequently, the outcomes may be insufficient and highly dependent on whitelist. Furthermore, whitelisting also requires time and effort from people, who may make mistakes or have prejudices. Lago et al. (2019) experimented with textual and visual data to find fake news using both traditional and modern techniques. Splicebuster, an advanced image splicing detector, is used for image analysis with an ensemble of Random Forest (RF), LR, and CNN based classifiers for classification of fake news. But the disparities between the visual and textual modalities are ignored in the late fusion process. A multimodal framework based on an ensemble of textual and visual information was presented by Meel and Vishwakarma (2021). Word and sentence encoders, as well as an attention layer at the word and sentence levels, were employed to create the hierarchical attention network (HAN). Additionally, they created a visual summary and extracted the news article for text semantics in the title and body. Similarity between created a visual summary and text semantics was checked for fake news classification. This model takes full length of news articles in consideration, which may be excessively long or short. In the case of text-semantic features, this might result in overfitting or underfitting, as well as the loss of crucial characteristics for detecting fake news.
Unimodal and multimodal techniques were used by Segura-Bedmar and Alonso-Bartolome (2022) to classify disinformation. Using a CNN-based architecture, their multimodal approach combines textual and visual information. Images helped spread deceptive information, sarcasm, and fake connections. Davoudi et al. (2022) tracked down news items using metadata including origin, author, and URL domain. Pre-trained models, a statistical feature fusion network, a novel reasoning strategy, and variables from news articles were used as part of an ensemble model that also incorporated these features as statistical characteristics. Chen et al. (2022b) proposed a model for fusing information from several sources. The features extracted from user nodes include user information, propagation, and time series. The model created by Das et al. (2022) is comprehensive, considering a wide range of features observed in real life and in the media. To simulate the propagation tree’s and stance network’s temporal evolution pattern, a recurrent neural network (RNN) is used in the dynamic analysis. Wei et al. (2022) implemented of a two-stage network, firstly, two unimodal networks are trained to learn cross-modal correlation by contrastive learning, and then the network is fine-tuned to detect fake news. A similar strategy was devised by Singhal et al. (2022) to selectively extract important data from the modality that was dominant while eliminating extraneous information from the modalities that were weaker. Using pre-existing contextual representations, Koloski et al. (2022) showed that knowledge graph-based document representations can attain superior performance. The QA-AXDS presented by Chi and Liao (2022) is a rumor-detection and user-interaction system predicated on a quantitative argumentation model in the form of a dialogue tree. The model suggested by Raza and Ding (2022) is built on the transformer architecture, and it consists of two parts: an encoder component to derive representations from the fake news data, and a decoder subsystem to detect behavior based on past data. Jarrahi and Safari (2022) combined CNN with 3D input to detect bogus news on social media. They focused on how publishers’ features may help their readers.

We propose a novel SSM multimodal transcend the current fake news detection approaches, such as the need for hand-crafted features and reliance on broad features. Our model captures the semantic elements of texts and images, as well as the physical characteristics of the visual modalities, and it works collaboratively through five sub-networks to do so. When compared to current methods, our model has outperformed previous techniques at identifying fake news in complicated scenarios.
3. Methodology This section presents the architecture and processing of the components of SSM. In order to uncover the hidden patterns of fake news, a Hyperbolic Hierarchical Attention Network (CZhang and Gao, 2021) (Hype-HAN) is being trained with the textual data, which is the union of the title and body of the news. The news headline is compared to the summary of the news article. The similarity between the two reveals the correlation of a headline with a news content summary. Furthermore, image semantic features are extracted and compared with the summary to find the semantic similarity between visual and textual content. Image forensics is used to combat forged images (Meel and Vishwakarma, 2021). Finally, we combine the independent models as shown schematically in Fig. 1. Fig. 1 [Images not available. See PDF.]

3.1. Pre-processing and word embeddings During this phase, known as pre-processing, the data is cleaned by removing any redundant, superfluous, or irrelevant information. In order to prepare text data for analysis, the NLTK python library’s Stop Word Removal, Stemming and Lemmatization, Normalization, and Tokenization procedures are used. In the process of stop-word removal, insignificant words and symbols are eliminated, while stemming and lemmatization break down sentences into their component parts, normalization ensures that sentences conform to industry standards, and tokenization breaks down longer strings into more manageable chunks. Stemming is a simple heuristic procedure that removes affixes from words to make them more inflectional and easier to convert to their base forms. In some cases, the original word’s root form may be considered the definitive one. Noise in the form of abbreviations, misspellings, and out-of-vocabulary words is common in online content and social media data, making text normalization crucial. To get a vector representation of text tokens, we employ a pre-trained GloVe word embedding (Pennington et al., 2014). Using an unsupervised learning technique like GloVe, we can build word embeddings by summing up the global word-word co-occurrence matrix extracted from a corpus. When compared to other word vector formats, GloVe’s use of a co-occurrence matrix to capture global statistics and the meaning of words is advantageous. In order to ensure the integrity of the multimedia files, the “Beautiful Soup” Python module (Hajba and Hajba, 2018) is used to verify the links to the included images, and if any links are broken or images are missing, the associated multimedia files are scraped from the web. Each instance is finalized with its three parameters—headline/title, body/text, and image—completed through the pre-processing of text and multimedia data in isolation and their subsequent concatenation.
3.2. Textual stylometric feature learning The HAN is able to identify hidden stylistic and topical patterns of words and sentences in fake news data (Yang et al., 2016). Due to the necessity for large embedding sizes in complex networks and the difficulty of comprehending hierarchical links in Euclidean space, we confine the computing process by relocating it to hyperbolic space, therefore taking use of its numerous advantages. Since the hyperbolic coordinate itself is informative, we can use it to represent not only the overt relationship between words and sentences but also the more subtle connection between concepts among words.
The Hype-HAN uses a hierarchical attention network in hyperbolic space to learn a representation of the original content. Fig. 2 shows the graphical representation of Hype-HAN. Fig. 2 [Images not available. See PDF.]
Hyperbolic hierarchical attention network.

Assume that in the document under consideration, the input embedding of the \( {n}^{th} \) word in the \( {m}^{th} \) phrase is \( {e}_{nm} \). This sentence’s Euclidean hidden state of \( {e}_{nm} \) can be built from forward and backward reading. We describe the Euclidean-GRU layers as Eq. 1. 1 \[ {e}_{nm}^{\varepsilon }=\left[\overrightarrow{GRU}\left({e}_{nm}\right),\overleftrightarrow{GRU}\left({e}_{mn}\right)\right] \]CZhang and Gao (2021) have set a goal for themselves to learn the hyperbolic word centroid \( {T}_w^h \) together by reading through all of the training materials. When determining the relative importance of hyperbolic words based on their distance from one another, \( {T}_w^h \) can serve as a baseline for comparison. They consider yet another layer on top of the hidden state as Eq. 2 to learn \( {T}_w^h \). 2 \[ {e}_{mn}^{\varepsilon^{\prime }}=\tanh \left({W}_w{e}_{mn}^{\varepsilon }+{b}_w\right) \]
The following step activates \( {e}_{mn}^{\varepsilon^{\prime }} \) as \( {e}_{mn\prime}^{h^{\prime }} \). The attention weights for individual words are calculated by Eq. 3. 3 \[ {\partial}_{mn}=\exp \left(-{\delta}_w{d}_h\left({T}_w^h,{e}_{mn}^h\right)-{T}_w\right) \]
Once the hyperbolic attention weights have been recorded, Einstein midpoint is used to aggregate the semantic meaning of words that appear in the same sentences as Eq. 4. 4 \[ {\displaystyle \begin{array}{cc}\hfill & {s}_m^{\mathcal{K}w}=\sum \limits_n\left[\frac{\partial_{mn}\gamma \left({e}_{mn}^{\mathcal{K}}\right)}{\sum \limits_l{\partial}_{mn}\gamma \left({e}_{mn}^{\mathcal{K}}\right)}\right]\hfill \\ {}\hfill & \gamma \left({e}_{mn}^{\mathcal{K}}\right)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-{\left\Vert {e}_{mn}^{\mathcal{K}}\right\Vert}^2}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-\frac{\sin {e}^2\left({r}_{mn}\right)}{{\mathrm{cose}}^2\left({r}_{mn}\right)}}},\hfill \\ {}\hfill & \hfill \end{array}} \]\( \gamma \left({e}_{mn}^{\mathcal{K}}\right) \) is known as the Lorentz factor, and the learned representation for the \( {m}^{th} \) sentence is written as \( {s}_m^{\mathcal{K}w} \). Sentence-level representations are generated by employing Mobius-GRU units to encode each sentence in the news content and then aggregating those sentences using Einstein’s midpoint. This process is analogous to the encoding done by the word-level encoder.
Following the lead of the research (CZhang and Gao, 2021), we propose a hyperbolic-space hierarchical attention network for acquiring stylometric representations of the text of news articles. We are aware that not every sentence in a post’s source may contain useful information. We employ a hierarchical attention-based network to determine the relevance of distinct phrases. Assume that in the candidate thread, the input embedding of the \( {n}^{th} \) word in the \( {m}^{th} \) phrase is \( {e}_{mn} \). To learn \( {s}_m^{\mathcal{K}w} \), the representation of the \( {m}^{th} \) sentence, we employ a hyperbolic word-level encoder to learn more about the background of Hype-HAN. Similar to the word-level encoder, we now employ Mobius- Gated recurrent unit \( M- GRU\Big) \) units to encode each phrase in the original post. To learn the sentence representation \( {s}_m^{\mathfrak{p}} \) from the sentence vector \( {s}_m^{{\mathfrak{p}}_w} \) produced by the word-level encoder, we first need to capture the sentence-level context. To encode texts, we employ a \( M- GRU \) encoder based on the Poincaré ball model. The results from the Eq. 5. 5 \[ M- GRU\mathrm{as}{s}_m^{\mathfrak{p}}=\left[{\overrightarrow{GRU}}_{mob}\left({s}_m^{{\mathfrak{p}}_w}\right),{\overleftarrow{GRU}}_{mob}\left({s}_m^{{\mathfrak{p}}_w}\right)\right] \]
As depicted in Fig. 2, \( {S}_m^p \) is the final context-aware representation of the \( {m}^{th} \) phrase in the source post in the hyperbolic space (Poincaré ball model), i.e., \( {S}_m^{\mathfrak{p}}\epsilon {\mathbb{H}}_c^{2{d}_s} \), where \( {d}_s \) is the input embedding dimension for the terms \( {e}_{mn} \) in the source post’s sentences. The average length of a sentence has been fixed by padding or shortening to 32 words. An average of 40 sentences per article. The validation set is used for hyperparameter adjustment. Following processing by Bidirectional GRU, a 100-dimensional word/sentence annotation is obtained from a GloVe word embedding and a 50-dimensional GRU annotation. With a dropout regularization probability of 0.5, the activation function of ReLu, loss calculated by binary cross-entropy, and optimizer Adam, we are training a network with a batch size of 32 epochs. In the final step, we apply SoftMax to obtain the probability for authentic/fake binary classification.
3.3. Headline and news summary measure A news content summary generator and a similarity measure are included in this sub-module of the framework. The Algorithm 1 represents the flow of operations for the sub-module.
3.3.1. News content summary Social media posts with eye-catching headlines and photos are designed to attract more readers to the website, however in the case of fake news, the article’s actual material has no resemblance to its catchy headline (Meel and Vishwakarma, 2019). Clickbait is another name for this form of plagiarism (Bondielli and Marcelloni, 2019). The goal of this section is to provide a summary of the input news story that highlights the most crucial details in light of the title. For this goal, we have employed the well-known and powerful TextRank extractive summarization algorithm (Mihalcea and Tarau, 2004) because of its high performance, fast execution time, and easy availability for implementation. The input text is represented in the form of a graph, where vertices in the algorithms are the sentences to be scored, and edges are the relationships between them. This type of connection is based on the degree to which two phrases are connected in terms of their common content. Each edge in the graph is then assigned a weight that represents the significance of the relationship between the phrase pairs/vertices that it connects. After the graph is constructed, each sentence in the text is given a score using a weighted graph-based rating. After that, we invert the sentence scores and arrange the sentences in descending order. At last, the five highest-scoring sentences are chosen for inclusion in the summary.
3.3.2. Text similarity measure Tokens from the headlines are now compared against those from the news article’s summary. Similarity between the article’s context and subject impacts its credibility (Meel and Vishwakarma, 2021). Words are compared to one another to determine their level of semantic similarity using the cosine similarity measure. If the token count is more than or equal to twelve percent of the total number of tokens in the news text, the news is considered legitimate. If the two do not align, there is a red warning indicating that the news is fraudulent. We calculate the degree of cosine similarity between two vectors in n-dimensional space by dividing the dot product of two vectors by the product of their magnitudes. The cosine similarity between sentences, X and Y can be calculated as Eq. 7. 7 \[ \mathrm{Cosine}\kern0.35em \mathrm{Similarity}= CS=\frac{\sum \limits_{i=1}^N{X}_i{Y}_i}{\sqrt{\sum \limits_{i=1}^N{X}_i^2}\sqrt{\sum \limits_{i=1}^N{Y}_i^2}} \]where \( {X}_i \) and \( {Y}_i \) are the constituents of vectors X and Y, respectively.

3.4.1. Text similarity measure According to the study (Qi et al., 2019), fabricated news images are more likely to evoke an emotional response than authentic news images, and this is reflected in the study of emotional components at both the semantic and physical levels. At the model-building phase, we combine the results of the forgery detection module with the low-level feature set obtained from the convolutional neural networks to conduct a physical-level examination of the images. We begin the process by encoding the input image with the EfficientNetB7 pre-training model to improve the visual portion’s semantic expression. We used a 1024-dimensional fully connected layer as an encoding stage before the classification layer of the pre-trained EfficientNetB7 model. A 1024-dimensional vector is used to store the image data. Eq. 8 demonstrates the method: 8 \[ {S}_v= EfficientNetB7(v) \]where v is the input image and \( {S}_v \) is the visual semantic features retrieved by EfficientNetB7.
The image’s semantic elements are then sent into an attention mechanism to draw focus to the areas where there is a high concentration of emotional expression (Vaswani et al., 2017). Once obtained, each characteristic will be assigned a weight to indicate its “importance” in the visual modal representation, based on Eq. 9. 9 \[ {\displaystyle \begin{array}{c}{k}^v={Z}^T\tanh \left({W}^v{S}^v+{b}^v\right)\hfill \\ {}{\alpha}^v=\frac{\exp \left({k}^v\right)}{\sum \limits_i\exp \left({k}^v\right)}\hfill \\ {}{\mathrm{sem}}^v=\sum \limits_i{\alpha}_i{S}^v\hfill \end{array}} \]where \( {W}^v \) is the weight matrix, \( {b}^v \) the bias term, \( {Z}^T \) the transposed weight vector, and k is the scoring function that assigns a weight to each eigenvector. The hierarchy of the input picture is then computed as the weighted sum of distinct eigenvectors, it is performed by using the swish activation function to acquire the normalized weight of the \( {i}^{th} \) eigenvector \( {k}^v \). Vectors are initialized at random and then learned concurrently. As this stage, we have a high-quality semantic representation of the original image. Specifically, we used bidirectional GRU (BiGRU) to construct the image-to-sequence module in order to improve the picture’s semantic expression (Lang, 1979). Aligning image features with text features is a common task for the image-to-sequence module, Consequently, it is widely used to create image descriptions.
In this work, we introduce the image-to-sequence module. We pass the image’s feature expression to BiGRU to extract the visual modality’s semantic vector. This phase is analogous to the embedding layer in text analysis, and it takes the study of the image’s semantic features to the level of the semantic sequence. Eq. 10 depicts the proposed method in its mathematical form, furthermore, utilizing the image’s semantics information is superior to using the image’s features directly (Ruffo et al., 2023). 10 \[ {f}^v= BiGRU\left({sem}^v\right) \]where, \( {sem}^v \) denotes the EfficientNetB7 retrieved advanced semantic representation, and \( {f}^v \) shows the visual semantic progression extracted using BiGRU.
3.4.2. Image similarity measure Comparing the textual information of an article with its accompanying visual data also is technique for detecting fake news (Lago et al., 2019). We have built a mechanism that measures the degree of similarity between texts and photos for fake news detection. Using the image feature learning module and summary generating module, we were able to get a vector representation of a summary and photos in the previous module. We utilize a fully connected layer as the final layer of each sub-network and require the two sub-networks to share the weights of the final layer to ensure that they learn the same representation space of image and text patterns (Zhen et al., 2019). By combining the features of both the picture and the text, we are able to derive their shared semantic representation, \( {s}^{text},{s}^{image} \). The goal is to create an image representation for a group of images and texts that is visually as close as feasible to the original represented in Eq. 11. 11 \[ s=\frac{s^{\mathrm{text}}\cdotp {s}^{\mathrm{image}}}{\left\Vert {s}^{\mathrm{text}}\right\Vert \times \left\Vert {s}^{\mathrm{image}}\right\Vert } \]where \( {s}^{text} \) and \( {s}^{image} \) represent the semantic sequence of the image and the feature sequence of the text, respectively. In this case, the value of s can take on any value between −1 and 1, and the higher the value, the greater the resemblance between the text and the image. We use the sigmoid activation function here to map the similarity between [0,1]. This allows us to map the similarity between 0 and 1, which was our goal as eq Eq. 12. 12 \[ {f}^s= sigmoid(s) \]
The news items that are created by a mismatch between the textual and visual information are considerably more susceptible to being fabricated than news articles that are created by matching images and text when seen from the standpoint of pure similarity (Zhao et al., 2019). Following this, we are able to create a loss function that is based on cross-entropy in the following manner as Eq. 13. 13 \[ {\displaystyle \begin{array}{c}{\mathcal{L}}_s\left({\theta}_{\mathrm{text}},{\theta}_{\mathrm{image}}\right)=-{\mathbbm{E}}_{\left(a,y\right)\sim \left(A,Y\right)}\left(y\log \left(1-{f}^s\right)+\left(1-y\right)\log {f}^s\right)\hfill \\ {}\left({\hat{\theta}}_{\mathrm{text}},{\hat{\theta}}_{\mathrm{image}}\right)={\arg}_{\theta_t,{\theta}_v}^{\mathrm{min}}{\mathcal{L}}_s\left({\theta}_{\mathrm{text}},{\theta}_{\mathrm{image}}\right)\hfill \end{array}} \]
We set up a fully connected layer with shared parameters of dimension 256 for image and text. In the joint loss function, \( \alpha \) measures the difference in image semantics, and \( \beta \) measures the difference in text summary. The values for \( \alpha \) and \( \beta \) are set to 0.7 and 0.3, respectively.

Considering the importance of forgery detection, once the image has been altered, the created event may be convincingly narrated in text, making it incredibly difficult to identify the forgery even with advanced deep learning techniques (Vinyals et al., 2017). In order to improve the accuracy with which altered (photoshopped) images can be identified, researchers have turned to the image forensic technique of ELA (Meel and Vishwakarma, 2021). The intended architecture’s exceptional performance and efficacy are bolstered by the fact that it is a comprehensive solution to the problem of disinformation in all its manifestations on the internet. The SSM framework incorporates a variety of forgery forms, such as photographs taken out of context, doctored photos, and a phony writing style, into a unified multimodal binary classification system.

The experimental findings reveal that the single-modality like Hybrid CNN-RNN (Ajao et al., 2018), and LR, SVM, NB with Lidstone Smoothing (Agarwalla et al., 2019) approach to recognizing fake news is significantly less effective than the multimodal approach. Although visual modalities are effective in detecting fake news, a single visual modal approach performs less well than a multimodal one. This indicates the effectiveness of the multimodal feature integration strategy in spotting fake news. Since Ensemble of HAN, NVI, ELA, Semantic matching (Meel and Vishwakarma, 2021) outperforms TF-IDF, RF, LR, CNN, cosine similarity, Jaccard’s similarity (Lago et al., 2019) in multimodal models, this finding suggests that HAN and Hype-HAN applications can further enhance model accuracy. While MVAE (Khattar et al., 2019) accuracy is higher than that of competing approaches, it is still inferior to that of ensemble of HAN. Whereas MVNN (Qi et al., 2019) considers the fusion of several visual elements, the method (Lago et al., 2019) based on late fusion does not take into account the differences between the visual and textual modalities. A Multimodal CNN (Segura-Bedmar and Alonso-Bartolome, 2022) implements a simple and less complicated implementation, but no similarity or picture forgery detection algorithms are used. DSS (Davoudi et al., 2022) does not incorporate multimodal image features. FR-Detect (Jarrahi and Safari, 2022) implemented a sentence-level convolutional neural network (SLCNN) with a input size limitation, it will be more suitable for social media pots and news as suggested by the author.
In this study, our proposed SMM is compared to state-of-the-art approaches, it demonstrates a superior detection performance on all three datasets. As the number of classifiers in the model grows, its accuracy improves; it makes more effective use of both textual and visual information; and its final classification is optimized since the framework takes the best parts of several approaches to detection. It is evident that our method excels when applied to larger data sets. The training of the approach proposed in this article can be assisted since larger data sets have a greater diversity of data.

The intended architecture is comprehensive, it can deal with the many different types of fake text and visual information that are common on the internet, which helps explain why it performs so well and achieves such impressive results. The proposed SSM is evaluated on three mentioned datasets, and it outperformed other baseline models and state-of-the-art models in comparison. The proposed framework incorporates a variety of forgery forms, such as photographs taken out of context, doctored photos, and a phony writing style.
5. Conclusions In this study, a novel Stylometric and Semantic similarity-based multimodal fake news detection (SSM) is suggested to solve the deficiencies and limitations of existing systems. In order to discover the hidden patterns of fake news, a Hyperbolic Hierarchical Attention Network is initially trained with textual data, which consists of the union of the news’ title and body. The second comparison involves the title and the article’s summary. The resemblance between the two demonstrates the relationship between a news headline and a synopsis of its substance. Thirdly, picture semantic attributes are extracted and compared to the summary in order to determine the semantic similarity between visual and written material. Image forensics is employed to counteract falsified pictures, as a fourth point. Finally, we merge the diverse representations of separate models for classification tasks. SSM is examined on three public datasets of fake news, and the results reveal that SSM outperforms the baseline and state-of-the-art techniques for detecting fake news using multimodal data. Furthermore, it is demonstrated that SSM is superior to rival systems when it comes to detecting fake news in complex settings. We plan to continue improving the method by fusing features from multiple sources to create more relevant multimodal characteristics. This approach will also be used for additional multimodal data-based complicated problem scenarios.
