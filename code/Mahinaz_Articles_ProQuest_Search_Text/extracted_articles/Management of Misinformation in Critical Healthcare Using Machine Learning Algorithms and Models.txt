Headnote The volume of activities and information disseminated daily on the Internet through different social media platforms and channels is increasing geometrically, and we can simply assert that we are approaching an era of connectedness. However, with affordable mobile devices and more Internet penetration, the spread of fake news and misinformation is also emerging as a global social and economic menace. This paper reviews the trends of misinformation in the health sector and the various technological solutions, especially intelligent algorithms, that have been developed to contain this social evil in recent times. The paper adopts the systematic review approach, and the surveyed papers are drawn from ScienceDirect and Scopus databases. The findings reveal that efforts are on to contain the spate of misinformation in the healthcare sector, and that machine learning algorithms are the popular tools currently used by researchers in creating solutions that could deter the spread of fake news and misinformation in the healthcare sector globally.
Introduction
Global economic growth and stability are predicated on a strong, vibrant and healthy society, as it is evident that it takes healthy people to build a strong and economically viable nation irrespective of race and geographic location. Health and wellness are some of the important factors that guarantee sustainable socioeconomic, cultural harmony and coexistence in a country, and a major catalyst for societal and individual development. According to Raji (2018), health in the context of the World Health Organization (WHO) is not just the nonexistence of sickness, diseases and infirmities, but a total state of wholeness that entails physical, mental and social wellbeing of a person or society. The health status of an individual or a group may be ascertained by the existence of a well-articulated health information system (Nwankwo, 2017; Umezuruike et al., 2017a and 2017b; Nwankwo and Umezuruike, 2018; Umezuruike et al., 2019 and 2020; and Victor-Ikoh et al., 2022). Consequently, an efficient and effective healthcare delivery system thrives on efficient information infrastructure.
Information, as one of the critical resources for human existence, provides facts and insights derivable from conscious and learned experiences that facilitate proper and knowledgeable decision-making process (Silva and Lima, 2017; and Chen etai., 2022). Itis an essential component of our daily lives. Irrespective of the domain, lack of relevant and timely information may result in great and unquantifiable losses to individuals, government and organizations. Devoid of accurate information, organizations and businesses struggle to make accurate predictions about future changes and trends in their industries and markets. In the healthcare sector, diseases cannot be cured if the health industry lacks relevant information. Information is a lifesaving tool, especially in the healthcare sector, as it is necessary for the doctors, nurses and healthcare administrators, who have access to patients' health record and data, to use it in making critical care decisions that can make or break their patients' lives. Transferring health data to the hands of the right people as quickly as possible is dependent on health information systems that seamlessly and intelligently integrate healthcare and information technology. A health information system allows healthcare organizations to collect, store, manage, analyze, and optimize patient treatment histories and other critical data, as the application of health information technology improves the quality and effectiveness of healthcare, while also promoting individual and public health and increasing diagnostic accuracy (Sarkar, 2022). The essentials of health information management center on curating the medical or health records of an individual or a group, a task that is becoming increasingly important in the healthcare system as data influx increases due to information digitization (Holland, 2018). The fact remains that accurate information supports more informed decision-making. In the healthcare sector, non-availability of accurate information is deadly, as it adversely impacts the entire healthcare industry value chain, with resultant loss of lives and critical healthcare resources. Misinformation in the healthcare sector adds to the difficulty in managing different healthcare challenges that may arise, as recorded during the Covid-19 pandemic (Caceres et al., 2022). Misinformation, especially in the healthcare sector, can cause rumors, stigma and discrimination and false theories, and shape beliefs and attitudes, ultimately leading to confusion that hinders individual and public health advancement during crises (Islam et al., 2020). Some examples include conspiracy theories about Bill Gates and discussions about various non-approved Covid-19 drugs. Furthermore, health misinformation influences people's decisions, resulting in poor physical and mental health outcomes as well as viral spread (Bunker, 2020; and Tasnim et al., 2020).
The influx of and rise in information on the Internet and other media has had a farreaching effect on information dissemination as it serves as a medium for connecting people and organizations seamlessly, irrespective of their geographical location and boundaries. This has also led to the spread of false and unverified information that has adversely impacted businesses, organizations, livelihood and health (Nwankwo and Ukhurebor, 2019; and Ваша, 2022). For instance, in 2021 alone, there were alleged deaths of over 50,000 Covid-19 patients on grounds of misinformation arising from vaccine management, routine care, and outpatient care (Isasi et al., 2021; and NDTV, 2021). The availability of unverified and misleading information in public domain has made the information management landscape more vulnerable with malevolent users brandishing false, misleading and fake reportage aimed at deceiving the public and causing more harm (Khan and Idris, 2019; Kim and Dennis, 2019; Talwar et al., 2019; Samuli et al., 2020; Zarocostas, 2020; and Yasmim et al., 2021). While the evolution of computer technologies and the Internet has been applauded for democratizing public health information and opening up previously unexplored avenues for health information technology and information sharing (Raazia and Madiha, 2021), it has also brought with it a bigger problem of misinformation and disinformation. The problem of health misinformation is painful to ignore, as it has socioeconomic (Salman and Maged, 2021) and political consequences (Christoph et al., 2020). The level of misinformation, especially during healthcare emergencies such as the ebola outbreak in West Africa and the Covid-19 pandemic, is evident to the fact that healthcare misinformation management is critical to human existence in the Information Age (Caceres et al., 2022). Machine learning (ML) has demonstrated immense usefulness in several socioeconomic sectors in societies (Acheme et al., 2020; Nwankwo et al., 2020; Chinedu et al., 2021; Nwankwo and Ukhurebor, 2021; Nwankwo et al., 2021; Adetunji et al., 2022; Nwankwo et al., 2022a, 2022b and 2022c; Olayinka et al., 2022; and Osikemekha et al., 2022). In this era of information overload, ML may emerge as an efficient and effective tool for containing misinformation and fake news (Nwankwo and Ukhurebor, 2020). The aim of this paper is to review the management of misinformation in critical healthcare using advanced technologies such as ML algorithms.
Objective
The main objectives of the study are to:
* Examine the different forms of misinformation in the healthcare sector, especially during epidemics and pandemics;
* Identify the threats of misinformation in the healthcare delivery sector;
* Identify the various measures for mitigating health misinformation;
* Examine the effect of misinformation on the healthcare delivery space as well as the collective impact in a developing economy; and
* S urvey the various ML models employed in containing the effects of misinformation.
To achieve the above objectives, the following research questions were formulated:
* Does misinformation exist in practical terms in the healthcare delivery space and if it does exist, what are its distinct forms?
* Does misinformation in the healthcare delivery space pose any meaningful threat to the society?
* Is there a possible measure to curb misinformation in healthcare information management?
* To what extent does misinformation in the healthcare sector impact the socioeconomic values of the society?
* To what extent are ML algorithms relevant in managing misinformation in the healthcare delivery space?
Data and Methodology
The systematic review approach has been used in this study. We used secondary data derived from papers published in reputable journals. The published papers were drawn from two popular repositories, viz., ScienceDirect and Scopus databases. We employed desk search on the chosen databases to access the publications based on the following keywords: misinformation during the pandemic, Covid-19 deaths from misinformation, managing misinformation in the healthcare sector, effects of misinformation in healthcare delivery, and methods of managing misinformation during the pandemic. The population size comprises 2,000 published papers from 2018 to 2022, including review papers, full research papers, short papers, and technical reports. We employed judgmental sampling to select the most relevant papers based on preset criteria. The preset criteria are: currency, context, applicability, and socioeconomic value. A total of 30 papers that met all the criteria were selected and used for further analysis.
Nature of Healthcare Misinformation
According to the office of the United States Surgeon General, "health misinformation is information that is false, inaccurate, or misleading according to the best available evidence at the time within the healthcare delivery space" (United States Government, 2021). There is no one-size-fits-all way of classifying health misinformation. The Office of the Surgeon General has classified health misinformation into the following:
a. Memes;
b. Websites with false health content;
c. Incoherent quotations;
d. Cherry-picked statistics;
e. Misleading diagrams and images;
f. Recycled old images; and
g. Photoshopped or deepfake videos.
Brennen et al. (2020) classified health misinformation as follows: misinformation, disinformation, malinformation, and midinformation. Regardless of the system of classification or the context for which the classification is made, the common denominator is the medium. In recent times, health misinformation may be propagated through several media channels, including the Internet, print media, broadcast media, social groups, etc. Allcott et al. (2019) noted that false content has risen steadily on the social media like Facebook and Twitter from 2016, though there has been a decline on Facebook in recent times as opposed to Twitter and other channels. Internet users have increasingly used social media to seek and share health information over the last two decades. These social platforms have been witnessing increased participation of health information consumers, regardless of gender or age (Chouet al., 2018; and Suarez-Lledo and JAlvarez-Galvez, 2021). This medium is also being used by health professionals and organizations to disseminate health-related knowledge on healthy habits and medical information for disease prevention, as it represents an unprecedented opportunity to increase health literacy, self-efficacy, and treatment adherence among populations. These public tools, however, have created unprecedented social and health risks (Vosoughi et al., 2018). Despite the fact that social media platforms such as Facebook, WhatsApp, Twitter, Instagram, etc. have shown to be helpful for health promotion, recent research suggests that false or misleading health information may spread more readily through social media than accurate scientific knowledge (Suarez-Lledo and JAlvarez-Galvez, 2021). Understanding how health misinformation spreads and how it may influence choices and health behaviors is therefore important (Pagoto et al., 2019). Health misinformation is readily becoming a norm in modern societies, but due to the dynamics of the social media ecosystem and the breadth of healthcare domain, it is almost impossible to define health misinformation (Wang et al., 2019). According to Chou et al. (2018), it is a health-related claim based on anecdotal, false, or misleading evidence because there is a lack of current scientific knowledge. This broad definition takes into account both false information that are not intentionally created to harm, misinform, and true information that is purposefully spread to harm a specific person, social group, institution, or country, which can be termed as disinformation and malinformation. The WHO defined infomedic as "an abundance of information, some of which is accurate and some of which is not, making it very challenging for people to locate dependable sources and sound advice when they require it" (WHO, 2020).
Indeed, the spread of health misinformation through the social media space has recently emerged as a major public health concern (Suarez-Lledo and JAlvarez-Galvez, 2021). The current demand to regulate the quality and public availability of online information is based on the lack of control over health information on social media. While there is comprehensive agreement among health professionals and policymakers today on the need to control health misinformation, there is still little evidence about the effects that the dissemination of false or misleading health messages through social media could have on public health in the near future. Although recent studies have explored novel ways to combat health misinformation online, more research is needed to characterize and capture this complex social phenomenon (Bode and Vraga, 2018; and Broniatowski et al., 2018).
Healthcare Misinformation During the Pandemic
Controlling the spread of Covid-19 proved to be difficult for nations, as the confusion and false information that accompanied it worsened the health outcomes and disease burden (Tasnim et al., 2020). False information influenced people's decisions to wash their hands more often, practice and maintain social withdrawal, wear a mask, and start vaccinations (Caceres et al., 2022). Several myths have also spread, such as vaccines negatively affecting female fertility and vaccines altering recipients' genetic makeup (Adams, 2021). False information also misled people into seeking cure through consumption of alcohol, cow excrements, cow urine, colloidal silver, teas, and essential oils for Covid-19 infection, with no strong scientifically proven evidence to support the efficacy of their claims (Sahoo et al., 2020).
Challenges of Healthcare Misinformation
The challenges of misinformation in the healthcare sector could lead to several complications and misconceptions between the healthcare providers and the public, leading to distrust in the system. In Nigeria, the rise in number of mobile doctors and herbal medicine practitioners on roadsides and mass transit buses have also aggravated the already bad situation with unsubstantiated and unverifiable claims and counterclaims on the efficacy of their medicine and treatment, leading to critical healthcare emergencies and subsequent loss of lives. According to Borges do Nascimento et al. (2022), misinformation within the health system could lead to the following:
* Misallocation of resources and increasing stress among medical providers;
* Decreased access to healthcare;
* Increased vaccine hesitancy and conspiracy beliefs;
* Increased illegal promotion of sale of controlled substances; and
* Delayed delivery of high-quality care and proper treatment to patients, which could further have a negative effect on public healthcare systems.
Information Technology in Healthcare
The evolution of computer technologies has impacted every sphere of human life, including educational, retail, legal, engineering, research and development, governance and healthcare sectors. The healthcare sector has been one of the key beneficiaries of the advancement in computers and information technology, as healthcare institutions, irrespective of their size and nomenclature, implemented new technologies to respond to a changing operational and regulatory environment to improve the overall quality of healthcare delivery (VanOrden, 2019).
The transformation in the healthcare sector can be attributed to the application of technology. Health information technology has positively influenced patient care. Use of technology to store, retrieve, share, and use health information and data has improved healthcare delivery to a great extent, as patients' information can be moved via computer networks for timely treatment and referral by physicians remotely (Figueroa et al., 2019). Alotaibi and Federico (2017) asserted that health information technology can help in the development of robust national healthcare systems that use data-enabled infrastructure on a continuous basis to support policy and planning, public health, and care personalization.
Though there are benefits in the application of information technology in the healthcare sector with respect to healthcare information management, the challenge of misinformation is of great concern as it portends grave consequences to public health and safety. According to Davis and Cole (2022), one of the most pressing challenges of our time is medical misinformation, which they described as a monster in the healthcare sector and a dangerous scourge with deadly consequences.
Managing Healthcare Misinformation
The spread of misinformation has the potential to harm both individuals and society. When compared to traditional media in the past, there has been an increase in the volume of fake news and misinformation with simplicity in the creation of social media posts (Bojjireddy et al., 2021). Controlling the spread of health misinformation on social media has long been a public concern, as such misinformation can have negative consequences for public health. Sanaullah et al. (2022) argued that during the pandemic, the spread of misinformation on social media and other platforms can be extremely damaging to the physical and mental wellbeing of people, especially in the healthcare sector. The following traditional approaches have been used to reduce or counter the rising spate of misinformation:
* Encouraging reduction in social media usage (Allcott et al., 2020);
* Providing Internet and social media users with reminders as to the value of accurate information (Pennycook et al., 2020); and
* Increased user exposure to real and accurate health information (Breza et al., 2021).
In addition to the traditional methods, the management and prevention of misinformation in critical healthcare sector could be achieved with very low error margin and high level of accuracy using ML algorithms. According to Kent (2021), ML algorithm could assist public health officials in identifying Covid-19-related conspiracy theories on social media, potentially reducing misinformation spread online.
Impact of Healthcare Misinformation During the Pandemic
Misinformation has a far-reaching effect on every sphere of human endeavor. Misinformation on social media, especially during a pandemic, can have adverse global socioeconomic consequences, giving rise to economic recession and social unrest in some quarters, and increase in cybercrime activities. Greenspan and Loftus (2020) opined that misinformation also impacts individuals' mental health, as it can impair recall of previous experiences or similar but accurate information heard in the past, a phenomenon known as misinformation effect. The spread of false information, use of "bubble filters", and exaggeration of facts caused a global wave of stress, anxiety, confusion, and depression. During the pandemic, social media sites increased anxiety and panic among people in Iraqi Kurdistan, according to a study by Ahmad and Murad (2020). During the first few months of the pandemic, misinformation about impending lockdowns spread online, causing panic buying and a shortage of critical supplies (Ferreira-Caceres et al., 2022).
Several conspiracy theories were circulating in China, Iran, Russia, the UK and the US during the Covid-19 outbreak, according to Islam et al. (2020), and some of them were spread globally. One of the theories suggested that Covid-19 was a bioweapon created by international agencies. It also claimed that multiple countries created and spread the lethal coronavirus in China as part of an economic and psychological war against the country (Breland, 2020). On the other hand, some claimed that the virus was created in a laboratory as part of China's biowarfare program and that a Chinese scientist engineered it as a weapon, and this increased the level of global insecurity during the pandemic (Gregory, 2020).
Relevance of ML Algorithm in Managing Misinformation in Healthcare Delivery ML has advantages in handling large amounts of complex healthcare information (Ngiam and Khor, 2019). Advantages of ML models include flexibility and scalability compared with traditional biostatistical methods, ability to analyze diverse data types (e.g., demographic data, laboratory findings, imaging data, and doctors' free-text notes) and incorporate them into predictions for disease risk, diagnosis, prognosis, and appropriate treatments. Another advantage of using ML is that it is automatic in nature (Mahesh, 2020).

Convergence of Identified ML Workflows for Management of Misinformation
Managing misinformation in critical healthcare using ML models is somewhat complex and requires a thoughtful and systematic approach. Misinformation, especially in critical healthcare contexts, can have severe consequences on public health, patient outcomes, and the overall healthcare system. Utilizing ML models to address this issue involves a combination of data analysis, model development, and deployment strategies. The common focal point of the surveyed papers is the adoption of the following workflows.
Data Collection and Analysis
Data Sources: Gather diverse and representative data sources, including medical literature, clinical trial data, authoritative medical websites, social media platforms, and online forums, where healthcare information is discussed.
Pre-Processing: Cleaning and pre-processing of the healthcare dataset to remove noise, irrelevant information, and duplicate content. Structured and unstructured data are processed to extract meaningful features.
Labeling: Annotation of the data to create a labeled dataset that differentiates between accurate healthcare information and misinformation.
Feature Extraction: Extract relevant features from the data, such as medical terms, context, sentiment, and user credibility metrics.
Model Development
Model Selection: Some of the authors used appropriate ML algorithms for the task, such as NLP models like transformers (e.g., BERT, GPT) due to their strong performance on textrelated tasks.
Training: Training of the selected model using the labeled dataset. Fine-tuning on a healthcare-specific domain may be necessary to improve performance.
Validation and Testing: Assessing the model's performance using metrics like accuracy, precision, recall, and Fl-score; utilizing techniques like cross-validation to ensure robustness.
Misinformation Detection
Classification: Implementing a binary or multi-class classification approach to distinguish between accurate healthcare information and misinformation.
NLP Techniques: Leverage NLP techniques like sentiment analysis, entity recognition, and semantic analysis to identify discrepancies, contradictions, and inconsistencies in the content.
Ensemble Methods: Combine the outputs of multiple models to improve detection accuracy. Ensemble techniques, such as bagging and boosting, can enhance the overall performance.
Model Interpretability and Transparency
Explainable Artificial Intelligence: Incorporate model interpretability techniques to provide insights into how the model reaches its decisions. This is essential in healthcare settings to build trust and ensure accountability.
Feature Importance: Identify which features contribute most to the model's predictions. This can help identify key indicators of misinformation.
Deployment and Integration
Real-Time Monitoring: Implement the model in a real-time monitoring system that scans various sources of healthcare information for potential misinformation.
Feedback Eoop: Design a feedback loop where user feedback and manual review contribute to continuous model improvement.
Human-in-the-Eoop: Involve healthcare experts in the decision-making process, as they can provide domain-specific insights that the model might miss.
Misinformation has not only affected the patient's behavior in the healthcare system but has impeded the treatment process. The varieties of fake news, disinformation and malinformation available before and during the pandemic further aggravated the patients' care and individual wellbeing, making it difficult for critical healthcare delivery, in terms of vaccination and treatment, to reach the people. The vast volume of misinformation in the public domain, especially through the different social media platforms like Twitter, Facebook, Instagram, WhatsApp, etc., negated the activities of the healthcare providers in the frontline. The application of ML and DL algorithms for sifting and classifying false, fake and misleading information on the Internet has shown that misinformation can be curtailed on the social media space and in turn the healthcare delivery sector. Several ML models deployed showed high accuracy in the classification and detection of misinformation and fake news in circulation on the social media space. The models used include support vector machine, random forest, artificial neural networks, and LSTM-based variational autoencoders. The ANN model was shown to detect misinformation at an accuracy of 99.68%, the DistilBERT classification model at 99.4%, the SVM and RF model at 75%. Furthermore, it was discovered that a combination of models or ensemble techniques showed more classification and prediction accuracy for the identification of infodemics from the social media space and could provide more insights for the identification of fake and misinforming messages on the social media and other platforms. The results obtained from the different models have shown that the application of DL and ML model in the management of misinformation in the critical healthcare sector would help in reduction of fake news, misinformation, malinformation and false alarm with respect to healthcare information.
Conclusion
Misinformation, in all its ramifications, is a plague that has the capacity to induce mistrust, destabilization and confusion in the society. It is an information epidemic propelled by the vast number of users on the social media landscape, and has become a sociocultural and economic time bomb. Lately, the government of Nigeria has launched a massive drive against fake news and their perpetrators, but with little impact. With the emergence of the Covid-19 pandemic, the rate of misinformation with respect to the pandemic and its effect on the health sector raised concerns with respect to vaccination and treatment. The extensive application of ML algorithms and models to sift and classify false content, memes, fake news and other types of misinformation in the healthcare sector can help improve the healthcare delivery services, reduce the rate of misdiagnosis and treatment, and improve user consciousness and trust in healthcare information. *
