Public perceptions research plays an important part in technology and policy development, feeding in what people actually think, rather than what experts think they think 1 . It is worth considering because if people do not want or do not engage with the technology or policy in question, it is likely to fail. Smart meters are currently being disseminated in many industrialized countries around the world (including Austria, Denmark, France and the USA 2,3 ) in order to increase electricity grid efficiencies. However, in some places the roll-out has stalled due to public opposition, as has happened in, for example, The Netherlands 2 and California 3 .
Misinformation about smart meters, such as that they are linked to cancer cases, has been disseminated in some areas, but it is unclear to what extent opposition to smart meters is related to exposure to misinformation. Now, writing in the Journal of Risk Research , Jay Hmielowski at the University of Florida and Alex Kirkpatrick and Amanda Boyd at Washington State University 4 observe that familiarity with misinformation about smart meters was associated with concerns about — and lower support for — smart meters. In addition, people who had higher knowledge about smart meters were less affected by health misinformation.
Hmielowski and colleagues used an online survey to examine people’s familiarity with media and misinformation about smart meters as well as their views about smart meters and related policy 4 . They recruited a broadly representative sample, in terms of age and gender, of adults (over the age of 18) living in US states where smart meters are prevalent (that is, where at least 50% of the population have a smart meter). Participants were not required to be familiar with smart meters and were provided with images and a description of smart meters prior to completing survey questions.
The survey results indicated people’s attention to media information about smart meters was indirectly linked to lower support for government and business policies on smart meters. The more people attended to media information about smart meters, the more familiar they were likely to be with cost and health misinformation, which related to higher concerns about cost and health respectively. These higher levels of concern were ultimately linked to lower support for government and business policies on smart meters (Fig. 1). Hmielowski and colleagues showed that participants’ attention to media related to increased familiarity with health and cost misinformation relating to smart meters, which in turn related to greater health and cost concerns about smart meters, respectively. Both health and cost concerns related to lower support for government and business policies relating to smart meters. Note that causality is assumed but not tested by the data so relationships may be reversed or bidirectional.

Notably, the study also examined people’s knowledge about smart meters; participants in the study were asked to answer a series of true or false questions assessing knowledge about smart meters. They found that high levels of knowledge buffered participants against the impact of misinformation on related concerns for health misinformation but not for cost misinformation. Examining the statements used to examine familiarity with cost misinformation further indicates that it is possible that the cost misinformation and health misinformation statements used could vary in terms of how credible they are. Health misinformation statements referred to relationships between smart meters and cancer and potential negative health effects related to electromagnetic fields or radio frequencies used by smart meters; evidence indicates that any links here very unlikely 5 . Cost misinformation statements referred to potential costs of purchasing and installing a smart meter and incorrect billing. It is arguable that these cost issues are possible, even if unlikely. It is conceivable that participants, even those with higher knowledge about the topic in question, may be impacted by misinformation that is more plausible.
A broader issue in the field is how to identify and combat misinformation. Some misinformation may be clearly identified whereas other information may be harder to disprove, or indeed may not be disprovable. It has been proposed that misinformation can be tackled in two key ways 6 : through structural changes to online websites and communications where misinformation is removed; or through helping people identify and ignore misinformation. Clearly identifiable misinformation may be tackled through structural changes where, for example, bots programmed to spread misinformation or certain known responsible platforms or individuals are blocked — though there is a careful balance to be struck here with ensuring that freedom of speech is maintained 6 .
Misinformation that is less clearly disproved, and/or disprovable, is much harder to tackle in this way. Helping people identify the credibility of information may be the best defence for this kind of information. Increasing knowledge about a topic appears to be one way of doing this. However, given mixed evidence around the utility of knowledge in defending against the impact of misinformation, we must also consider which factors interact with knowledge to determine how misinformation is received. Discussing the credibility of misinformation may also be a dangerous tactic, given that research has also observed that attempts to correct misinformation can have paradoxical effects, with the potential that the information is actually better remembered in the future and given more credence 7 .
We encourage the further exploration of ways to protect ourselves from misinformation. Experience in spotting misinformation has been found to help people develop resistance to it, enabling participants to identify deception strategies 8 ; this approach relies on training, though. A promising direction is a combination of structural approaches and personal training where digital tools could help us identify misinformation 9 , though further development of these is needed. Ironically, we suggest that trust could be an issue in the uptake of these tools.
With their study, Hmielowski and colleagues highlight important circumstances in which people are immune to misinformation and those in which they are not. Given the insidious rise in misinformation, we should develop appropriate intelligence, and trusted intelligent systems, to further these defences.
