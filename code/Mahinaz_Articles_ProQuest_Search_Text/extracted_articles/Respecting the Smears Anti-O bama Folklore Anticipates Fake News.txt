Headnote From allegations that he wouldn't sing the national anthem to accusations that he ordered the spread of the Ebola virus, Barack Obama was plagued by fake news throughout his candidacies and two terms as president of the United States. Neither journalists covering his presidency nor his own campaign staff persistently or publicly debunked these claims. This tolerance for the circulation of spurious accusations may have contributed to the proliferation of fake news in 2016 and 2017.
Keywords
AFS ethnographic thesaurus: Contemporary legend, African American, folk belief, rumor
on October 24, 2008, i delivered an American Folklore Society (AFS) paper entitled "What Do Barack Obama and Snapple Iced Tea Have in Common?" (Turner 2008). The 2008 meetings were about 3 weeks prior to a presidential election day-the election in which the Republican ballot was topped by John McCain and the Democratic ticket by Barack Obama. After documenting what I would argue to be more rumors, legends, and conspiracy theories than any prior presidential candidate had triggered, I closed that paper by speculating that the election results would offer evidence as to just how credible voters were finding the Obama lore. I was shortsighted. I should have said that the 2008, 2012, and 2016 election results would signal just how credible voters found anti-Obama rumors and legends to be.
Although I argue that media and Obama supporters' superficial attention to discrediting anti-Obama lore may have laid the tracks for the steam engine of2016/2017 fake news accusations, I cannot take credit for seeing the fake news train coming. My focus was on the etic genre labels familiar to folklorists: rumor, legend, and conspiracy theory. I'm accustomed to them functioning as a kind of supplemental news, a means through which communities with shared belief systems reshape legitimate news in ways that conform with their worldview. Since others in these panels have parsed the sundry definitions of fake news, I won't repeat those here except to say that I've heard no definition of fake news that doesn't apply to these texts about Barack Obama.
There are copious anti-Obama texts from the earliest days of his campaign. In the book I am writing on this subject, there's a chapter on patriotism. These allegations are among the earliest texts with fauxtography "proving" that Obama wouldn't sing the national anthem or salute the flag, had an American flag removed from the exterior of his campaign plane, wouldn't wear a flag pin, and dissed the Boy Scouts of America. There was a rumor that he lowered the White House flag to half-staff after the death of Whitney Houston but not after the death of Nancy Reagan. There's a chapter on the beliefs that Obama was a Muslim. Chronologically, these surface much sooner than the birther beliefs, a cluster of notions claiming that circumstances surrounding his birth made him ineligible to be president of the United States. There are the beliefs that he started the Ebola epidemic so that whites could be killed off and the United States populated with Muslims, or that he's gay and had arranged for numerous lovers who referred to him as "Bathhouse Barry" to be killed so that they couldn't out him. And there are almost as many rumors about Michelle Obama as there are about Barack. It has been claimed that she hired far more assistants than any prior First Lady, that her senior thesis reflected an anti-white agenda, that she wanted a picture of herself wearing a royal crown on a US postage stamp, and (drumroll, please) that she was born a man and had a sex-change operation. Since Obama left office, the rumors have continued: that he left roaches in the White House; that after he moved out, a stash of illegal drugs was discovered in his living quarters; more ominously, that he and "deep state" operatives are sabotaging the current administration. Lest you think the rumors are subsiding, there's a photo circulating with a caption that says "President Obama gave Presidential Medal of Freedom to Harvey Weinstein," and a doctored photo "proves" the case.
In the early days of the campaign following Obama's announcement of his candidacy on December 10, 2007, very few of these texts were covered by the mainstream media. The Obama campaign took the position that to deny them in any kind of a conspicuous way would be to plant them in the minds of people who hadn't yet heard them.
As folklorists, we know how complicated the debunking of a contemporary legend by the maligned party can be. The Waldorf-Astoria Hotel, one of the first entities to be accused of malfeasance in a contemporary legend-namely, that they charged an exorbitant price for a cake recipe-chose not to publicly contradict the message but supplied anyone who inquired about it with the facts, as well as a free recipe (Brunvand 1984). The contemporary legend circulated for decades and still surfaces from time to time, albeit not always about the Waldorf-Astoria. When the tenacious legend that Procter & Gamble was actually a satanic enterprise and its logo was proof of demonic affiliation resurfaced in the 1980s, the company launched an aggressive refutation effort. The texts continued to circulate, although sales of the products did not demonstrably suffer (Fine 1992). After the owners of Snapple Iced Tea went on Good Morning America to discredit the rumors that they supported the Ku Klux Klan, I heard from many of the people I was interviewing about the legend that they knew that Snapple was supporting white supremacy because the owners were confronted with the evidence on Good Morning America. In short, there are few, if any, precedents that demonstrate that by unequivocally denying a text, a company can halt its dissemination.
However, an inquiry from a reporter caused the Obama campaign in June 2008 to create a "Fight The Smears" tab on their campaign website (Pickler 2008). It wasn't a rumor about Barack Obama that prompted the course correction; it was about Michelle, and the accusation was that she had used the word "whitey" from the pulpit of Jeremiah Wright's church. And for the duration of the campaign, if you went to the website for Obama, this feature worked like a campaign-specific Snopes. But prior to the election, the Obamas rarely addressed the texts head-on. Instead, they clearly attempted to debunk the rumors by showcasing behaviors and practices that would negate them. Although his use of a flag pin had been irregular during the early days of
the campaign, Obama soon made it a permanent fixture on his lapel. To this day, it is hard to find an image of him that doesn't contain the flag pin or abundant patriotic iconography. The entire January 2009 inauguration ceremony can be read as an attempt by Obama and his team to discredit the slurs that had accumulated during the campaign. From his overcoat during the parade to his tuxedo at the inauguration balls, every garment he wore had a flag pin. Much to the consternation of many of his supporters, he chose
an evangelical faith leader, Pastor Rick Warren, to read the invocation, and, no doubt to stifle any concerns that he was being sworn in with the Quran, he requested to be sworn in with the Bible used by Abraham Lincoln. These are just a few examples. It is worth noting that denials of the Obama rumors came not just from his campaign, but also from his opponents. About a month before Election Day, John McCain did a question-and-answer session with a crowd of enthusiastic supporters. A woman expressed her fervent hope that McCain would win and said she was receiving emails that Barack Obama was a Muslim. The crowd cheered, and McCain said, "You don't have to be afraid of Obama; he's a decent person and a family man. . . . I just disagree with him" (Associated Press 2008). Obama supporters eventually came to pay attention to the cycle of beliefs known as the birther stories. These accusations claim that Obama had misrepresented his citizenship and was ineligible to be the president of the United States. Although the supposition that Obama was not a citizen also circulated in his days as a candidate, it was not nearly as prominent as the belief that he was a Muslim. It was after his election that these rumors and legends about falsified birth records proliferated. The earliest advocates were not recognizable names; the lawyers who most steadfastly tried to get traction around removing Obama from office were Philip J. Berg and Orly Taitz.1 Once again, mainstream news sources paid only fleeting attention to them, although anyone perusing the websites and blogs affiliated with the far right could find abundant coverage of the rumors.
Networks and newspapers really started to pay attention to them when Donald Trump gave them credence. When he became involved, they were reported in major newspapers such as the New York Times, the Washington Post, and similar big-city daily papers. Trump's accusations were prominently covered by all of the major television and radio networks, outlets that had barely paid attention to Taitz, Berg, and the early adopters of birtherism.
Trump's involvement caught the attention of comedians and late-night talk show hosts, who began to skewer him and those who embraced his position. Suddenly, birtherism was being ridiculed regularly. Oddly enough, the story came to be about Trump, and he was criticized, notably at the 2011 White House Correspondents' Dinner. Some political pundits now speculate that Trump decided to pursue the presidency to get back at the elite-establishment types who ridiculed him that night.
Trump took credit for forcing Obama to release his "long form" birth certificate, which also took place in April 2011, the same month as the Correspondents' Dinner. Although Trump sometimes claims that his intervention forced Obama to prove his legitimacy once and for all, that may not have had a significant impact on the belief cycle. Many of the most ardent birthers maintain that this document is as fraudulent as they believe the short-form one to be. And, as is so often the case, Trump himself is inconsistent on this point. In some contexts, he claims to believe Obama is a citizen; in others, he raises doubts.
While birther beliefs continued to circulate, they did retreat into the background after Trump's sustained engagement and the subsequent lampooning of him. In his essay "Humor as an Integral Part of the Contemporary Legend Process," Bill Ellis demonstrates that humor can dilute the potency of contemporary legends (2005). I don't have nearly enough "proof" of this, but I would argue that truly clever and wellexecuted ridicule of a belief works better than a logical, sincere denial. To espouse a birther view was to lay yourself open for ridicule. Wikipedia maintains a convenient entry entitled "Barack Obama Presidential Eligibility Litigation," which inventories all of the litigation initiated by birthers in their attempts to discredit the president.2 There were numerous such cases filed between 2008 and 2012, but they began to diminish in number at around the time that birtherism became a joke.
The rumors and legends about Barack and Michelle Obama did not end with the birther texts. Throughout Obama's second administration, the themes discernible in the early (2007-2008) texts were updated and attached to contemporary events. As late as 2016, Obama was accused of forbidding the distribution of Christmas cards to the American military during the holidays, ostensibly because as a Muslim, he abhorred Christmas.
Journalists who have interviewed me over the years espoused the somewhat common, but flawed, view that only a small number of illogical fringe right-wing extremists accepted and disseminated such beliefs. The decisions by the pundits and politicians to minimize attempts to rebut or correct them meant they became a part of the record. There's no official public place where we can easily find the White House or the president denying Christmas card distribution had been prohibited, so that becomes part of the evidence that it could have happened.
Again, it is probably the case that "the hard-core" Obama-haters would not have accepted such denials. Whether or not statements of the facts as delineated by the president would have truly satisfied his critics may not be the point. By letting the negative musings go largely unchecked and unrebuked, both Obama's advisors and the journalists who covered his presidency missed opportunities to definitively clarify the record. it is, after all, the record of the 44th president of the United States. Whether they were right-wing bloggers or Russian antagonists, it could be claimed that they got away with undermining the stature of a sitting president of the United States. Not surprisingly, it didn't end there, and the nation was not ready for the tidal wave of fake news that erupted with the 2016 election. The mainstream media and established political authorities ignored, tolerated, and/or dismissed rumors, legends, and conspiracy theories that surfaced about Barack Obama, and this inattention contributed to the ease with which authentically "fake news" is considered plausible or legitimate.
