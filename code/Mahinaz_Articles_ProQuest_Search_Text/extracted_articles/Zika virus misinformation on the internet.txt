Introduction
The recent outbreak of Zika virus infections in South and Central America has led to significant sustained media coverage. As with previous epidemics which captured global attention, such as Ebola, misinformation has been spread about the disease, its pathophysiology, prevention and treatment. The internet has become a favored mechanism for the spread of misinformation [1,2] .
We aimed to investigate the nature of misinformation about the Zika virus on the internet, and to compare the degree of misinformation on Google.com (Alphabet Inc.) and Bing.com (Microsoft Corporation), the two most popular search engines.
Methods
We searched the first twenty pages of Google and Bing search results, on the default setting which shows ten results per page excluding advertisements. Searches were performed on 25 February 2016, and the search term used was "Zika virus". Two independent reviewers (AV and SSN) read through all the search results, and identified those websites which appeared to be supportive of viewpoints and ideas about Zika that went against the most recent guidelines issued by the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) [3] .
Results
Our review found 6 websites in the first 20 pages of Google search results with misinformation. Interestingly, Bing only had 1 website with misinformation in its first 20 pages. The list of websites and a summary of each is provided in the Supplementary material . We have condensed the main arguments in these websites about the current Zika virus epidemic and its link with microcephaly in Table 1 .
Discussion
A 2012 survey revealed that 72% of American internet users used the internet as a source of medical information, with 77% of those beginning their search at a search engine like Google or Bing [4] . There is significant evidence that free speech on the internet allows for fringe voices to gain more attention than they would in more restrictive settings [5] .
Our investigation revealed that the two most popular search engines, Google and Bing, only had a few results in the first 20 pages that argued against the scientific consensus on Zika as represented by the CDC guidelines. The arguments made in these websites primarily revolved around portraying Zika as part of a conspiracy by elite interests and corporations to profit and/or to reduce world population, with several questions being raised on the reliability of the science on Zika, the causes of its spread, and its link to microcephaly. Interestingly, Bing actually had less misinformation in the first 20 pages than Google, in spite of it being the less popular search engine.
Conclusions
In the age of mass media, the ability to spread public health information is accompanied by the risk of incorrect information being spread by malicious agents or conspiracy theorists. There need to be concerted efforts aimed at combating these messages, with appropriate publicity given to relevant research findings.
