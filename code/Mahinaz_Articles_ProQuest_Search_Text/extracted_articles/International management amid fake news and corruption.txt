Introduction and problem
Despite the efforts made by European countries to introduce essential anticorruption reforms, bribery remains at very high levels across Europe and particularly in countries such as Romania, Bulgaria and Hungary. Corruption is a phenomenon that costs the European Union’s (EU) economy around €120bn annually (European Commission, 2021). It is exemplified by the abuse of functions, bribery and influence-peddling but can also be found in the form of nepotism and conflicts of interest (Altbach, 2004).
Transparency International states that “corruption represents the abuse of entrusted powers for private gain” (Transparency International, 2021a, 2021b), although precise definitions vary regionally and even between countries (Teichmann, 2017). Nonetheless, it is essential to distinguish between different levels of corruption (Mauro and Driscoll, 1997). According to Gan Integrity, there are three types of corruption: petty corruption generally involves low-level bureaucrats who abuse limited authority for personal gain; grand corruption mainly involves the political elites, whose illegal activities impact the legal, economic and political frameworks of entire countries and the corporations who operate within them; and systemic corruption results when private or public organizations create norms of governance that allow such malpractice to spread (Gan Integrity, 2021).
As mentioned by Transparency International, the ongoing COVID-19 pandemic has aggravated the phenomenon of corruption in Eastern Europe. Many citizens have leveraged personal connections or paid bribes to receive medical attention, with Romania and Bulgaria among the countries suffering the highest rates of health-related bribery (Manea, 2014). Even 30 years after the fall of its communist regime, corruption still represents a significant problem in Eastern European countries; Romania, Bulgaria and Hungary remain the most corrupt countries in the EU, according to Transparency International’s Corruption Perceptions Index.
The prevalence of corruption in Eastern European countries, alongside the widespread fake news problem, has raised concerns for international managers. The ineffective enforcement of antibribery legislation impacts the country on several levels. It is repeatedly claimed that legal proceedings in these countries can be affected via political pressure or bribes. The corruption revealed in high-profile cases is likely to represent only the tip of the iceberg. Focusing on Eastern European countries, with an additional focus on Romania, this study will critically examine the circumstances under which it is possible to manufacture legal proceedings and spread fake news across Europe and how this affects international investors in Western Europe.
This study examines and highlights the challenges faced by international managers when looking to invest in Eastern European countries. It discusses the problem of corruption and its effects on business and how it is possible to bribe journalists to publish fake news against competitors, or how it is possible to bribe public officials to fabricate legal proceedings against commercial competitors. The study contributes to the existing literature by suggesting new and immediate alternatives for international managers to help neutralize or overcome these threats. Indeed, there have already been several criticisms in the literature about corruption in Eastern European countries. Still, the literature has not adequately described the effects of fake news and corruption on international managers wishing to invest in these countries. There is, therefore, a significant research gap. This research gap has practical relevance because, although emerging markets such as Romania, Bulgaria and Hungary remain attractive to international managers, the high levels of corruption found there mean that these opportunities should be treated with a healthy degree of skepticism.
In the first part of this paper, corruption is discussed. A chapter about fake news follows this section. Subsequently, international managers’ challenges to corruption and fake news are analyzed. Ultimately, key issues are identified, and potential implications and recommendations are introduced.
Literature
There is an extensive literature on corruption and it is defined as the abuse of power by a public official for personal gain (Ashforth and Anand, 2003). According to Bahoo et al. (2020), there is a fundamental conceptual distinction between private corruption involving managers, firms or employees and public corruption involving politicians and also government officials. Furthermore, public and private corruption can also be classified as low or high (Bahoo et al. , 2020). Firms wishing to invest in markets in transition prefer the arbitrariness of widespread corruption to avoid known additional costs in favor of additional uncertainty (Cuervo-Cazurra, 2008). According to Rašković et al. (2020), to help understand the role of institutions in emerging markets, institutional theory is particularly relevant and research on corruption in transition economies has largely relied on it (Jiménez et al. , 2017).
Aim and methodology
For this study, informal interviews with presumed providers of illegal services were used to investigate the previously unexplored innovative research question (Teichmann, 2017). Most importantly, they helped to gain initial access to the field.
For this reason, alleged perpetrators were interviewed and not any participants, as the latter are not very familiar with bribery. Even if one might assume that they have paid bribes from time to time, they usually do not have experience with the challenges resulting from bribery in multinational corporations. For this reason, only alleged perpetrators who can be assumed to have experience or knowledge in the field of bribery in multinational corporations were chosen.
Statistical methods have limited use in predominantly qualitative studies; for this reason, theoretical saturation was the main consideration in selecting the number of participants (O’reilly and Parker, 2013). Interviews were conducted until participants’ statements began to repeat themselves (Teichmann, 2017).
Data collection
For this study, 10 informal (unrecorded) interviews with presumed providers of illegal services were conducted. Informal interviews were conducted with individuals who can be assumed to have experience or knowledge in the field of corruption in multinational corporations. The first author undertook to maintain their anonymity, so for reasons of confidentiality, it is not possible to provide more detailed information about the interviewees. Conversations took place in informal settings and responses were recorded using memory protocols.
As bribery is a global phenomenon and multinational corporations operate in many countries, alleged perpetrators of illegal financial services from several geographical areas were interviewed.
Findings
Our interviewees agree with previous findings on corruption and dirty practices in Eastern Europe. Corrupt practices in Eastern European countries range from the highest levels of government to the lowest levels of private enterprise (Fazekas and King, 2019).
According to participant no. 1 public behavior perpetuates corruption in the business sector, especially in the form of petty corruption, which is most often related to value-added tax (VAT) and invoicing evasion. According to participant no. 2, grand corruption is most common at the intersection of government and business in the form of overvaluation of public procurement procedures and public procurement commissions.
Participant no. 3 also argues that grand corruption provides easier access to the business sector. He argues that people who have close associates in government can win tenders or have easier access to infrastructure investments. So people in government get money from major investments such as stadiums or highways.
Participant no. 4 argues that for businesses dealing in trade in Eastern Europe, bribing customs officials is a popular practice.
According to participant no. 5, corruption in Eastern Europe is often voluntary, because people try to bribe doctors, teachers or civil servants in the hope of a preferential service or treatment.
Participant no. 6 felt that in Romania, the state has been captured by domestic actors, this type of corruption is linked to a slow and continuous privatization process.
Participant no. 7 argued that in many Eastern European countries corruption is still extremely widespread in the education system. For instance, it is extremely common for students to seek help from an acquaintance or to use gifts when it comes to getting a better grade or admission to a good university.
Participant no. 8 added that in Hungary, including social aids and dorm beds are distributed on the basis of petty deals.
According to participant no. 9, in some Eastern European countries, it is even possible for businessmen to bribe journalists to publish damaging fake news against competitors to weaken them and gain market share.
In the opinion of participant no. 10 among the biggest problems facing Eastern European countries are clientelism and nepotism.
Thanks to the views provided by interviewees we can thus indicate the importance of exploring the socio-cognitive aspects of corruption as a type of institution and the need to look beyond organizational boundaries when exploring corruption in the business environment to understand the environment and various external factors to which actors respond (Takacs Haynes and Rašković, 2021).
Corruption
In 1999, the OECD Convention on Combating Bribery of Foreign Public Officials in International Business Transactions (the Antibribery Convention) came into force. Under the terms of the convention, member countries of the OECD Working Group on Bribery could enact laws criminalizing the bribery of foreign public officials in connection with international business transactions (OECD, 2000).
Gan Integrity defines bribery as:
[…] a criminal and corrupt practice where an entity offers something of value to a corporate or a public official in exchange for their cooperation in influencing a decision-making process, committing or allowing fraud against the official’s organization, or otherwise violating their official duties. (Gan Integrity, 2021)
Corruption affects both developed and developing countries at different stages of economic progress and under various political systems (Misangyi, 2008; Ehrlich and Lui, 1999). Although corruption may vary from one EU country to another, it damages the entire EU by reducing public finances, lowering investment levels and hindering the legitimate operation of the internal market (European Commission, 2021). Corruption is a considerable obstacle to stable policymaking and economic growth (Gupta, 1995).
Bribery is a clandestine and complex phenomenon, making it challenging to measure and quantify (OECD, 2020). As a result, it is difficult to implement measures to combat bribery. Still, multinational corporations are viewed as crucial in this respect because the problem would ultimately disappear if they stopped paying bribes. Michiel van Hulten, Director of Transparency International EU, suggests:
[…] there are many immediate actions that can be taken to remedy these problems, such as increasing lobbying transparency at the EU and national levels and tackling tax avoidance. And EU policies to protect whistle-blowers and fight money laundering must be effectively and swiftly transposed into national law.
Most international organizations have informed employees that so-called “facilitation payments” are no longer permitted because they present an existential risk to the company (Follett, 2015). It is not in the interests of a company’s board of directors or its shareholders to condone the payment of bribes. Nonetheless, employees based in Eastern European countries occasionally continue to do so. Many organizations use incentives and encourage participation in online programs to educate staff about anticorruption policies as a deterrent.
Of course, fighting corruption is about more than refusing bribes instead of the result of longer-term planning for business integrity and compliance. Therefore, creating a compliant environment for everyone and reducing the chances of encountering noncompliant situations will reduce the risk of corruption (OECD, 2016).
Corruption in Eastern European countries
In several Eastern European countries, such as Romania, Bulgaria and Hungary, bribery may seem necessary in most commercial transactions. For this reason, it is perhaps not best addressed through market self-regulation. On the other hand, the belief that corruption could be eliminated by creating conditions of perfect competition through political reforms seems an oversimplification (Bliss and Tella, 1997).
Despite improvements since the fall of communism in 1989, corruption remains a major problem in Romania and increases the risk of doing business in the country (Ristei, 2010). Although Romanian courts have imprisoned government ministers, ex-ministers, business tycoons and high-profile functionaries over the last 30 years, the country attracts EU condemnation for its failure to combat corruption. For example, in 2014, Alina Bica, the former Chief Prosecutor of the Directorate for Investigating Organized Crime and Terrorism, was sentenced to three-and-a-half years in prison for accepting a bribe from a local businessperson to cancel a pending case against him.
Recent studies estimate Romania’s annual gross domestic product (GDP) losses at around €38bn due to corruption. However, to better apprehend the actual consequences of corruption, we can evaluate it at three levels: at the national level, it can affect entire countries, with overwhelming effects on the public sector; at the corporate level, it can expose private businesses to higher risks; and at the individual level, it can inconvenience private citizens in many ways (Teichmann and Sergi, 2018).
One path chosen by many countries in their struggle to reduce bribery is the creation of new laws or the improvement of old ones (Van Schoor, 2017). However, in opposition to such attempts, the Romanian parliament passed a bill to reduce existing protections against corruption after the head of the country’s Social Democrat party was sentenced to jail in 2018. A further measure could be increasing people’s awareness of the negative impact of bribery and the dissemination of fake news. The need to do so is apparent: in the 2017 Special Eurobarometer, 68% of Romanian citizens declared themselves directly affected by corruption in their daily lives (Euractiv, 2019). A final approach to fighting corruption in Romania took the form of tough sanctions against company management, which had failed to take the necessary steps to manage corruption risks (Teichmann, 2017).
Because companies operating in countries such as Hungary, Romania, Bulgaria and Slovenia are exposed to corruption, international managers must identify ways of protecting their businesses (Krastev et al. , 2019). The Romanian Anti-Corruption Directorate (DNA) investigates medium and high-profile corruption cases, including the misuse of EU allowances (minor crimes are managed through tribunals conducted via the Prosecutor’s Office). Anticorruption laws include those covered by the Criminal Code and Law no. 78 on Preventing, Discovering and Punishing Corruption Offences, Law no. 656/07.12.2002 on Preventing and Sanctioning of Money Laundering, and Law no. 82/1991on Accounting Registrations. Anti-corruption legislation criminalizes the abuse of power, money laundering, peddling influence, the bribery of foreign officials and bribery which is both active and passive (Gan Integrity, 2020).
As a next step, Romania has passed an Act of parliament specifically targeting corruption, alongside a National Anticorruption Strategy, to benefit all institutions involved in battling the problem. Nonetheless, Romania has far to travel in combating corruption. Subsequently, companies and international managers connected to Romania are advised to ensure their business operations fall within the national anticorruption laws.
But it is not just Romania’s regulatory climate that poses problems and makes doing business difficult. According to the World Bank’s latest ranking, Hungary has dropped from 48th to 53rd place on ease of doing business after the Czech Republic, Poland and Slovakia. As in other Eastern European countries, lack of skilled labor is a major obstacle to investment in Hungary. The retail, banking and telecommunications sectors have been hit particularly hard by new taxes introduced by the government. Hungary also has the highest VAT – 27% – in the EU.
Fake news
According to Transparency International, “‘Fake News’ refers to both the distribution of incorrect information as well as to efforts to discredit accurate reporting” (Transparency International, 2021a, 2021b). Recently, social media has facilitated the spread of misleading information, a phenomenon that has shaken society’s confidence in news media outlets and democracy itself (Kietzmann et al. , 2020).
To obtain the personal and financial information of individuals and corporations, cyber attackers have used misleading information on social media, thereby undermining a nation’s security (Petratos, 2021). Social media can also damage a person’s public image because content that harms reputations can easily be copied, distributed and archived, making it difficult to reverse the damage in the long term (Sands et al. , 2020). For example, Facebook recorded 3.8 billion fake news views in 2020, peaking when the COVID-19 crisis began (Garcia and Ragione, 2021). Even now, multiple social media posts continue to deny the effectiveness of vaccination against serious infectious diseases, despite overwhelming evidence to the contrary on a global scale (Park, 2020). For these reasons, the EU is working with online platforms such as Facebook and Twitter to remove inaccurate and fraudulent content and promote authoritative sources (Pavleska et al. , 2018).
In Austria, the government has delayed the implementation of a national anticorruption strategy under the cover of COVID-19. In October 2021, allegations emerged against former Chancellor Sebastian Kurz that he used public funds in his 2017 election campaign to bribe journalists and pollsters (Transparency International, 2021a, 2021b).
Spreading fake information has also enabled dishonest companies to weaken and harm innocent competitors. The repercussions include reductions in sales and revenues and lasting harm to the reputation of those affected. Many companies now fight fake news by hiring third-party firms that deploy large numbers of human monitors and the latest artificial intelligence technology to scour social media for damaging posts to protect their share price (Campbell et al. , 2021). Counterdisinformation strategies, such as having human fact-checkers for websites or artificial intelligence to detect bots on social networks, could also be used to prevent damage from the spread of misleading information (Campbell et al. , 2021). A partnership between the private and public sectors can also mitigate cyber risks by forming a united front with cyber defenses (Petratos, 2021).
According to Interpol, the rapid spread of fake news and misinformation can also increase cyber-attack sophistication. Criminals are also taking advantage of the situation created by the COVID-19 pandemic and security vulnerabilities to steal data and generate profits as organizations and businesses rapidly deploy remote systems and networks to support staff working from home (Interpol, 2020).
Fake news in Eastern Europe
The phenomenon of fake news is pervasive in Eastern European countries. For example, 100 fake accounts based in Romania were removed by Facebook in 2020 over suspicions of interference in the US presidential election, with the pages posing as Trump supporters (Forbes, 2020a, 2020b). Its transmission is a productive business for Hungary, Romania and Serbia. Some fake news sources can be traced to former or current politicians and usually operate through network portals or are controlled by an entity coordinating multiple websites (Szakacs, 2020).
The media industry in Eastern European countries is deeply implicated in corruption and is dominated by a few moguls. For example, in Romania, former Social Democrat deputy Sebastian Ghita, the “Romania TV” channel head, faces four corruption, tax evasion and money laundering counts. Despite police surveillance, Ghita was able to flee the country and settle in Belgrade under the shelter of the Serbian regime. DNA prosecutors allege that between 2007 and 2014, he received bribes of between €3m and €10m. The founder of the Antena 3 channel, Dan Voiculescu, was sentenced to 10 years in prison for money laundering. He was also repeatedly accused of controlling the country’s politics through his media channel.
In Eastern European countries, the dissemination of misleading information is a political phenomenon and affects the corporate world (Belova and Georgieva, 2018). It is well known that in several countries journalists can be bribed to publish harmful fake news about competitors. Public officials can be similarly induced to manufacture criminal proceedings against those competitors (Tandoc et al. , 2019).
Businesspeople often wish to weaken their market rivals and make market gains while competitors are tied up with misleading information and criminal proceedings. Accusations of founding a criminal organization, bribing public officials, embezzlement and money laundering are widespread. These charges can be based on the false testimonies of “witnesses” and planted “evidence” from house searches (Teichmann, 2021a, 2021b, 2021c). Such falsehoods can lead to asset seizures, further house searches or the execution of (international) arrest warrants.
Challenges for international managers
Eastern European countries are generally seen as having positive investment prospects. Predominantly, countries near Western countries are also the most developed (Ben-Ami, 2014).
Based on a 2017 Deloitte report, countries in the Central and Eastern Europe region, such as Poland, the Czech Republic, Slovakia, Hungary and Romania, grew economically faster than any other region in the world except Asia-Pacific. Although investing in these countries has many positive aspects, such as skilled labor force, strategic location and relatively low labor costs compared to the Western countries, there are still many issues that pose problems for international managers looking to invest in these countries. Some of the fundamental problems are underdeveloped infrastructure, weak governance, a less developed bureaucracy and the judicial system (Buckley, 2017).
Major reforms are needed in some Eastern countries such as Romania, Bulgaria, Hungary and Slovenia to improve the judicial system and increase transparency in public procurement, corporate insolvency and investor property rights (Djankov, 2016) (Court of Justice of the European Union, 2021). Due to their strategic location, EU membership and cheap labor force could offer many investment opportunities for international managers. For this reason, it is necessary to focus on the challenges they face and the necessary precautions before committing financial resources to this country.
Companies in Eastern Europe have regularly been exposed to incompetent and corrupt judicial and political systems, political uncertainty and inefficient bureaucracy, despite the fact that the countries in question are EU members. (Frâncu, 2014). In addition, the infrastructure in some of these countries remains poorly developed and their legislative frameworks are subject to rapid change (TMF Group, 2014). These problems, coupled with large-scale emigration over the past two decades, with the population shrinking from 22.5 million in 2000 to 19 million in 2021, have made doing business in Eastern European countries a challenge compared to more developed Western countries. According to a 2019 Statista survey, widespread corruption is a major reason why many young people and skilled professionals have fled their home countries (Statista, 2021). The diminished labor pool has impacted the ability of firms, especially those in Romania, to recruit new employees (International Trade Administration, 2021).
Another major problem in some Eastern European countries is the corrupt medical system, where patients routinely pay small bribes to medical staff to receive basic care, often on a preferential basis. According to Forbes, Romania and Bulgaria currently have the most corrupt medical systems in Europe. In Romania, for example, steps have been taken to tackle this problem, with salaries for medical staff doubling in the last five years. However, the Romanian health system has struggled to cope with the COVID-19 pandemic, affecting it and demonstrating its many shortcomings. Romania had one of the highest death rates in the world from COVID-19 in 2021. Among European nations, only Bulgaria has vaccinated a smaller percentage of its population (26.5%) at the time of writing.
Chronic underfunding of the educational system has led to similar issues. For instance, Romania spends less than other European countries at all levels of education 2.8% of GDP, with more funding channeled to the upper secondary and tertiary levels than compulsory education (Kitchen, 2017). In complete opposition to Nordic education models, corruption is endemic in Romanian schools, with teachers often bribed to grade exams more leniently or boost class grades (Borcan et al. , 2017). Moreover, alarmingly high rates of early school leavers make it harder to source skilled workers, particularly given the brain drain of university graduates tempted by the higher wages offered in Western countries (Iacob, 2018).
Because Romania is not a eurozone member, payments are made in the national currency (Romanian New Leu) (Barabas, 2018). Nevertheless, high-value goods (real estate and cars) are priced in euros, and debts are commonly denominated in euros for many companies and consumers, creating inefficiencies caused by exchange rate variations and higher transaction charges (International Trade Administration, 2021). Thus, although emerging markets such as Romania remain attractive to international managers, the high levels of corruption found there require such opportunities to be treated with a healthy degree of skepticism (Mirzayev, 2020).
Selected key issues
Lack of private property rights
It is widely known that weak private property rights can restrain economic growth. Support for institutions that safeguard the property rights of households and firms increases the effectiveness of the state (Besly and Ghatak, 2009). However, in several Eastern European countries, corruption, bureaucracy and inconsistent enforcement of laws – including those related to property rights – only disrupt the business climate and discourage investment (US Department of State, 2019).
An inefficient and corrupt judicial system
Transparency International’s 2020 Corruption Index ranked Bulgaria and Romania as the most corrupt EU member states. It is claimed that criminal proceedings in Eastern Europe are routinely subject to attempted manipulation (Kühne, 2020). The confidence of international investors in the Romanian judicial system is regularly shaken by the failure of measures to combat corruption, and there is little faith in its adequacy as an institution (US Department of State, 2019).
It has been repeatedly claimed that in countries such as Romania and Bulgaria, legal proceedings can be influenced by political pressure or bribes. As a result, when white-collar criminal cases involving these countries are heard in Western courts, it is almost tough to rely on the facts established by Eastern European prosecutors without verification (Teichmann, 2021a, 2021b, 2021c). Investigations conducted by Eastern European prosecutors in cases of money laundering and corruption are considered to be unreliable.
Likewise, several concerns have been raised by foreign companies committed to trade and investment in Romania regarding the courts’ lack of expertise in commercial legislation, alongside the lengthy and challenging enforcement of laws through the judicial process (US Department of State, 2019). Also questionable is the role of prosecutors in high-profile corruption cases where suspects have been acquitted. Considerable doubts remain about Romania’s fight against corruption and judicial independence (Council of Europe, 2018).
Bribing journalists to spread fake news
In several Eastern European countries, the media industry is deeply involved in corruption. It can sometimes be dominated by influential politicians and businessmen who have been repeatedly accused of controlling politics in their countries through media channels (Stetka and Örnebring, 2013).
Corporate and political interference in news reporting is a long-established fact of life in Eastern Europe. Because journalists are known to accept bribes to cover news that may harm commercial competitors, it is important to remember that any news story may have been manipulated or even entirely manufactured.
Bribing public officials
In many Eastern European countries, it is possible to pursue contrived charges against competitors through the courts. It is not uncommon for unscrupulous business people to weaken their competitors through such fabrications and the resulting protracted legal hearings (Teichmann, 2021a, 2021b, 2021c). Although a competitor is occupied with such proceedings, their market share can be taken. Accusations of money laundering, embezzlement, bribery of public officials and other criminal acts can be made using invented witness statements and planted “evidence” uncovered during house searches (Teichmann, 2021a, 2021b, 2021c).
According to Anticorruption Law no. 78/2000, a person found guilty of receiving bribes can be sentenced to imprisonment between 3 and 10 years, whereas those paying bribes face a sentence of two to seven years. These sentences can be increased by a third if the receiver of the bribe is a judge, prosecutor, dignitary or has committed an administrative offense (Transparency International UK, 2019).
Yet, these punishments do not appear to have deterred offenders: Romania’s reputation for influencing proceedings through the payment of bribes or political pressure remains unchanged (Teichmann, 2021a, 2021b, 2021c). This may be because corruption begins when citizens enter the educational systems of Romania and other Eastern European countries (Borcan et al. , 2017). When bribery in the form of payments for teachers to pass exams and boost grades begins so early, a culture of corruption is easily transmitted to future generations of judges, doctors, politicians, teachers or prosecutors (Iosip, 2018). This corruption impacts the business environment through trumped-up proceedings against innocent competitors or genuine cases wherein evidence is distorted or disappears at the right time (Teichmann, 2021a, 2021b, 2021c).
Implications abroad
Frozen bank accounts
In countries such as Bulgaria, Hungary, Poland and Romania, it is entirely possible that journalists may publish harmful and misleading facts and prosecutors may have accepted bribes. Some prosecutors are even prepared to open criminal proceedings against political or economic competitors (Teichmann, 2021a, 2021b, 2021c). The corruption of these countries’ judicial systems entails the need to critically review their investigations.
When accusations of corporate bribery and corruption are investigated, the authorities apply asset seizures and account suspensions. In such cases, a company’s operations can be effectively brought to a halt. Business partners may then cancel their contracts with the company in question, mainly when the authorities publicize their investigations. For this reason, it is necessary to attend closely to the results of work carried out by Eastern European law enforcement agencies (Teichmann, 2021a, 2021b, 2021c). Legal proceedings in Eastern Europe are often politically motivated and even affected by serious procedural shortcomings (Kühne, 2010). Prosecutors may have been bribed and news may be partly or entirely fictitious. Hence, press stories should not be regarded as an adequate basis for assuming a criminal offense has occurred or applying asset seizures and dispositions (Teichmann, 2021a, 2021b, 2021c).
Interpol Red Notices
Interpol defines a Red Notice as “a request to law enforcement worldwide to locate and provisionally arrest a person pending extradition, surrender or similar legal action” (Interpol, 2021). Of the eight available notices, the Red Notice most closely resembles an international arrest warrant (The United States Department of Justice Archives, 2020).
International and European arrest warrants exist to ensure criminals cannot evade justice before the law by escaping to another country. However, several Eastern European countries have weaponized against important political and business figures. For instance, the Romanian state has used European arrest warrants to pursue successful businesspeople on several occasions (Teichmann, 2021a, 2021b, 2021c). The Romanian state continues to manipulate the legal system to attack its opponents (Falasca et al. , 2016). European arrest warrants issued in Romania often prejudice fair trials and make it difficult for Western judges to treat them confidently and appropriately. Particular caution must therefore be exercised when collaborating with the Eastern European judiciary. These considerations point to the urgent need to revise the European Arrest Warrant, so confidence in EU law is not undermined and maintains judicial decision-making quality.
Discussions and recommendations
Countries such as Hungary, Romania, Bulgaria and Slovenia are very important markets in Eastern Europe and, due to their strategic position and EU membership, offer a promising destination for investment (Jurubita et al. , 2021). However, international managers often complain about regulatory instability, relative failure to fight corruption and the low quality of institutions in these countries (US Department of State, 2019). Indeed, compared to Western nations, the investment market in Eastern European countries presents considerable challenges in the form of poor infrastructure, high levels of corruption and complex bureaucracy. In many cases, infrastructure developments have been left unfinished and funds have been cashed in by unscrupulous individuals involved in the projects.
For instance, international managers operating in Romania have criticized periodic legislative changes without consulting private sector representatives first (Ene, 2019). Frequent reorganizations of public institutions create a sense of instability and increase investor distrust (International Trade Administration, 2021). At the same time, many administrative agencies have yet to be digitalized, posing further problems for international managers.
Corruption in Eastern European countries is a major constraint to their development. The fight against it is, at least at a declarative level, an essential objective for public and private organizations (Mendelski, 2021). Corporations should join this fight by increasing their monitoring activities, offering bonuses for compliance and encouraging whistleblowers to communicate their concerns. Additional training would enable employees to understand the risks they pose to companies when engaging in illegal actions and the legal repercussions of bribery or other forms of corruption (Teichmann, 2017). Whistleblowing could be of value to antibribery initiatives as well as multinational corporations (Teichmann, 2017). Hence, current channels for informers should be enhanced by guaranteeing the safety and anonymity of employees. For larger companies, one solution could be to set up a compliance function implemented by dedicated officers or units that invest in staff integrity training and introduce internal and outsourced whistleblowing policies (OECD, 2016).
It is worth noting that current legislation requires Whistleblowing Directive no. 2019/1937 to be implemented in Romania by December 2021 for companies employing more than 250 staff and by December 2023 for companies with between 50 and 250 employees (Banu, 2020). More specific legislation differentiating between unregulated lobbying and the offenses of influence-peddling or abuse of office could improve Romanian society (Coman, 2006).
In Poland, a draft law on the protection of whistleblowers with the aim of ensuring the implementation of the Directive and establishing the first comprehensive regulation on the protection of whistleblowers was published on October 18, 2021 (DataGuidance, 2021). Eventually, private entities with at least 50 employees and financial institutions will be required to implement internal whistleblowing procedures (Mondaq, 2021).
On February 1, 2021, in the Czech Republic, the government approved a bill drafted by the Ministry of Justice. Under it, by March 2022, companies with more than 25 employees, investment companies and funds and public contracting entities will have to establish internal reporting channels for processing and receiving reports of unlawful conduct (Employment Law Worldview, 2021).
Businesses should also commit to transparency by disclosing information on the implementation of integrity policies, political contributions and donations, which can be seen as a means of enhancing corporate reputation (Galloppo et al. , 2015).
Start-ups can also strategically build partnerships with high-status individuals and organizations to build their prominence among a broad group of stakeholders at an early stage in their business cycle when they have a limited reputation. This tactic is called “reputation lending” (Harvard Business Review, 2017).
With the growth of online communities, misinformation has become increasingly prevalent. (Ciampaglia, 2018). Because such content is hard to regulate, fake news will continue to afflict societies for a long time to come (Belin, 2021). We live in a time of fake news and corruption, which is why to protect their brand reputations, international managers must constantly monitor any news concerning their brands and develop action plans to deal with misleading information (Forbes, 2020a, 2020b). For this reason, strategies are needed to actively monitor social platforms, create a communication protocol for crises and maintain a good online reputation for the company (Belin, 2021). A wide range of tools can be used to fight misinformation, such as apps that use artificial intelligence to detect bots or websites with human fact-checkers, but implementing these measures requires businesses to invest in research and development to find new cyber defenses against misleading information (Petratos, 2021).
Reform of the judiciary is also needed to improve the integrity and accountability of government institutions (Bobek and Kosař, 2014). Both private citizens and companies and other organizations need to trust the judiciary. Ultimately, private property rights cannot be guaranteed in weak judiciary systems.
International managers should also consider elements recommended by governments and international guidelines for developing integrity measures. To this end, international managers should include a commitment to reject any involvement in corrupt practices, especially as investors or customers place an even higher value on ethical behavior in corrupt environments, also take into account the business environment and local context and policies should consist of internal reporting channels, rules on conflicts of interest and gifts and procurement principles for business needs (OECD, 2016). Romania’s educational system is one of the most corrupt in Europe, although its infrastructure is among the least developed. In this context, a survey of companies operating in Romania found that education, technology and infrastructure projects should be prioritized to improve the country’s productivity (Nistor, 2014). The same survey indicated that levels of FDI in Romania were negatively impacted by the COVID-19 pandemic, with 46% of investors stating they had interrupted, paused or reduced their investment plans in the country (Ion and Milcev, 2020). Finally, because of mixed and challenging market conditions, potential international managers should apply due diligence when planning to invest in Romania or other Eastern European countries (Klonowski, 2007).
Conclusions
Examining challenges to foreign investment, this study found that corruption remains a significant problem in Eastern European countries and that it can affect their Western counterparts. Corruption affects health, education and judicial systems, slowing the pace of countries’ long-term progress by bribing public officials and journalists to fabricate lawsuits or publish damaging fake news to discredit competitors. Although some Eastern European countries offer many opportunities due to their fascinating geographical location, EU membership, low labor costs, widespread corruption and poor infrastructure present more challenges for investment and international managers. For these reasons, investors should exercise great caution before committing resources to these countries.
We live in a world of falsehoods, and misinformation has only increased with the growth of the online community. For this reason, it is important to point out that misleading information can have a damaging effect on businesses. There is a need for international managers to better understand the impacts of falsehoods and how they can develop management strategies toward addressing misleading information specifically. Businesses need to shield themselves and counter misleading information by debunking it, destroying the channels and content of deliberate misinformation and training managers to correct falsehoods before they spread.
