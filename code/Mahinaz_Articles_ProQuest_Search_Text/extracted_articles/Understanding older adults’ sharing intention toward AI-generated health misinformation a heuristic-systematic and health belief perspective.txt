Introduction The development of the Internet and social media has expanded public access to health information and has embedded health information dissemination more deeply within community interactions and interpersonal sharing processes. Online platforms provide convenient channels for the public to acquire health knowledge, exchange health-related experiences, and obtain disease prevention advice 1 . However, health content that lacks scientific evidence or contains misleading claims may also enter everyday life through platform-based dissemination mechanisms and familiar social networks 2 , gaining greater visibility under specific public health circumstances. For older adults, health information is closely related to disease prevention, daily health management, and everyday decision-making. As older adults increasingly use the Internet and social media 3,4 , they may encounter and further disseminate unverified health content while seeking health information 5, 6–7 .
With the integration of generative artificial intelligence into social media content production, the presentation of health misinformation has further changed. Compared with health misinformation in traditional text, image, or conventional short-video formats, AI-generated content can produce highly realistic, contextualized, and narrative-based videos at relatively low cost. Relevant reports indicate that generative AI has been used to mass-produce health misinformation videos, for example, by synthesizing pseudo-authoritative figures or packaging false health advice as ordinary people’s experiential sharing, thereby creating an appearance of authority, authenticity, and everyday lived experience 8 9 . The risks of such content lie not only in the fact that the health claims themselves may lack scientific evidence, but also in its ability to simultaneously simulate information sources, visual credibility, and seemingly reasonable explanations, allowing false health advice to enter users’ information environments in a more persuasive form. Against this background, older adults, as important recipients and sharers of health information, have become a key audience group that warrants attention in relation to AI-generated health misinformation.
In this communication context, older adults’ judgment of health information becomes particularly salient. Older adults usually have strong health information needs and tend to be sensitive to disease prevention, chronic disease management, and simple, actionable health advice. Within family, friendship, and acquaintance networks, they may also forward health information to others as a way of offering reminders, expressing care, and providing help 10 . Meanwhile, some older adults may face difficulties in digital information identification, medical knowledge evaluation, and platform content assessment. When health misinformation appears in the form of “expert explanation,” “authoritative reminder,” or “personal experience,” and is accompanied by relatively clear explanations and actionable suggestions, older adults’ judgments of its credibility, usefulness, and sharing value may be jointly influenced by source identity, information structure, and health-related psychological judgments.
Existing studies have provided extensive discussions on the evaluation and sharing of health information. Prior research has shown that source expertise and credibility can influence audiences’ acceptance of information, that evidence and reasoning in the message can affect its persuasiveness and practical value, and that individuals’ understanding of health risks and action benefits can further shape health-related behavioral intentions 11, 12–13 . In this sense, health information sharing is not merely an act of forwarding information, but a communication behavior embedded in information judgment, risk understanding, and value evaluation.
However, in health misinformation videos, source cues, message features, visual presentation, and health claims are often not presented separately. Audiences usually encounter an information environment jointly constructed by source identity, content organization, and health advice. Existing research suggests that users’ discernment of health misinformation in short-video environments is shaped by multiple dimensions, including information quality, user characteristics, and external environments, and that multimodal health misinformation often constructs credibility through the intersection of authority signals, presenter attributes, narrative strategies, and engagement cues 14, 15–16 . In AI-generated health misinformation videos, this configuration of cues may become more concentrated, as fabricated source identities, realistic visual appearances, and seemingly coherent health explanations can be presented simultaneously within the same video. Therefore, it is necessary to further examine how these informational features are associated with older adults’ health-related judgments, information value judgments, and sharing intentions.
In addition, older adults’ health information sharing also has relational and social characteristics. Particularly in the Chinese social context, health information often circulates within family, friendship, and acquaintance networks. In these everyday communication settings, forwarding health-related content may be associated with multiple considerations, including the perceived value of the information, concern for people in one’s social circle, and the intention to provide timely reminders or practical advice 14,15,17 . Therefore, older adults’ sharing intention toward health information should be examined not only in relation to their evaluations of the information content, but also in relation to the motivational conditions that may connect these evaluations with sharing. Accordingly, the present study also explores whether context-specific altruistic sharing motivation may condition the relationships between perceived threat, perceived benefit, and sharing intention.
Based on the above discussion, this study focuses on older adults’ evaluations of and sharing intentions toward AI-generated health misinformation videos. Drawing on the Heuristic–Systematic Model and the Health Belief Model as theoretical references, this study analyzes the issue from three perspectives: source cues, message argumentation, and health-related psychological judgments. Specifically, this study employs a 2 (AI-fabricated source: pseudo-layperson source vs. pseudo-authoritative source) × 2 (argument strength: weak vs. strong) between-subjects experimental design to examine how AI-fabricated source and argument strength are associated with older adults’ sharing intention. It further examines the indirect associations of these two experimental factors with sharing intention through perceived threat and perceived benefit, while also considering whether context-specific altruistic sharing motivation may shape the relationships between these health-related evaluations and sharing intention. By bringing AI-generated content features and health-related evaluations into a common analytical framework, this study seeks to provide empirical evidence for understanding the dissemination risks of AI-generated health misinformation among older adults and to inform health misinformation governance and media literacy interventions targeting this population.
Literature review AI-generated health misinformation on social media Health misinformation refers to health-related claims that are based on anecdotal evidence or are false or misleading due to a lack of reliable scientific evidence or deviation from established medical consensus 18 . In the social media environment, health misinformation may gain dissemination advantages because it is often easy to produce, emotionally engaging, and highly shareable. Such content is often packaged as understandable, actionable, and easily shareable “everyday advice,” enabling it to spread rapidly through the combined effects of algorithmic recommendation and interpersonal networks. Prior research has demonstrated that false information can spread more rapidly, broadly, and deeply than true information on social media platforms 19 .
With the maturation of deepfake technologies and the widespread adoption of generative artificial intelligence, individuals and organizations are now able to produce and disseminate misinformation at scale and at low cost using a wide range of tools, platforms, and technical resources 20,21 . AI-generated health misinformation, including videos and images, has become increasingly visible on social media. Through highly realistic visual rendering and synthetic voice technologies, such content creates compelling visual presentations and situational contexts that enhance its deceptive potential and perceived credibility 22 . Particularly on short-video platforms, AIGC can simultaneously generate scripts, voiceovers, lip synchronization, and visual scenes, resulting in messages that appear more authentic in terms of visual realism, contextual completeness, and narrative coherence. As a result, audiences are more likely to interpret such content as actionable health advice or credible risk information 23 .
Older adults tend to be more sensitive to health risks and have a stronger demand for everyday disease prevention and health maintenance information. Prior research suggests that older users may face particular risks in misinformation sharing in certain platform contexts 6 . Research examining how older adults process and respond to online information and misinformation further indicates that their credibility judgments are shaped jointly by digital skills, prior experiences, and situational cues 24 . Therefore, in the context of generative AI, it is necessary to further clarify under what communication conditions and cue configurations AI-generated health misinformation is more likely to shape older adults’ health judgments and sharing intentions, and through which key psychological pathways these associations may occur.
Heuristic-Systematic Model (HSM) The heuristic–systematic model (HSM) is a dual-process framework that integrates both systematic and heuristic modes of information processing, providing a comprehensive explanation of the complex psychological and behavioral processes underlying individuals’ responses to persuasive messages 25 .
Heuristic cues refer to contextual signals that are not directly related to the message content itself but enable individuals to make rapid judgments. These cues function as cognitive shortcuts that facilitate decision-making without requiring detailed evaluation of the information. For example, individuals may assume that widely shared opinions are accurate or rely on expert authority as a basis for judgment 26 . In contrast, message-related factors, such as argument quality, logical coherence, and evidential strength, are commonly treated as cues associated with more content-oriented evaluation. This mode of processing requires individuals to engage in thorough analysis and evaluation of message quality, logical coherence, and evidential strength, and therefore involves greater cognitive effort 27 .
HSM posits that heuristic and systematic processing are not mutually exclusive but can operate simultaneously 26 . In the context of evaluating health information, individuals’ judgments regarding the effectiveness of health advice are influenced both by external cues, such as source characteristics and presentation features, and by internal message factors, including argument quality and informational content. These two types of cues do not function independently but jointly shape message evaluation and persuasion outcomes 28 . Accordingly, incorporating both heuristic and systematic cues in the study of health information on social media allows for a more comprehensive understanding of how individuals evaluate health messages and form subsequent behavioral intentions. Owing to its applicability across different information-processing contexts, HSM has been widely applied in diverse research domains, including information technology, information management, risk communication, and consumer behavior 29 .
Guided by the HSM perspective, the present study conceptualizes AI-fabricated source as a source-related cue and argument strength as a message-related cue in AI-generated health misinformation videos. This distinction provides a theoretical basis for examining how fabricated source presentation and seemingly coherent message argumentation are associated with older adults’ evaluations of AI-generated health misinformation and their subsequent sharing intention.
Health Belief Model (HBM) The Health Belief Model (HBM), originally proposed by Rosenstock 30 , has been widely used to explain why individuals engage in disease prevention, screening, and health management behaviors 31 . The model emphasizes that health-related behaviors are shaped not only by objective medical knowledge, but also by individuals’ subjective health beliefs. According to this model, individuals’ perceptions of health risks, evaluations of the benefits of action, and assessments of potential barriers may influence whether they undertake such actions 11 . HBM therefore provides an important theoretical perspective for understanding how individuals form health-related judgments based on perceived health risks and perceived benefits of action.
In the present study, HBM is used to understand older adults’ cognitive evaluations of the health risks and potential value presented in AI-generated health misinformation. Although sharing health misinformation is a communication behavior, the content being shared often concerns disease risks, health consequences, preventive advice, or lifestyle adjustments. Thus, individuals’ willingness to share such information may depend not only on their evaluation of the information source and message presentation, but also on how they understand the health threats and potential benefits embedded in the information. On this basis, the present study draws on the core constructs of HBM to examine the health-related cognitive evaluations that may precede sharing intention.
Specifically, perceived threat is used to describe older adults’ subjective judgment of the risks presented in health information. In the classical HBM, perceived threat is commonly understood as consisting of perceived susceptibility and perceived severity. Perceived susceptibility refers to individuals’ judgment of the likelihood that they may experience a particular health risk, whereas perceived severity refers to individuals’ judgment of the seriousness of the health consequences associated with that risk 31 . In the context of this study, perceived threat mainly refers to whether older adults perceive the health issue presented in the video as serious, relevant, and worthy of attention. When AI-generated health misinformation describes a health issue as common, hidden, or likely to have serious consequences, older adults may develop stronger perceptions of risk.
Perceived benefit is used to describe older adults’ evaluation of the expected health-related usefulness and instrumental value of the information and the recommended action. In the classical HBM, perceived benefit emphasizes individuals’ assessment of the positive outcomes that may result from taking a health-related action 11,30 . In the present study, perceived benefit refers to the extent to which older adults perceive the health information as useful for their own health, practically valuable, potentially helpful in preventing or improving health problems, and relevant to daily health management. Thus, perceived benefit captures individuals’ evaluation of the personal and instrumental value of the health information and its recommended action.
Introducing HBM into the study of AI-generated health misinformation sharing helps explain how sharing intention in health communication may be shaped by health-related evaluations. When older adults form sharing intentions, they may not only judge whether the information is credible or persuasive, but also evaluate whether the health issue involved is important and whether the information has practical value. Perceived threat and perceived benefit can therefore be used to capture individuals’ evaluations of health risk and the expected usefulness of the recommended health action, respectively.
On this basis, the Heuristic–Systematic Model (HSM) and HBM serve different but related theoretical functions in this study. HSM provides a theoretical lens for distinguishing how source-related cues and message-related cues may be associated with older adults’ evaluations of AI-generated health misinformation. HBM further helps explain how these information cues may be linked to individuals’ judgments of health risks and informational benefits. In this study, sharing intention refers to participants’ general willingness to disseminate the presented health misinformation video. It is conceptually distinct from the specific motives underlying such willingness.
Compared with information adoption or trust, sharing intention is important because it concerns whether health misinformation may enter interpersonal and platform-based circulation. However, sharing intention alone does not reveal whether participants want to share because they regard the information as useful, credible, socially expected, interesting, or helpful to others. Therefore, this study integrates perceived threat and perceived benefit as health-related evaluative variables and introduces context-specific altruistic sharing motivation as a separate moderator. This allows the study to examine whether other-oriented motivation conditions the relationship between health belief evaluations and general sharing intention, without assuming that sharing intention itself is necessarily prosocial.
Research model and hypotheses The effect of AI-fabricated sources on sharing intention Information source refers to the entity that creates, hosts, or disseminates informational content and serves as a critical basis for individuals’ perception and evaluation of that content 32 . According to source credibility theory, when individuals perceive a source as possessing high levels of expertise and trustworthiness, they are more likely to accept its informational claims 33,34 and subsequently develop more favorable attitudes and behavioral intentions 35 . In the context of health communication, health misinformation often enhances its persuasive power by disguising accounts as medical experts, mainstream media outlets, or professional research institutions, or by fabricating seemingly authoritative sources, thereby leveraging authority cues to attract users’ attention 36,37 .
Authority cues may function as heuristic cues by encouraging individuals to rely on source-related shortcuts during judgment 38 . Individuals tend to be less vigilant toward familiar and authoritative sources and are less likely to engage in effortful evaluation of the content disseminated by such sources 39 . Accordingly, when an expert serves as the information source, audiences are likely to rely on the authority heuristic 38 . Furthermore, Wang et al., 17 found that authority orientation significantly influences older adults’ health information sharing behavior in China, such that older adults who trust authoritative sources are more likely to share health information on WeChat.
Therefore , the following hypothesis is proposed :
H1: In AI-generated health misinformation videos , pseudo-authoritative sources will lead to higher sharing intention among older adults than pseudo-layperson sources.
The effect of argument strength on sharing intention Argument strength refers to the quality of the supporting reasons provided within a message, including whether the information contains clear evidence, logical reasoning, and factual support. It is commonly treated as a message-quality cue that may encourage more careful evaluation of message content 40 . Strong arguments typically include specific evidence, clear causal explanations, and logically coherent support for the message claims. In contrast, weak arguments rely more on vague statements, incomplete explanations, or anecdotal descriptions, lacking clear evidentiary support 40 .
As older adults are increasingly exposed to large volumes of online health information, the abundance of content places greater demands on their ability to evaluate information quality. This heightened informational environment requires older adults to engage in more careful selection and evaluation to ensure that the health information they encounter is accurate and useful 10 . Prior research has demonstrated that, compared to messages with weak arguments, those containing strong arguments are more likely to be perceived as credible and persuasive 28 . Arguments supported by high-quality evidence, logical reasoning, and scientific justification are generally regarded as more trustworthy and persuasive 41 .
Therefore, the following hypothesis is proposed:
H2: In AI-generated health misinformation videos , content with strong argument strength , compared to content with weak argument strength , will lead to higher sharing intention among older adults.
The interaction effect of AI-fabricated sources and argument strength on sharing intention In the evaluation of health information, core message features and persuasive cues do not operate independently 28 . The attenuation hypothesis within the HSM provides a useful theoretical explanation for the possible joint influence of these cues. From this perspective, when message content provides clearer and more diagnostic information, the relative influence of source-related cues may become weaker 42,43 . In other words, stronger argumentation may provide audiences with more content-based information for evaluation, thereby reducing the extent to which source authority differentiates sharing intention 43 .
Compared with everyday information, health and medical information is often scientific and specialized, involving biomedical mechanisms and technical terminology that may be difficult for many lay individuals to evaluate directly 44 . Under such conditions, when the message lacks clear and diagnostic explanatory support, audiences may have fewer content-based bases for evaluation, and source authority may become a more salient cue in judging whether the information is credible, useful, or worth sharing 12,45 . This may be particularly relevant for older adults, who may face higher verification costs, information overload, and varying levels of eHealth literacy in online environments 10,46 .
Taken together, in the context of AI-generated health misinformation videos, argument strength may shape the extent to which message content provides a basis for evaluation, thereby conditioning the association between AI-fabricated source and sharing intention. When argument strength is high, the difference in sharing intention between pseudo-authoritative and pseudo-layperson sources may become smaller because the message content itself appears more explanatory and useful. In contrast, when argument strength is low, the pseudo-authoritative source configuration may become more salient and generate higher sharing intention than the pseudo-layperson source configuration.
Therefore, the following hypothesis is proposed:
H3: AI-fabricated source and argument strength will interact to influence older adults’ sharing intention. Specifically , when argument strength is high , the difference in sharing intention between pseudo-layperson and pseudo-authoritative sources will be minimal; when argument strength is low , pseudo-authoritative sources will generate higher sharing intention than pseudo-layperson sources.
The indirect roles of perceived threat and perceived benefit The Health Belief Model (HBM) posits that individuals’ engagement in health-related behaviors is primarily determined by their subjective evaluations of disease threat and the expected benefits of taking action 11,30 . In mediated environments, informational cues influence individuals’ perceptions of risk severity and the effectiveness of recommended actions, thereby shaping their behavioral intentions 11 . Meta-analytic evidence has further demonstrated that perceived threat and perceived benefit are both key predictors of health-related behaviors 47 . Within social media contexts, sharing intention can be understood as a willingness to pass health-related content on to others. Such willingness may be shaped not only by whether the information is accepted as credible, but also by whether it is perceived as relevant, useful, and worthy of attention. When a health message makes a risk appear serious or personally relevant, individuals may be more likely to regard it as information that deserves further attention 10 . Similarly, when the message is perceived as offering practical value or actionable guidance, individuals may be more likely to consider it worth sharing 48 . Therefore, perceived threat and perceived benefit provide important cognitive bases through which information cues may be associated with sharing intention.
In the context of AI-generated health misinformation videos, perceptions of threat and benefit are unlikely to form in isolation but are instead shaped by persuasive cues embedded in the message. Existing health communication research has shown that source expertise and argument quality influence individuals’ evaluations of information quality and risk, which subsequently affect judgments and behavioral intentions 28 . With regard to source cues, pseudo-authoritative source cues may increase the perceived weight of the health risk explanations and action recommendations presented in the video, thereby increasing perceptions of risk severity and urgency while simultaneously strengthening beliefs that the recommended action is worthwhile. This process can elevate both perceived threat and perceived benefit ,13,28 . This mechanism may be particularly salient among older adults, as the high verification costs and complexity of online health information make them more likely to rely on external cues, such as source characteristics and presentation features, for rapid evaluation. Qualitative research on older Chinese social media users further suggests that older adults develop cue-based credibility assessment strategies and are more likely to rely on heuristic processing when making sharing decisions 49 .
With respect to argument strength, stronger arguments typically provide clearer explanatory frameworks and more coherent causal reasoning, making risk claims appear more credible and recommended actions more effective, thereby reinforcing perceptions of both threat and benefit. Research on older adults’ health information sharing has further shown that argument quality and source credibility influence sharing intention through cognitive evaluation variables, such as perceived usefulness 13 . Specifically, when messages provide stronger logical consistency and clearer evidentiary support, older adults may perceive the information as more valuable and therefore report stronger sharing intention.
Based on this reasoning, the following hypotheses are proposed:
H4a: AI-fabricated source is indirectly associated with sharing intention through perceived threat.
H4b: Argument strength is indirectly associated with sharing intention through perceived threat.
H4c: AI-fabricated source is indirectly associated with sharing intention through perceived benefit.
H4d: Argument strength is indirectly associated with sharing intention through perceived benefit.
Exploratory consideration of altruistic sharing motivation The Health Belief Model emphasizes that individuals’ health-related behavioral intentions are closely associated with their subjective judgments of health risks and action benefits. Perceived threat reflects individuals’ judgments of disease risk, serious consequences, and their likelihood of being affected, whereas perceived benefit reflects individuals’ evaluations of the positive outcomes that a health recommendation or action may bring 11 . In the context of health information sharing, these health-related evaluations may provide a cognitive basis for subsequent sharing decisions. Information that signals a relevant health risk or offers apparently useful guidance may therefore be more likely to enter individuals’ consideration of whether to share it.
However, health information sharing differs from self-protective health behaviors in the general sense. Once health information is shared, its potential audience extends beyond the sharer to family members, friends, or other individuals within the sharer’s social relationships. For this reason, willingness to share health information may be linked to other-oriented considerations. However, sharing intention itself should be distinguished from the specific motives underlying sharing. A person may intend to share health information because it appears useful, credible, interesting, socially expected, or helpful to others. Sharing intention therefore captures the likelihood of disseminating information, whereas context-specific altruistic sharing motivation reflects the other-oriented considerations that may underlie such dissemination.
Prior research has identified altruism as a relevant motivational factor in health information dissemination 50,51 . Altruism is generally understood as an other-benefiting behavioral orientation, emphasizing actions based on helping others, promoting others’ welfare, or contributing to society 52 . In health information dissemination, this other-oriented motivation may be reflected in the desire to help others avoid risks, remind people around oneself to pay attention to health issues, or provide potentially useful health advice 53 . For older adults, such motivation may be particularly relevant because health information practices are often embedded in family, friendship, and acquaintance networks.
In the present study, altruism is operationalized as context-specific altruistic sharing motivation within the process of health information sharing, rather than as a general altruistic personality trait. Specifically, this study focuses on whether individuals tend to regard sharing health information as a way to help family members and friends, remind people around them to pay attention to health risks, or provide useful health advice. Compared with general altruistic tendency as a stable personality trait, this understanding is closer to altruistic sharing motivation in the context of health information dissemination. Apuke and Omar’s 50 study on social media health information sharing also suggests that altruistic motivation can function as a cognitive–motivational factor activated in specific communication contexts, shaping individuals’ understanding of and behavioral intention toward health information sharing.
In the process of health information sharing, perceived threat and perceived benefit first reflect individuals’ health-related judgments of the information content. Perceived threat concerns whether the information indicates a health risk that deserves attention, whereas perceived benefit concerns whether the information is perceived as having practical value. For older adults with stronger altruistic sharing motivation, perceived threat may not only indicate a risk that they themselves should notice, but may also be understood as a reason to remind others of the relevant risk. Similarly, perceived benefit may not only mean that the information is useful for themselves, but may also provide a basis for offering practical advice to others. By contrast, when individuals have weaker altruistic sharing motivation, perceived threat and perceived benefit may remain closer to personal information evaluation and may be less likely to develop into other-oriented sharing intention.
Thus, perceived threat and perceived benefit provide a cognitive basis for judging whether health information is worthy of attention and dissemination, while altruistic sharing motivation may influence the connection between this cognitive basis and sharing intention. Based on the above reasoning, the following exploratory hypotheses are proposed:
H5a: Altruistic sharing motivation positively moderates the relationship between perceived threat and sharing intention , such that the positive relationship between perceived threat and sharing intention is stronger among older adults with higher levels of altruistic sharing motivation.
H5b: Altruistic sharing motivation positively moderates the relationship between perceived benefit and sharing intention , such that the positive relationship between perceived benefit and sharing intention is stronger among older adults with higher levels of altruistic sharing motivation.

Methods Overview To test the proposed hypotheses and examine the psychological mechanisms associated with older adults’ sharing intention toward AI-generated health misinformation, this study employed a 2 (AI-fabricated source: pseudo-layperson vs. pseudo-authoritative) × 2 (argument strength: weak vs. strong) between-subjects experimental design. The experiment was conducted through an online survey platform. Participants were randomly assigned to one of the four experimental conditions, viewed an AI-generated health misinformation video, and then completed measures of perceived threat, perceived benefit, altruistic sharing motivation, and sharing intention. By manipulating the AI-fabricated source and argument strength of the videos, this study examined how these informational features were associated with older adults’ sharing intention.
An a priori power analysis was conducted using G*Power 3.1 to determine the minimum required sample size. The results indicated that, with an effect size of f = 0.25, a significance level (α) of 0.05, and statistical power (1-β) of 0.80, the minimum total sample size required for the four experimental conditions was 128, corresponding to at least 32 valid participants per group.
The online experiment was administered using Wenjuanxing (https://www.wjx.cn/). Participants were recruited through a snowball sampling approach on the WeChat platform. To reduce demand characteristics, the recruitment notice did not disclose the full purpose of the study. Data were collected online between January 18 and January 24, 2026. A total of 326 older adults from different regions of China voluntarily participated in the online experiment and completed the experimental questionnaire.
To ensure data quality, an instructional manipulation check (IMC) was included. Participants who failed the IMC were excluded ( N  = 6), resulting in a final sample of N  = 320. The sample was evenly distributed across the four experimental conditions, with 80 participants in each group.
All data were collected anonymously to protect participants’ privacy. Informed consent was obtained before participation, and participants were informed of the anonymous and confidential nature of data collection. Demographic characteristics of the sample are reported in Table 1 ( N  = 320).

Experimental stimulus and procedure Seasonal influenza poses a persistent health threat to older adults. Although public health authorities have long promoted vaccination, older adults are more vulnerable to influenza infection and related complications during peak influenza seasons due to factors such as age-related immune decline. This vulnerability also increases their demand for relevant health information. As social media has become an important channel for obtaining and sharing health information, older adults may be more likely to encounter various types of information related to disease prevention, nutritional supplementation, and everyday self-care in platform environments, including misleading health claims. In the Chinese context, some health misinformation may draw on cultural beliefs such as “food–medicine homology” and older adults’ preference for simple and accessible self-care practices, thereby enhancing its apparent plausibility. A systematic review of health misinformation also showed that topics closely related to older adults commonly include medications, nutrition, cancer, and infectious diseases 54 .
Based on these considerations, this study selected influenza prevention and treatment as the topic of the experimental stimuli. Dandelion is widely recognized among older adults as a traditional herbal remedy, whereas vitamin B is often understood as a representative modern nutritional supplement. The combination of the two constitutes a plausible form of misleading “East–West integrative” health advice and can effectively simulate the type of false health claim that may appear in Chinese social media health communication. Accordingly, this study selected the false claim that “vitamin B combined with dandelion can prevent and treat influenza” as the focal misinformation topic and developed four versions of AI-generated health misinformation videos as experimental stimuli.
The four AI-generated health misinformation video stimuli were each approximately one minute in length and were produced to simulate the presentation format of short videos on Douyin. To ensure experimental control, the overarching health claim, final recommendation, video length, platform-style interface, comment counts, like counts, AI-generated content disclosure cue, publisher ID, and account name were kept as consistent as possible across conditions. The manipulated features varied according to the experimental design and included AI-fabricated source cues and argument strength. During the script development stage, the research team first developed a standardized prompt framework based on the experimental design and used ChatGPT-5.1 to generate the initial textual content. Specifically, around the focal misinformation claim that “vitamin B combined with dandelion can prevent and treat influenza,” ChatGPT was instructed to generate false narratives emphasizing a purported immunological synergistic effect between the two. All generated scripts were subsequently reviewed, revised, and finalized by the research team to ensure consistency with the experimental manipulation goals and to avoid irrelevant content differences across conditions.
During script generation, the research team adopted a standardized prompt framework consisting of “core topic + role specification + key elements + target length” and designed differentiated generation instructions according to the four experimental conditions. Each version was generated in an independent, context-cleared session to prevent prior prompts or outputs from influencing subsequent script generation. The research team then used CapCut, a video editing software developed by ByteDance, together with its built-in AI digital human functionality, to convert the finalized scripts into narrated video stimuli and to operationalize the manipulation of AI-fabricated source. The full Chinese scripts used in the experiment, along with English translations for reference, are provided in Appendix A. The standardized prompt framework and representative prompts used to generate the initial scripts are provided in Appendix B.
For the manipulation of AI-fabricated source, this study jointly operationalized pseudo-authoritative and pseudo-layperson sources through cues such as clothing, background, on-screen title, and vocal style. In the pseudo-authoritative condition, the video featured a middle-aged male AI-generated character wearing a white lab coat, presented against a professional medical laboratory background, and accompanied by a fictitious institutional on-screen title identifying him as the “Director of the China Health Research Center.” In the pseudo-layperson condition, the video featured a middle-aged male AI-generated character wearing casual home attire and situated in a warm and familiar living room setting. To reduce irrelevant differences, the facial appearance of the presenter was kept identical across the two source conditions, whereas clothing, background, and on-screen title information were varied as source cues. These visual and textual cues were designed to jointly serve the experimental manipulation of AI-fabricated source, while other non-manipulated video features were kept as consistent as possible.
In terms of auditory presentation, the pseudo-authoritative character was paired with a calm, formal, and professionally articulated voice, whereas the pseudo-layperson character was paired with a friendly and conversational tone. The four videos were matched in overall visual style, lip-sync accuracy, and image clarity to enhance the internal validity of the experimental manipulation. The pseudo-authoritative and pseudo-layperson character stimuli are shown in Fig. 2.
Because source in short-video environments is often communicated through multiple audiovisual cues rather than through a single textual label, the source manipulation in this study should be understood as a AI-fabricated source cue configuration. Specifically, clothing, background, on-screen title, and vocal style were jointly used to construct pseudo-authoritative and pseudo-layperson identities. Therefore, the observed effects should be interpreted as the effects of a multimodal source cue configuration rather than the isolated effect of any single source cue.

In this study, argument strength was defined as the degree of apparent evidential support and the adequacy of reasoning presented in the message, rather than as a function of evidence type or linguistic style itself. It should be emphasized that, because the experimental materials were health misinformation, strong arguments did not indicate that the message content was supported by genuine scientific evidence. Rather, strong arguments referred to messages that presented a more specific, coherent, and seemingly mechanism-based argumentative structure, thereby providing stronger apparent evidential support for the false health claim. In contrast, weak arguments were characterized by limited evidential support, less adequate reasoning, or more general and incomplete explanations.
In the specific manipulation, the strong argument condition enhanced the apparent diagnosticity of the message content by providing more explicit mechanism-based explanations or outcome-related descriptions. For example, the strong argument version more specifically explained how vitamin B and dandelion were falsely constructed as jointly improving immunity, reducing inflammatory responses, or enhancing protection against influenza. The weak argument condition adopted more general and less elaborated statements, such as merely emphasizing that the two “help improve resistance” or are “good for the body.” To minimize potential confounding, the research team kept the message length, overall structure, final recommendation, and non-manipulated linguistic style as consistent as possible across conditions, so that differences between conditions primarily reflected the degree of evidential support and adequacy of causal reasoning, rather than source credibility cues or other stylistic factors.
The experiment was framed as an online experiment on health information communication. After entering the questionnaire, participants first read the informed consent statement. They were then randomly assigned to one of the four experimental conditions and viewed the corresponding version of the AI-generated health misinformation video. After watching the video, participants completed measures of perceived threat, perceived benefit, altruistic sharing motivation, and sharing intention, followed by data quality control items and demographic questions.
Given that this study involved AI-generated health misinformation, a full debriefing was conducted after participants completed the questionnaire. Specifically, participants were explicitly informed that the claim presented in the video—that taking vitamin B and drinking dandelion-infused water could prevent or treat influenza—was AI-generated health misinformation and lacked support from clinical medical evidence. They were also informed that the characters appearing in the videos were AI-fabricated. The research team further explained that, although vitamin B and dandelion each have certain nutritional or medicinal properties, the specific claim that combining the two could prevent or treat influenza had not been scientifically validated, and blindly following such advice could affect normal nutritional intake or delay appropriate medical treatment.
Participants were then provided with accurate influenza prevention information from authoritative public health agencies, including recommendations such as vaccination, mask-wearing, and timely medical consultation. The research team explicitly reminded participants not to imitate or further share the misinformation presented in the videos and provided them with access to professional medical consultation resources. These procedures were implemented to mitigate potential lingering belief effects caused by the misinformation and to ensure that the study adhered to ethical research standards.
Measures To ensure the appropriateness of the measurement instruments for the present research context, scale items were adapted from established prior studies and contextually modified for the scenario of AI-generated health misinformation videos. Each construct was measured using a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 1 = strongly disagree to 5 = strongly agree. To ensure that participants could accurately understand the questionnaire items, all measurement items for the four latent variables were translated into Chinese and adapted to the context of AI-generated health misinformation videos. A standard translation-back-translation procedure and expert review were conducted, and wording adjustments were further refined through expert review to enhance cultural and contextual appropriateness.
Perceived threat ( Cronbach’s α = 0.769, M = 3.383, SD =0.696) was adapted from established scales developed by Kocoglu-Tanyer et al., 55 , Ng et al., 31 , and Rosenstock 30 . The construct was measured using three items: (1)After watching the video, I believe that influenza may have serious health consequences for older adults like me; (2)After watching the video, I think that the likelihood that I or my family members may contract influenza in the current season or near future is relatively high; (3)Overall, I perceive the influenza risk presented in the video as serious and worthy of attention.
Perceived benefit ( Cronbach’s α = 0.729, M = 3.313, SD =0.655) was also adapted from Kocoglu-Tanyer et al., 55 , Ng et al., 31 , and Rosenstock 30 and measured with four items: (1) I think the health information provided in the video is helpful for my health; (2) I feel that the information conveyed in this health video has practical value and is worth learning about or referring to; (3) If I follow the suggestions in the video, it may help prevent or improve related health problems; (4) Overall, this health video has certain practical significance for daily health management.
Altruistic sharing motivation ( Cronbach’s α = 0.779, M = 3.388, SD =0.873) was adapted from the measurement approach proposed by Liu et al., 56 and contextualized to the health information sharing scenario. In this study, this construct was intended to capture context-specific other-oriented motivation in health information sharing rather than general altruistic disposition. The items focused on whether participants understood sharing health information as a way to help family members or friends, remind people around them of potential health risks, or provide potentially useful health advice. The scale included three items: (1) When I consider sharing health information, I mainly think about whether it may help my family or friends; (2) I am willing to forward this type of health information because I do not want people around me to miss potentially useful disease-prevention methods; (3) Even if it does not directly benefit me personally, I am willing to share this type of information as long as it can remind others to pay attention to health risks.
Sharing intention ( Cronbach’s α = 0.728, M = 3.566, SD =0.613) was measured using four items adapted from Ha and Ahn 57 and Shang et al., 13 : (1) I think this information is worth sharing with others; (2) I will share the information with others; (3) I intend to share the information with others; (4) If I encountered this health video on a social media platform, I would share it. These items assessed participants’ self-reported intention to share the AI-generated health misinformation video presented in the experiment.
In addition, gender, age, education level, media exposure frequency, and perceived AI generation of the video were measured and included as control variables in the subsequent statistical analyses. An Instructional Manipulation Check (IMC) item was incorporated to assess participants’ attention. The item stated: “To ensure that you are answering carefully, please select ‘Agree’ for this question,” with response options ranging from “strongly agree” to “strongly disagree.” To assess potential nonresponse bias, early and late respondents were compared. No significant differences were observed between early and late respondents on the examined variables, providing no indication of substantial late-response differences 58 .
Results The results are reported in four steps. First, a stimulus material validation pretest was conducted to assess whether the AI-fabricated source and argument strength manipulations produced the expected differences in perceived source characteristics and perceived argument strength. Second, ANCOVA was used to examine the main and interaction effects of AI-fabricated source and argument strength on sharing intention. Third, the measurement model was evaluated to assess the reliability, convergent validity, and discriminant validity of the latent constructs. Finally, PLS-SEM was used to examine the direct and indirect relationships in the proposed research model, together with the exploratory moderating role of altruistic sharing motivation.
The analytical strategy distinguished between two levels of examination. ANCOVA was used to examine the factorial effects of AI-fabricated source and argument strength on sharing intention, including their main effects and interaction effect. PLS-SEM was subsequently used to examine the structural model specified in the proposed research framework. This model included direct paths from AI-fabricated source and argument strength to sharing intention, indirect paths through perceived threat and perceived benefit, and moderation paths involving altruistic sharing motivation. Accordingly, the PLS-SEM analysis examined the general direct and indirect relationships in the model, while also exploring whether altruistic sharing motivation conditioned the relationships between perceived threat, perceived benefit, and sharing intention. The analysis did not estimate conditional indirect effects within each specific source-by-argument combination.
Stimulus validation and manipulation check Before the main experiment, an online stimulus-material validation pretest using an independent sample was conducted from December 10 to December 17, 2025. Participants were recruited through a chain-referral procedure initiated within the researchers’ personal networks. The study invitation and questionnaire link were initially circulated among family members, friends, and acquaintances and were subsequently forwarded to eligible older adults within their social networks. A total of 200 older adults participated in the pretest, and 184 valid responses were retained after data-quality screening, with 46 participants in each of the four experimental conditions. Participants in the pretest did not take part in the main experiment, which was conducted from January 18 to January 24, 2026.
The pretest adopted the same 2 (AI-fabricated source: pseudo-layperson vs. pseudo-authoritative) × 2 (argument strength: weak vs. strong) between-subjects design as the main experiment. As reported in Appendix C, participants reported stronger authoritative-source perceptions in the pseudo-authoritative condition and stronger layperson-source perceptions in the pseudo-layperson condition. In addition, the strong-argument condition received higher ratings for argument sufficiency, clarity, and coherence than the weak-argument condition.
Analysis of main effects and interaction effects Prior to conducting the analysis of covariance (ANCOVA), the assumption of homogeneity of variance for the dependent variable, sharing intention, was tested. Levene’s test indicated that the error variances did not differ significantly across groups, F(3, 316) = 1.220, p = .303, suggesting that the assumption of homogeneity of variance was satisfied and that the data were suitable for subsequent analysis.
To examine the effects of AI-fabricated source and argument strength on sharing intention, a 2 (AI-fabricated source: pseudo-layperson vs. pseudo-authoritative) × 2 (argument strength: weak vs. strong) between-subjects ANCOVA was conducted, with sharing intention as the dependent variable. Gender, age, education, media exposure frequency, and perceived AI generation of the video were included in the ANCOVA model to statistically control for their potential effects (see Table 2).
For sharing intention, the ANCOVA revealed a significant main effect of argument strength, F(1 , 311)  = 158.561, p < .001, η p ² = 0.338. Content with strong argument strength ( M  = 3.907) elicited higher sharing intention than content with weak argument strength ( M  = 3.225). The main effect of AI-fabricated source was also significant, F(1 , 311) = 10.163 , p < .01, η p ² = 0.032, indicating that videos featuring a pseudo-authoritative source ( M  = 3.653) generated higher sharing intention than those featuring a pseudo-layperson source ( M  = 3.479). The significant main effect of argument strength supported H2, whereas the significant main effect of AI-fabricated source was consistent with H1 but required further interpretation in light of the interaction effect reported below.
In addition, a significant interaction effect between AI-fabricated source and argument strength was observed (see Table 2), F(1 , 311)  = 30.532, p < .001, η p ² = 0.089, suggesting that the effect of AI-fabricated source on sharing intention varied across levels of argument strength. To further interpret the direction and nature of this interaction, simple effects analyses were conducted.
The results showed that under the weak argument strength condition, the pseudo-authoritative source ( M  = 3.462) elicited higher sharing intention than the pseudo-layperson source ( M  = 2.987). In contrast, under the strong argument strength condition, sharing intention was slightly higher in the pseudo-layperson condition ( M  = 3.971) than in the pseudo-authoritative condition ( M  = 3.843), but this difference was not statistically significant. These findings indicate that the advantage of pseudo-authoritative sources mainly emerged when argument strength was weak. When argument strength was strong, the difference between pseudo-authoritative and pseudo-layperson sources became minimal. Taken together, the significant overall source effect was consistent with H1, but its direction and magnitude depended on argument strength. H1 therefore received qualified support. The observed interaction pattern was consistent with H3 (see Fig. 3).

Measurement model Before examining the structural relationships, the reflective measurement model was assessed for indicator reliability, internal consistency reliability, convergent validity, and discriminant validity. As shown in Table 4, all indicator loadings exceeded 0.70. Both composite reliability and Cronbach’s α values were above 0.70, demonstrating satisfactory internal consistency and reliability of the measures 59 . In addition, the average variance extracted (AVE) values for all constructs were greater than 0.50, indicating that each construct explained more than half of the variance of its indicators and providing evidence of adequate convergent validity 59,60 .
As shown in Table 6, the Fornell–Larcker criterion indicates adequate discriminant validity, as the square root of the AVE for each construct (diagonal elements) exceeds the corresponding inter-construct correlations (off-diagonal elements) 60 . In addition, discriminant validity was further assessed using the heterotrait–monotrait ratio (HTMT) of correlations. Discriminant validity is supported when HTMT values do not exceed the recommended threshold of 0.85 61 .
Bootstrapping was employed to obtain empirical HTMT confidence intervals. A total of 5,000 bootstrap samples were generated using the complete bootstrapping procedure 59 , and 95% confidence intervals were examined. To establish discriminant validity, the HTMT confidence intervals should not include the value of 1. As shown in Table 5, the HTMT values (reported outside the parentheses) are all below the maximum acceptable threshold, with the highest HTMT value being 0.783 61 . The corresponding HTMT confidence intervals (reported in parentheses) do not include the value of 1. If a confidence interval includes 1, this would indicate insufficient discriminant validity 61 . Overall, the HTMT results provide strong evidence for the discriminant validity of the measurement model in this study (see Tables 5 and 6).

Structural model The PLS-SEM analysis was conducted using SmartPLS 4.1. Perceived threat, perceived benefit, altruistic sharing motivation, and sharing intention were specified as reflective constructs, while AI-fabricated source and argument strength were dummy coded and entered as exogenous predictors. The structural model included direct paths from AI-fabricated source and argument strength to sharing intention, paths from AI-fabricated source and argument strength to perceived threat and perceived benefit, and paths from perceived threat and perceived benefit to sharing intention. In addition, interaction terms between altruistic sharing motivation and perceived threat and between altruistic sharing motivation and perceived benefit were included to examine the exploratory moderating effects.
Control variables were included in the structural model, and the reported path coefficients for the hypothesized relationships were estimated after accounting for these variables. Before examining the structural paths, inner variance inflation factor (VIF) values were assessed to evaluate collinearity among the predictors in the structural model. The results showed that all inner VIF values ranged from 1.000 to 1.917. Specifically, in the model predicting sharing intention, the VIF values for AI-fabricated source, argument strength, altruistic sharing motivation, perceived threat, perceived benefit, altruistic sharing motivation × perceived threat, and altruistic sharing motivation × perceived benefit were 1.125, 1.416, 1.228, 1.598, 1.917, 1.648, and 1.707, respectively. All values were below the more conservative threshold of 3.0, indicating that multicollinearity among the predictors was not a substantial concern in the structural model 62 . Therefore, the estimated structural path coefficients were unlikely to have been materially affected by severe multicollinearity. Model fit was evaluated using the standardized root-mean-square residual (SRMR). An SRMR value below 0.08 is considered acceptable 61 . The SRMR value of the present model was 0.067, indicating satisfactory model fit and supporting further analysis. Bootstrapping was performed with 5000 subsamples. The estimated path coefficients and hypothesis-testing results are presented in Table 7; Fig. 4.
First, regarding the direct effects, AI-fabricated source did not significantly predict sharing intention ( β  = 0.127, p > .05, f² = 0.006), whereas argument strength had a significant positive effect on sharing intention ( β  = 0.850, p < .001, f² = 0.208). The non-significant direct effect of AI-fabricated source should be interpreted together with the ANCOVA and indirect-effect results. Whereas the significant main effect identified in the ANCOVA reflects the overall difference in sharing intention across source conditions, the PLS-SEM direct path represents the remaining association after perceived threat, perceived benefit, and the other predictors were taken into account. Thus, the direct association between AI-fabricated source and sharing intention was attenuated in the full structural model, while its indirect effects through perceived threat and perceived benefit remained significant.
Argument strength was dummy-coded as 0 and 1, representing the weak- and strong-argument conditions, respectively. For binary predictors, SmartPLS 4 retains the original 0/1 coding; accordingly, the estimated path coefficient reflects the change in the endogenous variable, expressed in standard-deviation units, associated with moving from the reference category to the focal category. Thus, β  = 0.850 indicates that, after accounting for the other predictors in the structural model, moving from the weak-to the strong-argument condition was associated with an approximately 0.85-standard-deviation increase in sharing intention. Importantly, this magnitude is consistent with the observed experimental data. Sharing intention increased from 3.225 in the weak-argument condition to 3.907 in the strong-argument condition, a raw difference of 0.682, which corresponds to approximately 1.11 times the overall standard deviation of sharing intention ( SD = 0.613). Moreover, the inner VIF for argument strength in predicting sharing intention was only 1.416. Taken together, the observed between-condition difference and the low collinearity provide support for the plausibility of the relatively large path coefficient.
Second, AI-fabricated source had significant positive effects on both perceived threat ( β  = 0.396, p < .001, f² = 0.048) and perceived benefit ( β  = 0.495, p < .001, f² = 0.085). Argument strength also significantly predicted perceived threat ( β  = 0.769, p < .001, f² = 0.181) and perceived benefit ( β  = 0.936, p < .001, f² = 0.303). Furthermore, both perceived threat ( β  = 0.137, p < .05, f² = 0.019) and perceived benefit ( β  = 0.152, p < .05, f² = 0.020) positively predicted sharing intention.
Regarding the explained variance of the endogenous constructs, R² values of approximately 0.670 are considered substantial, values around 0.333 indicate moderate explanatory power, and values around 0.190 indicate weak explanatory power. In the present model, AI-fabricated source and argument strength jointly explained approximately 18.7% of the variance in perceived threat and 27.9% of the variance in perceived benefit. Moreover, the model accounted for 38.9% of the variance in sharing intention. Overall, the explanatory power of the model can be considered acceptable.
In addition to examining the R² values of all endogenous constructs, the model was further evaluated using effect size ( f² ). The f² statistic provides insights into the relative contribution of each construct to the model, thereby complementing the overall statistical significance of the relationships 59 . According to Cohen 63 , f² values of 0.02, 0.15, and 0.35 may be interpreted as small, medium, and large effects, respectively, whereas values below 0.02 indicate negligible effects. In the present model, the f² values ranged from 0.006 to 0.303, indicating that the individual predictors differed in the magnitude of their contributions to the explained variance of the endogenous constructs.

Specific indirect effects were further examined to assess the roles of perceived threat and perceived benefit in the relationships between AI-fabricated source, argument strength, and sharing intention. The bootstrapping results showed that the 95% confidence intervals for all four specific indirect effects did not include zero, indicating significant indirect associations between the experimental variables and sharing intention through perceived threat and perceived benefit.
Specifically, AI-fabricated source was indirectly associated with sharing intention through perceived threat (β = 0.054, p < .05, 95% CI [0.009, 0.114]) and perceived benefit (β = 0.075, p < .05, 95% CI [0.011, 0.158]). Considering the non-significant direct path from AI-fabricated source to sharing intention (β = 0.127, p > .05), these findings suggest that the association between AI-fabricated source and sharing intention was mainly reflected through perceived threat and perceived benefit. In other words, the pseudo-authoritative source was not directly associated with sharing intention after perceived threat and perceived benefit were included; rather, it was associated with sharing intention through higher subjective evaluations of health risk and informational value (see Table 8).
Argument strength was also indirectly associated with sharing intention through the two health belief pathways. Specifically, argument strength was indirectly associated with sharing intention through perceived threat (β = 0.106, p < .05, 95% CI [0.021, 0.196]) and perceived benefit (β = 0.143, p < .05, 95% CI [0.022, 0.275]). Meanwhile, the direct effect of argument strength on sharing intention remained significant (β = 0.850, p < .001), indicating that argument strength had both a direct effect on sharing intention and indirect effects through perceived threat and perceived benefit (see Table 8).
It should be noted that the mediators and the dependent variable were measured in the same post-exposure survey. Therefore, these findings should be interpreted as indirect associations consistent with the proposed theoretical model rather than as definitive evidence of causal mediation. Overall, the results supported H4a, H4b, H4c, and H4d.

Conclusion and implications Discussion of the results The ANCOVA results showed that both AI-fabricated source and argument strength were associated with older adults’ sharing intention. Compared with pseudo-layperson sources, pseudo-authoritative sources generated higher sharing intention, suggesting that fabricated authoritative-source cues remain relevant to older adults’ evaluation of AI-generated health misinformation videos. In the context of AI-generated health misinformation videos, pseudo-authoritative source cues may increase the perceived credibility and professional appearance of health claims, especially for older adults in online health information environments who may have limited medical knowledge or less experience in evaluating digital health information. This result is consistent with previous research on health information sharing and source credibility 13,17 . In addition, strong arguments were associated with higher sharing intention than weak arguments. When health information is presented in a clearer, more coherent, and more explanatory manner, older adults in this online experimental context may be more likely to perceive it as having practical value and as worth sharing. This finding is consistent with research on argument quality, which suggests that the clarity, relevance, and evidential support of message content can shape individuals’ evaluation of persuasive information 41 .
The interaction between AI-fabricated source and argument strength further showed that the relationship between AI-fabricated source and sharing intention varied across levels of argument strength. Under weak-argument conditions, pseudo-authoritative sources were associated with higher sharing intention than pseudo-layperson sources. This suggests that when message content lacks sufficient explanation or evidential support, pseudo-authoritative source cues may make otherwise vague or weakly supported health suggestions appear more credible and worthy of consideration, thereby making AI-fabricated source more salient in older adults’ judgments of whether the information is worth sharing. By contrast, under strong-argument conditions, the difference in sharing intention between pseudo-authoritative and pseudo-layperson sources was not statistically pronounced. One possible explanation is that when health information is presented with clearer reasoning, more coherent explanations, and seemingly sufficient support, the message content itself may provide a stronger basis for evaluating its usefulness and sharing value. In this case, the distinction between a pseudo-authoritative source and a pseudo-layperson source becomes less central to reported sharing intention. This pattern is consistent with the attenuation effect described in dual-process theories, whereby the influence of source cues may be weakened when message content provides clearer and more diagnostic information 43 .
Overall, the ANCOVA results indicate that, in AI-generated health misinformation video contexts, fabricated authority-related source cues and seemingly well-supported message content may be associated with older social media users’ willingness to share health information. The potential dissemination risk of such content is therefore related not only to who appears to be speaking, but also to how the information is visually presented, verbally organized, and justified. This finding highlights the need to examine AI-generated health misinformation as a configuration of audiovisual source cues, platform-like presentation, and seemingly coherent health explanations, rather than as a single textual claim or an isolated source cue.
The PLS-SEM results showed that, in the structural model incorporating perceived threat and perceived benefit, the direct effect of source type on sharing intention was not significant, whereas the direct effect of argument strength on sharing intention remained significant. This finding suggests that source type did not directly predict individuals’ sharing intention, but was mainly associated with sharing intention through perceived threat and perceived benefit. By contrast, the relationship between argument strength and sharing intention did not fully depend on health belief variables. Even after perceived threat and perceived benefit were included in the model, the persuasiveness of the message content itself remained significantly associated with sharing intention. These results indicate that source type and argument strength showed different association patterns with sharing intention after perceived threat and perceived benefit were included in the structural model. The analysis of specific indirect effects further supported this interpretation by showing significant indirect associations through perceived threat and perceived benefit. Since the mediators and sharing intention were measured in the same post-exposure questionnaire, these indirect effects should be understood as associations consistent with the proposed theoretical model rather than as definitive evidence of causal mediation.
With regard to source type, pseudo-layperson and pseudo-authoritative sources may shape individuals’ understanding of the nature of health information. Compared with a pseudo-layperson source, a pseudo-authoritative source is more likely to convey cues of professionalism, reliability, and normativity, leading individuals to perceive the relevant health issue as more serious and personally relevant, thereby increasing perceived threat. At the same time, a pseudo-authoritative source may also make individuals more likely to regard the information as having knowledge value, practical guidance, and relevance for health decision-making, thereby enhancing perceived benefit. Therefore, the association between source type and sharing intention lies in the way different source cues shape individuals’ perceptions of health risks and health benefits. A different pattern emerged for argument strength. Stronger arguments usually contain more sufficient evidence, clearer causal explanations, and more explicit behavioral recommendations. As a result, they are more likely to increase individuals’ perceptions of the severity and relevance of the health issue, while also making them more likely to believe that the information has practical value. Thus, argument strength not only reflects the persuasiveness of the message content itself, but also further influences sharing intention through perceived threat and perceived benefit. This suggests that, in the context of health information dissemination, individuals’ willingness to share information is not merely a direct response to the strength of message arguments, but is also shaped by their cognitive evaluation of the health-related meaning conveyed by the information.
In terms of the relative magnitude of the specific indirect effects, the indirect association through perceived benefit was slightly larger than that through perceived threat. This pattern suggests that perceived benefit may be relatively more closely related to older adults’ general sharing intention in the present study. One possible explanation is that perceived benefit is more directly connected to whether a health message is regarded as useful, actionable, or practically valuable. By contrast, perceived threat mainly reflects the perceived seriousness or relevance of the health risk described in the message. Such risk-related evaluation may increase attention to the issue, but it may not by itself provide a sufficiently clear basis for sharing unless the information is also perceived as useful or practically valuable.
The exploratory moderation analysis revealed different patterns for perceived benefit and perceived threat. An interaction was observed between context-specific altruistic sharing motivation and perceived benefit, whereas the corresponding interaction with perceived threat was not statistically significant. This pattern may suggest that the perceived practical value of health information is more readily connected with other-oriented sharing considerations than the perceived seriousness of the health risk itself. Older adults who regard sharing as a way of helping or reminding others may be more inclined to share information they perceive as useful, a possibility consistent with previous research on prosocial motivation and health information sharing 56,64,65 . Given that health information often circulates within family and acquaintance networks, perceived usefulness may serve as one consideration in decisions about whether information is worth passing on to others.
Theoretical implications First, this study introduces AI-generated content as a specific context in research on health information sharing intention, thereby refining the theoretical understanding of its antecedents among older adults. Existing research on health information sharing has comparatively less often examined technologically fabricated health misinformation in experimental settings and has less often examined the psychological evaluations and motivational factors associated with such sharing decisions. Focusing on AI-generated health misinformation videos, this study distinguishes between two types of fabricated sources—pseudo-authoritative and pseudo-layperson—and incorporates argument strength as an additional experimental factor. In doing so, it examines how different informational features may jointly relate to older adults’ sharing intentions. These findings provide insights into the cognitive and social factors associated with sharing intentions among older adults who participate in digital health information environments.
Second, this study applies the principles of the Heuristic–Systematic Model (HSM) and the Health Belief Model (HBM) to the context of generative AI, offering a contextual perspective on how these frameworks may be applied to health misinformation. Whereas much prior work has examined health misinformation in text-based or static image formats, this study focuses on AI-generated health misinformation videos as a specific type of audiovisual stimulus. Notably, the AI-generated characters used in this study share certain communicative features with virtual influencers. While existing research on virtual influencers has primarily centered on branding, marketing, and fan engagement, this study situates them within a health information context, thereby highlighting the scope and potential social-psychological implications of this line of research.
Third, from the perspective of older adults’ digital media participation and social engagement, this study contributes to a better understanding of how digitally active older adults interact with information and others in platform-based communication environments. As population aging continues and more older adults participate in social media-based health communication, their sharing intentions have become a relevant issue for health communication research. The findings of this study provide insight into how older adults in digital health information environments may form sharing tendencies based on informational cues and health beliefs, while the generalizability of these findings to less digitally active older adults should be interpreted with caution.
Practical implications This study offers practical implications for health misinformation governance, the enhancement of older adults’ media literacy, platform content regulation, and intergenerational digital reverse support.
First, the findings suggest that media literacy initiatives for older adults should pay greater attention to the specific features of AI-generated health videos. Compared with text-based misinformation, AI-generated videos may appear more realistic, context-rich, and persuasive because they combine synthetic presenters, voices, visual cues, and platform-style presentation. Therefore, media literacy education should not only teach older adults to verify health information sources, but also help them recognize common indicators of AI-generated content, understand the possible risks of fabricated health videos, and identify commercial or engagement-driven motives embedded in health-related media. Such targeted support may improve older adults’ ability to evaluate AI-generated health information before forming sharing intentions.
Second, platforms should develop more context-sensitive and risk-based governance practices for AI-generated health content. In this study, the experimental stimuli included a platform-style AI-disclosure cue, yet many participants did not report identifying the videos as AI-generated. Since the salience and comprehension of this cue were not experimentally manipulated, this finding should not be interpreted as direct evidence that the label was ineffective. Rather, it suggests that standard AI-disclosure cues may need to be more visible and easier for older adults to understand in health-related short-video contexts. Platforms could use more salient and multimodal disclosure methods, such as prominent visual labels, brief risk explanations, or contextual prompts, especially for high-stakes domains such as health and finance.
In addition to disclosure, AI-generated health content should be more closely connected with platform moderation and recommendation systems. Content that has undergone professional verification and is assessed as lower risk could be more clearly differentiated, whereas unverified or potentially misleading content could be accompanied by stronger warnings or reduced distribution. Such measures may reduce the likelihood that older adults develop sharing intentions based on incomplete or insufficiently evaluated information, while still allowing appropriate uses of AI-generated content in health communication.
Third, intergenerational digital support may provide a complementary approach to helping older adults evaluate AI-generated health information. In the Chinese social context, older adults often encounter and discuss health information within familiar interpersonal networks. Younger family members may have greater familiarity with digital platforms, AI-generated content, and online verification practices, enabling them to provide additional sources, explanations, or technical assistance when older adults assess uncertain health claims.
In practice, younger family members may share authoritative sources, evidence-based explanations, or supplementary information in family communication settings. Such support may be more acceptable when framed as collaborative assistance rather than correction or blame. Communities and platforms could also develop accessible resources for intergenerational learning, such as short educational videos, simplified verification guides, or family-oriented information prompts. These approaches may provide older adults with additional support for evaluating AI-generated health information in everyday communication contexts.
Limitations and future research Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, although the use of AI-generated videos enhanced the ecological validity of the experimental stimuli, the controlled experimental setting cannot fully capture how older adults encounter, interpret, and share health information on real social media platforms. In everyday platform environments, information exposure and sharing are shaped by algorithmic recommendations, social relationships, repeated encounters, and interactional contexts. Moreover, this study focused on sharing intention rather than actual sharing behavior. Future research could integrate platform behavioral data, field experiments, or longitudinal designs to examine whether sharing intentions translate into actual sharing behavior and how AI-generated health misinformation diffuses within older adults’ social networks.
Second, the representativeness of the sample is limited. Participants were recruited through an online snowball sampling procedure via WeChat, which enabled efficient access to older adults but may also have introduced sampling bias. The participants in this study were likely to have relatively better digital access, higher levels of online participation, and greater familiarity with social media than many older adults in the broader population. Therefore, the findings may not be directly generalizable to rural older adults, older adults with lower educational attainment, or those with limited digital literacy. Future studies should combine online and offline recruitment strategies and include more diverse samples to examine whether the relationships observed in this study remain stable across different groups of older adults.
Third, the experimental design has certain constraints. This study compared different forms of AI-generated health misinformation by manipulating AI-fabricated source and argument strength, but it did not include a human-generated health video as a comparison condition. Therefore, the findings cannot determine whether AI-generated health misinformation is more persuasive, more credible, or more likely to be shared than comparable human-generated misinformation. Future research could include human-generated comparison conditions to examine whether AI-generated modality, synthetic presenters, or AI-related disclosure cues produce effects beyond those of similar human-generated content.
Fourth, the exploratory moderation analyses involving altruistic sharing motivation should be interpreted cautiously. The construct was measured within the same health information sharing context as the dependent variable, and several items explicitly referred to sharing or forwarding information in order to help family members or friends. Although the discriminant validity results indicated that altruistic sharing motivation and sharing intention were empirically distinguishable, statistical discriminant validity does not fully eliminate concerns about their conceptual proximity. Moreover, the observed interaction between altruistic sharing motivation and perceived benefit was small and close to the conventional significance threshold, whereas the interaction involving perceived threat was not statistically significant. These exploratory findings should therefore be interpreted as preliminary evidence that requires further replication rather than as definitive evidence of a moderating effect. Future research should replicate these relationships using general measures of altruistic orientation, prosocial values, or empathic concern, as well as experimentally manipulated other-oriented goals that are measured independently of the specific sharing outcome. In addition, this study discussed the Chinese cultural context, traditional health beliefs, and relational motivations for sharing primarily through theoretical reasoning and contextual interpretation. Future studies could directly measure factors such as traditional health beliefs, authority orientation, family responsibility, and relational norms to examine how these cultural factors shape older adults’ responses to AI-generated health misinformation.

Research team review and finalization After the initial scripts were generated, the research team reviewed and revised the outputs according to the experimental design. The revision process focused on four aspects. First, the core false health claim and final recommendation were kept consistent across the four conditions. Second, the source cues were adjusted to clearly distinguish the pseudo-layperson and pseudo-authoritative conditions. Third, the degree of apparent evidential support and causal reasoning was adjusted to distinguish weak and strong argument strength. Fourth, irrelevant differences in script length, overall structure, and non-manipulated wording style were minimized. The finalized Chinese scripts were the materials actually presented to participants. The English translations are provided solely for reference and were not used in the experiment.
Appendix C. Stimulus Material Validation Study: Pretest Purpose of the Pretest Before the main experiment, an independent stimulus-material validation pretest was conducted from December 10 to December 17, 2025. The main experiment was subsequently conducted from January 18 to January 24, 2026. The purpose of the pretest was to examine whether the four AI-generated health misinformation videos effectively implemented the two core manipulations in the research design, namely AI-fabricated source and argument strength.
Specifically, the pretest had three main purposes. First, it examined whether participants perceived the pseudo-authoritative source condition as having stronger authoritative-source characteristics than the pseudo-layperson source condition. Second, it examined whether participants perceived the strong condition as providing more sufficient, clearer, and more coherent reasoning than the weak condition. Third, it provided a supplementary assessment of relevant perceived characteristics of the experimental materials, including participants’ evaluations of the credibility of the health claim, source credibility, and video presentation quality, in order to further confirm that the four sets of stimuli had clear manipulation distinctions and basic material comparability before being used in the main experiment.
Research Design and Participants The pretest was conducted online from December 10 to December 17, 2025, approximately one month before the main experiment, which took place from January 18 to January 24, 2026. Participants were recruited through a chain-referral procedure initiated through the researchers’ personal networks. The study invitation and questionnaire link were initially circulated among family members, friends, and acquaintances and were subsequently forwarded to older adults within their social networks who met the study’s eligibility criteria. Participation was voluntary, and individuals decided independently whether to take part.
The recruitment notice described the study in general terms as research on the presentation and audience evaluation of health-related short videos. To reduce demand characteristics and avoid influencing participants’ initial evaluations, it did not disclose the specific experimental manipulations or identify the focal health claim as misinformation before participation.
The pretest employed the same 2 (AI-fabricated source: pseudo-layperson vs. pseudo-authoritative) × 2 (argument strength: weak vs. strong) between-subjects design as the main experiment. Participants were randomly assigned to one of the four experimental conditions and viewed the corresponding AI-generated health misinformation video.
A total of 200 older adults participated in the pretest. After excluding participants who failed the attention check, did not complete the questionnaire, or provided responses that did not meet the study’s data-quality criteria, 184 valid responses were retained, with 46 participants in each experimental condition. The pretest sample was independent of the main-experiment sample, and individuals who participated in the pretest were not eligible to participate in the subsequent main experiment, thereby avoiding repeated exposure to the experimental materials.
Pretest Procedure In accordance with the approved study protocol, participants first read the informed consent statement and were told that the study focused on the presentation of health-related short videos on social media and audience evaluations of such content. To avoid influencing participants’ natural judgments of the experimental materials, participants were not informed before the study that the health claim presented in the video was misinformation. They were then randomly assigned to view one of the four video stimuli. After watching the video, participants completed a material evaluation questionnaire, which included measures of perceived authoritative-source characteristics, perceived layperson-source characteristics, perceived argument strength, perceived credibility, source credibility, and video presentation quality. Attention-check items and demographic questions were included at the end of the questionnaire.
Because the pretest video materials involved AI-generated health misinformation, participants received a full debriefing after completing the questionnaire. The research team explicitly informed participants that the claim presented in the video—namely, that “vitamin B combined with dandelion tea can prevent or treat influenza”—was fabricated for research purposes and lacked supporting clinical medical evidence. Participants were also reminded not to believe, imitate, or further disseminate the health advice presented in the video, and they were provided with accurate information about influenza prevention.
Measures All evaluation items in the pretest were measured using a five-point Likert scale, where 1 indicated “strongly disagree” and 5 indicated “strongly agree.”
Perceived authoritative-source characteristics were used to examine whether the pseudo-authoritative source manipulation was effective. The relevant items mainly assessed whether participants perceived the narrator in the video as having a medical or health-related professional background, as resembling a professional institution or researcher, as displaying characteristics of an authoritative health information source, and as possessing strong health knowledge. The scale showed good internal consistency, Cronbach’s α  = 0.793.
Perceived layperson-source characteristics were used to examine whether the pseudo-layperson source manipulation was effective. The relevant items mainly assessed whether participants perceived the narrator in the video as more like an ordinary internet user, as sharing personal life experience, and as closer to a non-professional information sharer. The scale showed good internal consistency, Cronbach’s α = 0.759.
Perceived argument strength was used to examine whether the weak-versus-strong manipulation was effective. The relevant items mainly assessed whether participants perceived the claim in the video as providing sufficient reasons, clear explanations, logical arguments, specific supporting information, and relatively complete causal explanations. The scale showed good internal consistency, Cronbach’s α = 0.883.
Perceived credibility was used to assess participants’ judgments of the credibility of the health claim presented in the video. The relevant items mainly assessed whether participants perceived the health claim in the video as credible, true, accurate, and worth believing. This variable served as a supplementary perceptual indicator in the stimulus material validation study and was used to help assess the basic information evaluations elicited by the materials across different experimental conditions. The scale showed good internal consistency, Cronbach’s α = 0.873.
Source credibility served as an auxiliary validation indicator for the AI-fabricated source manipulation. It was used to assess whether participants perceived the narrator in the video as trustworthy, reliable, a credible information source, and whether the narrator’s claims were worth considering. The scale showed good internal consistency, Cronbach’s α = 0.853.
Video presentation quality was used to examine whether the four sets of video materials were basically comparable in terms of non-manipulated characteristics. The relevant items mainly assessed whether participants perceived the video as resembling health-related short videos on social media platforms, whether the person and mode of expression in the video appeared natural, whether participants could understand the main content of the video, and whether the video length was appropriate for a social media short video. This indicator was not a core variable in the formal theoretical model, but rather a supplementary material evaluation indicator in the stimulus material validation study. It was used to determine whether videos in different experimental conditions differed markedly in platform realism, presentation naturalness, content comprehensibility, and length appropriateness. The indicator showed acceptable internal consistency, Cronbach’s α = 0.776.
Pretest Results This study used 2 × 2 analyses of variance to examine the manipulation effects of the experimental materials, with AI-fabricated source and argument strength as two between-subjects factors.
First, the analysis of variance with perceived authoritative-source characteristics as the dependent variable showed a significant main effect of AI-fabricated source, F (1, 180) = 18.555, p ηp² = 0.093. Participants in the pseudo-authoritative source condition perceived the video narrator as having stronger authoritative-source characteristics, with significantly higher scores than participants in the pseudo-layperson source condition. Specifically, the mean perceived authoritative-source characteristics score in the pseudo-authoritative source condition was M  = 3.385, SD = 0.667, whereas the mean score in the pseudo-layperson source condition was M  = 2.967, SD = 0.649. At the same time, the main effect of argument strength on perceived authoritative-source characteristics was not significant, F (1, 180) = 1.803, p = .181, ηp² = 0.010. The interaction effect between AI-fabricated source and argument strength was also not significant, F (1, 180) = 0.050, p = .823, ηp² = 0.001. These results indicate that participants’ judgments of authoritative-source characteristics were mainly influenced by the AI-fabricated source manipulation rather than by differences in argument strength.
Second, the analysis of variance with perceived layperson-source characteristics as the dependent variable also showed a significant main effect of AI-fabricated source, F (1, 180) = 11.324, p ηp² = 0.059. Participants in the pseudo-layperson source condition ( M  = 3.398, SD = 0.731) were more likely to identify the video narrator as an ordinary internet user or a sharer of life experience, with significantly higher scores than participants in the pseudo-authoritative source condition ( M  = 3.047, SD = 0.680). The main effect of argument strength was not significant, F (1, 180) = 0.753, p = .387, ηp² = 0.004, and the interaction effect between AI-fabricated source and argument strength was also not significant, F (1, 180) = 0.348, p = .556, ηp² = 0.002. These results further indicate that participants were able to distinguish between the pseudo-layperson source and the pseudo-authoritative source at the level of perceived source characteristics, suggesting that the AI-fabricated source manipulation achieved its intended effect.
Third, the analysis of variance with perceived argument strength as the dependent variable showed a significant main effect of argument strength, F (1, 180) = 16.770, p ηp² = 0.085. Participants in the strong condition perceived the video as providing more sufficient, clearer, and more coherent reasoning, and their perceived argument strength scores were significantly higher than those in the weak condition. Specifically, the mean perceived argument strength score in the strong condition was M = 3.302, SD = 0.817, whereas the mean score in the weak condition was M = 2.843, SD = 0.696. At the same time, the main effect of AI-fabricated source on perceived argument strength was not significant, F (1, 180) = 0.461, p = .498, ηp² = 0.003, and the interaction effect between AI-fabricated source and argument strength was also not significant, F (1, 180) = 1.311, p = .254, ηp² = 0.007. These results indicate that the weak and strong materials produced differences in perceived argument strength at the participant perception level, and that these differences were not caused by the AI-fabricated source condition.
Furthermore, this study analyzed perceived credibility to understand participants’ evaluations of the credibility of the health claim in the video across different experimental conditions. The results showed that the effect of argument strength on perceived credibility was not significant, F (1, 180) = 0.330, p = .566, ηp² = 0.002. Participants in the strong condition did not perceive the health claim as significantly more credible than those in the weak condition. At the same time, the correlation between perceived argument strength and perceived credibility was r = .28, indicating that the two were related to some extent but did not completely overlap. Discriminant validity tests further showed that perceived argument strength and perceived credibility could be distinguished as two different perceptual dimensions. These results indicate that the weak and strong materials mainly produced the expected differences in terms of reason sufficiency, explanation clarity, and argument coherence, without simultaneously producing significant differences in the perceived credibility of the health claim.
Subsequently, the analysis with source credibility as the dependent variable showed a significant main effect of AI-fabricated source, F (1, 180) = 8.863, p = .003, ηp² = 0.047. Participants in the pseudo-authoritative source condition perceived the video narrator as more credible and reliable, with significantly higher source credibility scores than participants in the pseudo-layperson source condition. This result indicates that the pseudo-authoritative source condition, as a composite AI-fabricated source cue, effectively increased participants’ perceptions of source credibility. It should be noted that the pseudo-authoritative source condition simultaneously included visual cues, textual cues, and vocal cues, such as a white lab coat, professional title, medical setting, and formal speaking style. Therefore, the pretest validated the overall effect of this composite AI-fabricated source cue and was not intended to identify the independent effect of any specific authority cue.
Finally, the analysis of variance with video presentation quality as the dependent variable showed no significant differences across the four experimental conditions, Fs   ps > 0.222. Specifically, participants generally perceived the four videos as relatively easy to understand, as resembling health-related short videos on social media platforms in their mode of presentation, and as having a length generally appropriate for use as experimental stimuli. These results indicate that this study did not find systematic differences across the four videos in non-manipulated characteristics such as platform realism, presentation naturalness, content comprehensibility, and length appropriateness. Therefore, differences between conditions in the main experiment are unlikely to be primarily attributable to video naturalness, comprehension difficulty, or presentation quality.
Conclusion of the Pretest Overall, the pretest results support the use of the four AI-generated health misinformation videos as stimulus materials in the main experiment. The pseudo-authoritative source condition was perceived by participants as having stronger authoritative-source characteristics than the pseudo-layperson source condition, whereas the pseudo-layperson source condition was perceived as having stronger layperson-source characteristics. These results indicate that the AI-fabricated source manipulation achieved its intended effect. The strong condition was perceived by participants as providing more sufficient, clearer, and more coherent reasoning than the weak condition, indicating that the argument strength manipulation also achieved its intended effect.
At the same time, the supplementary test of perceived credibility showed that the weak and strong conditions did not produce significant differences in participants’ evaluations of the credibility of the health claim. Although perceived argument strength and perceived credibility were related to some extent, they did not completely overlap. This suggests that the argument strength manipulation in this study mainly reflected differences in surface argument quality, rather than a simultaneous increase in the perceived credibility of the false health claim. In addition, the four videos did not differ significantly in video presentation quality, indicating that videos across different experimental conditions were basically comparable in terms of non-manipulated characteristics, including platform realism, presentation naturalness, content comprehensibility, and length appropriateness.
Therefore, the pretest results indicate that the stimulus materials used in this study were able to produce the expected differences in AI-fabricated source and argument strength at the participant perception level. It should be noted that the pseudo-authoritative source condition consisted of multiple cues, including visual, textual, contextual, and vocal cues, rather than representing an isolated manipulation of a single authority cue. The main experiment therefore examines the effects of AI-fabricated source and surface argument strength on older adults’ intention to share health misinformation.
