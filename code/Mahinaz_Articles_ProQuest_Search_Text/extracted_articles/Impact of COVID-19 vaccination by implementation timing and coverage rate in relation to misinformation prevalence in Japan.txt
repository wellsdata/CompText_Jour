1 Introduction COVID-19 was first reported in December 2019 in China and has spread all over the world. In Japan, 3468, 14,917, and 39,060 individuals died of the disease in 2020, 2021, and 2022, respectively [ 1 ]. Non-pharmaceutical interventions, such as mask wearing and physical distancing, were implemented during the pandemic. In addition, effective COVID-19 vaccines have been developed and administered [ 2 ]. Vaccination in Japan was rolled out first for healthcare workers in February 2021, for vulnerable populations including older adults and those with comorbidities in April 2021, and then for all aged 12 years or older in June 2021.
However, how vaccination strategy regarding implementation timing and rollout rate can change the casualties of the pandemic has not been fully explored. If we had started vaccination earlier and covered a higher proportion of the population, the COVID-19 death toll could have been reduced. Conversely, late vaccine introduction or low vaccination coverage would have increased mortality. Our previous study showed a strong association between vaccine hesitancy and beliefs about vaccine-related misinformation, although the prevalence of individuals who believed such misinformation in Japan was as low as ∼8 % [ 3 ]. We do not know how better or worse management of such misinformation would have affected the course of the COVID-19 pandemic.
In this study, we performed counterfactual scenario simulations with mathematical modeling to analyze the number of COVID-19 deaths in Japan that could have been averted by better management of vaccine hesitancy. We also estimated the number of deaths that would have increased if we had failed to deal with the misinformation problem. Furthermore, utilizing the developed model, we investigated how the shift in vaccine implementation timing would have altered the eventual COVID-19 death toll in the country.
2 Material and methods
2.1 Epidemiological data about COVID-19 in Japan The weekly numbers of COVID-19 cases and deaths by 10-year age group were retrieved from the COVerAGE-DB ( https://osf.io/mpwjq). The time-varying proportions of SARS-CoV-2 variants were obtained from CoVariants ( https://covariants.org) (Supplementary Fig. 1).
2.2 Data about COVID-19 vaccination and misinformation beliefs Daily vaccine administration data by 10-year age group in Japan are available in the Vaccination Record System (VRS, https://cio.go.jp/vrs). The number of vaccine administrations was converted to a rate per population in each age group, using data acquired from national government statistics ( https://www.stat.go.jp/data/). The prevalence of people who believed vaccine-related misinformation by vaccination willingness was estimated using data from our previous study of the Japan COVID-19 and Society Internet Survey (JACSIS), an online survey conducted in September and October 2021 for 31,000 participants aged 15 or older from all over Japan [ 3 , 4 ].
2.3 Data of vaccine effectiveness Over 99.9 % of the vaccines used in Japan in 2021 were mRNA-based, BNT162b2 or mRNA-1273 [ 2 ]. The waning efficacies of mRNA vaccines in preventing symptomatic infection and death were estimated with a logistic regression model using data from published studies [ 5–7 ]. We assumed the vaccination showed efficacy two weeks after the second shot of the mRNA vaccines. The vaccine efficacies were calculated for two different age groups (≤59 years old and 60 ≤ years old) and viral variants (pre-Delta variants and the Delta variant), separately (Supplementary Fig. 2).
Vaccine efficacy to prevent transmission was surrogated by its effect on asymptomatic infection, which was reported to be 68.3 % for the pre-Delta variants and 45.9 % for the Delta variant [ 8 , 9 ]. Although time-varying effectiveness values for asymptomatic infection were not reported in those published studies, another study suggested the effect disappeared six months after vaccination [ 10 ]. Therefore, we assumed that the observed efficacies were obtained four weeks after the second dose of mRNA vaccination and decayed to <1 % at six months.
2.4 Mathematical model A mathematical model was developed to predict the number of COVID-19 deaths from the reported case counts, vaccination rates, waning vaccine effectiveness, and SARS-CoV-2 variant proportions. Briefly, a pool of infected individuals received new infections weekly from the observed data. After a certain waiting time and fatality rate, some portions of the infected individuals in the pool were considered dead. The flow was structured for each 10-year age group and viral variant separately. The fatality rate was subject to change depending on age, viral variant, and whether and when the infected individuals were vaccinated. Supplementary Methods describe the details of the model and its algorithm in pseudocode. The parameters of the model, including “case fatality rate,” “time lag from the report of infection to the report of death,” and “increase of case fatality rate from pre-Delta to Delta variant,” were estimated using the maximum likelihood method with the maxLik function in R to fit the observed COVID-19 death counts in 2021, assuming a Poisson distribution (Supplementary Tables). The 95 % prediction intervals for simulated death counts were computed using the Monte Carlo method from 1000 iterations.
To determine the indirect effect of vaccination on COVID-19 transmission, week-by-week reproduction numbers R t were calculated from the ratio of weekly numbers of new cases. In counterfactual scenarios described below, R t was subject to change by coefficients calculated according to the proportion of individuals who had already been infected or vaccinated and the waning immune effect to prevent transmission. The COVID-19 case numbers were regenerated following a Poisson distribution using the computed R t in each counterfactual scenario. Because particular age groups have been reported to contribute to COVID-19 transmission more considerably than other age groups [ 11 , 12 ], we assumed that vaccination coverage in different age groups affects the disease transmission dynamics differently. We referred to the next-generation contact matrix from our previous study, which was constructed to match the age distribution of COVID-19 cases in Japan [ 13 ]. A more detailed methodology for the model can be found in Supplementary Methods.
2.5 Counterfactual scenarios Ten counterfactual scenarios were simulated using the developed mathematical model: 1) “No vaccination.” (2) “Worse management of misinformation,” in which all individuals who believed vaccine-related misinformation would not be vaccinated. 3) “Better management of misinformation,” in which unvaccinated people who believed misinformation would be vaccinated at the same rate as those who did not believe misinformation. 4) “Full vaccination,” in which all eligible individuals, excluding those who cannot be vaccinated for medical reasons, would be vaccinated. 5) “One-month delayed implementation,” 6) “Two-month delayed implementation,” 7) “Three-month delayed implementation,” 8) “One-month early implementation,” 9) “Two-month early implementation,” and 10) “Three-month early implementation.”
2.6 Ethical consideration The protocol for the JACSIS study was reviewed and approved by the Institutional Review Board of the Osaka International Cancer Institute (No. 20084). All study participants agreed that their response data would be used for analysis and publication. The survey data were anonymized before the analyses.
3 Results From 30 December 2020 to 7 December 2021 (pre-Omicron phase), 14,994 COVID-19 deaths were recorded in Japan (Supplementary Fig. 1). The VRS, official data on COVID-19 vaccination from the Japanese government, showed that 83.4 % of eligible people had been vaccinated by the time ( Table 1 ). Using the vaccination rate and other data on COVID-19 epidemiology, a mathematical model predicting COVID-19 death counts from case numbers was developed to fit the number of reported COVID-19 deaths in 2021 ( Fig. 1 and Supplementary Fig. 3. See the Material and Methods for details).
Using this model, we simulated the number of COVID-19 deaths under various vaccination scenarios ( Fig. 2 ). Counterfactual scenarios concerning misinformation management were set up using data from the JACSIS study that had been presented in our previous study [ 3 ]. A reanalysis of the data found that even among people who are not vaccine-hesitant, 8.5 % believed vaccine-related misinformation. If all of those individuals had been unvaccinated (“Worse misinformation management” scenario), vaccination coverage would have dropped from 83.4 % to 76.6 % ( Table 1 ). Conversely, 36.6 % of people who refused to get vaccinated believed misinformation. If they had changed their minds and received vaccination to the same level as those who did not believe misinformation (“Better misinformation management” scenario), the vaccination coverage would have been 88.0 %. We could have increased the coverage to 99.0 % in a scenario where all eligible people who could be vaccinated had actually gotten vaccinated (“Full vaccination” scenario).
Our simulation for the “No vaccination” scenario, considering the effect of vaccination on preventing death, estimated that COVID-19 vaccines had averted 6627 deaths in 2021 ( Fig. 3 A ). As the vaccination coverage increased rapidly around July and August ( Fig. 2 ), the scenario simulation showed a large surge in death counts from August to September in the counterfactual scenario without vaccination. In the “Worse misinformation management” scenario, there would have been 397 more deaths. In contrast, the death toll would have decreased by 100 in the “Better misinformation management” scenario. A total of 281 lives would have been saved in the “Full vaccination” scenario.
We then tested how the timing of vaccine implementation would have affected the COVID-19 death toll using our model ( Fig. 3 B). If we had delayed the implementation of vaccination by one month, two months, or three months, the COVID-19 death tolls would have increased by 1581, 4085, or 5816. On the other hand, one-, two-, and three-month early vaccinations could have saved 977, 2336, and 3889 lives, respectively. Two- and three-month early vaccine implementation would have suppressed the surge in deaths observed between April and June 2021.
Vaccination can prevent not only death but also symptomatic infection [ 14 , 15 ], which means that there would have been more cases than reported COVID-19 cases if no vaccines had been administered ( Fig. 4 ). Considering such averted cases (“Direct effects on death and symp inf” in the figures), the counterfactual scenario simulation without vaccination resulted in 13,833 more fatalities ( Fig. 5 ). Similarly, the influence of vaccination became more prominent when the effect of preventing symptomatic infection by vaccination was additionally considered.
Furthermore, COVID-19 vaccination has been reported to prevent the infection itself, and, therefore, the transmission of the disease, especially before the emergence of the Omicron variant [ 16–18 ]. In other words, vaccination must have changed not only fatalities but also the dynamics of the COVID-19 epidemic waves. Hence, we modeled and simulated the change in case numbers by vaccination in our counterfactual scenarios ( Fig. 4 ), assuming that the effective reproduction number was subject to change by population immunity, especially in the transmission-driving population aged 20s–50s (See the Supplementary Methods for details).
Using this model (“Direct + indirect effects on death, symp inf, and transmission” in the figures), the “No vaccination” scenario showed that the averted death count by vaccination was 30,117 ( Fig. 5 A). If all people who believed misinformation had not gotten vaccinated, COVID-19 mortality would have increased by 1020 ( Fig. 5 B), whereas reducing the influence of vaccine-related misinformation could have decreased the death toll by 431 ( Fig. 5 C). A one-month delay in vaccine implementation would have led to 4796 more deaths, and shifting one month earlier could have saved 2571 lives. Changing the timing shift from one month to three months would have altered the situation more evidently: an additional 22,216 deaths by delayed implementation and 7003 averted deaths by early implementation ( Fig. 5 DE and Fig. 6 ).
4 Discussion In this study, we reported the impact of vaccine implementation timing and coverage on COVID-19 mortality in Japan using theoretical mathematical modeling. In particular, we analyzed how misinformation management could have influenced the death toll, utilizing our survey data of 31,000 participants.
COVID-19 vaccination coverage in Japan is very high, reaching >80 % of eligible people, >90 % of those aged 50 years or older, and > 95 % of those in their 80s or older by the end of 2021 ( Table 1 ). Our previous study showed that 88.9 % of people in Japan did not believe vaccine-related misinformation [ 3 ]. Hence, despite a strong association between misinformation beliefs and vaccine hesitancy, the present study found that simply increasing vaccination coverage by better management of misinformation would only have a marginal impact on COVID-19 mortality.
In contrast, the timing of the vaccination rollout could have a more substantial impact. Considering both the direct effect on illness and fatality and the indirect effect on changing the dynamics of COVID-19 spread by vaccination, our analysis revealed that a three-month delay in COVID-19 vaccination implementation would have resulted in >20,000 more deaths. This estimation is comparable to another published study that analyzed similar counterfactual scenarios in Japan using different approaches [ 19 ]. Nevertheless, the pandemic response in Japan still could have performed better. A three-month earlier vaccine implementation would have almost halved the COVID-19 death toll. In fact, the administration of COVID-19 vaccination in Japan occurred several months later than in some countries, such as the U.S., the U.K., Israel, and Qatar [ 20 ]. At the same time, many other countries started vaccination much later than Japan. We have confronted the dilemma between saving citizens in one's own country and global vaccine equity [ 21 , 22 ].
One of the limitations of the present study is its generalizability, as we used data on vaccine hesitancy and COVID-19 epidemiology only from Japan. In addition, the mathematical model did not consider behavioral changes in counterfactual scenarios. For example, people might have avoided interactions with others more strictly if vaccines had been unavailable in 2021. Collateral mortality due to the COVID-19 pandemic was not considered in the model either, such as those caused by suicide, disrupted health systems, or sequelae of COVID-19 [ 23 , 24 ].
5 Conclusions The study highlights the critical role of the timing of vaccine implementation and the success in limiting the influence of misinformation in the response to the COVID-19 pandemic in Japan, owing to the sufficiently high vaccination coverage. However, we cannot be sure this will be the case in the next pandemic. Our simulations showed that better or worse management of misinformation would have changed the COVID-19 death tolls in Japan in 2021 by approximately 400–1000; these numbers are not negligible. This study underscores the importance of analyzing how misinformation management and vaccination strategies can affect mortality during a pandemic.
