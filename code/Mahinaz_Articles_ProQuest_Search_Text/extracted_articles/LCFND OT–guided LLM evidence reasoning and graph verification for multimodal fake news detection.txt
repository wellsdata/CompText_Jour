Introduction In many multimodal fake news cases, the text and image are not obviously unrelated. Instead, they often describe the same event topic while differing in local factual details such as entities, locations, time, or event attributes. For example, an authentic image may be reused with a changed location, an outdated timestamp, or a modified event description. These cases are difficult because the overall topic remains aligned even though the post is misleading at the factual level, so models that rely mainly on global semantic compatibility can miss the decisive local contradiction. Motivation of the proposed framework. Multimodal posts can remain globally aligned in topic while still containing localized factual contradiction. In such cases, global fusion can preserve high semantic similarity yet miss the decisive token-region inconsistency, which motivates OT-based conflict localization, evidence-grounded LLM reasoning, and graph-based verification

Most existing multimodal fake news detectors learn a fused representation of text and image and predict veracity from global cross-modal compatibility. However, when the two modalities are topically aligned, global fusion can suppress the small but decisive mismatch between a textual claim and a visual region. Recent large-model approaches can produce more fluent explanations, but many still use the language model either as a direct classifier or as a post-hoc rationalizer, so the generated text is not always tightly grounded in the localized conflict that triggered the prediction.
We therefore treat multimodal fake news detection as localized cross-modal conflict verification. LCFND first identifies high-conflict token-region pairs with optimal transport, then converts them into structured evidence through supervised LLM adaptation, and finally verifies the resulting evidence with a heterogeneous evidence graph.
Experiments on Weibo, Twitter, and GossipCop show consistent in-domain gains over matched-backbone baselines. Source-only transfer experiments also improve cross-dataset robustness across six source → target settings. The full online pipeline incurs a latency of 1.47 s per sample with a 7B evidence generator on two L40 GPUs, so the framework is better suited to offline or high-risk verification than to large-scale real-time screening.
Recent advances in multimodal semantic generation and knowledge-enhanced graph reasoning have facilitated more interpretable verification pipelines. Yet most existing approaches still lack dedicated mechanisms for intra-post localized conflict verification—a gap that LCFND is designed to address (Fig. 1).
The contributions of this work are threefold: - We introduce an OT-based conflict localization mechanism that uses cost-weighted transport signals to identify localized cross-modal conflicts between text spans and image regions.

- We design an evidence-grounded LLM supervision strategy in which the LLM is trained to generate structured evidence rather than fake/real labels, using OT-grounded fields with training-split label-informed weak relation calibration.

- We develop a heterogeneous evidence graph with reproducible instantiation rules that integrates OT conflict priors and LLM-generated evidence for final veracity prediction, while making the edge construction and uncertainty handling explicit.


Related work Global consistency vs. local contradiction Multimodal fake-news detection has evolved from global text-image fusion to finer cross-modal interaction. Early methods mainly relied on recurrent, adversarial, or variational fusion to learn joint representations for veracity prediction (Jin et al. 2017; Wang et al. 2018; Khattar et al. 2019). Later work introduced co-attention, hierarchical contextual modeling, ambiguity learning, multi-grained interaction, and feature-enhanced attention to refine cross-modal matching (Wu et al. 2021; Qian et al. 2021; Chen et al. 2022; Zhou et al. 2023; Zheng et al. 2022). General vision-language pretraining encoders further expanded multimodal representation learning (Kim et al. 2021; Girdhar et al. 2023; Li et al. 2023b), whereas knowledge-augmented or retrieval-augmented fake-news detectors incorporate external graphs, retrieved facts, or LVLM reasoning (Hu et al. 2021; Liu et al. 2024). More recent work also explores retrieval-augmented rumor detection, event-driven multi-view learning, attribution-oriented benchmark design, and LVLM-enhanced detection (Hu et al. 2023; Ma et al. 2024; Guo et al. 2025). Recent LVLM-based detectors also move toward explainable multimodal reasoning (Qi et al. 2024), although several of these systems additionally rely on external retrieval rather than intra-post evidence alone. Recent evidence-driven and rationale-guided methods such as MFIR (Wu et al. 2023b), the Unified Evidence Enhancement Inference Framework (Wu et al. 2024a), HCCIN (Wu et al. 2024b), See How You Read? (Wu et al. 2023a), and MMRGV (Chen et al. 2026) further emphasize structured reasoning over flat fusion. CFFN (Li et al. 2023a) is also closely related to our motivation because it models cross-modal consistency and inconsistency through fine-grained word-region interactions. Different from CFFN, which separates high- and low-relevance word-region pairs for consistency learning, LCFND uses optimal transport with a global matching constraint to identify localized conflict pairs, converts them into structured evidence through an evidence-grounded LLM, and verifies the evidence in a heterogeneous graph. Hybrid LLM-enhanced fake-news detectors such as FND-LLM (Wang et al. 2024) are also related to our evidence-generation stage, but LCFND differs by using the LLM to generate localized structured evidence rather than to directly predict the final veracity label. Fine-grained multimodal semantic generation and alignment have also been studied in adjacent multimodal sentiment-analysis tasks. For example, SemCap (Jiang et al. 2026) and multi-level textual-visual alignment models (Li et al. 2024) show that region-level semantic grounding and fine-grained cross-modal alignment can improve multimodal interpretation. Although these studies focus on multimodal aspect-based sentiment analysis rather than factual misinformation verification, they are relevant to our use of region-level captions and token-region alignment. Yet most of these methods still make decisions from overall text-image compatibility. When a post reuses a topically relevant image but changes a local factual detail such as entity, location, or time, global compatibility can remain high even though the post is misleading.
Out-of-context misinformation has been studied more directly in multimodal verification settings. Prior work has examined claim verification over images (Zlatkova et al. 2019), web-retrieved external evidence for social-media rumour verification (Dougrez-Lewis et al. 2022), benchmark construction for out-of-context multimodal media (Luo et al. 2021), and explainable OOC detection with MLLMs plus external evidence (Li et al. 2025). More recent datasets and analyses further highlight unimodal bias and the tendency of current systems to rely on similarity more than factuality (Papadopoulos et al. 2024, 2025). These studies underscore the importance of evidence grounding, but they do not explicitly model token-region conflict localization and within-post evidence verification in the setting considered here. Cross-modal OT has mainly been used as an alignment objective in retrieval, VQA, and image-text matching, where the goal is to maximize correspondence between matched pairs. In contrast, LCFND uses the same transport machinery for conflict localization: the cost-weighted transport signal s ij = P ij ∗ C ij isolates within-post mismatched token-region pairs and forwards them as discrete evidence packets, rather than producing a global alignment score. Graph verification and affective cues Graph-based rumor and fake-news detectors are useful when relational structure carries diagnostic information that flat fusion cannot represent well. Prior work has modeled temporal context, propagation structure, and multi-view relations with graph-based architectures (Bian et al. 2020; Ying et al. 2023). In parallel, affective inconsistency, causal intervention, and debiasing strategies have been explored to reduce spurious multimodal correlations (Zhang et al. 2021; Chen et al. 2023; Zeng et al. 2024). These directions suggest that misinformation is often better characterized by structured dependencies than by isolated features. The setting in this paper differs from propagation-based rumor verification. We focus on contradiction inside a single post after localized conflict cues have already been extracted. The graph therefore serves as an intra-post verifier that evaluates the consistency among claim spans, visual regions, generated evidence nodes, and auxiliary affective cues.
Several knowledge-augmented fake-news detection methods rely on external knowledge graphs, retrieved facts, or entity-centric background knowledge to enrich veracity prediction (Hu et al. 2021; Liu et al. 2024). LCFND instead builds an intra-post evidence graph from localized token-region conflict cues, generated evidence fields, and auxiliary affective signals, without querying external knowledge.
Problem formulation and design rationale Given a multimodal sample x = ( T , I ) , where T is the text and I is the associated image, the task is to learn a classifier f ( x ) → y , y ∈ { 0 , 1 } , where y = 1 denotes fake news and y = 0 denotes real news.
We focus on the challenge of detecting local contradiction when text and image remain globally related but differ in factual details. The model contains three linked stages: OT-based localized cross-modal conflict discovery, evidence-grounded LLM reasoning, and graph-based evidence verification.
We summarize the pipeline with explicit intermediate outputs: E loc = D ( T , I ) , E llm = L ( E loc , T , I ) , E ver = V ( E loc , E llm ) , y = R ( [ E loc ; E llm ; E ver ] ) , where D ( · ) , L ( · ) , V ( · ) , and R ( · ) denote the OT stage, the evidence reasoner, the graph verifier, and final prediction, respectively.
Design rationale The framework follows three constraints. First, local contradiction should be localized before global fusion so that decisive token-region mismatch is not diluted by coarse semantic compatibility. Second, localized conflict should be converted into explicit evidence fields grounded in identifiable spans and regions. Third, generated evidence should be verified structurally before final prediction, because not every local mismatch implies factual contradiction.
Proposed method Overview LCFND processes each sample in three steps. The OT module produces localized conflict priors and evidence packets. The LLM module converts these packets into structured evidence fields. The graph module then evaluates the consistency of the localized cues and generated evidence before final prediction.

Local conflict discovery via optimal transport This module takes token embeddings and visual patch embeddings as input and produces two outputs: an OT conflict representation for classification and a set of localized evidence packets for LLM supervision.
The input text is tokenized by a BERT-base tokenizer, truncated to at most 128 non-special tokens, and encoded into token features. For the image branch, the input image is resized to 224 × 224 and encoded by ResNet-50. The last convolutional feature map is flattened into a 7 × 7 grid, yielding m = 49 visual patches. Both modalities are projected into the same d -dimensional comparison space before OT cost computation. Let { t i } i = 1 n be text token embeddings and { v j } j = 1 49 be image patch embeddings. We L2-normalize both modalities before computing cosine similarity. We define the OT cost matrix as C ij = 1 - cos ( t i , v j ) + λ g + ( t i , v j ) , where g + ( · ) = softplus ( g ( · ) ) is a non-negative learnable discriminative term and λ balances prior similarity with task-specific conflict learning. In implementation, g ( t i , v j ) is a two-layer MLP with input [ t i ; v j ; t i ⊙ v j ; | t i - v j | ] ∈ R 4 d , hidden size 256, GELU activation, dropout 0.1, and scalar output. The softplus transform keeps the OT cost matrix non-negative and improves numerical stability during Sinkhorn optimization.
We use uniform marginals a i = 1 / n and b j = 1 / 49 . The entropy-regularized OT plan is obtained by P ∗ = arg min P ∈ Π ( a , b ) ⟨ P , C ⟩ + ε H ( P ) , where Π ( a , b ) is the transport polytope under marginals a and b . We compute the plan with 30 Sinkhorn iterations. The entropy-regularized formulation follows Sinkhorn distance computation (Cuturi 2013). We refer readers to the computational OT survey for broader background on this formulation (Peyre and Cuturi 2019).
The OT conflict score is defined as d ot = ∑ i = 1 n ∑ j = 1 m P ij ∗ C ij . This score measures localized cross-modal mismatch. Unlike standard attention, OT imposes a global mass-conservation constraint and can provide a more globally constrained alignment for mismatched pairs (Han et al. 2024).
We use balanced OT to obtain a globally constrained token-region alignment plan. Since the column marginals are fixed under balanced OT with uniform visual priors, patch inconsistency cannot be inferred from received transport mass alone. We therefore derive patch conflict intensity from the cost-weighted transport signal rather than from column sums. We use balanced OT instead of unbalanced OT because the target here is globally constrained correspondence redistribution rather than partial matching under missing mass. Let V = [ v 1 , ⋯ , v m ] ⊤ ∈ R m × d , P ∗ ∈ R n × m . We first compute the cost-weighted conflict intensity received by each patch: q j = ∑ i = 1 n P ij ∗ C ij , j = 1 , ⋯ , m . We refer to q j as the patch-level inconsistency score.
We then normalize the conflict intensity to [0, 1] within each sample: q ^ j = q j - min j q j max j q j - min j q j + δ , where δ is a small constant for numerical stability. In implementation, δ = 10 - 8 . We use q ^ j to derive conflict-aware patch decomposition weights: w j inc = q ^ j , w j c = 1 - q ^ j , v i n c , j = w j inc v j , v c , j = w j c v j , V c = [ v c , 1 , ⋯ , v c , m ] ⊤ , V inc = [ v i n c , 1 , ⋯ , v i n c , m ] ⊤ . The corresponding separation regularization is L sep = ‖ V c ⊤ V inc ‖ F 2 . The OT branch outputs a compact evidence vector z ot = ϕ ot ( d ot , H ( P ∗ ) , pool ( C ) , pool ( P ∗ ) , pool ( V c ) , pool ( V inc ) ) , where ϕ ot is an MLP and pool ( · ) denotes channel-wise mean pooling followed by linear projection to the shared hidden size. To prevent global compatibility from masking local contradiction, we derive patch-wise conflict priors from the cost-weighted OT plan. Patches receiving high transport mass under high transport cost are treated as locally inconsistent cues, while patches with low conflict intensity are treated as consistency-dominant evidence. We further derive token-side and patch-side conflict priors by transport-weighted marginalization, c i t = ∑ j = 1 m P ij ∗ C ij , c j v = ∑ i = 1 n P ij ∗ C ij , and normalize them within each sample before injecting them into graph nodes and cross-modal edges. For each selected pair, we construct an OT evidence packet E ot = { text span , visual region , region caption , OCR , conflict score } . These top-conflict pairs are used both as localized evidence inputs and as weak supervision for the subsequent LLM stage. The cost-weighted transport score highlights pairs that retain non-negligible alignment mass while incurring high local cost, which makes them suitable for constructing evidence packets. Evidence-grounded LLM contradiction reasoning Given the OT evidence packets, the LLM module translates localized cross-modal conflicts into structured evidence for downstream verification.
We define the LLM input as X llm = [ T ; E ot ; V ; O ] , where T denotes the original post text, E ot denotes the OT evidence packets, V denotes region-level captions or visual attributes extracted from selected patches, and O denotes OCR tokens recognized from the image when available.
The target output is a structured evidence tuple Y llm = { c , e v , r , τ , ρ } , where c is the event-level claim, e v is the visual evidence, r ∈ { support , contradict , irrelevant , insufficient } is the evidence relation, τ is the contradiction type, and ρ is the grounded rationale. In implementation, the tuple is serialized as a constrained JSON-style template to stabilize decoding and facilitate graph construction. The node and edge construction scheme and the edge-type assignment rules are summarized in Tables 1 and 2, respectively.
We construct the SFT targets automatically from OT-localized evidence and training labels. For each training sample, we first select the sentence or clause that covers the largest amount of top-conflict text spans as the claim candidate c . We then aggregate the corresponding high-conflict visual regions, their region captions, and OCR tokens to form the visual evidence field e v . The evidence relation r is assigned by combining the post-level label with OT conflict confidence: fake samples are mapped to contradict or insufficient depending on whether the evidence packet contains an explicit mismatch anchor, while real samples are mapped to support or irrelevant . The contradiction type τ is obtained with lightweight rules over named entities, time expressions, location mentions, numeric attributes, and event descriptors, yielding categories such as entity , location , time , attribute , and event . Finally, the rationale ρ is deterministically templated from ( c , e v , r , τ ) . Only the coarse relation field uses label-informed weak supervision; all other structured fields are grounded in OT localization, OCR, captions, and deterministic rules. This is not test-time label leakage: validation and test evidence generation never receives the ground-truth veracity label, and inference-time relation decoding uses only the post text, OT packets, region captions, and OCR tokens. Nevertheless, training-time calibration may introduce a label-prior bias, because fake and real posts are associated with different relation polarities during target construction. We therefore treat r as label-informed weak supervision rather than fully label-free evidence generation, and evaluate this dependency in Table 20.
Because weak supervision can be noisy, we assign each structured target a confidence weight w sft = α · s ¯ top + ( 1 - α ) · a rule , where s ¯ top is the average OT conflict score of the selected evidence packet and a rule ∈ [ 0 , 1 ] measures rule agreement among claim span extraction, contradiction-type assignment, and OCR/region-caption consistency. In implementation, α = 0.6 . Low-confidence targets are down-weighted during SFT, which reduces the impact of ambiguous or weakly grounded supervision.
The supervised fine-tuning objective is L sft = - ∑ l = 1 L w sft log p θ ( y l ∣ y < l , X llm ) , where θ = θ 0 + Δ θ LoRA under parameter-efficient adaptation.
We encode the generated evidence fields with a lightweight evidence encoder and obtain the LLM evidence representation z llm = ϕ llm ( c , e v , r , τ , ρ ) , which is passed to the graph verifier and the final prediction layer.
Heterogeneous evidence graph verification For contextual comparisons and robustness analysis, we additionally retain an auxiliary frozen retrieval cache. The cache stores the query string, crawl timestamp, retrieved URL, retrieval rank, publication-time metadata when available, and the extracted snippet or archived page content. We use this cache only as a separate contextual reference setting; it is excluded from the main retrieval-free P1 pipeline and is described apart from the core method. Later webpage updates are ignored once the snapshot is frozen, and all comparisons are run against the same cache rather than against a live search API.
For Chinese Weibo, the retrieval cache uses Chinese queries and Chinese semantic matching; for English datasets, the cache uses English queries and English semantic matching. We do not translate pages across languages when caching retrieval evidence.
We build a heterogeneous evidence graph with semantic-emotional channels and explicit LLM evidence nodes: - Semantic channel. Text-side semantic nodes are extracted automatically as named entities and noun phrases from the post text. For English datasets we use spaCy named-entity recognition and noun-phrase extraction with the en_core_web_sm pipeline, and for Chinese we use HanLP/LTP-based word segmentation, named-entity recognition, and phrase extraction under the same “entity + noun phrase” heuristic. We keep at most N t = 8 text nodes per sample; if more candidates are found, we rank them by phrase-level OT conflict prior and retain the top nodes. Image-side semantic nodes are derived from the 7 × 7 visual grid, ranked by OT patch priors, and truncated to N v = 10 visual region nodes.

- LLM evidence channel. We convert the generated claim, visual evidence, contradiction type, and rationale summary into evidence nodes. Their textual content is encoded by the same evidence encoder used for z llm and projected to the shared hidden size.

- Emotional channel. Text and image are mapped into emotion embeddings. We use a frozen emotion encoder for text and a frozen affective image encoder for vision to obtain low-dimensional valence-arousal embeddings, then project them into the same 256-dimensional hidden space as the semantic nodes. We use two emotion nodes per sample, one for text and one for image.

Formally, the graph is written as G = ( V T , V I , V E , V L , E ) , where V T denotes text claim or phrase nodes, V I denotes visual region nodes, V E denotes emotional nodes, V L denotes LLM evidence nodes, and E contains semantic, OT-conflict, evidence-alignment, and affective relations. Text semantic nodes are fully connected. Visual region nodes are connected by a k -nearest-neighbor graph with k = 3 . For each text node, we connect the top-2 visual nodes with the largest OT-derived conflict priors. We further add claim-region OT edges, claim-evidence edges, visual-evidence-region edges, contradiction-type edges, and emotion-evidence edges. For reproducibility, we assign edge types with explicit rules rather than ad hoc heuristics. Support edges are created when the same evidence pair is simultaneously semantically matched by the evidence encoder and classified as entailment by a lightweight textual-consistency scorer; conflict edges are created when contradiction probability exceeds a validation-selected threshold or when OCR/region metadata are inconsistent with the claim; alignment edges are created when entity overlap and cosine similarity both exceed their respective thresholds; and co-occurrence edges are created deterministically for nodes that share the same post, retrieval context, or visual cluster. Pairs whose support and conflict scores are both weak are marked as uncertain and may still participate in soft message passing, but they are excluded from hard decision thresholds.
For a semantic node u i , the initial representation is h i ( 0 ) = [ e i ; c i ] , where e i is the content embedding and c i is the OT-derived conflict prior. LLM evidence nodes are initialized in the same way, with generated evidence embeddings replacing entity or region features where appropriate. Emotional nodes are initialized analogously with emotion embeddings. For each node, attention coefficients are computed separately for different edge types, and the updated representation is obtained by relation-specific aggregation followed by residual fusion.
Graph verification is performed with relation-aware message passing: H ( ℓ + 1 ) = HGAT ( H ( ℓ ) , A ot , A llm , A emo ) , where HGAT denotes heterogeneous graph attention with OT-conflict adjacency A ot , LLM-evidence adjacency A llm , and emotion-related adjacency A emo . We use two message-passing layers, four attention heads per relation, residual connections, layer normalization, and dropout 0.1. The final graph evidence is z graph = ϕ graph ( readout ( H ( L ) ) ) . where readout ( · ) denotes attention pooling over graph nodes. This stage aggregates OT conflict cues, generated evidence, and affective information into a unified contradiction representation.
We further define a contradiction coherence score s coh = σ ( w coh ⊤ [ z ot ; z llm ; z graph ] + b coh ) , which estimates whether the claim node, visual evidence node, and contradiction-type node remain mutually consistent after message passing. During training, s coh is supervised by the same post label as an auxiliary verifier head. In practice, this score is used for analysis and ablation rather than as a separate decision rule.
Training procedure and objectives The final prediction is obtained by fusing the multi-stage evidence: p ( y | x ) = softmax ( W f [ z ot ; z llm ; z graph ] + b f ) . Because the LLM stage produces discrete structured text, the whole pipeline is not optimized as a single end-to-end differentiable graph. Instead, we train it in three stages.
Stage 1: OT and classifier pretraining We first optimize the text encoder, visual encoder, OT branch, graph verifier without LLM evidence, and the final classifier with L s t a g e 1 = L cls + β 1 L aux + β 3 L sep , where L aux = L ot + L graph , and L ot and L graph are auxiliary binary classification losses applied to OT evidence and graph evidence, respectively. We formally define each loss term below.
L cls is the standard cross-entropy loss over the final veracity prediction: L cls = - 1 N ∑ k = 1 N y k log p ( y k | x k ) + ( 1 - y k ) log ( 1 - p ( y k | x k ) ) , where p ( y k | x k ) = softmax ( W f [ z ot ; z llm ; z graph ] + b f ) is the predicted veracity probability.
L ot is a binary auxiliary loss applied to the OT conflict representation z ot through a linear classifier head W ot : L ot = - 1 N ∑ k = 1 N y k log σ ( W ot ⊤ z ot ( k ) ) + ( 1 - y k ) log ( 1 - σ ( W ot ⊤ z ot ( k ) ) ) , where σ ( · ) is the sigmoid function. This auxiliary loss ensures that the OT branch alone can produce a discriminative conflict representation.
L graph is a binary auxiliary loss applied to the graph evidence representation z graph through a linear classifier head W graph : L graph = - 1 N ∑ k = 1 N [ y k log σ ( W graph ⊤ z graph ( k ) ) + ( 1 - y k ) × log ( 1 - σ ( W graph ⊤ z graph ( k ) ) ) ] . The supervision targets for L ot and L graph are the same post-level veracity label y used for L cls . In Stage 1 pretraining, L ot and L graph each contribute to β 1 L aux , providing gradient signals that encourage the OT and graph modules to produce veracity-discriminative representations before LLM evidence is available. During Stage 3, the OT and graph branches remain fixed as evidence providers, and the verifier is optimized only with the stage-3 fusion objective in (1); the auxiliary branch losses are not used in this stage.
Stage 2: evidence-reasoner SFT We next construct OT-guided structured targets on the training split only and optimize the evidence reasoner with L s t a g e 2 = γ L sft , where L sft is the weighted token-level likelihood defined in Section 4.3.
Stage 3: evidence-aware verifier training During Stage 3 training, we freeze the LoRA-adapted LLM, generate frozen structured evidence offline for the training and validation splits, parse it into graph nodes, and optimize the evidence-aware verifier and final classifier. After model selection, we generate evidence for the test split only for evaluation, without using ground-truth labels. Structured-output fallback The above algorithm describes the single-pass evidence generation and fusion prediction used in our implementation. During validation and testing, the evidence generator is required to emit a valid JSON object under the predefined schema. If the output cannot be parsed after one constrained regeneration attempt, we replace the missing field with an insufficient fallback token and proceed with the OT conflict prior and the graph nodes that can be constructed from OCR, captions, and localized regions. This fallback does not use ground-truth labels. It is triggered only when the structured output violates the schema, and it is therefore a reproducibility safeguard rather than a prediction safeguard.
We partially fine-tune the top four BERT layers and the last ResNet stage, while the remaining backbone layers are frozen.
Experiments Experimental settings Datasets and metrics We evaluate on Weibo, Twitter, and GossipCop. Weibo and Twitter are split by event following standard practice, while GossipCop uses the fixed split files adopted in this work from the FakeNewsNet release (Shu et al. 2020). We keep only samples with both text and image available, remove corrupt image files and exact duplicate image-text pairs, and use only the post text plus the associated image in all experiments, without user metadata, comments, or propagation graphs. We report F1 as the primary metric and additionally provide AUC and Macro-F1 for the imbalanced GossipCop dataset. For Weibo and Twitter, weighted Precision, Recall, and F1 are computed independently by the same class-wise evaluation script. For GossipCop, the fake-class weight 3.16 is computed as the real-to-fake ratio in the training split. The main protocol removes exact duplicates only. To further assess the risk of cross-split visual leakage, we additionally report a perceptual near-duplicate filtering analysis based on image hashing in Table 32. Comparison protocol We separate comparisons into three main protocol families plus one matched-input contextual setting. P1 denotes the controlled matched-backbone protocol: methods are evaluated on the same splits with the same primary text and visual backbones, BERT and ResNet-50, no external retrieval, and the same early-stopping and evaluation scripts. P1 is used for the main matched-backbone ranking. P2 is used only for controlled backbone-robustness analysis, where only the visual backbone is changed to ViT-B/16 or Swin-T while the rest of the comparison setting remains controlled. P3b is used as a matched-input contextual comparison rather than as a main-ranking protocol. P3 results are reported-only contextual references. Unless otherwise stated, main comparison results are averaged over five random seeds { 42 , 123 , 456 , 789 , 1024 } and are reported as mean ± standard deviation. FND-LLM-M and MMRGV-lite use the same splits, Qwen2-7B, OCR/caption inputs, and retrieval-disabled setting as LCFND, but are not intended as faithful full reproductions of the original systems; they are controlled matched-input approximations designed to test whether LCFND’s OT-localized evidence and graph verification add value under the same input budget. Recent LVLM-based systems such as FKA-Owl (Liu et al. 2024) and FND-LLM (Wang et al. 2024) are kept under their reported settings and are shown only in the contextual comparison table (Table 16) rather than in the P1 main-result tables. The goal of the comparison is not to claim universal superiority over all LVLM-based systems under heterogeneous settings, but to isolate the contribution of localized conflict discovery and evidence-graph verification under controlled input and backbone conditions.
Baselines and transfer setting Baselines are grouped into unimodal encoders, early/late fusion models, local interaction models, and recent knowledge-augmented multimodal detectors. The matched-backbone main comparison (P1) includes BERT, ResNet-50, EANN (Wang et al. 2018), MVAE (Khattar et al. 2019), SpotFake+ (Singhal et al. 2020), SAFE (Zhou et al. 2020), MCAN (Wu et al. 2021), HMCAN (Qian et al. 2021), CAFE (Chen et al. 2022), BMR (Ying et al. 2023), and MGCA (Guo et al. 2025). Recent LVLM-based systems such as FKA-Owl (Liu et al. 2024) and FND-LLM (Wang et al. 2024) are shown separately as reported references and are not included in the P1 ranking. For cross-dataset transfer, all methods use a shared multilingual text encoder and ResNet-50 visual encoder. In each source → target direction, training uses only source-train, model selection uses only source-val, and evaluation is conducted directly on target-test without target-domain tuning.
Implementation details Unless otherwise stated, the hidden dimension is 256 for all task-specific modules. We use bert-base-chinese on Weibo and bert-base-unc ased on Twitter and GossipCop, with ResNet-50 as the visual backbone (Devlin et al. 2019; He et al. 2016). Text is truncated to 128 non-special tokens and images are resized to 224 × 224 , after which the last ResNet feature map is flattened into a 7 × 7 patch grid. For Weibo, semantic matching and retrieval-free evidence ranking are performed with a Chinese encoder; for Twitter and GossipCop, we use a standard English encoder. We do not translate the original text for ranking or graph construction. Instead, we keep claims and evidence in their source language and select the encoder and threshold on the validation split for each dataset. For the evidence reasoner, we fine-tune Qwen2-7B-Instruct with LoRA using rank 16, α = 32 , dropout 0.05, and target modules {q_proj,k_proj,v_proj,o_proj} . The graph verifier uses two heterogeneous attention layers with four heads per relation. We set ε = 0.1 , λ = 0.5 , K = 5 , α = 0.6 , β 1 = 0.5 , and β 3 = 0.1 . AdamW is used with learning rate 2 × 10 - 5 for unfrozen backbone layers and 1 × 10 - 4 for newly added OT/graph modules, while the LoRA adapter uses learning rate 2 × 10 - 4 . Training runs for at most 50 epochs with early stopping based on validation F1.
Table 4 summarizes the source of each structured evidence field. Only the coarse relation polarity uses the post-level label during training-target construction as weak calibration, while the remaining fields are derived from OT localization, OCR, captions, and deterministic rules. OCR tokens are extracted with the PaddleOCR multilingual toolkit, and region captions are generated with a frozen BLIP-2 captioning module (Li et al. 2023b).
Visual evidence pipeline The visual evidence pipeline does not use a separate forgery-detection ensemble; instead, it combines three complementary signals. (i)  OT conflict-localized regions : the top- K patches with the highest cost-weighted conflict scores q ^ j are selected as candidate contradictory regions (Section 4.2); (ii)  OCR tokens : PaddleOCR (v3) extracts multilingual scene text, and tokens overlapping with the selected patch regions are retained as visual-textual anchors; (iii)  Region captions : BLIP-2 generates a one-sentence caption for each of the top- K candidate patches using the frozen image encoder and a prompted decoder with greedy decoding (temperature 0.0, max 32 tokens per caption). For Chinese Weibo, PaddleOCR runs in Chinese mode; for English datasets, it runs in English mode. The OCR engine and captioner are not fine-tuned on the target datasets; they are used as fixed preprocessors. Region captions and OCR tokens are concatenated with the OT evidence packets to form the LLM input (Section 4.3) and are also injected as graph node features (Section 4.4). We do not compute forgery confidence or artifact-type scores because the goal of this pipeline is localized conflict localization rather than image manipulation detection.
Decoding is deterministic (temperature 0.0), outputs follow a fixed JSON schema, and parse failures trigger one constrained retry before assigning insufficient relation (Table 5).

Under this protocol, LCFND achieves the top F1 among the controlled baselines on all three datasets. Compared with MGCA, it improves F1 by 1.47, 1.73, and 1.67 points on Weibo, Twitter, and GossipCop, respectively. The gain is also reflected in AUC, with improvements of 1.07, 1.43, and 1.43 points. Classifier-side efficiency We next compare trainable parameter counts and cached-evidence inference latency under the matched protocol (Table 9). Latency is measured after evidence has been cached, so it reflects the graph-verification stage rather than one-time OCR, captioning, or LLM evidence generation; those end-to-end costs are reported separately in Table 35.
Under cached evidence, LCFND remains competitive with the top matched baseline in inference time (60.9 vs. 62.8 ms/sample) while using fewer trainable classifier-side parameters than MGCA (68.4M vs. ∼ 157M). The parameter efficiency stems from the evidence-graph architecture, which operates over a compact set of structured evidence nodes rather than over dense cross-modal feature maps. Reported LVLM references are orders of magnitude slower and are not directly comparable to this cached-evidence setting; their end-to-end costs are discussed in Section 5.8.
Having established competitive accuracy and efficiency under the matched protocol, we next verify that the observed improvements are statistically reliable. We conduct paired bootstrap significance testing between LCFND and the strongest matched-backbone baseline MGCA (Table 10). For each seed, we use 10,000 paired bootstrap resamples over test instances, and the reported confidence intervals summarize the resulting five-run estimates under the same protocol.
The observed F1 improvements are statistically significant on all three datasets, with p -values below 0.05, and the confidence intervals of the gains remain positive under this matched-backbone protocol. These results rule out the possibility that the improvements are driven solely by random seed variation, and they confirm that LCFND’s advantage over the top matched-backbone baseline is systematic rather than incidental.
Having confirmed that LCFND outperforms MGCA under the default ResNet-50 backbone, we next ask whether the gain is an artifact of the specific visual encoder. To isolate the effect of the backbone, we test whether the gain persists when only the visual encoder is strengthened while keeping the OT-localization and graph-verification pipeline unchanged (Table 11 and Fig. 4).
When ViT-B/16 is used, patch tokens are treated as visual region embeddings; when Swin-T is used, the final-stage token features are treated as region-level visual embeddings. Stronger visual backbones improve both MGCA and LCFND, and the absolute gain of LCFND over MGCA becomes smaller under ViT-B/16 or Swin-T. Even so, LCFND remains higher than the corresponding MGCA variant under each tested backbone, indicating that explicit OT-guided conflict localization adds value beyond stronger visual encoders alone.

Localized-conflict subsets Table 13 compares performance on the full test split against the top-30% samples ranked by cost-weighted OT conflict, i.e., posts where localized inconsistency is expected to be most decisive.
The gain over MGCA nearly doubles on the high-conflict subset (+3.16 vs. +1.63 F1), which supports the claim that LCFND is especially helpful when the decisive signal lies in localized text-image inconsistency rather than global semantic mismatch.
Auxiliary OCR and caption controls Table 14 asks whether the improvement mainly comes from exposing the model to additional OCR and region-caption text under matched settings.
Adding OCR and captions to a simple BERT/ResNet fusion model improves average F1 from 79.86 to 81.78, and adding the same information to MGCA improves average F1 from 86.97 to 87.48. However, both remain below full LCFND. Direct LLM classification based on OCR and captions also remains below LCFND, which suggests that simply exposing the model to more textual evidence is insufficient in this setting. The OCR/caption + graph variant without OT performs better than the direct LLM classifier but still trails full LCFND, which is consistent with localized conflict selection contributing to the conversion of auxiliary evidence into useful verification signals.
Contextual comparison under separated protocols To prevent uncontrolled comparisons across heterogeneous settings, we organize the comparison into distinct protocol tiers. Table 15 reports P1 (matched-backbone) and P2 (backbone substitution) results under controlled conditions. Under both protocols, LCFND consistently ranks first across all three datasets, with gains over MGCA ranging from 0.74 to 1.73 F1 points depending on the backbone and dataset.
Table 16 reports only P3 results from the original papers. These rows are not directly comparable with P1 or P2 because they may use different visual backbones, external retrieval, LVLM prompting strategies, training data, or evaluation protocols. Therefore, Table 16 is provided only to situate our results within the broader literature and should not be interpreted as a leaderboard against LCFND.
Table 17 reports P3b matched-input approximations. In this setting, retrieval is disabled, the same splits are used, and the same Qwen2-7B evidence generator, OCR/caption inputs, and input budget are adopted where applicable. These rows are not faithful full reproductions of the original systems; rather, they are controlled approximations designed to test whether OT-localized evidence packets and graph verification add value under equalized inputs. Under this matched-input setting, LCFND outperforms FND-LLM-M-II and MMRGV-lite, suggesting that localized conflict discovery and evidence-graph verification contribute beyond structured LLM prompting alone.
Therefore, our main direct ranking claim is restricted to P1 matched-backbone results. P3 reported results are contextual only, and P3b results support a controlled input-budget analysis rather than a universal comparison against all LVLM-based misinformation detectors.

Table 19 provides a finer-grained component ablation with both weighted F1 and GossipCop fake-class F1. Removing OT conflict priors causes the largest average degradation (2.93 F1 points), followed by the graph verifier (2.69 points) and LLM evidence nodes (1.98 points). On the class-imbalanced GossipCop dataset (24% fake), OT removal still causes the largest fake-class F1 drop (−3.80 points), while removing emotion nodes yields a small positive shift (+0.35 points). This confirms that the component contributions are robust to class imbalance and not artifacts of weighted averaging, but it also shows that the fake-class metric is not monotonic for every single ablation. Replacing the evidence graph with text concatenation leads to a 2.18-point drop. Emotion nodes remain useful on the average metric even though the fake-class metric slightly improves when they are removed.
Table 20 reports the effect of label-informed relation calibration. The label-free variant derives the relation field only from OT conflict strength and rule-based anchors, without label polarity calibration; it decreases average F1 by 0.96 points, showing that calibration is useful but not the sole source of gain. The model remains above variants without the relation field (−1.62) or with shuffled relation labels (−2.92), indicating that performance is not driven only by a fake → contradict / real → support prior. The masked-evidence input probe further lowers average F1 by 2.24 points when localized anchors are hidden at inference, which is inconsistent with a label-prior-only decision rule. The OCR/caption stress tests in Table 31 show the same pattern under upstream evidence corruption.
To further examine whether label-informed relation calibration overfits to specific contradiction patterns, we add a contradiction-type holdout diagnostic. The remaining structured fields, including OT spans, visual regions, OCR tokens, region captions, and rule-derived contradiction anchors, are kept unchanged. Generalization and robustness Inference-time stress test of cascaded evidence construction To evaluate whether errors from the cascaded evidence pipeline propagate to the final verifier, we conduct an inference-time stress test without retraining. The trained LCFND parameters are fixed; upstream components are perturbed before evidence construction and graph inference. OCR tokens are randomly deleted at 30%; region captions are replaced with captions from other images in the same mini-batch at 30%; and named-entity/noun-phrase graph nodes are dropped at 30%. Table 31 reports these settings and two combined rows that apply multiple perturbations simultaneously.
Table 31 shows that caption noise has a larger effect than OCR deletion. Combined OCR and caption noise at 30% yields a 2.60-point average F1 drop, and adding simultaneous NER/phrase node dropout further reduces average F1 to 85.52 (−3.08), which is the largest degradation in this stress test. This pattern is consistent with cascade-style error propagation from OCR, captioning, and text-side semantic nodes into LLM evidence and graph verification. The framework remains sensitive to systematic OCR failures and hallucinated region captions, especially when the decisive contradiction involves small image text or fine-grained object attributes.
To reduce the risk of cross-split visual leakage, we additionally apply perceptual near-duplicate filtering based on image hashing (Hamming distance ≤ 5 between pHash values across train and validation/test splits). Only 1.53–2.58% of samples are removed, and LCFND remains higher than MGCA by 1.36–1.52 F1 points after filtering (Table 32). Efficiency analysis Table 35 summarizes the system-level deployment trade-off, while Table 36 provides a module-level decomposition of the online cost. The module-level decomposition in Table 36 shows that the computational bottleneck is not OT localization or graph verification. OT localization adds only 16 ms per sample, and graph verification adds 25 ms per sample. The dominant cost comes from LLM evidence generation, which accounts for 1075 ms and 19.4 GB under full online inference, followed by BLIP-2 region captioning. This explains why the cached-evidence setting is much faster and why the full online version is more suitable for offline or high-risk verification scenarios. It also suggests that future deployment can reduce cost through evidence caching, batching, 4-bit quantization, or distilling the 7B evidence generator into a smaller task-specific structured-evidence model.
Table 37 reveals four dominant failure patterns. Sarcasm and irony are the most frequent category in Weibo and remain common in Twitter and GossipCop, showing that pragmatic intent inversion is still difficult for the current content-centric pipeline. Low-quality images are another major source of errors, especially on Twitter, where compression and reposting make local visual evidence noisier. Domain-knowledge and temporal errors show that retrieval-free settings still struggle when external world knowledge or time-sensitive context is required. Weak-alignment cases remain challenging when text and image are only loosely related.
Discussion and limitations LCFND targets multimodal posts in which text and image remain topically aligned but differ in local factual details. Its main contribution is to expose intermediate evidence fields and verify them before final prediction, rather than to rely only on pooled global fusion features.
The evidence-construction stage uses training-time label-informed relation calibration. Because validation/test evidence generation receives no ground-truth veracity labels, this design should be interpreted as a training-time supervision bias rather than test-time label leakage; however, it may still bias the reasoner toward label-associated relation patterns learned during training. The label-free ablation (−0.96 average F1) shows that calibration helps without being the sole source of gain. More importantly, the graph consistency checks should be read as evidence-consistency diagnostics rather than as direct proof of semantic truthfulness, which is why we report independent human judgments of evidence sufficiency and relation faithfulness in Table 26. Although validation and test evidence generation do not use ground-truth labels, the current evidence reasoner is not fully label-free because the relation field is weakly calibrated by training labels during SFT target construction. Future work will explore human-annotated evidence supervision or fully label-free relation induction. Inference-time stress tests confirm that OCR, caption, and NER/phrase errors propagate through evidence generation and graph verification; combined OCR/caption/NER noise at 30% produces the largest degradation in Table 31. The framework remains sensitive to systematic OCR failures and hallucinated region captions when contradictions depend on small image text or fine-grained attributes (Dhuliawala et al. 2024). Gains over MGCA shrink under stronger visual backbones while remaining positive. MMRGV and FND-LLM are reported under P3 as contextual references rather than P1 competitors; FND-LLM-M and MMRGV-lite are matched-input approximate reproductions rather than full paper reproductions. Although the contradiction-type holdout diagnostic shows that LCFND retains non-trivial cross-type generalization, performance still decreases when relation calibration for a specific contradiction type is removed. This indicates that the current evidence reasoner partially benefits from type-associated relation supervision. Fully label-free contradiction typing and human-annotated evidence supervision remain important directions for future work. Graph-based verification in adjacent tasks Graph-based evidence reasoning has also been explored in adjacent fact-verification tasks. For example, TFGIN (Wu et al. 2025) introduces tight-fitting graph inference for table-based fact verification, which strengthens subgraph coherence and statement verification. Although table-based fact verification differs from multimodal social-media misinformation detection, such methods are relevant to our work because both use graph structure to improve evidence-coherence reasoning rather than relying only on flat sequence representations. LCFND differs in that its graph is built from intra-post multimodal evidence, including text spans, visual regions, OCR/caption anchors, OT conflict priors, and LLM-generated evidence nodes, rather than from table cells and textual statements. Thus, prior graph-based verification work motivates our evidence-coherence design, while LCFND extends this idea to localized cross-modal conflict verification.
Our comparison with recent LVLM and retrieval-augmented systems remains limited. Full reproduction of systems such as FKA-Owl and LEMMA would require their external knowledge retrieval, LVLM adaptation, prompting strategy, and benchmark-specific pipelines. We therefore treat such methods as contextual references rather than direct competitors under the P1 matched-backbone protocol. The purpose of our experiments is to validate the contribution of intra-post OT-guided localized conflict modeling under controlled conditions, not to establish a universal leaderboard across incompatible protocols. Future work will extend LCFND with retrieval-augmented evidence and stronger LVLM backbones to enable broader comparison with fully knowledge-enhanced systems.
The framework is retrieval-free in its main protocol and remains challenged by sarcasm, temporal reuse requiring external knowledge, and domain-specific claims. The frozen retrieval cache used for contextual comparisons reduces drift, but it does not eliminate source-availability bias or archival incompleteness. Full online inference (1.47 s per sample) is better suited to offline or high-risk verification than large-scale real-time screening; cached evidence, batched processing, or quantized deployment are more practical options in memory-constrained deployment settings.
Conclusion We study multimodal fake-news detection in cases where text and image are globally related but locally inconsistent. LCFND localizes cross-modal conflict with OT, converts the cues into structured evidence with a supervised LLM, and evaluates them with a heterogeneous evidence graph.
Under the controlled matched-backbone protocol, the complete pipeline improves F1 over the top P1 baseline MGCA by 1.47, 1.73, and 1.67 points on Weibo, Twitter, and GossipCop, and also improves source-only cross-dataset transfer. The gain remains positive under ViT-B/16 and Swin-T backbone substitution, and OT localization remains necessary even under these stronger backbones. The goal of our comparison is not to establish a universal leaderboard across incompatible LVLM, retrieval-augmented, and backbone-specific protocols. Instead, we show that under controlled matched settings, localized conflict discovery provides consistent gains and remains complementary to stronger visual backbones. Reported LVLM/LLM systems under different retrieval or prompting protocols are treated as contextual references rather than direct P1 competitors. Future work may integrate stronger LVLM backbones, retrieval-augmented external evidence, more efficient evidence generation for low-resource deployment, and human-annotated evidence supervision or fully label-free relation induction.

Given the post text, OCR text, and region captions, decide whether the post is fake or real. Do not use external search or retrieval. Return a JSON object with fields: claim , visual_summary , reasoning , and label . The label must be either fake or real . Post text: {{text}}. OCR: {{ocr}}. Region captions: {{captions}}.

A.2 FND-LLM-M-II matched+structured prompt You are a multimodal evidence generator. Given the post text, OCR text, and region captions, produce structured evidence without using external retrieval. Return only valid JSON with fields: claim , visual_evide nce , relation , contradiction_type , ratio nale , and label . relation must be one of support , contradict , irrelevant , or insuf ficient . contradiction_type must be one of entity , location , time , attribute , event , or none . The label must be inferred only from the provided text, OCR, and captions. Post text: {{text}}. OCR: {{ocr}}. Region captions: {{captions}}.

A.3 MMRGV-lite rationale prompts Prompt 1, claim extraction: Extract the central factual claim from the post text in one sentence. Use only the given post text.
Prompt 2, visual rationale: Summarize the visual content from OCR and region captions in one or two sentences. Do not use external retrieval.
Prompt 3, cross-modal rationale: Compare the extracted claim with the visual rationale. State whether the image supports, contradicts, is irrelevant to, or is insufficient for the claim. The three rationale embeddings are concatenated and passed to an MLP classifier.

A.4 Human annotation rubric Annotators judge four binary fields for each generated evidence item. Claim grounding is positive when the generated claim overlaps the OT-selected text span or its parent clause and does not introduce a new event. Visual-evidence relevance is positive when the generated visual evidence is grounded in at least one selected region, caption, or OCR anchor. Relation consistency is positive when the predicted relation agrees with the human-judged relation between the claim and the localized visual evidence. Type correctness is positive when the contradiction type matches the dominant inconsistency category among entity, location, time, attribute, and event. Annotators are shown the post text, image, OT evidence packet, OCR/captions, and generated evidence, but not the model prediction or ground-truth veracity label.
