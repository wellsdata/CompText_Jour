Headnote Abstract
This study examines how fake news affects the stock prices of publicly traded football clubs in Turkey. This research integrates attention-based metrics from Google Trends to construct a specialised search-interest index for detecting fake news episodes using data covering the period from January 2021 to February 2026. The methodological framework is distinguished by its use of econometric enhancements, including the Kolari-Pynnönen test for industry-wide cross-sectional dependence and the standardised residual test robust to event-induced variance. To ensure the highest level of inferential validity despite a concentrated number of clusters, the analysis implements a wild cluster bootstrap procedure utilising a refined weight distribution. The empirical findings demonstrate a degree of market resilience, as speculative narratives fail to generate enduring price distortions or structural shifts in volatility regimes. Instead, the results reveal that the equity market for these clubs exhibits substantial informational efficiency, with investors rapidly discounting sensationalised noise in favour of fundamental economic and sporting drivers. Furthermore, panel fixed-effects estimations confirm that market uncertainty remains governed by established financial mechanisms, specifically volatility persistence, rather than being destabilised by the rising frequency of digital disinformation. This research underscores the capacity of disciplined investor bases to mitigate the risks of information asymmetry, offering critical insights into the corporate sustainability and informational integrity of publicly traded sports entities in the digital era.
Keywords: event study, fake news, financial performance, football club, information asymmetry, information manipulation, market resilience.

Introduction
The advent of the Internet and social media has precipitated a paradigm shiftin how individuals communicate and access information. In particular, social media platforms have accelerated the spread of misinformation (Jain, 2023). Social media has also emerged as a significant factor influencing the scope and dynamics of traditional media. It has been demonstrated that intensive social media activity, particularly when involving misleading or sensational content, increases news coverage and viewership in traditional media outlets (Petratos, 2021; Ren et al., 2024; Tariq, 2025). The effectiveness of social media platforms in content moderation remains questionable, especially regarding misinformation and fake news, leading to the persistent dissemination of false information (Habes et al., 2023; Zhang et al., 2019). Exposure to such misinformation has been shown to trigger negative emotional responses, including fear, disgust, and surprise (Vosoughi et al., 2018). Furthermore, advancements in artificial intelligence have further facilitated the production of fake news (Aïmeur et al., 2023).
The phenomenon of fake news is not a recent development; nevertheless, the 2016 U.S. presidential election occasioned a notable escalation in global discourse concerning the issue. It is evident that elements such as manipulation, disinformation, rumours, and conspiracy theories have existed since the inception of human communication itself (Beauvais, 2022; Kalsnes, 2018). This evolution of misinformation within digital communication systems has important implications for a variety of contexts, including professional sports, where the flow of information directly affects economic valuation.
Since the early 1990s, new media development and rising financial investments in football have reshaped the relationship between clubs and the media. Numerous clubs have modified their ownership and organisational structures in pursuit of financial gains or financial survival. Concurrently, the growing demand for real-time information has led clubs to recognise the importance of external communication (Cleland, 2009). In this context, the accuracy and dissemination of information are critical. This is particularly evident in publicly traded football clubs, where media narratives-both traditional and social-directly influence investor behaviour and stock price dynamics.
The dissemination of false information pertaining to football clubs has the potential to exert a considerable influence on the stock market, given its capacity to impact investor sentiment, stock price volatility, and trading behaviour associated with the publicly traded shares of these entities. Empirical studies have demonstrated that news pertaining to football clubs, notably information concerning key assets such as players and coaches, has the potential to generate abnormal returns and substantial stock price reactions (Dayi et al., 2018; Fűrész & Rappai, 2022). Fake news related to football clubs affects stock markets primarily through investor sentiment, speculative trading, and increased price volatility. The emotional attachment of fans and the importance investors assign to intangible assets such as player and coach information, brand value, and club reputation further amplify these market reactions (Dayi et al., 2018; Fűrész & Rappai, 2022; Herbert et al., 2024).
Furthermore, sports clubs are increasingly using mechanisms such as brand value and fan tokens to leverage intangible assets and strengthen fan engagement, with the potential to enhance emotional attachment and economic value (Castillo-Apraiz et al., 2025). Concurrently, the strategic financial decisions of clubs, including sustainable investment and growth-oriented initiatives, in conjunction with social media activities, have the potential to influence investor perceptions and stock market performance (Ntasis & Strigas, 2023; Sóti et al., 2020).
Nevertheless, the dissemination of false information has been demonstrated to not only facilitate the emergence of crises, but also impede effective intervention efforts. Consequently, combating misinformation is of critical importance for maintaining stability, fostering trust, and supporting peace and prosperity, particularly in environments characterised by ongoing tensions (Micevičienė et al., 2024).
Despite the growing literature on fake news and financial markets, there is still limited evidence on how search-based attention signals can be systematically used to identify misinformation-driven events, particularly in emerging markets where event clustering and cross-sectional dependence are more pronounced. Our identification strategy does not include a systematic filter for concurrent real corporate events (earnings announcements, transfer completions, governance actions). These methodological challenges are especially relevant in such contexts, where evidence remains scarce despite heightened vulnerability to information asymmetries and digital misinformation. This gap is particularly important in sports-related financial markets, which represent economically significant and highly liquid segments characterised by substantial trading volumes and strong investor attention. This study aims to investigate how fake news-induced attention shocks influence the stock market behaviour of publicly listed Turkish football clubs in an emerging market context.
This study examines the stock market consequences of fake news in publicly listed Turkish football clubs by combining Google Trends-based attention metrics with event-study techniques and panel estimations. Turkey provides a unique setting, as it is one of the few markets where all publicly traded professional football clubs are listed on Borsa İstanbul. The sports segment in capital markets is characterised by high trading activity and strong investor participation, largely driven by fan-based ownership structures. Rather than focusing on a selective sample, this study covers the entire population of listed sports clubs in Turkey. These firms constitute highly visible and influential actors within the national sports economy, offering an ideal laboratory for examining the effects of informational inefficiencies driven by information asymmetry and digital misinformation in an emerging market context. In doing so, the study contributes to the literature by enhancing understanding of informational efficiency, market resilience, and corporate sustainability under increasing digital disinformation. In addition to short-term market reactions, it is also relevant from a corporate sustainability perspective to understand how misinformation affects publicly traded football clubs. Persistent information asymmetries have the potential to weaken market confidence, increase uncertainty, and undermine the long-term financial resilience of sports organisations. Consequently, an examination of the market consequences of fake news contributes to broader discussions on sustainability, governance, and informational efficiency in digitalised financial environments.
The remainder of this paper is organised as follows. Section 1 reviews the relevant literature and identifies the research gap. Section 2 presents the methodological framework and empirical strategy. Section 3 describes the dataset and variable construction. Section 4 reports the empirical findings, and the final section concludes with theoretical implications, practical contributions, and directions for future research.
1. Review of the scientific literature
A body of research has examined the relationship between fake news and sports clubs, highlighting its impact on stock market reactions, information asymmetry, and investor behaviour in football club equities.
Previous studies show that financial market fake news tends to attract significantly greater investor attention in comparison to accurate news reporting. This heightened attention often results in abnormal trading volumes during the period in which the fake news is disseminated. Nevertheless, it has been demonstrated that stock price reactions to false information are, as a rule, less pronounced than responses to authentic news. This finding indicates that markets tend to exhibit a degree of skepticism towards fake news (Clarke et al., 2020). The role of news in shaping market reactions and investor behaviour is well-documented (Duan et al., 2024). This phenomenon is especially evident in the context of football clubs, where investors closely monitor not only financial indicators but also information regarding sporting developments.
Evidence shows that football club stock prices are influenced by sporting and news-related events. In particular, Ersan and Demir (2017) document that transfer news and rumours become more prominent during off-season periods, with associated financial effects reaching up to 38.75% annually, alongside increased trading activity driven by higher news intensity. Evidence from the COVID-19 suspension period further indicates that negative news significantly increases stock price volatility in major Turkish clubs such as Galatasaray, Fenerbahçe, and Beşiktaş (Ozdurak & Ulusoy, 2020). In addition, the football stock markets exhibit asymmetric reactions, with negative news having a stronger and more persistent effect on prices than positive news (Berkowitz & Depken, 2018). These patterns are particularly relevant in football markets, where investor reactions are increasingly influenced by digitally mediated information flows that extend beyond traditional financial disclosures.
Football is not merely a sport, but a global phenomenon with significant economic and cultural dimensions. In professional football markets, fan engagement has increasingly shifted to digital platforms such as Instagram, TikTok, and X, which facilitate rapid information diffusion while also increasing exposure to unverified or misleading content (Atanasova & Ivanov, 2025; Price et al., 2013). This environment strengthens the role of information in shaping investor perceptions, particularly in publicly traded football clubs where intangible assets such as brand value, media rights, and player contracts are key determinants of firm value (Herbert et al., 2024). Thereby reinforcing the importance of non-financial information channels in shaping valuation dynamics in football equity markets.
A study focusing on Trabzonspor, a football club listed on the Borsa Istanbul stock exchange, found that managerial contract announcements had a stronger impact on stock returns than player transfers (Dayi et al., 2018). Moreover, an event study of 272 transfers demonstrated that transfer announcements exert an influence on stock prices. In a substantial number of cases, information leakage occurred prior to the official announcement. This finding suggests that transfer news and rumours can influence investor behaviour and lead to price movements in the stock market (Fűrész & Rappai, 2022). Using stock price data from Borussia Dortmund, news-based models were tested in the football sector, which is characterised by frequent and measurable news flow. By combining traditional and reverse news models, the relationship between sports-related news and stock price movements was examined, revealing that sporting performance and corporate governance factors have a significant impact on stock prices (Stadtmann, 2006).
In the literature, despite the plethora of methodological approaches for detecting the effects of fake news, including machine learning techniques, analytical methods, and measurement frameworks for assessing their impact on financial markets (Conroy et al., 2015; D'Ulizia et al., 2021; Mridha et al., 2021; Shu et al., 2017; Zhou & Zafarani, 2020), the topic remains relatively underexplored within the context of sports economics. In the case of football clubs that are publicly traded, the firm value is contingent on intangible factors, including brand value (Flostrand et al., 2020; Mills & Robson, 2020; Mishra & Samu, 2021), player transfers (Rojas Torrijos & Mello, 2021; Runsewe et al., 2024), fan loyalty, and expectations of sporting success. These characteristics render football clubs more susceptible to the impact of media coverage, particularly in the context of unverified or misleading information. It is evident that speculative news pertaining to transfer rumours, managerial changes, financial difficulties, or sporting performance has the capacity to influence investor perceptions with great rapidity. This, in turn, can result in significant fluctuations in stock prices. Nevertheless, studies that systematically examine the impact of fake news on the financial performance of sports clubs and stock market reactions remain limited in number. As is evident from extant research, the majority of studies have focused on match outcomes, transfer announcements and the effects of social media on stock prices. However, the influence of misleading information has been largely overlooked. This discrepancy assumes particular significance in the context of football clubs, where investor behaviour is profoundly influenced by fan participation.
Consequently, examining the effects of disinformation in publicly listed football clubs offers important implications for sports economics and financial market efficiency. Moreover, these dynamics are important for sustainability and governance, as information quality and transparency strengthen market stability and investor confidence. In this regard, Borsa Istanbul-listed football clubs, characterised by highly engaged fan bases, strong media visibility, and distinctive investor structures, constitute a particularly suitable setting for analysing how fake news and information flows influence investor behaviour in emerging market contexts.
2. Methodology
This study employs an event study methodology grounded in the frameworks of Brown and Warner (1985) and MacKinlay (1997), augmented by several econometric enhancements to address challenges specific to the present context. Four identification problems inform the design: (i) potential contamination of expected return estimates by event-related price movements; (ii) cross-sectional dependence arising from industry clustering among football club equities listed on the same exchange; (iii) non-normality of abnormal return distributions characteristic of securities with concentrated retail investor bases. The analysis proceeds in the stages: estimation of a market model over a contamination-free window; computation and testing of abnormal returns using four complementary statistics robust to distributional violations; assessment of conditional volatility dynamics via rolling realised volatility and panel regression.
The methodological framework has been developed to ensure robust inference under event clustering, cross-sectional dependence, and non-normal return distributions, which are prevalent in football-related financial markets.
The extant literature suggests that while fake news and sentiment-based signals can trigger short-term market reactions, the magnitude and persistence of these effects remain ambiguous, particularly in emerging and sports-related financial markets. Evidence from football club equities further indicates context-specific and limited insights, leaving broader implications for emerging markets underexplored. Building on these gaps, this study develops testable hypotheses to examine the impact of attention shocks induced by fake news on trading behaviour and stock price dynamics in publicly listed football clubs:

Abnormal return distributions in event studies often deviate from normality. Therefore, statistical inference relies on a battery of complementary test statistics. Initial inference follows the fixedvariance approach (MacKinlay, 1997). Additionally, the standardised residual test (Patell, 1976) and the non-parametric sign test (Cowan, 1992) are reported. However, because the Patell Z-statistic is sensitive to event-induced variance inflations, the standardised cross-sectional test (t BMP ) proposed by Boehmer et al. (1991) is also utilised. Given that the analysed securities belong to the same industry, temporal clustering of events inherently introduces cross-sectional correlation in abnormal returns, which can severely understate conventional standard errors. To robustly control for this clustering bias, the Kolari and Pynnönen (2010) test (t KP ) is employed as the primary inference statistic. This test modifies the BMP statistic by adjusting the denominator using r, the average pairwise crosscorrelation of abnormal returns. When r > 0, the t KP statistic appropriately penalises the standard error, ensuring correct test size under industry clustering.

Because the sample contains only four cross-sectional clusters, conventional cluster-robust standard errors are severely downward biased. Furthermore, the standard wild cluster bootstrap employing Rademacher weights is mechanically invalid in this setting, as it generates only 24 = 16 unique pseudo-samples (Cameron et al., 2008). To achieve asymptotically refined inference, we implement the wild cluster bootstrap utilising the 6-point weight distribution proposed by Webb (2023), conducting B = 999 replications at the club level.
In summary, the methodological framework integrates event-study and panel-data techniques to capture both immediate market reactions and dynamic volatility effects of fake news in a structurally clustered sports equity market.
3. Data
The empirical dataset covers the four largest publicly traded Turkish football clubs listed on Borsa İstanbul (BIST): Fenerbahçe Futbol A.Ş., Galatasaray Sportif A.Ş., Beşiktaş Futbol Yatırımları A.Ş., and Trabzonspor Sportif Yatırım ve Futbol İşletmeciliği A.Ş. Daily closing prices and the BIST-100 index were retrieved via the yfinance library for January 1, 2021 to February 24, 2026, yielding 1,278 trading-day observations per club. To measure misinformation-related public attention, a composite search-interest index was constructed from Google Trends using targeted Turkish- and English-language keywords associated with disinformation in sports and it is given in the table no. 2.
Google Trends returns a weekly index scaled 0-100 within each query period. To preserve index granularity across the 2021-2026 sample, data were downloaded in overlapping annual segments (January-December for each year), with a two-month overlap between adjacent segments to enable rescaling. Normalisation followed the standard Google Trends methodology: within-segment values were divided by the segment maximum and rescaled to a common 0-100 range using the overlap period as a calibration anchor. A composite daily index for each club was then constructed as the weighted sum of individual keyword indices, aligned to BIST trading days. A fake news event is defined as any trading day for which the club-sp ecific composite weighted search-interest index exceeds its rolling 90- day mean by more than 1.5 standard deviations. This threshold is consistent with the abnormal-attention approach used in Da et al. (2011) for Google Search Volume Index-based event identification. For manual validation, a random subsample of 29 headlines was coded independently by two researchers. Each event was classified as: (1) genuine misinformation / fake news, or (0) legitimate controversial news that generated search interest without constituting verifiable disinformation. Inter-rater reliability was assessed using Cohen's κ, yielding κ = 0.76, which indicates substantial agreement (Landis & Koch, 1977). Mean daily returns are close to zero for all clubs and Fenerbahçe exhibits the highest daily return volatility. All series show pronounced negative skewness - except Trabzonspor, which is positively skewed - and total kurtosis substantially exceeding three, indicating heavy-tailed distributions. The CAR rises from -0.103% at t = -5 to a peak of +1.898% at t = +2, then partially reverses to +0.601% by t = +5. The 95% confidence band encompasses zero throughout the window, consistent with the absence of statistically significant effects under KP-t.
On the event day (t = 0), the AAR is +0.225% - positive in sign and statistically indistinguishable from zero across all statistics. The 95% confidence interval spans -0.90% to +1.35%, comfortably containing zero. These results provide no evidence of a systematic market-price response on the event day. The absence of a concentrated event-day effect is consistent with several mechanisms: the heterogeneous content of fake news dilutes directional impact in the cross-section; publication outside trading hours may shiftadjustment to t = +1; and the retail-dominated investor base of Turkish football clubs may respond with delay due to limited real-time fact-checking access.
The pre-event window [-5, -1] shows no systematic driftand the AAR at t = -1 of +0.325% is flagged as significant by Patell-Z but is not corroborated by BMP-t or KP-t, indicating that any apparent pre-event signal does not survive correction for cross-sectional dependence and should not be over-interpreted. Post-event adjustment is not monotonic. CAR increases from 0.692% on the event day to a peak of 1.898% at t = +2, indicating short-term continuation immediately following the fake-news event. Thereafter, CAR declines to 0.601% by t = +5, suggesting a partial correction of earlier gains. This pattern indicates that post-event dynamics are characterised by an initial continuation phase followed by a moderate reversal rather than an immediate adjustment toward zero. However, neither the negative AARs observed at t = +3 and t = +5 nor the overall correction phase achieves statistical significance under the KP-t statistic.
When considered as a whole, the evidence pertaining to abnormal returns indicates that the dissemination of false information does not result in a systematic distortion of the market valuation of publicly traded football clubs. Temporary fluctuations in the data are rendered insignificant once cross-sectional dependence and event-induced variance are controlled for, indicating that investors either rapidly discount speculative content or already price in substantial uncertainty. The findings of the present study align more closely with semi-strong informational efficiency than with behavioural overreaction.

The coefficient on the fake news indicator is positive but fails to reach statistical significance. This indicates that, while fake news events may trigger immediate abnormal returns, they do not induce a persistent, structurally higher volatility regime over a 20-day trading horizon.
The market rapidly absorbs the shock, preventing the event-induced variance from translating into a sustained one-month volatility drift. The coefficient on R 2 i,t-1 is positive and highly significant. This indicates strong volatility persistence, a well-known stylised fact in financial time series. Although our specification uses squared returns and therefore does not directly test the asymmetric leverage effect (Black, 1976; Christie, 1982), the finding that past return shocks - regardless of sign - predict future volatility is consistent with the broader literature on volatility clustering. Importantly, the fake news indicator remains insignificant, confirming that disinformation does not drive volatility.
Overall, the volatility analyses suggest that broader market conditions, rather than misinformation shocks, primarily drive volatility dynamics in football equity markets. The significance of lagged returns further indicates that conventional financial mechanisms remain the dominant source of risk formation.

Overall, the robustness analyses strengthen the central conclusion of this study: fake news episodes generate limited financial consequences in Turkish football equity markets regardless of event-window definition, sample partition, or alternative model specification. Across multiple inference procedures and sensitivity checks, misinformation appears to create temporary noise rather than persistent mispricing. These findings challenge the expectation that emotionally attached fan-investors necessarily exhibit stronger behavioural biases when exposed to unverified information.
The findings of the study provide limited and inconsistent evidence that fake news events systematically affect the equity valuations of Borsa Istanbul-listed football clubs. While certain isolated reactions are observed around specific events, these effects do not persist across alternative volatility specifications or aggregated analyses. This finding indicates that any market response is likely to be short-lived and event-specific, rather than indicative of a persistent pricing effect. The findings, when considered collectively, underscore the imperative for circumspect interpretation and imply that investor responses to misinformation in this milieu may be subdued or expeditiously rectified by market mechanisms.
Conclusions
This study examines how fake news affects football club stocks. By integrating high-frequency financial data with attention-based search metrics, the study offers significant insight into how the Turkish football equity market processes speculative noise in an increasingly digitised information landscape. The primary conclusion of this study is that the football clubs listed on Borsa İstanbul demonstrate a high degree of resilience against the potential disruptions of digital misinformation. The empirical evidence reveals that misinformation-related events fail to generate significant abnormal returns on the day of their occurrence, with the average abnormal return remaining statistically indistinguishable from zero across all robust inference tests. Although cumulative abnormal returns increase in the immediate post-event period, reaching a peak on the second trading day after the event, this initial continuation is followed by a partial correction rather than a complete reversal toward zero. Moreover, neither the continuation phase nor the subsequent correction achieves statistical significance under the cross-sectional dependence-adjusted KP-t statistic. These findings suggest that fake news does not generate a systematic post-event return pattern characterised by a clear post-event reversal. Taken together, these findings are consistent with a market in which unverified information is not systematically incorporated into asset prices. Furthermore, the analysis of market stability confirms that while the frequency of fake news has intensified markedly, these episodes do not result in structural shifts in market volatility. Panel OLS estimates using wild-cluster bootstrap inference demonstrate that the presence of fake news does not trigger a persistent, higher volatility regime over a 20-day trading horizon. Instead, market uncertainty in this sector remains governed by conventional financial mechanisms, particularly return persistence and lagged shock effects, rather than fake-news exposure. The absence of significant event-day abnormal returns is broadly consistent with Clarke et al. (2020), who argue that misinformation may attract investor attention without necessarily producing economically meaningful price distortions. While fake news can increase visibility and discussion, investors do not always incorporate such information into prices unless its credibility is perceived as sufficiently high. This finding contrasts with Berkowitz and Depken (2017), who document significant market reactions to football-related news in the United Kingdom. A plausible explanation is that their study examines verified football information, whereas the present study focuses on explicitly unverified or misleading content. The evidence therefore suggests that investors are capable of distinguishing between credible information and speculative rumours.
The absence of a persistent volatility response further complements this interpretation. Prior studies generally show that information shocks increase uncertainty when they alter expectations regarding future performance. However, fake news events in our sample do not generate a sustained increase in volatility over the subsequent trading weeks. One possible explanation is that misinformation possesses a relatively short informational lifespan. Consistent with Vosoughi et al. (2018), false information may spread rapidly but can also lose credibility quickly once challenged or contradicted, limiting its longer-term influence on market behaviour.
Finally, the finding that lagged return dynamics explain volatility more effectively than fake news exposure is consistent with the broader literature on volatility persistence. Rather than constituting an independent source of market instability, fake news appears to be absorbed within existing market dynamics. One possible explanation for the weak impact of misinformation is the information environment surrounding Turkish football clubs, which is characterised by frequent transfer rumours, media speculation, and recurring controversies. Repeated exposure to such information may encourage greater investor skepticism toward unverified claims, thereby reducing the likelihood that misinformation translates into significant pricing or volatility effects.
The lack of statistically significant heterogeneity in market reactions across the four clubs further suggests a uniform level of skepticism among Turkish sports investors. From a perspective of corporate sustainability and governance, these results highlight the necessity for football clubs to maintain transparent and proactive communication strategies. While the current investor base appears disciplined, the vulnerability of these clubs requires continuous monitoring to safeguard long-term financial integrity. Methodologically, the study underscores the importance of utilising econometric corrections, such as the Kolari-Pynnönen test and wild cluster bootstrap inference, to ensure valid conclusions in industry-concentrated sectors. While the research is constrained by the temporal clustering of events, it provides a robust foundation for future inquiries. Ultimately, this study has important implications for clubs, regulators, and digital platforms in the context of increasing misinformation. For club management, the evidence suggests that timely and consistent communication through official channels is crucial to mitigate potential short-term mispricing effects arising from fake news and social media-driven speculation. For financial regulators, the findings highlight the need to integrate alternative data sources, such as social media and search-based attention indicators, into market surveillance systems to improve the detection of abnormal attention spikes potentially linked to misinformation. Finally, for digital platform governance, stronger verification and monitoring mechanisms for unverified sports-related content may help reduce the diffusion of false narratives that can temporarily distort investor behaviour. Overall, these coordinated actions may enhance market integrity and informational efficiency in sports-related financial markets. Moreover, these dynamics are important for sustainability and governance, as information quality and transparency strengthen market stability and investor confidence.
This study has several limitations. First, the analysis is restricted to publicly traded football clubs listed on Borsa İstanbul, which may limit the external validity of the findings. Second, the exclusion of overlapping events reduces the effective sample size; however, this procedure is necessary to avoid cross-event contamination and preserve internal validity within the event-study framework. Given the magnitude of the exclusion, the retained sample primarily reflects isolated misinformation episodes and may underrepresent periods characterised by dense clusters of fake-news activity. Consequently, the findings should be interpreted as evidence regarding market responses to non-overlapping misinformation shocks rather than to sustained misinformation campaigns. Because no formal comparison between retained and excluded events was conducted, the possibility of selection effects cannot be completely ruled out. Third, fake-news identification relies on media-based classification, which may involve some degree of misclassification risk. Fourth, the study focuses on short-term market reactions and therefore does not capture potential long-term effects of misinformation. Fifth, no systematic filter was applied to exclude concurrent real corporate events that may coincide with fake-news days. Although such events may introduce additional noise into the measured returns, their direction is expected to be random. As a result, any resulting bias would be more likely to attenuate than inflate test statistics. Despite these limitations, several factors support the robustness of the findings. Alternative event-window specifications yield qualitatively similar results, and the insignificant findings observed in the high-intensity subsample suggest that the main conclusions are unlikely to be an artefact of concurrent real corporate events.
Future research could extend this study in several feasible directions. First, comparative analyses across European football leagues such as the Premier League and Bundesliga would help assess the external validity of the findings across different institutional settings. Second, distinguishing between different types of fake news (financial, sporting, and reputational) could provide a more granular understanding of how information shocks affect market behaviour. Third, machine learning-based event identification methods could be employed as a more objective alternative to Google Trends thresholds, improving the robustness of fake news detection. Finally, future studies could incorporate investor-level heterogeneity by distinguishing between retail and institutional investors to better capture differential behavioural responses.
