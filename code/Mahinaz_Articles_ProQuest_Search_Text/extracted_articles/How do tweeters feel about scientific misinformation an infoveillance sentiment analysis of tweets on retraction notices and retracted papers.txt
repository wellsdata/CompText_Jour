Introduction
Despite being highly trusted (Singh et al., 2014), science engages in the infodemic phenomenon (Masic, 2012) by either generating erroneous and fraudulent outputs or witnessing misinterpretation of true and genuine scientific knowledge (Lewandowsky et al., 2012; Alvarez-Galvez et al., 2021; Tangcharoensathien et al., 2020; Nascimento et al., 2022). To deal with the former, the scientific community has traditionally devised a retraction mechanism allowing authors or journals to withdraw inaccurate papers and thereby mitigating the effects of scientific (dis)misinformation (Williams & Wager, 2013), and maintaining the integrity of science (Moylan & Kowalczuk, 2016). It is usually accompanied by notices that are published on the journal or publisher websites to inform about the retraction causes and process. However, the effects of (dis)misinformation would continue even after their retraction or removal (Ecker et al., 2010). For scientific outputs, this may be indicated by the continued citation of the retracted papers (Bar-Ilan & Halevi, 2017; Campanario, 2000; Unger & Couzin, 2006; Van Der Vet & Nijveen, 2016), which is generally confirmative (Bar-Ilan & Halevi, 2017; Da Silva & Dobránszki, 2017; Hamilton, 2019; Theis-Mahon & Bakker, 2020). The increase in both the number of retracted papers and their citations may distort the science and undermine its authenticity and believability (Abhari et al., 2022; Bar-Ilan & Halevi, 2017). It doubles the necessity for their official and complete removal from academic records. Moreover, the retraction notices lack visibility (Xu & Hu, 2018) and are not well taken into account by readers (Voung, 2019). Consequently, the retraction mechanism needs additional control and supervision mechanisms to early detect faulty outputs and boost its effectiveness after retraction.
As a real-time popular social platform, Twitter may serve as a global sentiment analysis corpus (Cillóniz et al., 2020; Geronikolou et al., 2021; Pak & Paroubek, 2010). It also provides a popular altmetric platform (Bornmann & Haunschild, 2018a) for rapid citation to scientific papers within a short time after their publication, via broader conversational discussion crossing traditional disciplinary boundaries (Priem & Costello, 2010). Scholars, publishers, librarians, readers, and researchers use the media for disseminating, discussing, and criticizing scientific outputs (Collins, 2015; Collins et al., 2016; Costas et al., 2017; Jamali & Alimohammadi, 2015; Mohan & Munigal, 2012; Munigal, 2014; Puschmann, 2014; Shema et al., 2012).
Although social media, including Twitter, are witnessing a propagation of (dis)misinformation, they also show sensitivity to the phenomenon, as tweeters discuss scientific misconduct (Copiello, 2020; Marcus & Oransky, 2011), and the allegation of misconduct sparks social attention (Bornmann & Haunschild, 2018a; Shema et al., 2019). The discussions take place earlier than in traditional media (Sugawara et al., 2017) and continue even after retraction (Jan & Zainab, 2018a, b). Moreover, tweets are found to contain retraction-related keywords (Abhari et al., 2022), and some hints that raise doubts about retracted articles in their pre-retraction phase (Haunschild & Bornmann, 2021).
However, some research evidence implies that social reactions to scientific articles may be mostly superficial. For instance, users prefer to share URLs or mention the titles without digging deep into the content of the articles by criticizing or analyzing their different features (Didegah et al., 2018; Friedrich et al., 2015; Thelwall et al., 2013b). This gives rise to the question of whether Twitter users actively or passively address scientific (dis)misinformation propagated through retracted or retracted-would-be papers. To answer the question, the present study tries to analyze the content of the tweets to retracted articles in terms of their sentiments. To better understand the vigilance of users, the tweets to the retracted papers (hereafter, paper tweets) were split into pre- and post-retraction and also compared to tweets to retraction notices (hereafter, notice tweets). To test the activeness of tweeters in confronting the papers, four measures are defined and explored: - The portion of paper tweets not limited to linking to the retracted articles;

- The portion of paper tweets expressing negative sentiments;

- The portion of retweets of negative paper tweets;

- The strength of negative polarity.


Tweets are found to be generally neutral (Friedrich et al., 2015; Thelwall et al., 2013b). Moreover, the retracted papers are of different grades, regarding the severity of the errors or the misconduct committed (Serghiou, et al., 2021). Therefore, benchmarks were needed to better judge the tweeters’ positioning for and against the retracted papers. The sentiments expressed in paper tweets were thus compared to those expressed in the retraction notes revealing authorities’ positions towards the retracted papers, as well as note tweets, as the social resonance of the retraction decision. This would also serve as a controlling tool for any possible deficiencies of the dictionary-based approach in truly detecting sentiments.
Literature review
User-generated content on social networks
Social networks are both promising and problematic regarding the (dis)misinformation phenomenon. On the one hand, they have been witnessing an infodemic, i.e., an outbreak of misinformation, disinformation, or malinformation. These include low-quality, erroneous, or fake information intentionally or inadvertently generated, and spread (Swire-Thompson & Lazer, 2020) or genuine information used out of context to mislead, harm, or manipulate others (Vlachos, 2022). This has given rise to a wide range and growing body of research on the effects of (dis)misinformation on people's thoughts, beliefs, and behavior (Wang et al., 2019). The exposure of individuals and society to (dis)misinformation affects their lives (Southwell et al., 2019), health (Briand et al., 2021), memories (Brainerd & Reyna, 2005; Greenspan & Loftus, 2021; Lewandowsky et al., 2012; Loftus, 2005), judgments and attitudes (Baum, 2022; Cheng & Chen, 2020), behavior (Leitner et al., 2021) and reasoning and decision-making (Ecker, 2015).
On the other hand, social networks are believed and found to be rich in content (Wang et al., 2020), and provide unprecedented opportunities for knowledge discovery and data mining (Blake & Agarwal, 2017) paving the way to take advantage of the collective wisdom and social consciousness reflected in users’ social activities and interactions. The features turn the networks into self-organized problem-solving systems (Chamberlain, 2015) capable of tackling different challenges e.g., managing natural disasters and crises (Brandt, et al., 2019; Seddighi et al., 2020; Simon et al., 2015; Splendiani & Capriello, 2022), preventing or informing about diseases (Al-Surimi et al., 2016), and predicting scientific, social and political events (Chauhan et al., 2021; Eysenbach, 2012; Haunschild & Bornmann, 2021; Kallus, 2014).
The socially generated and shared content is mainly subjective (Musto & Dahanayake, 2021), echoing users’ perceptions, attitudes, opinions, and sentiments. Microblogging on Twitter is a popular way to express opinions (Nistor et al., 2021) and share knowledge and beliefs about global concerns (Nezhad & Deihimi, 2022), and different aspects of products and events (Cambria et al., 2017). This has paved the way to increasingly conduct sentiment analyses to mine people’s attitudes through their tweets about different subjects, e.g., important events (Ruz et al., 2020), drugs (Basiri et al., 2020), tourism destination management (Paolanti et al., 2021), commercial brands (Gautam & Yadav, 2014; Ghiassi et al., 2013), news (Gebremeskel, 2011), energy sources (Abdar et al., 2020; Kim et al., 2021) and electronic products (Neethu & Rajasree, 2013). Tweet data mining also showed useful in analyzing and forecasting the stock market (Chen & Lazer, 2013; Guo & Li, 2019; Mittal & Goel, 2012; Pagolu et al., 2016; Porshnev et al., 2013). During the Covid-19 pandemic, research on tweeters’ emotions about the disease and its related feature has been growing (Prabhakar Alhajji et al., 2020; Aljedaani et al., 2022a, 2022b; Basiri et al., 2021; Cascini et al., 2022; Chinnasamy et al., 2022; Dubey, 2021; Kaila & Prasad, 2020; Kaur & Sharma, 2020; Medford et al., 2020; Nezhad & Deihimi, 2022; Pastor, 2020; Ra et al., 2020; Rajput et al., 2020; Zulfiker et al., 2022).
This may also hold for scholarly communication. As, despite the prevalence of personal communication (Java et al., 2007), Twitter has been also widely used for professional and research objectives (Priem & Costello, 2010). Researchers use Twitter to disseminate and discuss their research (Zhu, 2014) and share research findings (Bik & Goldstein, 2013) with the scientific community and the general public (Kahle et al., 2016; van Noorden, 2014). The number of tweets about scientific articles may correlate to their citations (Celebi et al., 2022; Ortega, 2017; Thelwall et al., 2013a), predict their early impact (Eysenbach, 2012), and reflect scholarly impact (Priem & Costello, 2010). Moreover, Hassan et al. (2020) found a positive correlation between the sentiments expressed in tweets and the number of citations. However, Friedrich et al. (2015) found that tweets about scientific articles just mentioned the publication of the article and did not contain any sentiments, and only 20% of the tweets contained some opinions about the articles.
Retraction and Twitter
Scientific (dis)misinformation may be generated through inaccurate, unfalsifiable, or inconsistent scientific-sounding information and miscommunication of science (Lewandowsky et al., 2012; Tangcharoensathien et al., 2020; Alvarez-Galvez et al., 2021; Swire-Thompson, et al., 2022; Nascimento et al., 2022). The retraction mechanism, devised to hinder the propagation of the former, fails to eradicate the effects of scientific (dis)misinformation. This may be supported by research findings confirming a growing number of citations to retracted papers even after retraction (Bar-Ilan & Halevi, 2017; Kochan & Budd, 1992; Van Der Vet & Nijveen, 2016). In the cases where a decrease has been reported for post-retraction citations, it was accompanied by a reduction in negative citations (Yang et al., 2022). Moreover, despite the co-existence of negative, neutral, and positive recognition (Bar-Ilan & Halevi, 2017; Korpela, 2010), the two latter are prevalent (Da Silva & Dobránszki, 2017; Korpela, 2010; Moussa, 2022; Pfeifer & Snodgrass, 1990; Theis-Mahon & Bakker, 2020) without or rarely mentioning the retraction or the errors causing the retraction (Bar-Ilan & Halevi, 2017; Kochan & Budd, 1992; Theis-Mahon & Bakker, 2020). More interestingly, confirmative citations have been made to the articles retracted for ethical misconduct, data falsification, and false reporting (Bar-Ilan & Halevi, 2017), and it takes them decades to stop (Korpela, 2010). The study conducted by Heibi and Peroni (2021) is an exception in that they found out that along with the increase in the number of citations of a retracted article in the last 20 years, the number of citing articles acknowledging its retraction has also increased. Moreover, content analysis of the citations showed that the citing articles doubted, and in some cases, expressed strong and explicit negative opinions about the article.
In line with the exception, altmetric studies conducted on retracted papers reported social users to be sensitive to scientific misconduct. Copiello (2020) showed that retracted papers received more altmetrics than their non-retracted peers before retraction and the retraction probability can be therefore predicted by views, downloads, and mentions. According to Peng et al. (2022), retracted papers receive more attention after publication on social media, including Twitter, with a tendency to express criticism. Retraction of papers was also reported to result in higher altmetric attention scores (Serghiou et al., 2021; Shema et al., 2019). Increases have been reported in the number of tweets and social discussions about scientific misconduct and retracted papers (Bornmann & Haunschild, 2018a; Copiello, 2020; Marcus & Oransky, 2011; Sugawara et al., 2017). Social discussions were found to increase with the detection of scientific misconduct, even earlier than traditional media, and continue after their retraction (Jan & Zainab, 2018a, b). Also, research findings revealed that Mendeley readership continue to grow after retraction (Bar-Ilan & Halevi, 2018). Moreover, tweets on retracted papers may contain retraction-related keywords (Abhari et al., 2022) and cast doubts about the validity of the papers shortly after their publication (Haunschild & Bornmann, 2021).
However, research evidence also implies rather passive or semi-passive behavior of tweeters. For example, most peoples' feelings toward fake news and misinformation are generally more neutral than positive or negative, despite the latter being stronger (Sidiropoulou, 2021). This was also true for their reactions to scientific articles. For instance, Friedrich et al. (2015) showed that a majority of tweets to scientific articles are neutral (81.7%) and are therefore neither praise nor criticism but merely diffusion of the paper. This is also supported by the high rate of untweeted (Haunschild & Bornmann, 2018), unclicked links (Fang et al., 2021), or their preference for sharing or bookmarking URLs instead of analyzing or criticizing the contents (Didegah et al., 2018; Haunschild et al., 2019). Consequently, further studies are required to ensure the promise of social opinions reflected in user-generated content in tackling scientific (dis)misinformation.
Research questions
The present study is an effort to answer the following questions: - What proportion of the paper tweets were not limited to linking?

- What proportion of the paper tweets did contain negative opinions?

- What proportion of the negative paper tweets were retweeted?

- Were the paper tweets different from retraction notices and note tweets in terms of opinion strengths?


Research method
Using a content analysis method with an opinion mining approach, a collection of retraction notes and their related retracted papers was built and analyzed using the following steps.
Paper collection building
By searching in Scopus, a collection was built of the papers retracted during 2017–2022. The rationale for selecting the time span relies on the higher chance of receiving altmetrics for more recent items (Araújo et al., 2017). To ensure identifying the papers retracted by a retraction note, the search strategy initiated with the exact phrase "retraction notice" or "retraction note" in the title field. The retracted papers were then identified using their DOIs mentioned in the related notes. This resulted in identifying 1218 retracted papers, of which abstracts and bibliographic information were then extracted from the database.
Tweet collection building
Tweets were manually collected by searching the DOIs of the papers and notes in Altmetric.com through the "Altmetric It!" plugin. During the verification phase, a large number of the tweets were found to be incompletely recorded on Altmetric.com. The incomplete tweets were searched on Twitter and completed. Some of the tweets were no more available at the time, and could not be completed.
Any tweet was categorized into "before" or "after" retraction by contrasting its posting date to the retraction date of the related papers. The posts were tagged as “retweet” if containing the string “RT @”, and as “tweet” if not. Non-English tweets, accounting for about a quarter of the collection in 83 languages, were translated into English using the Google Translate command in Google Sheets.
Opinion mining
The opinions expressed in the retraction notes and tweets were mined using the SentiStrength opinion mining tool. It is a dictionary-based classifier developed by Mike Thelwall to analyze the opinions of short conversational English texts and is suitable for social studies (Thelwall & Buckley, 2013; Thelwall et al., 2011).
The software searches its dictionary for the sentiment terms and emoticons occurring in the text and estimates its sentiments based on the number of words found. Each text is provided with two positive and negative scores ranging from − 1 to − 5 and from + 1 to + 5 (with 1 indicating the weakest and 5 indicating the strongest feeling). For each tweet, the sum of the two positive and negative opinion scores was calculated to obtain just one opinion score. Based on the final score, each tweet was classified in the opinion polarity of positive, negative, or neutral.
Validation of the results
SentiStrength was evaluated by human-coded corpus in different contexts including Twitter, YouTube, Myspace, Digg, BBC Forums, Runners World, Shakespeare’s drama, reviews, and comments, and also in different languages like English, Early Modern English, Spanish, Turkish, and Arabic. The results reported accuracy values ranging from 44.6 to 78.1% (Al-Shabi, 2020; Islam & Zibran, 2018; Krouska et al., 2017; Rabab'Ah et al., 2016; Vural et al., 2013). Furthermore, the strengths of the correlations between the SentiStrength results and human raters ranged between 0.380 and 0.644 (Culpeper et al., 2018; Thelwall, 2017; Vilares, et al., 2015).
In the present study, the SentiStrength results were validated against a human-evaluated tweet subset to gauge its power in accurately detecting sentiments in the context of the retraction-related tweets. The subset consisted of 373 tweets randomly selected using the Morgan table and was analyzed, and tagged by the authors as being positive, neutral, or negative. A Kappa agreement score of 0.443 was yielded between the human and SentiStrength sentiment analyses, which is estimated to be moderate (Cohen, 1960). Accordingly, the tool correctly detected 64.3% of negative, 77.4% of neural, and 42.9% of positive sentiments. It should be mentioned that the authors evaluated the tweets based on their sentiments about the retracted or retracted-would-be papers, but not the general sentiments conveyed by the tweets. Consequently, the overall accuracy of the SentiStrength may be higher.
Findings
103 of the 1218 papers and 113 of the 1218 notices were tweeted. It reveals a high rate of untweetedness for the retracted papers (91.54%) and retraction notes (90.72%). The tweeted papers and notes were found to totally receive 30,408 and 5190 tweets or retweets, respectively. The notes received 2550 tweets (58.9%) and 1776 retweets (41.1%). The retracted papers received 12,327 tweets (40.1%) and 18,412 (59.9%) retweets.
Links
The links to URLs and DOIs were extracted from the tweets and analyzed. The results showed that 6940 (56.76%) contained both links and texts, 5016 tweets (41.02%) contained no URLs or DOIs but texts, and the rest 271 tweets were limited to links (2.22% of total tweets). Here, the word “text” encompasses any type of non-link content including emoticons, hashtags, mentions, and texts.
As Altmetric.com automatically collects (re)tweets to scholarly outputs based on their direct links, the posts containing no links were verified on both Altmetric.com and Twitter to find how they could be attributed to the related papers. The results showed that the tweets were mostly replies to other tweets linking to the retracted articles (4396 tweets accounting for 87.64% of the text-only tweets). The rest were the tweets incompletely recorded on Altmetric.com, and unavailable on Twitter and hence could not be verified (620 accounting for 12.36% of the tweets).
Of the 7211 tweets containing URLs, 6515 (90.35%) were linked to just the retracted articles, 256 (3.55%) just to the retraction notes, 425 (5.89%) to other sources, and finally 15 (0.21%) to more than one link type (including the retracted papers, notices, and other sources) (Fig. 1). To compare the sentiment received by the papers through the notices and tweets, the opinion strengths of the tweets were first averaged across the papers. The papers were then classified into the three opinion polarities based on the average opinion strengths of their tweets. As seen in Table 1 (9th row), the papers were mostly negatively treated on Twitter (χ = 68.99; Asymp. Sig. = 0.000).
In the present study, the whole body of a (re)tweet, including tags, was analyzed. The terms following tags may be generally neutral, (e.g., # may refer to paper topics, and @ indicates the username mentioned). This might affect the share of neutral (re)tweets. To control for the effect, the analyses were conducted also on a sub-sample of tweets containing none of the tags. The results summarized in Appendix 1 reconfirmed those reported in Table 1.
The sentiments expressed in the paper tweets
The paper tweets were found to be dominated by neutrality (6240 accounting for 50.62%) followed by negativity (4137 accounting for 33.56% of the total paper tweets). A relatively low percentage of the tweets were found to be positive (1950 accounting for 15.82%) (χ = 3576.37; Asymp. Sig. = 0.000) (Table 1, 6th row). As seen, it is approximately in the range of the positive polarity share detected for the benchmarks. Table 1 (4th and 5th rows) also illustrates the shares of polarities for pre- and post-retraction paper tweets. As seen, the ratio of neutral tweets increased from 49.87% in the pre-retraction phase, to 55.40% in the post-retraction phase. Positive tweets were observed to shrink from 16.33 to 12.54%. Contrarily, the share of negative tweets remained almost unchanged (33.80% vs. 32.06%).
The polarities of retweeted tweets
Table 1 (7th and 8th rows) also illustrates the shares of polarities for retweets. To get a better insight, let’s compare the share of retweets to that of the tweets in the same polarities. As seen, the portion of the neutral note tweets, which were retweeted almost resembled the share of neutral note tweets (64.13% vs. 63.84%). Slight differences were observed between the shares of retweets and their related note tweets in negative (17.96% vs. 21.14%) and positive (17.91% vs. 15.02%) polarities. However, the share of retweets of negative paper tweets was considerably higher compared to that of negative paper tweets (51.41% vs. 33.56%), leading to a relative reduction of the share of neutral retweets (31.91% vs. 50.62%). The retweets of positive paper tweets had almost the same share as the positive paper tweets (16.68% vs. 15.82%).
Comparing the opinion strengths of the tweets and notes
The Wilcoxon test was used to compare the opinion strengths of the tweets and notes. As mentioned above, the positive notices were totally manually verified and corrected if necessary. This was not possible for the tweets, given their relatively high number. Thus, the tests were limited to non-positive tweets to avoid misleading the results. The tests were conducted on the absolute values of the sentiments.
According to the results summarized in Table 2, the retraction notices were higher than the note tweets (Z =  − 16.27, Sig = 0.000) in their non-positive opinion strengths. It implies that from the two benchmarks selected in the present study, the notices were more negative than the note tweets. However, the notices were lower than the paper tweets for both pre- (Z =  − 43.85, Sig = 0.000) and post-retraction phases (Z =  − 19.96, Sig = 0.000), implying lower negativity.
Table 3 displays the results of the U-Mann–Whitney tests to compare note and paper tweets in terms of their opinion strengths. While the mean ranks of pre- and post-retraction paper tweets were statistically equal (Z =  − 0.133, Sig. = 0.894), the note tweets were comparatively higher than paper tweets in pre-retraction (Z =  − 9.325, Sig. = 0.000) and post-retraction phases (Z =  − 7.375, Sig. = 0.000) regarding the mean ranks of their opinion strength, signifying the lower negativity of the former.
Verification of note and paper tweets in polarities
To further verify the motivation underlying each opinion polarity, the content of the note and paper tweets was randomly verified. Some examples are provided in Appendix 2. The results yielded for positive note and paper tweets, showed that the positive polarity was related to different features, including: - Mentioning positive titles or reporting positive results of the retracted or retracted-would-be paper;

- Expressing excitement about, suggesting, or praising the act of retracting/appreciating other tweeters for their points;

- Challenging peer review or publishing;

- Providing positive evidence to challenge or refute the results of the retracted or retracted-would-be paper or mocking them;

- Confirming the results of or supporting the publication of the retracted or retracted-would-be paper.

As seen, the positivity did not necessarily correspond to a positive perception of the tweeters about the retracted (or retracted-would-be) papers, but also to their neutrality or excitement about and recommendation of the retraction.

A rough verification of the negative and neutral tweets led to some interesting results (Appendix 3). A summary of the underlying features is as follows: - Challenging the retraction;

- Challenging peer reviewing/publishing;

- Suggesting retraction and demanding improvement of peer reviewing;

- Encouraging pressures for retraction;

- Responding to the tweets challenging the retraction.


As observed in Appendix 3, there existed neutral tweets that showed motivations for or against the retraction or the retracted paper. Some neutral and negative tweets challenged the retraction and criticized the social pressure leading to it. However, except for examples no. 2 to 10, the rest of Appendix 3 presents negative and neutral tweets, which were for the retraction.
Accordingly, there could be latent positioning behind the texts unreachable to the dictionary-based method used in the present study. Although the real positioning of the tweeters did not necessarily correspond to what was detected by SentiStrength, the features against the retracted papers were seemingly prevalent in all of the polarities, reinforcing the negativity towards the retracted papers, reported in the present study.
Finally, as inferred from the above-mentioned results, tweets may deal with titles and abstracts of the papers and this may misleadingly contribute to the polarities of the tweets. To verify their probable effects, we first measured tweet-note, tweet-abstract, and tweet-title similarities using the Levenshtein Similarity measure, and then analyzed their non-parametric partial correlations with tweet opinion strength. The results are summarized in Appendix 4. As the correlation coefficients at zero level suggest, tweet opinion strength showed significant inverse correlations with tweet-note similarity (r =  − 0.206, Sig. = 0.000), tweet-abstract similarity (r =  − 0.096, Sig. = 0.040), and tweet-title similarity (r =  − 0.170, Sig. = 0.000). As seen in the control level, after removing the effects of other similarity measures, the correlation between tweet-note similarity and tweet opinion strength slightly reduced but remained significant and negative (r =  − 0.180, Sig. = 0.000), signifying that the more similar a tweet to a note, the more negative its opinion, and vice versa. Nevertheless, the correlation between tweet opinion strength and tweet-title similarity (r =  − 0.071, Sig. = 0.132) and tweet-abstract similarity (r = 0.028, Sig. = 0.548) became insignificant at the control level. It means that the inverse association between tweet-paper similarity and tweet sentiment may be explained by tweet-note similarity. In other words, the tweets’ negative opinions would not be associated with their dealing with the retracted papers’ content and vice versa, if their similarity to the notes is excluded.
Discussion
A high rate of untweetedness (91.54%) was observed among the retracted papers, which is in line with the previous studies signifying a zero-inflated phenomenon in Twitter data, weakening the promising characteristic of altmetrics to supplement citation (Bornmann & Haunschild, 2018c; Haunschild & Bornmann, 2018; Haustein et al., 2015). Haustein et al. (2015) reported 79.5% of untweetedness for a collection consisting of 1.3 million papers. According to Haunschild et al. (2018), the lowest-quality papers, i.e., the papers not recommended by faculty members of F1000 Prime, mostly remained untweeted in 2010 (95.63%), 2011 (87.99%), 2012 (72.47%), and 2013 (68.12%), averaging to 81.05%. Thus, the rate observed in the collection of retracted papers can be interpreted to be higher than what was witnessed by scientific papers in general, and low-quality papers in specific. To be sensitive to low-quality or flawed scientific outputs, a very lower untweetedness would be expected for retracted or retracted-would-papers. Conversely, one might argue that users might prefer to assert their skepticism toward low-quality papers by ignoring them. Nevertheless, according to Bornmann and Haunschild (2018b), tweet counts are weakly correlated to quality. Moreover, the high rate of untweetedness may be partially caused by the low visibility of retraction notes (Vuong, 2019), and perhaps retracted papers (due to e.g., their age, low popularity of the topics, and low rank of the journals). More research evidence is needed to make a definite judgment.
Moreover, a high proportion of paper tweets did not limit themselves to linking to URLs but contained non-URL content, including texts, hashtags, mentions, emojis, emoticons, etc. The finding is not in line with previous studies highlighting a high rate of just-linking tweets (Didegah et al., 2018). Although all the tweets may not be of the same value and efficacy in terms of meaningful content, it is promising in that active engagement of users in generating content implies increasing digital literacy, and the utility of online experiences for the generators and readers and is, therefore, believed to herald an information society (Ewing & Thomas, 2008).
The share of negative paper tweets was considerably higher than negative note tweets. However, both the tweet groups were mostly neutral, followed by negative polarity. This was contrary to the dominance of negative polarity observed among notices (even before being corrected for the positive polarity). The prevalence of neutral tweets is consistent with previous studies reporting a high rate of neutrality on Twitter (Tavoschi et al., 2020) in general and towards fake news and misinformation in specific (Sidiropoulou, 2021). The difference with the notices can be explained by the variety of tweeters and their tweeting motivations compared to the limited population of notice generators (i.e., journal editors), whose motivation is just to announce the retraction and its reasons.
The paper tweets were also found to demonstrate positive feelings, though with a comparatively lower share than those of the negative and neutral ones. A rough verification of their content revealed that their positivity cannot be necessarily attributed to the retracted paper, but also to other features, e.g., quoting positive titles or results of the retracted or retracted-would-be papers, expressing contentment with the retraction, suggesting retraction, and challenging or refuting the claims by providing positive evidence. In agreement with our findings, Peng et al. (2022) showed that around the retraction time, papers discuss the retraction event rather than the research findings. Further in-depth analyses are required to precisely determine the features receiving positive feelings and their underlying motivation.
The tweets in the polarities were not equal in terms of being retweeted. Twitter users were more interested in retweeting negative paper tweets. Since the retweet feature helps users share, propagate, or appreciate other users’ posts (Perdana et al., 2018), the finding may signify a propagation of the negative voice about the retracted papers.
Despite the lower share of negative tweet count, the paper tweets were found to be generally more negative than the notices and note tweets in terms of opinion strength. It is consistent with Sidiropoulou (2021) who discovered negativity as the more strongly expressed feeling towards fake news and misinformation. It is also in agreement with the results of Peng et al. (2022) who uncovered a tendency toward criticism of retracted papers in tweets.
Another important point is that note tweets were less negative than the notices. They were also lower in number and negativity compared to paper tweets. Moreover, negative note tweets were the least retweeted compared to other polarities in the same tweet group and to the same polarity in the paper tweet group. Consequently, the retraction mechanism lacks visibility in the social realm, as is in the science world (Vuong, 2019).
Generally speaking, the findings may have implications for the validity of content-based tweet analysis in providing hints about the low quality of research outputs. As criticism-expressing tweets could contain factual information about problematic papers (Peng et al., 2022), the content-based approach can also overcome the challenges of the usefulness of altmetrics counts, including tweets, in research evaluation. Since Copiello (2020), Serghiou et al. (2021), Shema et al. (2019), Bornmann and Haunschild, (2018a), Marcus and Oransky (2011), and Sugawara et al. (2017) concluded a higher altmetrics for retracted papers, implying an association between low-quality and altmetrics counts, while Bornmann and Haunschild (2018b) challenged the use of Twitter counts for research evaluation purposes due to its weak correlation with quality.
The findings may have also implications for altmetrics literature considering a complementary role of altmetrics for citation analysis. As, contrary to previous research findings that showed a large number of affirmative citations to retracted articles (Bar-Ilan & Halevi, 2017; Da Silva & Dobránszki, 2017), the findings of the present study showed that positive motivations among tweeters are lower than negative ones and not necessarily targeted towards the retracted paper itself.
Conclusion
Dissemination of low-quality and deceitful papers is one of the provenances of scientific (dis)misinformation, needing more controlling and illuminating measures. The present study continues the research line that digs into networked collective wisdom to find solutions for (dis)misinformation propagation. It analyzed the sentiments expressed in tweets to retracted papers and contrasted them against those expressed in two benchmarks, including notices and note tweets which served as the representations of authorities’ voices and their social resonance, respectively.
Due to the high rate of untweetedness observed for the retracted papers, Twitter, in its status quo, does not seem ready to act as a powerful monitoring mechanism to prevent the propagation of scientific (dis)misinformation. However, the very scarce number of the retracted or retracted-would-be papers which were in the spotlights of tweeters, witnessed their active engagement, as reflected in the high rate of the tweets not limited to links, which were largely opinionated, with the negative feelings being stronger and more prevalent in terms of retweets and tweets. These signals herald a potential hidden in social activities which would increase the visibility of and awareness about low-quality or flawed papers, even before being disclosed by official authorities. The potential, though not yet actualized, could be regarded as a kind of monitoring that can be applied by Twitter users who feel responsible and show sensitivity toward the quality of science. However, we cannot ignore the fact that, for now, these responsible, vigilant, and active users are actually scarce in number and selectively react to some papers. Therefore, it calls for librarians and editors to launch public campaigns and training programs to encourage people to participate in the open reviewing of scholarly articles on social networks. The high rate of untweetedness gives rise to the question of what kind of papers are more disposed to attract social attention. Further studies are required to answer the question.
Moreover, the SentiStrength results showed a moderate agreement with human-based sentiment analysis. The manual verification of the content of notices, note tweets, and paper tweets revealed some motivations underlying the apparently positive sentiments. They were diverse, but not necessarily aimed at the retracted paper, itself. That was also the case for negative and neutral tweet examples that revealed some hidden positioning attitudes towards the retraction or retracted papers. It emphasizes the necessity of digging deep into text content when relying on a dictionary-based approach. Given the high number of tweets, it was impossible to manually check them in the present study. The results should be, therefore, interpreted with caution. To overcome the limitation, further studies with advanced machine learning techniques are required to uncover the latent motivation and attitudes behind the texts. Moreover, comparing the contents of tweets of the retracted papers to their similar non-retracted peers is necessary to overcome the aforementioned limitations of retraction notes, as a benchmark. Also, there existed a lot of non-English tweets in the collection, translated using Google Translator, which may be affected by machine translation deficiencies. Replicating the present study on tweets in different languages using their own sentiment analysis tools may fix probably misleading results.
