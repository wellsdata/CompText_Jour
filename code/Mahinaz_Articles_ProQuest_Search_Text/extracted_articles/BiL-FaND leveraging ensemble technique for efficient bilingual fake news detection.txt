Introduction
Over the past few years, digital media has become one of the main streams for spreading news across societies and communities. Recently, as the media has grown more prevalent and news websites and blogs have become plentiful, many online pages that purport to report on current events have emerged. However, their legitimacy has been called into doubt. The introduction of social media has become a mountainous problem in spreading fake news around the globe. There are multiple types of fake news: deceptive news, false news, disinformation, satire, and hoaxes. All of these forms are used to mislead the users in one way or another. Fake news detection has become an essential field in machine learning, and a lot of work has been done, but the vast majority of the work is based on mono-lingual.
Fake news has become very popular among researchers due to the long-lasting effects of these types of news on a specific community or part of the world. These types of news are made to create a hoax among the readers about different religions, personalities, beliefs, etc. These types of news are also written to get specific business or political benefits around the globe. As we all know, a huge amount of news is being published daily, which can be natural or fake. According to many studies, the propagation speed of fake news is much faster than real news because it contains many targeting points [1]. Fake news has become a growing concern for many communities around the globe. The induction of the World Wide Web has drastically changed the speed of news propagation among users [2]. Due to the popularity of online media among users, the spread of fake news has become very easy. For specific reasons, like the authenticity of the fake news, the writers of this sort of news do not use mainstream media; instead, they use unauthorized websites, blog posts, and social media platforms. The writers of mainstream media outlets know they have been supervised strictly, so publishing fake news from them is very rare. The writers of these types of stories commonly try to hide their own authentic identities. They try to write news with anonymous identities, and these sorts of writers are challenging to identify. So, as the trend toward fake publications is increasing, efforts to tackle this problem are also geared up.
The primary goal of this project is to develop a comprehensive bilingual fake news detection system capable of reliably recognizing and reporting misleading or incorrect content transmitted in both English and Urdu. To assure the credibility of the information flow in different languages, the proposed system would use cutting-edge natural language processing techniques and machine learning algorithms, with a specific focus on cross-lingual comprehension. Compiling a solid and balanced multilingual dataset, including real and fake news in English and Urdu, is essential to this endeavor. This dataset will train and validate our machine-learning model, ensuring it can discriminate between real and incorrect information.
The exponential rise of digital media has resulted in an unparalleled distribution of valid and misleading news worldwide. The rapid production of social media and online news websites has accelerated the spread of false news, posing a massive threat to information reliability. Fake news, in its different forms, i.e., deceptive news, false news, disinformation, satire, and hoaxes, is intended to mislead readers for various reasons, including political gain, commercial gain, or social disturbance. Even though false news identification has become essential in machine learning, most present work focuses on monolingual fake news detection. Fake news is not limited to a single language; therefore, designing algorithms to detect fake news in several languages is critical. This study focuses on two linguistically distinct languages, English and Urdu. The research significantly contributes to the rapidly evolving field of fake news detection by introducing a multifaceted, bilingual approach in Urdu and English. The study sets new benchmarks for accuracy, reliability, and comprehensiveness in fake news detection by deploying machine learning techniques and innovative architectures. In summary, the paper made the following contributions:
- We developed a bilingual fake news detection system (BiL-FaND) that can handle Urdu and English, filling a gap in current monolingual methods.

- A multi-layered architecture was developed to analyze news articles using numerical, textual, category, and multi-media data to improve detail and comprehensiveness.

- Incorporated an ensemble of machine learning models to improve the system’s ability to recognize complicated patterns and tiny anomalies in data, considerably improving the accuracy of fake news identification.

- Enhanced number of data features so that each architecture layer could be fed enough data.

- Utilized state-of-the-art datasets in English and Urdu, thereby avoiding the drawbacks of translated datasets and ensuring more reliable results.

- Comprehensive comparison revealed that BiL-FaND outperformed existing monolingual systems in terms of accuracy, precision, and recall.

Literature review
Fake news is not a new phenomenon. It has always been an element of media history since the birth of the printing press, long before social media. Clickbait headlines persuade readers to click to discover more online by attempting to shock and astound us. What’s more ridiculous than reading about fabricated events that never occurred? Since the advent of the human race, the term ’news’ has come into being. And from that very day, news started to propagate over the communities. The news-carrying medium at that time was human beings. So, the probability of tempering the news was very low. The newspaper concept was invented in 1566, and the first printed newspaper was published in Germany in 1609. The trend of publishing fake and tempered news has existed since the early days. As time passed, the print media and digital media became more powerful and, simultaneously, the writers of fabricated news. This type of news has very long-lasting effects on societies, both financially and mentally.
The internet is a huge advancement, and many people use it. These users have access to a wide range of social networking channels. Anyone can make a post or distribute news through the internet. These websites do not verify individual users or the content of their posts. As a result, some people try to distribute fake news via these networks. These stories could be propaganda aimed at individuals, communities, organizations, or political parties. A person cannot detect all of the fake news. As a result, classifiers that can identify fake news automatically are in high demand [3].
Ahmed et al. [4] presented a comprehensive literature review describing the application of machine learning techniques, including different classifiers for detecting fake news. The study “Detecting Fake News Using Machine Learning: A Systematic Literature Review” thoroughly examines the role of machine learning in detecting and combating fake news. The authors conduct a thorough literature review to assess the importance of machine learning in false news detection, appropriately supervised classifiers, and machine learning classifier training for this purpose. The review contains 26 relevant publications and emphasizes the role of machine learning in solving the issues faced by fake news. By methodically reviewing existing literature, this work provides significant insights for researchers, practitioners, and policymakers interested in using machine learning approaches to counteract the spread of disinformation. The use of ML approaches to detect fake news is proposed in this paper. The experiments employ three standard methods: Naive Bayes, Neural Network, and Support Vector Machine. Normalization is crucial in cleaning data before applying machine learning to classify it. Their results demonstrate that Naive Bayes has an accuracy of 96.08% in detecting fake news. The accuracy of two more advanced approaches, CNN and SVM, is 99.90% [5].
Natali et al. proposed a unique approach for detecting false news that goes beyond simply analyzing the news text and considers additional critical aspects such as user replies and the source of information. Their CSI (Capture, Score, Integrate) strategy comprises three key components, each serving a distinct purpose in spotting fake news. The first component, “Capture,” is concerned with comprehending user behavior reacting to a particular news piece. This module uses neural networks to find patterns between user replies and article content. By analyzing these patterns, the program hopes to identify signs indicating whether a news piece is authentic or faked. For example, a disproportionately large number of suspicious user reactions may imply that the news piece is untrustworthy. The second module, “Score,” is intended to analyze the news source’s reliability and the users’ reactions to the news from this source. Furthermore, the users’ reactions to the source and their history of contact with it are considered. This thorough examination of the source and user reactions to it adds to generating a “score” that reflects the possibility of the news piece being fake. The third and final module, “Integrate,” combines the data and insights acquired from the Capture and Score modules to classify the news piece as true or false. This integration phase includes a complicated decision-making algorithm that considers the many criteria mentioned in earlier modules and draws a conclusion based on them [6].
Fake news detection on social media data is very important. To handle this issue, Chenguang et al. have proposed a model known as CARMN (Crossmodal Attention Residual and Multichannel CNN) [7]. The authors experimented with their fake news detection technique on social media data, and rather than using conventional features, they used text, images, and videos as the main features in their experiment. Jianbo et al. [8] proposed a multi-modal approach named “HAN: Hierarchical Attention Network.” Besides dealing with conventional features, they also used the news image as a feature. In the first step, they generate the image’s caption and then calculate the tokens for the generated caption, news headline, and news body. Then, they add headline tokens with caption tokens and calculate cosine similarity with the tokens of the news body. The less the cosine similarity is, the less the chance that the image is of the same news. The proposed model outclassed the state-of-the-art approaches by achieving an accuracy of 95.5%.
Developing an algorithm for fake news detection with the highest possible accuracy will be a revelation, significantly influencing the prevalent societal concerns and the present political landscape. However, it has various negative consequences, including no verification of the approved opinions’ origin, legitimacy, or integrity. As a result, Alonso et al. [9] suggested a novel approach for false news identification that includes sentiment as a critical characteristic to boost accuracy. It also evaluates the suggested method’s performance using three distinct data sets. The results suggest that the recommended solution works effectively. Furthermore, in this study, a comparison is conducted with other approaches. Sentiment Analysis (SA) is obtaining people’s opinions, feelings, and thoughts from their evaluations on social media platforms. They use different machine learning techniques for the sentiment analysis, and Urdu embeddings are generated [10].

Mudit et al. [16], introduced GAME-ON, a Graph Neural Network (GNN) based framework designed to detect fake news in multimodal data (text and multimedia). The framework aimed to improve upon previous models with limitations such as inefficient encoding of inter-modal relations and a propensity for overfitting due to deep networks with too many parameters. GAME-ON addressed these issues by enabling finer interaction between different data modalities, leading to more robust data representations. The framework was tested on two public datasets, Twitter and Weibo, where it outperformed existing models by 11% on Twitter and showed competitive performance on Weibo, all while using 65% fewer parameters.

The paper “Multimodal Fusion and Inconsistency Reasoning for Explainable Fake News Detection” by Lianwei Wu and his team proposed a new approach to address the challenges of detecting fake news in the era of multimodal content in social media. The paper provided a detailed description of the MFIR model, which involves 5-level hierarchical structures: feature representations, cross-modal infiltration fusion (CIF layer), multimodal inconsistency learning (MIL layer), explanation reasoning (ER layer), and task learning. The paper also presented experimental results on three widely-used datasets, demonstrating that MFIR outperforms state-of-the-art methods by up to 2.8% in terms of accuracy and provides reasonable interpretability to detection results. BiL-FaND: bilingual fake news detector
By keeping a view on the literature review, we proposed a methodology covering multiple aspects of fake news. We developed a system that handled features other than conventional features. From the literature, most approaches focus on a single language with limited features. Our approach differs from existing work in multiple ways:—First, we propose a bilingual approach for which we collected a bilingual dataset with an enhanced number of features, e.g., words per sentence, source of the news, domain of the news, language of the text, etc. We have relied on text-based and domain-wise classification (e.g., Politics, Health, World, Sport), source-wise, sentiment-based, and opinion-based classifications.
“BiL” stands for “Bi-Lingual,” indicating that the discussed approach or method is designed to work with two languages. In the context of our research, these two languages are Urdu and English. The second part, “FaND,” stands for “Fake News Detection.” This signifies the primary goal or task of the system, which was to detect and possibly filter out fake news content. Hence, “BiL-FaND” succinctly encapsulates the key aspects of our research—a system that detects fake news in a bilingual context (English and Urdu).

FaND using textual features
News articles typically begin with a headline, also known as the article’s title. The headline is frequently snappy and attractive to capture the reader’s attention. The byline, which follows the headline, shows the identity of the journalist or author of the story. The second crucial aspect is the lead, which is often the opening paragraph of the news story and gives a concise synopsis of the main topics. The body of the news story contains extensive information and frequently adheres to the “inverted pyramid” structure. This means that the most significant news is delivered first, then important details, and last extra background or context. Textual Features are the most critical and pioneer features of news articles. The more significant part of information is described in these features; that’s why they play a vital role in providing a ground for fake news detection. Here are some of the textual features of news articles that are used to detect fake news:
The headline is the first thing people see when they come across a news article, so it is essential to be aware of the potential for fake news headlines to be misleading or sensationalized. Some common red flags for fake news headlines include Exaggerated claims, Loaded language, and Unsubstantiated claims. Fake news headlines often make exaggerated claims that are not supported by evidence. For example, the headline “Breaking News: Trump Found Guilty of Treason” is likely to be fake news, as there would be no way for a news organization to know this information before it was made public. Fake news headlines often use loaded language, which is a language that is designed to evoke an emotional response from the reader. For example, a headline that says “Democrats Want to Take Away Your Guns” is likely to be fake news, as it uses loaded language to make Democrats sound like they are the enemy. Fake news headlines often make unsubstantiated claims that are not supported by evidence. For example, a headline that says “Study Finds Vaccines Cause Autism” is likely to be fake news, as there is no scientific evidence to support this claim. While real news has the majority of valid claims.
The body of the news article is where the most critical information is presented. However, fake news articles often contain false or misleading information in the body. Some common red flags for fake news articles include Factual errors and unsourced information. Fake news articles often contain factual errors. For example, an article that says “The Earth is Flat” is likely to be fake news, as there is overwhelming scientific evidence to support the fact that the Earth is round. Fake news articles often contain unsourced information, which is information that is not attributed to a credible source. For example, an article that says, “A study found that vaccines cause autism” is likely to be fake news, as it is not clear where this information came from.
It is essential to be aware of these textual features of news articles so that you can be more critical of the information that we consume. If we see a news article containing any of these red flags, it is best to be skeptical of the information and do our research to verify the facts. FaND using categorical features
In our dataset, “Categorical Features” were variables sorted into several categories but had no order or priority. Each category may be thought of as a distinct value of that characteristic. In data analysis and machine learning, categorical features are commonly used. Categorical features in the context of news might include factors such as the author, source, and domain of the news. These features helped in the analysis of news stories. Some categorical features used in our research are shown in Fig. 6
- Author : The individual or team who wrote the news piece is the author. Different writers’ writing styles, opinions, and areas of expertise may differ. This feature may be used to analyze or compare different writers’ works.

- Source : The entity that created or released the news is the source. This might be a newspaper, television network, radio station, or internet news source. Editorial policies, political leanings, and target audiences may differ amongst outlets. The source might be critical when determining the dependability or slant of the news.

- Domain : The web address (URL) where the news piece is published is the domain in the context of online news. This may be used to categorize news stories depending on whether they were published on websites ending in .com, .gov, .edu, .org, and so on.

- Category : The news category or genre is a nominal element that specifies the sort of news story. Politics, sports, entertainment, business, technology, and health are common categories. This feature can be used to categorize news stories and analyze various forms of news coverage.


Long short-term memory (LSTM) is a form of RNN architecture that is designed to manage and analyze sequential input. It was created primarily to address the constraints of regular RNNs, such as the vanishing gradient problem, which limits the network’s capacity to collect and learn long-term relationships in sequential data. LSTMs are made up of memory cells that can retain and update data for long periods of time. These memory cells are made up of three major parts: an input gate, a forget gate, and an output gate. These gates control the flow of information into, out of, and within memory cells, allowing LSTMs to retain or discard data at each time step.
The capacity of LSTMs to capture long-term dependence by utilizing their internal memory cells is its distinguishing property. This allows LSTMs to recall information from earlier time steps, making them useful for processing and comprehending data sequences. LSTMs can successfully represent and interpret textual data, such as phrases or paragraphs, in natural language processing by capturing the relationships between words or characters. Overall, LSTMs are a powerful type of neural network architecture that excels at capturing and utilizing long-term dependencies in sequential data, making them well-suited for time series data, natural language processing, and other domains where understanding and modeling sequential patterns is critical.
In the context of news data, using long short-term memory (LSTM) networks for handling categorical information such as author, source, domain, and category entails converting these categorical variables into a numerical format that the LSTM network can handle. This change can be accomplished using techniques such as one-shot encoding or embedding. Converting categorical data into binary vectors is known as one-hot encoding. Each category is represented by a vector with a ’1’ at the category position and a ’0’ elsewhere. For example, if three news sources (CNN, BBC, and Al Jazeera) are available, CNN may be encoded as [1, 0, 0], BBC as [0, 1, 0], and Al Jazeera as [0, 0, 1].
In contrast, embedding depicts categories in a dense vector space where the distance between vectors can be read as semantic similarity. Embeddings for categorical characteristics can be generated using word embedding approaches such as Word2Vec or GloVe. These embeddings capture the contextual linkages and similarities between distinct categories. Once the categorical data has been translated into numerical form, it may be fed into the LSTM network. LSTM networks are recurrent neural network(RNN) that can handle data sequences such as words or characters. The LSTM network can learn long-term dependencies and capture contextual information included in category characteristics.
The LSTM network’s output can subsequently be used for additional analysis or prediction activities. LSTM networks excel at processing sequential data, making them ideal for applications requiring news data, where the sequence of occurrences or categories is critical. Using the network’s capacity to incorporate temporal relationships and contextual information while processing categorical variables in news data may improve the modeling and prediction of news-related activities.

This section provides a complete performance analysis of numerous cutting-edge language models as benchmarks and compares them to our custom-defined architecture. The investigation is divided into three stages: first, we investigate the performance of baseline models (BERT, DeBERTa, XLNet, ELECTRA, RoBERTa, XLM-RoBERTa, and two specialized models for Urdu) on standard datasets, as stated in the base paper. In the era of digital information, the spread of fake news on social media platforms has been on a huge rise, as have the detection techniques for this fake news. For the ’English,’ the comparison was made with the paper in which fake news related to COVID-19 on social media platforms is detected using multiple models and excellent comparison on the efficiency of these models [31]. We also tested the same models on the reviews dataset of products based on fake and real reviews [32]. But all of the models in the base paper are in English. So, for Urdu we used custom model defined by [30, 33, 34] Some famous models for fake news detection are described as below:
- BERT (Bidirectional Encoder Representations from Transformers) : A transformer-based model that revolutionized NLP through its deep bidirectional context understanding, BERT excels in capturing nuanced language patterns, a pivotal capability in identifying subtle markers of misinformation.

- DeBERTa (Decoding-enhanced BERT with Disentangled Attention): Building upon BERT, DeBERTa improves on the BERT architecture by incorporating disentangled attention to enhance the representation of word relationships, further refining the detection process.

- XLNet: An extension of the transformer model, XLNet leverages permutation-based training, surpassing BERT in several benchmarks, especially in understanding the context in which information is presented, a key factor in ascertaining the authenticity of news.

- ELECTRA (Efficiently Learning an Encoder that Classifies Token Replacements Accurately): ELECTRA trains a discriminator to distinguish between real and fake tokens, rather than predicting masked ones, offering a cost-effective alternative for fake news detection.

- RoBERTa (A Robustly Optimized BERT Pretraining Approach): An optimized variant of BERT, RoBERTa modifies key hyperparameters, removing the next-sentence pretraining objective and training with much larger mini-batches and learning rates, resulting in improved detection accuracy.

- XLM-RoBERTa (Cross-lingual Language Model-RoBERTa): This model extends RoBERTa’s capabilities to multiple languages, though it has been primarily evaluated in monolingual contexts and may not fully capture the linguistic subtleties when applied to diverse datasets.


While these models set a strong precedent, they are primarily unimodal and language-restricted, focusing solely on textual data. This represents a limitation in the scope of fake news detection, as the multifaceted nature of news dissemination often involves a blend of text, imagery, and multi-language content. Our proposed architecture extends beyond these constraints, incorporating a multimodal approach integrating textual, numerical, categorical, and pictorial data to enhance the detection mechanism. This is particularly crucial when analyzing news from media outlets, where visual context can be as informative as textual content. Moreover, our model transcends linguistic barriers, effectively processing information in both English and Urdu, thus broadening the applicability of fake news detection. The inclusive design, which combines multiple data modalities and language support, presents a significant advancement over the existing models constrained to monolingual and text-only analysis.
The efficacy of our model is underscored by its performance on a proprietary dataset comprising news articles from media outlets, which typically exhibit greater complexity and variability than social media posts. This dataset encompasses a wide range of features, including but not limited to, the text of the news, numerical data such as the date of publication and number of shares, categorical data such as the news source and author, and associated images. A comprehensive comparative analysis reveals that our architecture not only competes favorably with the baseline models but also demonstrates superior performance in several metrics. Our findings articulate the potential of our multimodal, multi-language approach in creating a more robust and accurate fake news detection system that is adept at handling the intricacies of news from media outlets. Discussion
In the concluding remarks of this study, we presented a multimodal approach for bilingual fake news detection, using numerical, textual, categorical, and multimedia features to enhance the accuracy and reliability of fake news detection. Our custom-designed approach, distinguished by its multimodal and multilingual capabilities, was rigorously compared against several renowned transformer-based models, including BERT, DeBERTa, XLNet, ELECTRA, RoBERTa, and XLM-RoBERTa. The initial phase of our comparison involved a comprehensive examination of baseline models on well-established datasets, followed by an assessment of their performance on our unique dataset. This dual approach highlighted the robustness of the baseline models while also underscoring their limitations, particularly in terms of language restrictions. Our research is beyond the conventional unimodal, text-centric analysis by integrating multimodal inputs, including textual, numerical, categorical, and pictorial data. This integration is pivotal when analyzing news from diverse media outlets, as it allows for a more comprehensive understanding of the content, catering to the complexities in fake news detection. Moreover, our architecture’s bilingual support, particularly for English and Urdu, signifies a substantial advancement over the existing models, broadening the horizons for fake news detection across different linguistic contexts.
Our research’s limitation is the restricted data availability for validation purposes. The validation of our algorithms is naturally challenging due to fake news’s complex and dynamic nature. While we attempted to construct a representative dataset, the diversity of fake news and its ever-changing properties in real-world circumstances make comprehensive validation impossible. Furthermore, we accept that including sophisticated models and ensemble methods while improving the effectiveness of our approach adds a level of resource intensiveness. The computing demands of these techniques may limit our system’s deployment on lower-power devices or in resource-constrained contexts. We understand the significance of addressing these practical problems for the real-world applicability of our fake news. detection system.
Conclusion
Finally, our research, which focused on bilingual fake news detection using multiple features to improve the accuracy and reliability of recognizing disinformation, has concluded. During our study, we used multiple methodologies and models to handle this challenging challenge, including the numerical, textual, and category layers and the caption-generating layer. We used an LSTM model in the numerical layer and achieved an astonishing 94% accuracy. The test loss was regularly less than 0.15, while the validation loss was less than 0.1, demonstrating the model’s capacity to learn patterns and produce correct predictions. The loss plot and confusion matrix that accompanied it gave thorough insights into the model’s performance, emphasizing its resilience.
Similarly, we used Multilingual BERT in the textual layer to reach an accuracy of 87%. The high BLEU score of 0.82 for English captions and 0.75 for Urdu captions revealed that the produced captions and reference captions were well aligned. These data demonstrate the multilingual BERT model’s ability to capture the core of news stories. An LSTM model exhibited excellent accuracy in the category layer, reaching 97%. The model performed admirably, with the test loss falling below 0.15 and the validation loss continuing below 0.1. The loss plot and confusion matrix accompanying the model demonstrated its high prediction ability for categorical data. The addition of the caption generation layer to our study offered vital depth. We could predict the labels of news items by creating captions for them and assessing their semantic similarity with the associated titles using cosine similarity. This broadened the area of our investigation, revealing insights into the created text’s relevancy and correctness. Using the ensemble layer, we effectively merged the strengths of the numerical, textual, category, and caption-generating layers, resulting in improved accuracy, precision, recall, and F1 scores across the different features. The ensemble technique successfully merged the disparate input from each layer, resulting in more accurate forecasts.
Compared to other models, the LSTM model consistently beat logistic regression and support vector machine (SVM) models with an accuracy of 94%. The accompanying bar chart illustrated the LSTM model’s better performance, demonstrating its efficacy in the context of false news identification. Finally, our research contributed to multilingual fake news identification by exploiting several characteristics and combining sophisticated approaches. The outcomes of the numerical, textual, category and caption-generating layers improved the accuracy and reliability of detecting fake news. This study has practical implications for countering misinformation in multilingual contexts and advances natural language processing approaches in the context of fake news identification.
Future directions
This research is comprised of many models ensembled together. So, the room for improvement is always there. Some future directions are as follows:
- Extend the research to include more languages, providing a more comprehensive view of multilingual false news identification.

- Examine the effect of adding contextual information, such as temporal or geographical aspects, in detecting fake news in various situations and geographies.

- Examine the potential of transfer learning and domain adaptation strategies for improving model performance in detecting fake news in new and unknown areas.

- Extensive experiments and assessments on real-world datasets are required to evaluate the suggested framework’s efficacy and generalizability.

- Examine ensemble techniques, which mix numerous models, including various architectures and methodologies, to improve the overall performance and accuracy of fake news detection systems.
