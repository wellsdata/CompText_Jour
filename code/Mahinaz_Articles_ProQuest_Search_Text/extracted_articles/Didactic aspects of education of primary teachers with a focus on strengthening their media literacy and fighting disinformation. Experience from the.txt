Headnote ABSTRACT
The paper describes specific experiences with educational activities aimed at strengthening media literacy in the field of combating disinformation among primary and secondary school teachers, which were implemented in 20222023 through the Central European Digital Media Observatory. A total of 3633 primary and secondary school teachers from all over the Czech Republic participated in the education and evaluated the educational events. The article presents examples of specific educational activities oriented to the development of knowledge, skills and attitudes of students through teaching in primary or secondary school.
Keywords: teacher education, media literacy, disinformation, misinformation
INTRODUCTION
Media education focuses primarily on developing a critical and reflective approach to media and media content. The aim of media education is primarily to teach individuals how to use media and technology effectively and safely, how to understand and interpret media content, and how to recognize and analyze techniques used by the media to influence public opinion (Lee, 2010; Mikulcova & Kopecky, 2020; Vorac & Kopecky, 2021; Zaghloul, 1984). Media education also includes an understanding of the legal, ethical and social implications of media use.
The main objective of media education is to strengthen media and information literacy. UNESCO defines media and information literacy as: Media and information literacy is an interrelated set of competencies that help people to maximize advantages and minimize harm in the new information, digital and communication landscapes (UNESCO, 2024). Media and information literacy covers competencies that enable people to critically and effectively engage with information, the institutions that facilitate information and diverse types of content, and the discerning use of digital technologies. Capacities in these areas are indispensable for all citizens regardless of their age or background (UNESCO, 2024). This means that education does not provide individuals with ready-made information, for example, about whatis true and whatis false, but uses the empowerment approach (Potter, 2022), in which we equip individuals with usable knowledge and skills that they can then use in further work with the media.
Media education in primary or secondary schools is implemented in various ways - sometimes it takes the form of a separate subject, sometimes the topics related to the media are cross-sectionally integrated into various subjects (mother tongue, informatics, civics, etc.), sometimes media education is not carried out at all (Kopecky et al., 2021). In this article, we draw on experience from the Czech Republic, where media education is implemented mainly as a cross-curricular theme - in the vast majority of Czech primary and secondary schools, media education is not a separate subject with a fixed amount of time (Kopecky et al., 2021).
At the same time, the majority of Czech teachers do not undergo systematic education in the field of media education as part of their professional training. The education of teachers in this area therefore takes place primarily in the form of voluntary lifelong learning, Which is provided by various organizations within the framework of various educational programs and projects. And one of such programs is implemented within the scientific research hub CEDMO
Education of teachers within the CEDMO hub
We focus on an educational course for teachers implemented by the team of the Centre for the Prevention of Risky Virtual Communication of the Faculty of Education of Palacky University in Olomouc, through the Central European Digital Media Observatory (CEDMO), which is supported by the European Union (Contract number: 2020-EU-IA-0267). One of the objectives of this project is the systematic education of primary and secondary school teachers to strengthen their digital and media literacy, offering teachers specific educational activities that they can implement with their pupils in the school environment - across subjects.
Educational lectures and seminars were attended by a total of 3633 primary and secondary teachers from all over the Czech Republic during 2022-2023 (334 offline, 3299 online). The lessons were implemented in a hybrid form - ie. live seminars were streamed and supplemented with interactive elements that allowed participants who watched the lessons to participate in the activities via online streams. This made it possible to keep their attention, ensure that they are active and at the same time engage in the lessons interactively. Lessons for teachers always lasted 2 x 60 minutes and took place outside the normal teaching hours. Teachers who attended on-site lessons received free educational materials, and materials in electronic form were then available to everyone.
Objectives and structure of lessons
Due to the large number of topics possible, we focused primarily on the issue of information perception - knowledge, skills and attitudes related to the perception and production of information - both in the online environment (on the Internet) and beyond.
When structuring the lessons, we combined explanation with practical demonstrations in which the teachers could participate. At the beginning of each part of the lesson, a very specific goal was defined, which reflected the specific knowledge and skills of the teachers.
The objectives were based on Bloom's revised taxonomy of learning objectives, especially from its
higher levels (e.g. analysis, synthesis, evaluation or creativity, etc.). Therefore, most activities are not focused on simple recall, but on active work with content (Wilson, 2016).
Thematic areas and activities
The lessons consisted of several interconnected thematic blocks that addressed key aspects of media literacy, with an emphasis on online content, disinformation, and critical thinking. The most relevant areas included:
* Freedom of expression and its legal boundaries (including classroom activities distinguishing protected speech from criminal content; Listina zakladnich prav a svobod, 1992),
* The phenomenon of infodemics and the overwhelming volume of online information,
* Cognitive biases such as confirmation bias and echo chambers,
* The role of satire and how it is sometimes mistaken for disinformation,
* Fact-checking techniques (e.g. reverse image search, source verification),
* And the formal role of media education within the Czech national curriculum (MSMT, 2021)
These areas were addressed through short, interactive activities and classroom demonstrations. Teachers engaged with real-world examples and were encouraged to reflect on their pedagogical application.
Disinformation, misinformation, malinformation, fake news and their impact
A separate part of the educational lesson was focused on the differences between the terms disinformation, misinformation, fake news, and malinformation (Canadian Centre for Cyber Security, 2022).
It is essential to distinguish between these terms, as they represent different types of false or misleading content with different motivations and consequences:
* Misinformation refers to false or inaccurate information that is shared without harmful intent. It often originates from misunderstandings or lack of fact-checking-for example, someone unknowingly spreading an outdated or incorrect news story.
* Disinformation, by contrast, is false or misleading information that is intentionally created and disseminated to deceive or manipulate its audience. This includes propaganda, conspiracy theories, or fabricated news designed to influence public opinion.
* Malinformation describes true information that is used with harmful intent, often taken out of context or weaponized to cause damage. Examples include publishing private communications to shame or intimidate individuals.
* Fake news is a widely used, but often vague term It typically refers to entirely fabricated stories, designed to resemble legitimate journalism, and is most often a form of disinformation.
These distinctions are crucial for educators and students alike, as each type of problematic content requires a different strategy for identification, analysis, and response. For example, combating disinformation requires an understanding of intent, whereas identifying misinformation often involves verification and correction.
During the lesson, teachers were introduced to reallife case studies and prompted to explore the motivations behind the creation and sharing of false content. Emphasis was placed on identifying whether the source of falsehood was ignorance, ideology, financial motivation, or manipulation.
At the same time, we discussed situations directly related to school teaching, such as whether an elementary school teacher can be dismissed for spreading misinformation among pupils. This was based on real events that occurred during the war in Ukraine (2022-2023), when a teacher, Martina Bednaïova (Vaculik, 2023; CTK, 2023), claimed in class that no war was happening in Ukraine, discredited public service media, and recommended alternative outlets. The pupils recorded the lesson and shared it with the media. The teacher was subsequently dismissed. According to the court mling (Vaculik, 2023), the school was within its rights to terminate the teacher, although the court also determined that her actions did not amount to a criminal offense such as genocide denial (CTK, 2023).
We also posed a related question to teachers: Whether a teacher has the right to disseminate disinformation in their private time. Among the 591 teachers who responded, 62% believed that a teacher cannot be dismissed for activities outside of work, while 27% disagreed, and 11% were unsure. In general, teachers cannot be dismissed for spreading disinformation outside of work unless their behavior breaks the law (e.g., inciting panic, making threats), or they represent themselves as an official of the institution (Kopecky, 2022b)
Visual disinformation in history and present
In the introductory part of this thematic block, we inform teachers that disinformation in combination with various types of propaganda has accompanied all less or more important historical events - they have manifested themselves very intensively and spread, for example, during the covid-19 pandemic (Kopecky et al., 2022), but they are often accompanied by various elections, they were part of migration crises, a lot of disinformation is also related to the European Union, etc. Historically, they have been part of most military
conflicts - including the current war in Ukraine. (Bacio Terracino & Matasic, 2022; EUvsDisinfo, 2023) However, if we want to transform these topics didactically so that they are comprehensible to pupils, it is very advantageous to use visual forms through which disinformation is spread - i.e. photographs or other graphics and videos. In our lessons, we then introduce teachers to how photographs were manipulated in various ways in the past and present, how undesirable
persons or objects were removed from photographs under the influence of censorship and propaganda, deleted or added to the shot, and for what reason.
Activities for pupils aimed at strengthening media literacy
The most important part of the lesson was devoted to specific educational activities that are devoted to verifying the veracity of information and working with
the media and information, both at primary and secondary (or university) school.
Activities focused on critical thinking and verification of textual information
The first group of activities that can be successfully implemented with pupils already at the Ist level of elementary school was focused on critical work with information - specifically, it was an assessment of commonly used statements that we encounter both when working with the media and in everyday life. These were the statements:
1. The ostrich buries its head in the sand in case of danger.
2. Bulls react aggressively to red color, therefore bullfighter muletas are red.
3. Hedgehogs love apples that they carry on their spikes.
4. Napoleon Bonaparte was small in stature.
5. Mice love cheese
6. The Vikings wore horned helmets.
7. The chameleon changes color according to the background in which it is located.
All of the above statements are not true and differ from reality, yet they are often used, for example, in film or serial media production (e.g. fairy tales, children's series), many of the statements have been established in the language (e.g. burying your head in the sand like an ostrich, it is like red paint on a bull), some statements have penetrated into advertising and are treated as true and factually correct statements. Pupils or adults therefore commonly encounter them. The aim of the activity was to verify all the above statements (factchecking, debunking) through freely available Internet tools, but also e.g. encyclopedias or other reliable sources of information. The result of the activity is the realization that even in everyday life - e.g. in the media - we encounter information that may not be substantiated and true - and therefore it is necessary to approach it critically. The activity can be included in the 1st grade of elementary school and interconnected, for example, with excerpts from film/serial production designed just for children. For pupils of higher grades, it is possible to include, for example, verification of various types of text hoaxes.
Another group of activities was focused on the verification of visual content - especially photographs. The aim was to teach pupils to handle the so-called reverse photo search - i.e. finding the original sources of photographs. This skill is very important nowadays, when photos that have been edited, their context has been shifted (e.g. place and time), have been supplemented with false commentary, etc. are very often spread via the Internet. Because we wanted the activity to be usable by pupils of the 1st grade of elementary school, we started by verifying photographs of animals, while we also mixed fantastic animals, imaginary, coming for example from popular films or series. The task was then to use reverse search to find out what kind of animal it was, and to verify whether it really exists. Typical existing animals that can confuse pupils according to photographs include, for example, tardigrades, horseshoe crab or star-nosed mole.
Another activity was focused on the verification of so-called selfie pilots, which are spread mainly by Instagram (or other social networks). These "photos" capture the pilot's selfie in flight, thousands of meters above the surface, can easily confuse the lay social media user and convince him that it is reality, but in fact they are photomontages. The pupils' task is then to find out whether the "selfies" really capture reality, or to describe (or demonstrate) how the photographs were created. Understanding how easy it is to manipulate photos is key to critical work with visual misinformation or misinformation - a learner who understands how easy it is to create a photomontage of this type will not be manipulated so easily.
For example, the educational lessons can include verification of the famous photomontage of Albert Einstein leaving the atomic bomb explosion.
When assessing photos in the online environment, it is also necessary to point out how the tone and impact of the photo on the audience will change, for example, cropping the photo, working with detail, perspective, the angle from which the photo was captured, the filter that was applied to the photo, or tools such as cloning the image or working with the so-called magic wand. As part of the course, for example, we introduced an activity to teachers in which they are asked to assess Prince William's behaviour in front of journalists based on photographs taken in 2018 (below).
The first photograph was evaluated by most participants as a rude gesture (raised middle finger), but when looking at the same situation from a different point of view, they found that their first impression was wrong, and the prince was simply showing three raised fingers to announce the birth of his third child. From this example, it is clear how important it is not to be swept away by the first impression
Activities aimed at safe use of social networks
Pupils are in contact with various types of social media and social networks from an early age, which is why activities focused on this area have been included in educational events for teachers. One of the activities was, for example, the recognition of a fake and real celebrity profile in the environment of social networks (specifically on Instagram) - participants in educational events were provided with 3 profiles and had the task of determining which profile is official. The guide was the so-called verified checkmark, which the official profiles of famous personalities are equipped with, other clues included, for example, the number of followers, etc. Other activities related to social media focused on content, its sharing and its evaluation - participants were shown on several examples how easy it is to elicit in the reader a hasty conclusion that does not correspond to
reality and which is often accompanied by strong emotions. Unfortunately, strong emotions very often accompany the spread of disinformation and can in some cases end tragically, such as the attack on the US Capitol in 2021 (Duignan, 2023).
UNIQUE CHALLENGES OF THE CZECH MEDIA ENVIRONMENT
While many countries face global trends in misinformation and disinformation, the Czech Republic presents several unique features and challenges that significantly influence how media literacy education is perceived and implemented. Understanding these specifics is essential for designing effective and sensitive interventions. Distrust
in institutions and mainstream media Czech
society has long exhibited a relatively high level of distrust toward traditional media and public institutions, a legacy partly rooted in the country's communist past (OECD, 2021). This skepticism makes it easier for alternative and fringe media sources to gain credibility and foster misinformation. Public broadcasters such as Ceska televize or Cesky rozhlas are sometimes portrayed by disinformation actors as biased or controlled, leading some segments of the population to turn to unverified sources. Disinformation
targeting the European Union Anti-EU
narratives are common in the Czech disinformation ecosystem (European Digital Media Observatory [EDMO], 2023). These messages often portray the European Union as an entity that: -
erodes national sovereignty, -
promotes unwanted liberal ideologies (e.g., gender
identity, multiculturalism), or
allegedly forces policies such as migration quotas or energy restrictions. Such
claims are frequently disseminated through alternative websites and social media platforms and have a direct influence on political attitudes, especially around elections and referenda. Migration-related
disinformation Since
the European refugee crisis of 2015, migration has remained a central topic in Czech disinformation campaigns.
False or distorted stories about refugees- claiming they are violent, unwilling to integrate, or living luxuriously on welfare-are still widespread (European Parliament, 2021). These narratives often exploit fear, xenophobia, and cultural insecurity, and they are further amplified by social media algorithms that reward emotional engagement. Pro-Russian
propaganda and the war in Ukraine The
Russian invasion of Ukraine in 2022 triggered a wave of pro-Kremlin narratives in the Czech online space (Central European Digital Media Observatory [CEDMO], 2023; Gongala et al., 2024). Some of the most frequent disinformation claims included: -
- Denial of war crimes or civilian casualties, -
Depictions of Ukraine as a corrupt or Naziaffiliated state, -
Blaming NATO or the West for provoking conflict, -
- Presenting Russia as a legitimate liberator or peacekeeper.
These
messages are often disseminated by so-called "alternative media" outlets that claim to offer "hidden truths" not covered by mainstream media. A significant portion of the population, especially among older adults and marginalized communities, has been exposed to these narratives, complicating public discourse and societal cohesion. Social
media polarization and emotional manipulation In
recent years, Czech social media has become a highly polarized space, with users increasingly operating in ideological echo chambers. Strong emotional appeals, especially anger and fear, are commonly used to manipulate readers. Content that provokes outrage or confirms existing biases is more likely to be shared, regardless of its accuracy. This
environment presents major challenges for educators. Addressing disinformation or teaching students to verify information can provoke hostility from parents or school administrators, especially when the topics intersect with political views. Teachers must therefore not only navigate misinformation but also mediate between educational goals and sociopolitical sensitivities in their communities. EVALUATION

DISCUSSION AND RECOMMENDATION
Although media literacy is increasingly recognized as essential in the digital age, the Czech Republic faces several structural and systemic barriers that limit its effective implementation in schools. One of the core issues is the absence of a dedicated media education subject within the national curriculum. Media education is designated only as a cross-curricular theme, without a fixed number of hours, leaving its implementation entirely up to individual teachers. This leads to inconsistency and often neglect of the topic, especially in schools that prioritize traditional subject performance.
In addition, formal training in media literacy is largely absent from pre-service teacher education. Most future teachers do not receive systematic instruction in media education during their university studies, and inservice training is typically voluntary and project-based, reaching only those who already have an interest in the topic.
Further compounding these challenges is a lack of updated and accessible teaching materials. While teachers in other subjects have access to comprehensive textbook series, the supply of high-quality, up-to-date resources for media education remains limited. Many printed materials are outdated, and though some NGOs and academic institutions offer digital materials, these are unevenly distributed and not always well integrated into school practice.
Another major issue is curricular overload. Teachers often report that the existing curriculum is already too demanding, leaving little room to address crosscurricular themes such as media education. Even when teachers want to incorporate media literacy topics, they may not have the time or institutional support to do so meaningfully.
Lastly, political sensitivities surrounding mediarelated topics -particularly disinformation-pose a challenge. In some school communities, discussions about propaganda, fake news, or media manipulation may provoke parental backlash or accusations of political bias. This leads many teachers to avoid these topics altogether, further limiting students" exposure to critical media analysis.
Previous research (Kopecky et al., 2021) suggests that Czech teachers want media education to be actively included in teaching at primary and secondary schools, but at the same time they do not want this to happen at
the expense of other subjects. At the same time, research demonstrates that Czech teachers have in most cases not undergone systematic education in the field of media education as part of their professional training, and this situation has not fundamentally changed even after they have entered practice. For this reason, it is necessary:
To train teachers systematically in this area (already in higher education),
To offer teachers suggestions for activities aimed at supporting media education, which they can include in teaching in various subjects,
To provide teachers with didactic support (materials, etc.),
To strive to make media education an integral part of education - not just an optional part of it.
Ongoing revisions of educational programs do propose that media education should exist as a separate subject, or that the key topics of media education would have a minimum fixed time frame in the curricula. Although the state publicly proclaims the importance of strengthening media literacy among the population, it does not implement systemic measures. Its role is often substituted by private companies (02 Czech Republic, Google, Microsoft etc.).
The State of Media Education at Primary and Secondary Schools (ISNS, 2023) points out that 20% of primary schools and 10% of secondary schools in the Czech Republic do not pay attention to media education at all. Factors affecting implementation include lack of time, lack of teacher training in this area, lack of materials, etc. While teachers of regular compulsory subjects have many updated textbook series available to use in their teaching, the supply of textbooks for media education on the Czech market is very limited and printed textbooks are often outdated in this area. However, hundreds of other educational materials are freely available to teachers, containing, for example, suggestions for teaching activities and concrete examples of activities aimed at developing media literacy.
The need for media education remains. A study conducted in the Czech Republic (Mikulcova & Kopecky, 2020) on a sample of 1444 respondents from the 6th to the 9th grade of elementary school (11-15 years) points out that approximately 39% of them verify information in the online world, 17% do not verify information in the online world at all, the remaining group then decides according to the situation - e.g., 49% of pupils do not verify their photographs online. Both they and their teachers need education to support their media literacy.
Limits. This research by the Czech team within the CEDMO hub focused on a narrowly defined area of media education focused topics related to disinformation and misinformation. Another limitation is that teachers who are interested in these topics volunteered for the events, but a large proportion of teachers are not interested in education in this area. Their experiences might differ.
