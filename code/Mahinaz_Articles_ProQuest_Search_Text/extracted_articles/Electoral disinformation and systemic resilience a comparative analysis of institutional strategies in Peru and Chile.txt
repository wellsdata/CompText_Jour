1 Introduction: the epistemic crisis at the electoral threshold
The stability of contemporary democratic systems faces a structural crisis originating from the fragmentation of truth in the public sphere. Globally, and with a particular impact in Latin America, the deterioration of citizen trust in representative systems and the press has generated a scenario of systemic vulnerability (Economist Intelligence Unit [EIU], 2025;Latinobarómetro, 2023). In this context, electoral disinformation is no longer a marginal externality but has consolidated into a centralized and industrialized political strategy (Marino-Jiménez et al., 2025). By weaponizing digital ecosystems and leveraging algorithmic amplification, these campaigns deliberately exploit societal fissures to foster affective polarization (Tucker et al., 2018). Ultimately, this industrialization of deceit transcends the mere manipulation of electoral outcomes; it aggressively erodes the shared epistemological baseline essential for democratic deliberation, pushing fragile representative institutions toward a state of chronic instability (Bennett and Livingston, 2018;Waisbord, 2018). As posited byHan (2022), the truth is a necessary convention that holds society together; without it, society disintegrates from within.
This extended phenomenon is particularly embedded in regimes characterized as hybrid democracies , which preserve the electoral ritual but exhibit documented deficits in the rule of law and the independence of powers (Barrenechea and Encinas, 2022;V-Dem Institute, 2024). The complexity of this threat falls within what the literature terms “information disorder”, an ecosystem where false information and deliberate manipulation are disseminated to cause institutional harm (Wardle and Derakhshan, 2017). Additionally, the incorporation of artificial intelligence and algorithmic microsegmentation has escalated the capacity of disinforming actors to create tailored realities, exacerbating the polarization of the electorate (Goldstein et al., 2023;IDEA Internacional, 2024).
Facing this juncture, the dynamics of public communication employ key terms that shape media messages, redefine social meanings, and catalyze processes of aggression (Marino-Jiménez et al., 2025). Faced with this deepening credibility vacuum, electoral management bodies have been forced to expand their traditional logistical competencies to encompass proactive epistemic defense. Multilateral organizations suggest that current democratic resilience requires these institutions to assume roles of civic education and collaborative governance to protect the integrity of the process (Programa de las Naciones Unidas para el Desarrollo [PNUD], 2024).
To operationalize these new institutional mandates, Peru and Chile offer paradigmatic, yet contrasting, case studies within the Latin American context. Peru exemplifies a highly fragmented and polarized landscape where electoral authorities operate under severe institutional harassment and systemic distrust (Mendoza, 2022). In contrast, Chile represents a historically resilient system that recently faced unprecedented informational stress tests during its constitutional processes, necessitating rapid-response mechanisms against campaigns targeting democratic integrity (Anguita et al., 2023;Lazcano-Peña et al., 2025). Along these lines, the National Jury of Elections (JNE) of Peru, through its Guía contra la desinformación (JNE, 2025a) and its Fact-Checking Committee, proposes an educational and oversight intervention aimed at equipping citizens with tools for scrutiny. In parallel, the Electoral Service of Chile (Servel) has consolidated a containment model through the active monitoring of social media, debunking efforts and alliances with fact-checkers, traditional media, and technological companies to curb hostile narratives directed against electoral logistics.
Understanding these institutional responses requires a shift from linear analyses of isolated falsehoods to a structural approach. Within these contested electoral environments, the propagation of manipulated content is driven by deeply entrenched archetypes of systemic behavior. Two critical patterns stand out. First, the Escalate archetype manifests as a digital arms race, triggering a reinforcing cycle of mutual aggression and polarization as factions deploy retaliatory narratives. Second, the Success to the Successful archetype dictates the digital distribution of this content; algorithms disproportionately reward emotionally charged bias with visibility, effectively starving fact-based civic discourse of reach (Pjesivac and Little, 2025;Senge, 2006). Ultimately, information manipulation strategies succeed not merely by deceiving voters, but by exploiting these underlying feedback loops, structurally optimizing the ecosystem for disinformation and undermining long-term democratic integrity.
Therefore, the main objective of this paper is to comparatively analyze the intervention strategies deployed by the JNE (Peru) and Servel (Chile) against information disorder. Through the application of systems thinking models and theories of public communication, the study deconstructs both institutional architectures to identify their operational successes and normative limits. It advances the hypothesis that while official oversight and debunking instruments are crucial first lines of defense, their ultimate efficacy relies on serving as catalysts for a broader participatory ecosystem. Consequently, the work concludes by outlining how these institutional efforts can successfully bridge the civic transition toward the figure of the émirec —the critically empowered sender-receiver (Aparici and García-Marín, 2018;Cloutier, 1973;Marino-Jiménez, 2023a)—an indispensable condition for breaking negative feedback loops, establishing collaborative governance networks, and contributing to restoring the democratic health of the region.
2 Theoretical framework: the architecture of disinformation and the power of key terms
To explicitly ground the theoretical framework, it is necessary to establish the operative meaning of the three central analytical nodes that sustain this study: affective polarization, hybrid democracy, and echo chambers.
First, in the context of this research, affective polarization operates as a foundational dynamic where political adversaries are ontologically reduced to existential enemies, leading to the systematic closure of deliberative spaces. Drawing upon the scholarly contributions ofTucker et al. (2018)andChattopadhyay (2026), the operative meaning assigned to this concept focuses on how the strategic instrumentalization of key terms and disinformation bypasses rational deliberation to provoke intense emotional responses, such as tribal allegiance or visceral aversion. Ultimately, this dynamic establishes a destructive loop of reciprocal aggression that severely erodes institutional transparency and the shared epistemic baseline required for democratic stability.
Second, drawing upon the political science frameworks established byBarrenechea and Encinas (2022), as well as indices from theV-Dem Institute (2024)and theEconomist Intelligence Unit (2024), a hybrid democracy—or hybrid regime—is operatively defined as a political system that nominally preserves electoral rituals while simultaneously exhibiting documented deficits in the rule of law and the independence of powers. Within this study, the concept highlights regimes that combine democratic institutions with authoritarian practices, creating a highly vulnerable political culture defined by profound citizen distrust toward the state. This specific structural fragility generates a societal disconnect and a credibility vacuum that hostile, industrialized disinformation campaigns actively exploit.
Finally, grounded in the media and communication traditions ofJamieson and Capella (2010)andPariser (2011), echo chambers are conceptualized as structurally isolating, self-validating epistemic enclaves. The study assigns an operative meaning to this term that emphasizes the sociotechnical architecture of digital platforms, where mechanisms like algorithmic microsegmentation exponentially magnify the rhetorical dissemination of tailored realities. By enclosing citizens within these curated bubbles, echo chambers maximize emotional contagion and structurally optimize the ecosystem for disinformation, effectively precluding substantive ideological debate and eroding the unified epistemological baseline essential for a healthy public sphere.
2.1 The information ecosystem and the systemic threat
To understand the vulnerability of contemporary electoral processes, it is imperative to analyze the structural conditions that facilitate the viralization of falsehoods. In regimes conceptualized as hybrid democracies, the preservation of electoral formalities coexists with deficits in the rule of law and a profound distrust toward institutions (Barrenechea and Encinas, 2022). In this environment, a disconnect occurs where the reality narrated by official channels differs from citizen experience, creating a credibility vacuum that is filled by hostile narratives (Marino-Jiménez et al., 2023b).
The propagation of disinformation in these spaces is not due to the spontaneous generation of erroneous data, but rather to coordinated behavioral patterns. For instance,Rubio-Núñez et al. (2024)outline several applications of AI in electoral processes, many designed to manipulate and infoxicate reality. These include, but are not limited to, automated messaging systems used to spread deepfakes, cheapfakes, and hoaxes, as well as strategies to manufacture the appearance of grassroots social mobilization online; a phenomenon known as astroturfing. Such tactics have become defining features of contemporary political campaigns, characterized as hallmarks of a new era of algorithmic communication.
To study this phenomenon, international organizations have adopted analytical models such as the FIMI (Foreign Information Manipulation and Interference) framework, developed by the European External Action Service (European Union, 2023). The purpose of FIMI is to analyze, from a systematic perspective, how certain networks operate in a coordinated manner—even through behaviors that are not strictly illegal—with the intention of affecting democratic processes and values. Recently, Ibero-American scientific literature has successfully adapted this taxonomy to domestic electoral contexts, proving its efficacy beyond foreign interference (Serrano-Maíllo and Corredoira, 2024). Applied systematically by research hubs like the Observatorio Complutense de Desinformación , the FIMI methodology has been instrumental in tracking how hostile narratives damage confidence in electoral systems across Spain and Latin America (Cetina-Presuel and Gaete-Salgado, 2025). Adapting this taxonomy to the regional communicative ecosystem therefore allows disinformation to be identified not as an isolated anomaly, but as a systemic failure involving threat actors, vectors of algorithmic propagation, and structural vulnerabilities.
2.2 The epistemic load of key terms in electoral disinformation
The efficacy of contemporary manipulation architectures relies less on the mere fabrication of empirical falsehoods and more on the strategic instrumentalization of key terms (Marino-Jiménez et al., 2025). Interdisciplinary literature suggests that these lexical units transcend their basic referential function, operating instead as semiotic catalysts for affective polarization that fundamentally restructure the parameters of public discourse. This capacity to induce epistemic disruption is grounded in four interwoven analytical dimensions:- 1)
Neurocognitive affective processing: Current neuroscientific research establishes that highly charged key terms function as biological triggers, stimulating neural pathways associated with identity consolidation and threat detection (Lee and Satpute, 2024). Within the context of electoral disinformation, this neurocognitive vulnerability is systematically exploited: the deployment of semantically dense terminology bypasses rational deliberation to provoke intense affective responses—such as tribal allegiance or visceral aversion. This emotional hijacking effectively inhibits higher-order executive functions and critical reasoning, thereby obstructing the citizen's capacity to process empirically grounded counter-narratives or official evidence (Flores-Núñez et al., 2023).
- 2)
Semiotic reductionism and identity formation: In environments characterized by sociopolitical tension, key terms operate as floating signifiers that compress hyper-complex political phenomena into binary, reductionist schemas. These signs acquire profound polysemic depth as they are assimilated into specific sociocultural matrices (Greimas and Fontanille, 2002;Kress and Van Leeuwen, 2021). The systematic saturation of the public sphere with pejorative labels actively precludes substantive ideological debate, forging instead reactive, antagonized political identities predicated on fallacious dichotomies. This semiotic atomization severely constrains the electorate's ontological grasp of viable democratic alternatives.
- 3)
Performativity and strategic framing: Rooted in the linguistic turn, this dimension acknowledges the performative power of language to not merely describe, but to construct and legitimize intersubjective political realities (Rorty, 1990). By inoculating the public discourse with specific key terms, malign actors enforce rigid interpretive schemas, or frames (Entman, 1993;Goffman, 1974), which selectively amplify distinct facets of an event to dictate public perception. This framing mechanism effectively recalibrates the electorate's causal attributions and moral evaluations, manipulating the epistemic landscape without necessarily altering the underlying objective facts (Scheufele, 1999).
- 4)
Algorithmic amplification and structural echo: The inherent rhetorical efficacy of these key terms (Perelman and Olbrechts, 1994) is exponentially magnified by the structural imperatives of contemporary digital architectures. Mechanisms such as algorithmic microsegmentation and hashtag proliferation automate rhetorical dissemination, facilitating the targeted distribution of tailored realities (Majdik and Graham, 2024). This sociotechnical dynamic entrenches the architecture of filter bubbles and echo chambers, structurally isolating citizens within highly curated, self-validating epistemic enclaves. Consequently, emotional contagion and viral propagation are maximized, fundamentally eroding the shared epistemic baseline required for democratic deliberation (Jamieson and Capella, 2010;Pariser, 2011).
Ultimately, the multidimensional architecture of key terms exposes the profound epistemic asymmetry that characterizes contemporary electoral disinformation. These manipulative tactics systematically bypass rational deliberation through affective hijacking and structural saturation.Rubio-Núñez et al. (2024)argue that the contemporary public sphere has been fundamentally altered, rendering it unsuitable for democratic deliberation: “In a context of informational chaos, the casting of an informed vote is threatened by the amalgamation of opinions and claims, and the overlapping of true and false information that floods digital spaces, creating a kind of paralysis. (…) Furthermore, electoral contests increasingly unfold within an environment of cognitive warfare (…) in which emotionally charged communicative resources act as weapons to attract votes, consciously or not, pushing to the background the debate –and presentation– of future projects and solutions to the country's concrete needs, whether historical or circumstantial” (pp. 80–81).
While the verification mechanisms deployed by institutions such as the JNE and Servel provide an essential foundation for electoral transparency, the structural complexity of modern disinformation often extends beyond the reach of localized debunking. These official countermeasures, grounded in a necessary factual paradigm, must continuously navigate a landscape dominated by highly orchestrated semiotic and emotional offensives. Recognizing this dynamic is fundamental to the core objective of this study. By deconstructing the epistemic load of these manipulated narratives, it becomes evident that top-down institutional oversight functions most effectively when integrated into a broader systemic response. Consequently, this theoretical framework supports the paper's central thesis: the imperative to complement official efforts by fostering a civic transition toward the émirec , cultivating a critically autonomous electorate capable of dismantling pervasive negative feedback loops from within the social fabric itself.
2.3 Systemic dynamics and archetypes of conflict
The operationalization of key terms within hyper-polarized information ecosystems can be rigorously modeled through the lens of systems thinking (Arnold and Wade, 2015;Rodrich-Portugal et al., 2023;Senge, 2006). This methodological approach transcends simplistic analyses of linear causality, utilizing systemic archetypes to map the complex feedback loops that govern the long-term structural behavior of communicative networks. Specifically, two distinct archetypes can be identified to explain the systemic degradation of public discourse (Marino-Jiménez et al., 2025):
The first model is the Success to the Successful archetype, clearly observable within the structural imperatives of the digital attention economy (Metzler and Garcia, 2023). In this self-reinforcing cycle, sensationalist or fabricated content—strategically engineered with key terms to exploit cognitive biases—captures immediate algorithmic rewards through engagement metrics (clicks and impressions) (Milli et al., 2023). This short-term success, which can be seen exemplified by clickbaits, incentivizes political and media actors to systematically prioritize virality over veracity (Vosoughi et al., 2018), structurally undermining the overarching quality of the information ecosystem and marginalizing rigorous, fact-based journalism (Jácobo-Morales and Marino-Jiménez, 2024;Van Raemdonck and Meyer, 2024).
The second model is the Escalate archetype, a dominant dynamic within the contemporary political sphere. This paradigm delineates a destructive loop of reciprocal aggression: the deployment of incendiary rhetoric or targeted disinformation by one actor invariably triggers an escalating, reactive response from their opponent. The systemic consequence of this compounding mutual threat is the severe erosion of institutional transparency and the systematic closure of deliberative spaces. This collapse gives rise to profound affective polarization, wherein political adversaries are ontologically reduced to existential enemies (Chattopadhyay, 2026). Consequently, the efficacy of institutional interventions in the electoral domain must be rigorously evaluated based on their capacity to disrupt and recalibrate these deeply entrenched archetypal structures.
Understanding these self-reinforcing dynamics underscores the paper's central hypothesis: linear countermeasures, such as reactive fact-checking, frequently fail to dismantle the underlying architecture of disinformation. As summarized byBateman and Jackson (2024), exposure to factual information does not necessarily lead to a behavioural or attitudinal change, especially in polarized or politically charged topics, such as the ones prevailing during electoral processes. “In general, studies find strong evidence of confirmation bias: subjects are more susceptible to false claims that align with preexisting beliefs or allegiances and are more resistant to fact-checks associated with an opposing political party or its positions” (p. 32). Thus, achieving genuine electoral resilience requires transcending top-down containment and cultivating the émirec —empowering citizens to consciously break these structural negative feedback loops from within.
3 Methodology: a systemic-comparative approach to information disorder
3.1 Research design and epistemological justification
The analysis employs a qualitative comparative methodological design, structured as a multiple-case study, drawing upon a primary corpus of official institutional resolutions, operational guidelines, and public policy frameworks. The deliberate selection of the JNE in Peru and Servel in Chile responds directly to the empirical viability of contrasting the strategic interventions and normative frameworks of homologous state entities. Both institutions currently operate within deeply stressed informative ecosystems impacted by impending, highly polarized electoral processes: the 2025 presidential and parliamentary cycle in Chile, and the massive 2026 general, regional, and municipal electoral cycle in Peru.
The selection of Peru and Chile is theoretically driven by their contrasting vulnerabilities within the Latin American democratic spectrum. While they face identical FIMI tactics aimed at delegitimizing electoral logistics, their structural baselines differ radically: Peru faces these threats from the extreme fragility of a “hybrid regime” lacking a cohesive party system (Bornschier, 2016;Waldner and Lust, 2018), whereas Chile confronts them as a historically robust system currently experiencing the rapid civic exhaustion characteristic of a downgraded “flawed democracy” following recent social outbursts (Muñoz-Rodríguez et al., 2024). Comparing these juxtaposed realities provides a robust framework to test the elasticity and efficacy of distinct institutional countermeasures.
Rather than a purely inductive exploration, this study deductively applies the FIMI and DISARM frameworks (González et al., 2025) to evaluate whether theoretical systemic archetypes accurately map onto real-world institutional responses. It must be acknowledged that relying on secondary institutional data, legal frameworks, and official reports inherently limits the analysis to the visible, documented actions of these electoral bodies (Dalglish et al., 2020), potentially obscuring informal institutional negotiations or the internal, unrecorded deliberations that shape public policy (Hodgson, 2025).
This specific comparative design facilitates the precise identification of operative divergences, resource allocations, strategic focus areas, and normative limits in institutional responses to highly analogous digital manipulation tactics. To thoroughly substantiate the analysis, a systemic literature review was executed. The systemic approach utilizes empirical literature, raw institutional data, active legal resolutions, and official operational guides as primary data sources to map the interconnected components of a highly complex, nonlinear phenomenon (Prakash et al., 2023).
3.2 Integration of the FIMI taxonomy and DISARM framework
Applying established typologies of information disorder (Wardle and Derakhshan, 2017), the “electoral fraud” narrative functions primarily through fabricated content and false context . It deliberately detaches genuine logistical anomalies from their operational reality, repackaging them to artificially manufacture a crisis of legitimacy. This specific typology is highly corrosive because it weaponizes the democratic process against itself, utilizing the uncertainty of vote-counting to erode institutional trust.
To successfully operationalize the massive volume of qualitative, legal, and operational data collected from these electoral bodies, this methodology extensively adapts the FIMI framework and its associated DISARM (Disinformation Analysis and Response Measures, frequently adapted in institutional contexts as Risk Management) ontology (Terp and Breuer, 2022). Developed as a cognitive security adaptation of traditional cybersecurity frameworks, the DISARM ontology provides a standardized, open-source matrix for classifying the behavioral lifecycle of information operations. It structures disinformation campaigns into chronological phases—such as Plan, Prepare, Execute, and Assess—allowing analysts to systematically track how narratives are seeded, artificially amplified, and deliberately obscured. While the European External Action Service (EEAS) originally utilized FIMI to track and attribute foreign interference operations, DISARM's taxonomy of Tactics, Techniques, and Procedures (TTPs) provides an architectural matrix equally adept at mapping domestic disinformation campaigns designed by internal political actors.

To illustrate the operational utility of the FIMI matrix within the Andean context, one can trace a standard incident frequently observed in the Peruvian electoral landscape: the systematic propagation of “electoral fraud” narratives. During the Plan and Prepare phases of the DISARM ontology, threat actors synthesize “shallowfakes” or artificially altered images depicting allegedly manipulated voting tally sheets. This stage involves coordinated efforts by inauthentic networks to manufacture political misinformation that exploits pre-existing societal fissures (Terp and Breuer, 2022).
In the Execute phase, these audiovisual assets are injected into the ecosystem using algorithmic microsegmentation on platforms like TikTok—identified as a primary propagation vector for rapid, emotionally resonant disinformation during current electoral cycles. These narratives are intentionally designed to bypass rational scrutiny and trigger immediate affective responses, such as tribal allegiance or visceral outrage. Research indicates that such “emotionally charged communicative resources” function as neurocognitive triggers, inhibiting higher-order executive functions and obstructing a citizen's capacity to process official evidence or empirically grounded counter-narratives (Flores-Núñez et al., 2023).
Finally, the campaign launders these narratives into closed, encrypted networks, actively exploiting the underlying structural vulnerability of profound institutional distrust. This process facilitates the creation of filter bubbles and echo chambers that structurally isolate citizens within self-validating epistemic enclaves. By mapping this specific behavioral “kill chain”, the methodology transitions from a theoretical framework to a highly actionable diagnostic tool, allowing electoral bodies to pinpoint exactly where to apply systemic friction and strengthen democratic resilience (Jamieson and Capella, 2010;Pariser, 2011).
3.3 Systems thinking modeling: institutional countermeasures vs. causal loops Once categorized and tagged under the FIMI taxonomy, the normative resolutions and protocols deployed by the JNE and Servel are subjected to advanced systems thinking modeling. This method visualizes the underlying, invisible architecture of disinformation by identifying specific archetypal feedback loops, moving beyond simple cause-and-effect linear observations (Arnold and Wade, 2015).
By using the Success to the Successful and Escalate archetypes (Marino-Jiménez et al., 2025) as conceptual mapping tools, this methodology answers a critical question: Do the regulatory and operational measures implemented by these electoral bodies act solely upon the superficial symptoms of information disorder (such as the episodic, post-facto debunking of a specific FIMI incident), or do they possess the structural leverage necessary to alter the underlying root causes that perpetually fuel the cycles of aggression and algorithmic manipulation? To achieve this, the methodology unpacks the specific dynamics of each archetype. The Success to the Successful model reveals how the pursuit of immediate, algorithm-driven virality systematically undermines long-term informational quality, creating a reinforcing loop that rewards sensationalism over veracity (Kafiliveyjuyeh et al., 2025). Simultaneously, the Escalate archetype illustrates how reciprocal aggression and affective polarization create a detrimental feedback loop that steadily erodes dialogue and institutional transparency (Berntzen, 2025). By applying these models, the analysis transcends linear metrics—like the mere volume of debunked falsehoods—to evaluate whether institutional countermeasures actually propose specific corrective models. It tests if actions like Servel's communication's strategies or the JNE's ethical pacts successfully advocate for synergistic approaches in digital environments or establish dialogue-based frameworks that foster shared commitments over hostility (Table 2).

4 Regulatory review, institutional architectures, and systemic defense
4.1 Democratic classification and ecosystem vulnerability The comparative analysis of Peru and Chile necessitates situating their political systems within standardized, globally recognized regional metrics. According to the Democracy Index generated by theEIU (2024), both nations exhibit distinct, concerning characteristics that acutely highlight the broader institutional decline currently afflicting Latin America.
The Hybrid Regime in Peru: The EIU classifies Peru strictly as a “hybrid regime”. This specific block groups nations that nominally combine democratic institutions with authoritarian practices, displaying profound weaknesses in governance, rule of law, and a highly vulnerable political culture. In the context of the impending 2026 General, Regional, and Municipal Elections (ONPE, 2026), Peru faces an exceptionally complex scenario with extreme political fragmentation—featuring almost 40 registered political parties actively competing (CSIS, 2026). Furthermore, there is a staggering disconnect between the citizenry and the state. Recent data indicates that 91% of the population does not feel represented by Congress (Instituto de Estudios Peruanos [IEP], 2025), and trust in political parties sits at a dismal 9% (Latinobarómetro, 2023). Since 2021, a systematic domestic campaign utilizing FIMI TTPs has propagated “narratives of fraud”, transforming the ecosystem into a highly fertile breeding ground for industrialized disinformation (International IDEA, 2026).
The Flawed Democracy in Chile: Conversely, Chile has experienced a marked reduction in its democratic quality indicators over recent years, dropping from a “full democracy” to a “flawed democracy” (EIU, 2024). According to the Democracy Index, Chile's downgrade is largely driven by declining scores in specific categories, notably the functioning of government and political participation; a trend that has intensified in the wake of the political crisis triggered by the 2019 “estallido social”, and the subsequent attempts of constitutional reform. While the Chilean system consistently organizes free, fair, and logistically sound elections, these severe deficiencies in political participation have been exacerbated by the profound civic exhaustion and disaffection stemming from multiple recent, unsuccessful constitutional plebiscites (Lazcano-Peña et al., 2025;Undurraga, 2023). Despite possessing significantly greater baseline institutional solidity than its neighbors, Chile's recent crises of representation have deeply exposed its vulnerabilities to polarising disinformation. This is particularly evident in the rise of coordinated digital communities dedicated to spreading FIMI-style narratives that aim to artificially install the idea of electoral fraud and cast doubt on the logistical integrity of Servel's vote-counting mechanisms (Servel, 2025). Although such narratives remain less prominent than ad-hominem attacks between candidates or the manipulation of information targeting government authorities, the integrity of electoral information has become a salient concern in Chile's public debate, as the preservation of public trust and the sound functioning of its electoral system remain overarching priorities (Observatorio Complutense de Desinformación, 2026) 1 .
4.2 Peru: the JNE's fact-checking architecture and normative containment
Operating within a high-threat, low-trust ecosystem, the National Jury of Elections (JNE) of Peru has aggressively expanded its operational mandate to combat digital falsehoods ahead of the complex 2026 electoral cycle. The JNE's institutional approach is heavily characterized by a robust, formalized institutionalization of internal fact-checking protocols and highly structured normative agreements.
Resolution N° 4-2025-DCGI-JNE and the Fact-Checking Committee: To formalize its defense mechanisms against the massive volume of FIMI incidents, the JNE updated its institutional Fact-Checking Committee, legally codified via Resolution N° 4-2025-DCGI-JNE (JNE, 2025b). This is not an ad hoc group, but a formalized, multidisciplinary team comprising six titular members and five alternates drawn from the highest levels of the institution, including the Cabinet of Advisors, the Secretariat General, the Directorate of Communications and Image (DCI), and the National Directorate of Education and Civic Formation (DNEF). This internal committee is specifically tasked with monitoring and debunking false information in real-time across platforms like Facebook, WhatsApp, X, TikTok, and Instagram. The empirical data underscores the severity of the threat: over a 12-month period (March 2025–February 2026) leading into the 2026 cycle, the JNE registered exactly 1,000 alerts of disinformation. This figure, initially communicated via social channels (JNE, 2026a), is fully corroborated by official institutional reporting (JNE, 2026b). Crucially, over 75% of these alerts were heavily concentrated in the three-month span of December 2025, January 2026, and February 2026—peaking at 296 alerts in February alone—aligning with the intensity of primary elections and heightened campaign activity (JNE, 2026b). Within this surge, TikTok has continued to emerge as the primary propagation vector for audiovisual manipulation and deepfakes.
The Guía contra la desinformación : The operational blueprint guiding the JNE's efforts is comprehensively outlined in the Guía contra la desinformación - Segunda Edición (JNE, 2025a). This document establishes five highly specific strategic pillars of intervention:- Education and Awareness: Deployment of digital literacy workshops to teach verification.
- Monitoring and Early Detection: Utilizing AI-driven social listening tools and “Narrative Mapping” to classify recurring false narratives by risk level.
- Verification and Rapid Response: Immediate official messaging and collaboration with digital platforms to report violating content.
- Strategic Alliances: Formation of the Aliados contra la desinformación network with civil society.
- Institutional Strengthening: Proactive transparency and proposed adaptations to legal frameworks to explicitly sanction malicious disinformation intended to manipulate the vote.
The Pacto Ético Electoral (Ethical Electoral Pact): Complementing its digital defense, the JNE attempts to regulate the behavior of the threat actors themselves—the political parties. For the 2026 elections, 29 political organizations signed the Pacto Ético Electoral , committing to conduct campaigns based on programmatic proposals and explicitly rejecting reciprocal aggression and disinformation. This pact is overseen by a recently installed, independent citizen Tribunal of Honor (comprising three experts: Carol Zavaleta Cortijo, Mariela Noles Cotito, and Óscar Salazar Gamboa), functioning as a tool for democratic self-regulation.
Through the lens of systems thinking, the JNE's architecture primarily functions as a balancing loop attempting to counteract the Escalate archetype (Marino-Jiménez et al., 2025). The Ethical Pact directly attempts to cool the cycle of reciprocal aggression by imposing moral oversight on candidates. This aligns precisely with systemic corrective models that advocate for dialogue-based frameworks in politics to foster shared commitments over hostility. However, while the AI-assisted monitoring and the Fact-Checking Committee are technically efforts to disrupt the algorithm-driven virality of the Success to the Successful archetype, they confront severe structural limitations within a hybrid regime. In an environment characterized by deep systemic distrust, the “debunk” issued by an official state entity is frequently rejected outright by the highly polarized electorate due to confirmation bias. The JNE is effectively attempting to neutralize a highly orchestrated, emotionally saturated semiotic offensive with purely factual, localized, top-down data. Consequently, these institutional countermeasures underscore that official verification alone is insufficient; they must act as a catalyst for a structural civic transition. To truly dismantle these systemic feedback loops, the Peruvian model must evolve to cultivate the émirec , empowering the electorate to critically filter and manage manipulative narratives from within the digital network before they achieve viral saturation.
4.3 Chile: Servel's proactive containment and collaborative governance
In stark contrast to Peru's highly formalized, internally centralized fact-checking committee, the Electoral Service of Chile (Servel) has evolved its strategy toward a model of predictive technological containment and horizontally integrated collaborative governance, primarily focused on securing the 2025 presidential and parliamentary elections.
Technological containment and the Sistema de Tasación de Riesgo Digital : Servel's operational frontline relies heavily on advanced data analytics and active social media monitoring to protect electoral integrity (Servicio Electoral de Chile [Servel], 2023). To establish communicative guidelines for addressing disinformation, the institution employs sophisticated digital risk assessment frameworks grounded in big data analysis 2 . Specifically, the Communications' Office monitors digital communities through the implementation of social listening software, a practice in place since 2020 3 . These monitoring efforts inform institutional responses by assessing threat indicators directed at either the electoral authority or the electoral process itself. Accordingly, if a piece of content posed some risk but remained confined to a specific digital community, or if a narrative spread more broadly but did not target critical aspects of the election, the electoral body chose not to intervene (Observatorio Complutense de Desinformación, 2026, pp. 45–46).
On the contrary, when documented narratives did represent a threat –particularly those whose sustained objective is to artificially and systematically entrench the notion of electoral fraud, a tactic designed to irreparably damage Servel's institutional image and erode democratic trust (Santos et al., 2025;Servel, 2026)– the institutional response encompassed a range of measures: from direct communication with fact-checking outlets and news media to disseminate official information, to proactively publishing debunks through social media and its web repository of Noticias Falsas 4 . This mechanism focuses explicitly on neutralizing operational disinformation, acting in real time against false claims regarding vote-counting procedures, voter roll anomalies, or impediments to voting. Notably, during the 2025 electoral cycle, institutional responses prioritized direct communication with news media and fact-checkers over the direct publication of official debunks (Observatorio Complutense de Desinformación, 2026).
The Mesa de Gobernanza para la Integridad de la Información : The most structurally significant and innovative element of the Chilean model is the Mesa de Gobernanza para la Integridad de la Información en Procesos Electorales . Co-led by Servel and the United Nations Development Programme (UNDP), this multisectoral space represents a pioneering, horizontal approach to electoral integrity in Latin America (Programa de las Naciones Unidas para el Desarrollo [PNUD], 2025). Formally established in 2024 to anticipate the consecutive election cycles, it articulates coordinated defense efforts between public institutions, global tech platforms (including Meta, Google, TikTok, and X), traditional broadcast media via the National Television Association (Anatel), the National Television Council (CNTV), fact-checking organizations, and academia. The proactive effectiveness of this collaborative ecosystem has positioned Chile as a regional benchmark for institutional resilience against industrialized disinformation.
The defining regulatory output of this governance table was the signing of the Compromiso Ético por la Integridad de la Información Electoral in September 2025. Unlike a traditional political pact that merely regulates candidates (such as the Peruvian model), this commitment deeply integrates the actual structural vectors of propagation—global tech platforms and national television networks—into the defensive matrix. It mandates the promotion of verifiable information, the rapid containment of hate speech, the safeguarding of electoral logistics, and specifically targets the prevention of digital violence against women in politics, a growing democratic threat in the region (Consejo Nacional de Televisión [CNTV], 2025). Furthermore, broadcasters, represented by Anatel, explicitly committed to rigorous source verification during electoral coverage and debates, acknowledging their unique public responsibility in securing the information pipeline before it reaches the electorate (Anatel, 2025).
Analyzed through a systems thinking framework, the Chilean approach targets fundamentally distinct leverage points compared to the Peruvian model. By embedding global digital platforms and national media networks into a proactive co-governance matrix, Servel actively seeks to disrupt the algorithmic pathways that accelerate the Success to the Successful archetype in the spread of disinformation (Marino-Jiménez et al., 2025). Rather than remaining trapped in a reactive cycle of debunking content post-publication, the direct communication with news media and fact-checking outlets, as well as the Mesa de Gobernanza commitments, attempts to alter the underlying incentive structures of the information ecosystem. This structural intervention theoretically compels platforms to throttle the algorithmic visibility of manipulative FIMI tactics—contributing to neutralizing their primary propagation vectors before they can achieve critical mass and destabilize electoral trust.

However, this strategy frequently hits a psychological wall; it attempts to counter the neurocognitive manipulation of highly charged key terms with top-down factual data, failing to overcome the deep-seated confirmation bias and affective polarization inherent to the electorate. Conversely, Chile intervenes at a structurally distinct leverage point. Through direct communication with journalists and media, as well as with the commitments achieved in the Mesa de Gobernanza , Servel seeks to disrupt the Success to the Successful archetype by co-opting the algorithmic propagation vectors themselves, aiming to throttle the visibility of manipulative FIMI tactics before they achieve critical mass. Yet, the definitive diagnosis for both models is identical: whether attempting to regulate candidate hostility (Peru) or neutralizing digital platforms (Chile), these institutional architectures are significant, but remain insufficient on their own. Breaking these systemic negative feedback loops in either ecosystem demands an urgent civic transition toward the émirec model, effectively decentralizing the defense matrix and shifting the burden of critical verification to an empowered citizenry.
4.4 The civic transition: operationalizing the Émirec model The comparative analysis underscores that the institutional containment strategies deployed by both the JNE and Servel represent highly commendable and technically sophisticated efforts to safeguard electoral integrity. By establishing vital collaborative networks—such as the JNE's Aliados contra la desinformación network with civil society and Servel's multisectoral Mesa de Gobernanza alongside the UNDP, tech platforms, and academia—these institutions have built robust, inter-institutional first lines of defense. However, despite these significant strides in regulatory formality and cross-sector cooperation, these frameworks inevitably face structural limits when operating primarily as top-down mechanisms. In both hybrid regimes and flawed democracies, deep-seated confirmation bias and intense affective polarization create complex psychological barriers, which can lead citizens to reject factual evidence when it contradicts their deeply entrenched, identity-based political beliefs.
Consequently, the ultimate defense mechanism against industrialized disinformation is not merely institutional, but fundamentally civic. Contemporary communication theory posits that transitioning the electorate from passive prosumers toward the critically empowered figure of the émirec is a systemic imperative. While previous foundational literature has conceptualized the émirec primarily within the domains of media literacy and horizontal pedagogy (Aparici and García-Marín, 2018;Cloutier, 1973), this study advances the concept by operationalizing it as a mechanism for systemic democratic defense . Here, the émirec is not merely an empowered media consumer, but an indispensable structural node engineered to introduce cognitive friction into industrialized FIMI architectures, actively neutralizing algorithmic escalation within electoral crises. This is defined as a critically empowered, highly conscious communicative agent who actively evaluates, structurally verifies, and critically manages information prior to its dissemination within their own networks. The operationalization of the émirec requires embedding specific, practical cognitive friction mechanisms directly into the citizen's daily digital habits—utilizing reverse image searches, scrutinizing primary sources, and recognizing the neurocognitive manipulation embedded in sensationalist key terms.

Within this systemic architecture, state institutions no longer function as unilateral, top-down arbiters of truth. Instead, as the model demonstrates, they operate in a multi-sectoral manner—integrating electoral bodies, academia, media, and international organizations—to foster structured Dialogue and societal Participation . This collaborative environment is essential for generating institutional Transparency . When coupled with targeted democratic Formation , this transparency directly promotes and legitimizes the émirec as the primary, active informational agent within the ecosystem.
Once empowered, the émirec effectively disrupts the Success to the Successful and Escalate archetypes that historically fuel disinformation. Rather than succumbing to the affective hijacking of manipulative narratives, the émirec engages in critical Analysis . As depicted in the model, this agent contributes to public communication by explicitly deconstructing the epistemic load of key terms utilized by threat actors. By critically analyzing these terms rather than instinctively amplifying them, the émirec facilitates the diffusion of verifiable Truth .
This diffusion of truth generates dual systemic benefits that stabilize the entire ecosystem. Upwardly, truth promotes democratic health, reinforcing the overarching Democracy node and encouraging further civic Participation . Downwardly, the model dictates that truth improves the Reputation of institutions. In hybrid regimes (Peru) and flawed democracies (Chile) where institutional trust is chronically eroded, this bottom-up restoration of reputation is vital; it re-legitimizes the Institutions, thereby completing and reinforcing the virtuous feedback loop.
To effectively transition the émirec model from a normative theoretical ideal to an empirically measurable public policy objective, electoral management bodies must establish specific metrics for what this study terms cognitive friction. Future institutional interventions must evaluate the success of their democratic formation strategies not merely by the superficial reach of their digital literacy workshops, but by quantifying definitive behavioral shifts within the electorate. Potential empirical indicators include measuring the latency period before users share politically charged content, tracking the mass adoption rates of basic verification protocols (such as reverse image searches), and utilizing pre- and post-testing methodologies to assess citizens' capacity to consciously isolate and deconstruct the manipulative epistemic load of FIMI-driven key terms. Establishing these quantitative and qualitative metrics is the indispensable next step to empirically validate that the systemic negative feedback loops are being successfully dismantled from within the civic network.
5 Conclusions and strategic implications
The systemic-comparative analysis of electoral disinformation architectures in Peru and Chile yields profound implications for the protection of democratic integrity in Latin America. The overarching conclusion of this study is that highly technical, strictly institutional interventions—while serving as indispensable first lines of defense—are structurally insufficient to neutralize the root causes of information disorder. In ecosystems classified as hybrid regimes or flawed democracies, deep-seated systemic distrust and affective polarization act as powerful catalysts, transforming digital manipulation from a marginal externality into an industrialized epistemic threat (Barrenechea and Encinas, 2022).
Through the application of systems thinking, this research demonstrates that Peru and Chile currently intervene at fundamentally different leverage points within the disinformation ecosystem. The JNE in Peru has formalized a robust framework of normative containment, utilizing the Pacto Ético Electoral and proactive fact-checking to counteract the Escalate archetype of reciprocal political aggression. Conversely, the Electoral Service (Servel) in Chile demonstrates an evolutionary leap toward horizontal ecosystem defense. By pioneering the Mesa de Gobernanza with tech conglomerates and national media, Chile attempts to disrupt the Success to the Successful archetype, actively co-opting the algorithmic vectors that propagate FIMI tactics before they achieve critical mass.
Crucially, Servel's strategic prioritization of direct communication with journalists and news media for disseminating electoral information and debunks may serve a deeper democratic function than mere damage control. AsStrömbäck (2005)argues, different normative models of democracy impose distinct obligations upon journalism. From the perspective of both the participatory and deliberative models of democracy, journalism is not merely a passive conduit of information but an active agent responsible for fostering civic engagement, framing politics as open and inclusive, and sustaining the conditions for rational public deliberation. In this light, Servel's approach can be read as a deliberate attempt to reconstruct the gatekeeping function of journalism. Rather than acting as the sole authoritative source of electoral truth, Servel finds in journalism an ally in the midst of informational disorders, channeling verified information. The actual relationship between this strategy and any measurable reduction in the circulation of electoral disinformation requires separate examination, particularly given the persistently low levels of media trust in Chile (Mellado and Cruz, 2025). Nevertheless, this is precisely what makes it analytically surprising: it is plausible that Servel's own institutional credibility partially compensates for journalism's trust deficit, lending legitimacy to the media outlets that carry its verified information. Whether this credibility transfer effect holds empirically remains an open and worthwhile question for future research.
Yet, despite their varying degrees of sophistication, both institutional architectures inevitably collide with the psychological wall of confirmation bias. Top-down verification mechanisms consistently fail when forced to counter the neurocognitive manipulation embedded in highly charged key terms. Therefore, the definitive diagnosis for both models is that state entities cannot sustainably function as unilateral arbiters of truth in polarized societies.
Consequently, true democratic resilience requires an unavoidable, massive civic transition toward the émirec model. The defense of electoral integrity is not merely a logistical challenge; it is an ongoing battle to preserve the epistemological baseline of society itself. Decentralizing the defense matrix and shifting the burden of critical verification to an empowered citizenry introduces a massive layer of systemic friction capable of permanently decelerating the viral coefficient of FIMI operations.
Ultimately, this study posits that electoral management bodies across the region must transcend reactive debunking. Future lines of research and public policy must focus on empirically measuring the efficacy of digital literacy interventions that operationalize the émirec behavior. Winning the battle against industrialized deceit requires the relentless tracking of FIMI TTPs, the consolidation of collaborative governance networks, and above all, the deliberate cultivation of a critically autonomous electorate capable of managing truth within the public sphere.
