In July 2024, the Food and Drug Administration issued a new draft guidance, Addressing Misinformation About Medical Devices and Prescription Drugs: Questions and Answers; Draft Guidance for Industry [ 1 ].
According to the FDA, “This guidance responds to common questions firms may have when voluntarily addressing misinformation about or related to their approved/cleared medical products.” As it has previously done with misinformation about counterfeit drugs [ 2 ] and snake oil miracle cures [ 3 ], the FDA wants to assist manufacturers of FDA regulated products to present, in clear, truthful, and accurate terms, educational and marketing communications programs that set the record straight for patients, caregivers, and healthcare providers.
In the prescient words of Mark Twain, “a lie can travel halfway around the world while the truth is still putting on its shoes.” In a speed-of-light digital world, regulatory clarity will certainly be helpful in encouraging and emboldening industry to swiftly call out and correct product-related misinformation. But what about misinformation that could result from inaccurate information on FDA-approved labeling?
1 Inaccurate product labeling is healthcare misinformation As FDA has long recognized, truthful, accurate, and non-misleading labeling is foundational to health care decision-making because it is the primary tool for educating health care professionals about the benefits and risks associated with a particular product and helps safeguard public health. For vaccines and other FDA-regulated products, the FDA requires product labeling not only to summarize the safety and effectiveness data supporting approval, but also to describe limitations of the existing evidence to help ensure safe and effective use. Misrepresenting or failing to disclose available data relating to clinical benefit or failure strips health care professionals and patients of the ability to make informed decisions about vaccines, fuels misinformation, and endangers the public health.
As part of this process, data transparency is particularly important in labeling for vaccines - particularly for diseases such as influenza, which play a critical role in maintaining public health and have multiple different options available to help protect patients. Influenza vaccines provide important protection from influenza illness and its associated complications. Flu vaccines have consistently prevented millions of cases of illness and outpatient medical visits, as well as thousands of hospitalizations and deaths each season in the United States, even when vaccine effectiveness rates have been low. For example, during the unusually severe 2017–18 season, vaccination prevented an estimated 7.1 million illnesses, 3.7 million medical visits, 109,000 hospitalizations, and 8000 deaths, despite an overall estimated vaccine effectiveness rate of 38 % [ 4 ].
2 Vaccine labeling transparency and accelerated approval While appropriate labeling is critical in all cases, it is especially important in the context of Accelerated Approval, where the clinical benefit of a vaccine has not yet been confirmed.
FDA may grant accelerated approval of products that have been studied for their safety and effectiveness in treating serious or life-threatening illnesses and that provide meaningful therapeutic benefit to patients over existing treatments. Accelerated approval may be granted upon “the basis of adequate and well-controlled clinical trials establishing that the biological product has an effect on a surrogate endpoint that is reasonably likely, based on epidemiologic, therapeutic, pathophysiologic, or other evidence, to predict clinical benefit” [ 5 ].
As a condition of accelerated approval, confirmatory studies to verify efficacy or clinical benefit are generally required. FDA regulations further state that ordinarily the Agency will consider the conditions of accelerated approval subject to a post-marketing study satisfied when “FDA determines that the required … study verifies and describes … the product's clinical benefit and the [drug] product would be appropriate for approval under traditional procedures” [ 6 ]. When clinical benefit is uncertain, FDA has stated that it may be appropriate to provide in the product label's “Indications and Usage” section “additional context about the approval … by identifying the clinical outcome(s) that are expected (based on the effect demonstrated on the surrogate or intermediate clinical endpoint) but not yet established.”
3 Vaccines as a misinformation touchstone Vaccines are critical to preventing infectious diseases and their complications in the population, and they represent the greatest preventive intervention in ensuring the public health by reducing disease severity, interrupting transmission, and avoiding hospitalizations and death. Vaccines are the most effective tool in our public health arsenal when facing annual or seasonal threats, unexpected outbreaks, as well as for future pandemic preparedness. Successful immunization programs rest entirely on healthcare provider understanding of what vaccines do and, most importantly, in the public trust in our regulators and public health agencies providing clear and complete information.
Today, the greatest threat in the U.S. to the entire vaccine program is misinformation and loss of confidence as we grapple with translating complex scientific data into public health policies, recommendations, and behaviors. Misinformation fuels vaccine hesitancy and rejection, and in 2019 was identified by the WHO as one of the top ten threats to human health. There is a clear need to combat this through labeling accuracy and transparency which is the basis for educational materials and understanding vaccine options and the data supporting their labels.
For this reason, vaccine product labeling is essential to appropriate health care decision-making. This is particularly true since an increasing number of adult vaccines use the Accelerated Approval pathway application. Thus, revisiting the definitive FDA source document, the product label, is critical to maintaining trust and accurate information to the public and to healthcare providers. Appropriate labeling is critical in every case, but especially in the context of accelerated approval, where clinical benefit has not yet been confirmed.
4 Inaccurate or incomplete product labeling is misinformation In addition to the “Indications and Usage” section of a drug or biologic subject to Accelerated Approval, which is devised to reflect the evidence and its limitations, the “Clinical Studies” section of the product label – designated as “Section 14” – gives a more comprehensive summary to “facilitate an understanding of how to use the drug or biologic safely and effectively.”
Because access to all the available data and studies can be helpful in order to “facilitate an understanding of how to use the drug safely and effectively,” FDA wisely issued a guidance document specific to the requirements for Section 14 (“Section 14 Guidance”) that further details the interpretation of this regulatory standard by providing “concise, accurate summaries of information from studies concerning a drug's effectiveness (and sometimes safety) that practitioners consider important to clinical decision making.”
If the goal is full transparency, the Section 14 studies selected for inclusion should cover all data concerning efficacy or safety that might inform a clinician's s decision-making on whether to recommend a drug or vaccine for an individual patient versus simply including studies validating efficacy or safety. Typically, Section 14 will also cover “other important information about a drug's effectiveness,” including “[s]tudies that suggest lack of effectiveness in a clinical situation or lack of effect on a particular endpoint where the drug might have been expected to work.” Ultimately, data limitations from the available studies confirming efficacy or clinical benefit in accordance with the accelerated approval pathway are included and meaningfully explained in the product's labeling.
Limitations are a standard provided for in the “Indications and Usage” section with additional detail covered in Section 14 to give healthcare providers and patients the full breadth of scientific evidence to better understand safety and efficacy. Ideally, inclusion of data limitations helps ensure the process is transparent to the public and helps foster greater confidence in battling misinformation. This is particularly relevant in the case of vaccines for influenza given the severity of influenza among our most vulnerable populations, those 65 and older and people entering middle-age (45–65) with specific comorbidities (heart disease, diabetes, COPD/lung disease, etc.). Importantly, this transparency in information is urgently needed to gain trust in vaccines among racially diverse and ethnic communities, many of whom may be under-insured yet are at even greater risk for flu-related hospitalizations and death. This approach was not taken with influenza vaccines.
5 Example: Labeling for FluLaval and Fluad FluLaval's confirmatory study, required as a condition of accelerated approval, was unsuccessful in meeting its primary endpoints – indicating the inclusion of confirmatory trials failing to meet their primary endpoints. The FDA appropriately communicated the failure explaining that the accelerated approval requirement to verify clinical benefit was not fulfilled.
Consequently, the prescribing information at that point in time was updated to include results from the efficacy and immunogenicity studies. Additionally, Section 14 of the current FluLaval labeling includes a discussion of the design and results of each of the three confirmatory trials thereby ensuring label transparency. Once the required confirmatory trials were completed, the labeling for FluLaval was amended again to include reference to all the required confirmatory trials with Section 14 covering the design and results of each of the three confirmatory trials.
In the case of the Accelerated Approval history of FluLaval, the company was required to undertake additional confirmatory studies following the failure of its first one to meet its primary endpoints. In full transparency, FDA appropriately updated FluLaval's labeling to not only include the subsequent successful confirmatory studies but also the initial unsuccessful confirmatory study, to accurately and completely portray the limitations of the existing effectiveness data. This sequence of events, and the FDA's response, is exactly what should happen and resulted in full and transparent disclosure of information.
This full disclosure, however, was not evident in the labelling of Seqirus' Fluad (adjuvanted) influenza vaccine nor was an explanation of the failed absolute efficacy confirmatory study required as part of its Accelerated Approval pathway requirements. Neither the labeling for Fluad, nor the labeling for Fluad Quadrivalent, refers to the failed absolute efficacy confirmatory study, Efficacy Trial V118_18, despite the fact that FDA labeling regulations and guidance repeatedly underscore the importance of labeling that informs the safe and effective use of the product, including by describing limitations of the available evidence.
Additionally, FDA regulations require that labeling “must be updated when new information becomes available that causes the labeling to become inaccurate, false, or misleading.” As described below, the omission of information about the failure of Efficacy Trial V118_18 to achieve its efficacy endpoints and thereby display clinical benefit renders the current labeling for Fluad and Fluad Quadrivalent outdated and misleading. Further, the omission of information regarding the results of the failed confirmatory trial is inconsistent with FDA's precedent in the case of FluLaval, the only other influenza vaccine that was approved through the accelerated approval pathway for which the confirmatory study required as a condition of approval was unsuccessful in meeting its primary endpoints. In addition to being inconsistent with FDA regulations and guidance, failing to require that the labeling for Fluad be updated to reflect and disclose the failure of its required confirmatory study to meet its primary efficacy endpoints is arbitrary in light of FDA's directly applicable labeling precedent. The FDA has never clarified their decision in this regard.
Given that labeling is expected to accurately represent the essential scientific information available to enable health care providers and patients to make informed and thoughtful decisions about health care and vaccine options, these omissions are particularly troubling, as they could lead to lack of trust, and detrimental effects on the public health.
6 Implications and conclusions This inconsistency among influenza vaccine labelling helps feed misinformation and loss of confidence in the accelerated approval pathway. During the pandemic, great distrust of the accelerated approval pathway for COVID-19 vaccines occurred because of the perceived lack of transparency in the accelerated approval process. This has serious implications during future emergency epidemic or pandemic situations where public trust and confidence in the process and the full explanation of all the available data in the label are critical for vaccine acceptance. Having complete information and an explanation of the data limitations in a label, particularly in accepting a vaccine for use under the accelerated approval pathway, are essential for the public health.
Accurate and transparent labelling is critical to informing healthcare provider decisions regarding patient care by ensuring that benefits and risks are exhaustively addressed through all the data that supported approval - while also including data where a study failed to meet its clinical endpoints as a means of explaining a particular label designation and avoid misinterpretation. FDA has been the steadfast guardian of the public trust in labelling and is constantly striving to improve on its vigilance. Failing to provide accurate information in a product's label poses a threat not only to the health of individual patients, but also to the public health at large. This discrepancy in labeling should be addressed and corrected. Doing so demonstrates transparency and builds trust.
