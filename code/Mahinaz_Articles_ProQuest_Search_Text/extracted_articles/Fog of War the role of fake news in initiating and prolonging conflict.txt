1 Introduction
“The Fog of War”, an expression attributed to the German general Carl von Clausewitz, is commonly used to refer to the use of misinformation, disinformation, as well as fake news in times of conflict. In periods of upheaval, each side is interested in winning the war of minds and thus floods the information space with propaganda which presents their own position as justified and which will ensure public support for action.
The necessity for information warfare is a rather new phenomenon. Edward Bernays, the pioneer in PR management, maintained that the need to gain the favors of the people was a natural reaction to the widespread establishment of democracy after World War 1. In the shambles of old, imperial Europe, “the masses promised to become king,” he writes. To address this, elites “discovered a powerful help in influencing majorities. It has been found possible so to mold the mind of the masses that they will throw their newly gained strength in the desired direction” (Bernays, 1928/2005, p. 47)—through the use of propaganda. Chomsky once likened propaganda to a weapon to subdue the population: “Propaganda,” he said, “is to democracy what the bludgeon is to a totalitarian state” (Chomsky, 1992, online). It follows that propaganda is an inherent part of democratic societies in order to control what Lippmann called “the bewildered herd” (Lippmann, 1927/2015, p. 145). In particular in times of war or war mongering, propaganda is intense. The reason is simple: war is the most anti-democratic action conceivable, as it is accompanied by martial law and the infringement of civil rights and liberties, and it massively threatens the right to life. This means that people need to be persuaded that this state is in their inherent interest. To achieve this, massive propaganda campaigns are necessary to make the public ‘ready for war’ (Bourne, 1918;Crawford, 2021).
Fake news are a favorite tool to drum up public support and create an atmosphere of panic and fear. The enemy is ascribed actions and/or characteristics so devious that the only way to react is political or military escalation. The most widely known example for this is perhaps the lie that Iraq in 2003 under Saddam Hussein possessed weapons of mass destruction and had ties to al-Qaeda (Corn, 2023). Even though both these claims were soon shown to be brazen lies, the media’s refusal to boost these debunkers led to the illegal invasion of Iraq by US-led forces in March of 2003. By the time the debunkers reached the public, the damage had already been done.
This paper is concerned with the analysis of select articles in the context of conflict which were later exposed as fake. These articles will be analyzed for language and sourcing in order to identify possible common traits of fake news articles. Subsequently, it will investigate the publicity they received in other media outlets as well as whether or not a debunker was written after the fact.
2 Theoretical framework for analyzing fake news in war reporting
This study integratesMorelli’s (2014)war propaganda principles,Herman and Chomsky’s (1988/2002)Propaganda Model—focusing on sourcing filters—and fake news characteristics (Higdon, 2020) to examine how mainstream outlets launder unevidenced atrocity claims from anonymous/invested sources into the public domain to manufacture consent for war.
2.1 Core tools: propaganda principles and sourcing filters
Drawing onPonsonby’s (1928/2022)treatise of propaganda in World War 1,Morelli (2014)distills 10 principles of war propaganda (seeTable 1). In the context of fake news, principles 3, 5 and 6 are of particular interest, because they enable the vilification of the enemy by highlighting atrocities or foul play by enemy troops or the evilness of the enemy leader. In this sense, they are the principles that allow for both initiation and continuation of conflict.

8 Recognized intellectuals and artists support our cause.

10 Whoever casts doubt on our propaganda helps the enemy and is a traitor.

Principle 3, or the evil leader principle, aims at demonizing the enemy leader—and by association their troops—and is a preferred strategy to remind the public why a particular enemy must be fought and why permitting them to stay in power is not an option. Examples abound, such as stories of North Korean leader Kim Jong Un feeding his uncle to dogs (Baculinao and Smith, 2014) or the reflexive comparison of enemy leaders to Hitler (Beamish, 1990;Goldberg, 2011;Herman, 2022). The purpose is to describe opponents as so ruthless and so unscrupulous that the only way to manage them is to take them out.
Principle 5, or the atrocity principle, is designed to portray violence by the enemy as outrageous, indiscriminate and wanton. The idea is to push the population toward supporting actions targeted at bringing the violence to an end because of the despicable nature of the deed. People are led to believe that the enemy army mainly consists of lawless people without a conscience. This was employed, for instance, to galvanize support for the 1st Gulf War with false claims that Iraqi soldiers had taken Kuwaiti babies out of incubators and left them on the floor to die (Morelli, 2014, pp. 70–71). Stories like these can be used to illustrate the evil nature of the enemy and how unprecedented the breakdown of civilization is.
Principle 6 is the illegal weapons principle and aims to depict the enemy as being outside of shared norms of civilization by using weapons that are commonly seen as illegal (e.g., chemical weapons or cluster bombs) or by conducting an attack in a particularly shocking way (e.g., Kamikaze fighters at Pearl Harbor, civilian airplanes on 9/11). This was also applied before the 2nd Gulf War when claims were made that Iraqi leader Saddam Hussein could launch weapons of mass destruction at western countries within 45 minutes (BBC, 2004;Watt, 2004).
Many such stories are funneled to the media via official, mostly anonymous sources, and thereby target a major weakness of corporate media, which is the over-dependence on such official sources, as shown by Herman and Chomsky’s Propaganda Model (1988/2002) and swathes of other studies (e.g.,Tuchman, 1980;Parenti, 1986;Cook, 2005;Entman, 2004;Davies, 2008;MacLeod, 2019). Its result is the unreflected reinforcement of dominant ideology (Berkowitz and Douglas, 1993, p. 11). Anonymity is usually granted when “the value gained in making hidden information public outweighs the lack of transparency in withholding the source’s identity” (Carlson, 2012, p. 7)—potentially sensational news in the public interest that comes at the price of lack of accountability. “A well-placed source”, “a source with intimate knowledge of the matter”, or “intelligence officials” are only some examples of phrasings the press will use when someone divulges information ‘on the condition of anonymity’. When accountability is wavered for official sources, there is a risk that journalists are fed false or misleading information to push a certain agenda (Fernandez, 2021, p. 181), such as was the case with NYT-correspondent Judy Miller, who uncritically published claims about weapons of mass destruction in the run-up to the 2003 Iraq war (Greenwald, 2015). It bears mentioning that the CIA has been on record co-opting journalists or otherwise using premium access to intelligence circles as leverage to whitewash propaganda to the public (Stockwell, 1987).
2.2 Fake news as modern propaganda mechanism Fake news shows many of the aforementioned characteristics: sensationalism, emotional language and anonymous sources. The term itself describes “a news story that has been released through media and has information that is either greatly exaggerated or completely untrue” (Mara, 2019, p. 4). Higdon maintains that such fake news is frequently “legitimized by state-sponsored propaganda machines: they create a social climate in which falsehoods are believed because they fit with the propagandistic narratives dominating national discourse” (2020, p. 68). They are a crucial instrument of state propaganda to establish and maintain a discourse in which the government’s agendas, ideas and actions are naturalized and justified “by methodically priming the citizenry to accept fake news content as true” (ibid: 69).
While the term might be recent, the phenomenon certainly is not. According to the International Center for Journalists, “misinformation, disinformation and propaganda have been features of human communication since at least the Roman times”, but it was only through the invention of the printing press that the phenomenon received a considerable boost (Posetti and Matthews, 2018, p. 1), the first arguably being the ‘Black Legend’ that exaggerated stories of torture and executions during the Spanish Inquisition (BBC, 1994).
In wartimes, fake news have always played a crucial role. In World War 1, unverified stories of German atrocities were vital in incentivizing the British and US population to join the war effort (Higdon, 2020). In World War 2, the demonization and dehumanization of Jewish people served as a powerful catalyst to enforce their continued persecution and pursue the war to the bitter end (Posetti and Matthews, 2018;Steuter and Wills, 2009). The same applied to so-called false-flag operations, in which the enemy is ascribed a hostile act which either never took place or was conducted by the party making the claim (Griffin and Cobb, 2023, p. 455). Examples include the staged attack on a German customs post at the onset of World War 2, the Gulf of Tonkin incident that escalated the Vietnam War or the incubator story that enabled the 1st Gulf War (Higdon, 2020;Thomas, 2019). Portraying the enemy as insidious and brutal is thus an effective impetus in manufacturing consent for war as well as in rekindling support for an ongoing conflict.
Fake news can be both misinformation and disinformation. The two terms differ in that the former means “sharing false information without intention to deceive” while the latter constitutes “the spread of false information with the intention of harm or deception” (Scherling and Foltz, 2024, p. 27). Therefore, not all fake news is necessarily malicious in its intent, but it may be misleading based on an erroneous understanding of an issue. In the case of war reporting, however, history has shown that fake news is more likely to be disinformation (see examples of false flags), with an intention to create fear and moral outrage to muster support for a war.
One common characteristic of fake news is a misleading or sensationalist headline. The headline may, for instance, purport a nature of the story which is unsupported by the evidence presented in the article. It may present something as certain when it is in fact rumors or hearsay. This is problematic in particular because research has shown that “people are basing their decision to share an item on the headline rather than on the content’s veracity” (Higdon, 2020, pp. 145–6). To give an example: A discredited Guardian story on an alleged link between Donald Trump’s 2016 campaign manager Paul Manafort and Wikileaks founder Julian Assange was originally entitled “Manafort held secret talks with Assange in Ecuadorian embassy” (News Sniffer, n.d.)—something which the story content did not supply evidence for.
Use of dubious sources is yet another hallmark of fake news. Fake news articles commonly draw on unnamed, anonymous sources for their claims. This makes it impossible to confirm the veracity of the statements made. The aforementioned Manafort-Assange article, for example, is full of anonymous sources: “sources have said”, “the Guardian has been told”, “a well-placed source has told”, “according to two sources”, “one person familiar with Wikileaks said” or “one source in Quito” (Harding and Collyns, 2018). In fact, it is only anonymous sources that make all of the article’s central claims, none of which are verifiable.
Finally, an important part of fake news is language. More specifically, language that is intended to evoke a particular reaction in the audience which is in the interest of the producers of said news (such as government or intelligence officials). The language will tend to be sensationalist and emotional, or at least create mental pictures to this effect. The intention behind the use of such language is to create moral panic, moral outrage, radicalization and social division (Higdon, 2020, p. 154). In articles about the Russian bombing of a maternity clinic in Mariupol, Ukraine, for instance, “emotionally charged words like ‘desperate’, ‘devastating’, ‘barbaric’, ‘heartbreaking’, ‘harrowing’, ‘terrifying’, as well as of viscerally descriptive phrases such as ‘a heavily pregnant and bleeding woman’, ‘medieval siege warfare’, or ‘blood pools on the floor’” are used to achieve a particular effect—moral outrage (Scherling and Scherling, 2026: forthcoming). Readers are meant to feel empathy and possibly be moved to support intervention in favor of the suffering population.
These patterns of sourcing, of emotional language and sensationalism further intensify under crisis journalism’s structural pressures (Cottle, 2011;Allen, 2006), where “breaking news” speed, competition, and emotional intensity prioritize unverified visuals/atrocities over verification.Davies’ (2008)concept of “churnalism” provides an additional motivation for uncritical media uptake: outlets recycle wire/PR claims—designed to serve the interests of those providing them—under time and economic pressures. Emotional language, then, is not mere bias but also institutionalized for appeal or moral shock (Wahl-Jorgensen, 2020;Beckett and Deuze, 2016), not only in tabloids, but also in broadsheets.
None of the above aspects on its own is sufficient for a news text to qualify as ‘fake news’, but they can serve as warning signals—especially when they accumulate—that further scrutiny of the article’s veracity is justified, as the following analysis will attempt to illustrate.
3 Case studies
Based on the theoretical framework outlined above, this section will now investigate a number of fake news examples in the context of three different armed conflicts. The analysis first looks at the various aspects indicating that the story may be fake. It then shifts toward exploring the degree of publicity that the fake news story received and, finally, looks at whether or not the story was eventually retracted or at least flagged as problematic by the editors.
3.1 Corpus
For the analysis, three stories from three different conflicts—Afghanistan, Libya, Israel/Palestine—that were shown to be fake were selected. The selection was made based on (1) whether the story was salient at the time it happened, (2) whether it had been debunked, and (3) whether it had at least the potential to initiate or prolong an armed conflict.
The main search method employed was Google search. By using key words of the events (e.g., “Russia,” “Taliban,” “bounty”) and by limiting the search to the relevant time frame of the event in question, the search results were filtered to include only breaking news reports by mainstream media sources, rather than after-the-fact reflections of it. Not all articles found were also analyzed, as many were fairly similar in terms of their content (for instance, second-hand publishing based on the same news agency report).
Exclusion criteria included: (1) opinion pieces, editorials, or post-debunking analyzes; (2) non-mainstream, non-western outlets; and (3) duplicates or near-identical wire-service rewrites offering no new sourcing/language. This eventually yielded 37 articles that were included in the corpus. For the Afghanistan story, which was short-lived and a one-time event, 7 articles were analyzed. The Libya story, being a developing story over several months, yielded more hits, out of which 18 were used for the analysis. For the Israel/Palestine story, also a one-time and short-lived story, the number of articles used was 12.
3.2 Methodology
This study employs qualitative discourse analysis to examine linguistic and structural features of the selected articles, focusing on how they construct atrocity narratives and embed principles of war propaganda. Specifically, the articles were analyzed for any of the following aspects (seeTable 2):- Sources: are the sources named or unnamed, officials or eyewitnesses?
- Emotional language: are words of high emotional value used?
- Likelihood: are the claims presented as facts? What kind of reporting verbs are used? Is hedging used?
- War propaganda: are Morelli’s principles of war propaganda used? To what effect?

These coding categories were theoretically predetermined based on established methods in media research (e.g.,Hatcher, 2010;Morelli, 2014;Simon-Vandenbergen, 1997;Entman, 1991). If an article was first exclusively published by one media outlet, the follow-up articles were also analyzed with regard to ‘bandwagon journalism’ (Graf, 2022), i.e., the uncritical repetition of claims based on the assumption that they must be legitimate because another outlet already published them.
3.3 Analysis The three stories selected were (1) a story about Taliban fighters receiving Russian bounties to kill US and UK soldiers in Afghanistan in 2020, (2) a story suggesting that Libyan leader Muammar Gaddafi had supplied his troops with Viagra to facilitate mass rape from 2011, and (3) the story of Israeli infants allegedly beheaded during the Hamas attack on border communities in 2023. 3.3.1 Russian bounties, Afghanistan (2020)
The Russian bounty story was first broken by the New York Times in their article “Russia Secretly Offered Afghan Militants Bounties to Kill U.S. Troops, Intelligence Says”, published on June 26, 2020. Its main claim was that Russian officials were promising to pay money for the killing of coalition forces in Afghanistan, which included US forces. The story was essentially refuted roughly 2 weeks after its publication. On July 10, 2020, ABC News reported that “Defense Secretary Mark Esper and Gen. Mark Milley, the chairman of the Joint Chiefs of Staff, said Thursday that the intelligence on the Russian bounty program had not been corroborated by intelligence agencies and that they do not believe any attacks in Afghanistan that resulted in American casualties can be directly tied to it.” (Martinez, 2020). Early doubts in mainstream news appeared as early as June 28 (Knutson, 2020). The original New York Times story, however, still stands uncorrected and without an editor’s note at the time of writing.
The story was based on information supplied by sources claiming to be insiders. These sources are referred to as “American intelligence officials”, “officials briefed on the matter”, “the officials familiar with the intelligence” or “the officials [who] spoke on the condition of anonymity to describe the delicate intelligence and internal deliberations” (Savage et al., 2020)—classic anonymous sources. The only named sources are those that react to the claims or that provide other, circumstantial information.
The language used is rather tentative. Militants are “believed to have collected” bounties; Russia’s alleged operation “would be a significant and provocative escalation”; the intelligence information “is said to be based at least in part on interrogations of captured Afghan militants”; officials “were said to be confident about the intelligence”. This use of hedging language would imply a degree of caution regarding the veracity of the information. However, there are certain elements that contradict this cautiousness.
The main framing of the information contained in the article occurs in the very first paragraph: “American intelligence officials have concluded that a Russian military intelligence unit secretly offered bounties to Taliban-linked militants […], according to officials briefed on the matter”. Here, the use of the verb “conclude” seems to imply certainty, while “according to” presents the information as considerably more factual than, for instance, “officials have claimed”. This initial framing, then, carries over into the main article, and it is reinforced by the use of presuppositions. The article states that “[t]he officials did not describe the mechanics of the Russian operation, such as how targets were picked or how money changed hands”. The noun phrase “the mechanics of the Russian operation” implies the existence of this ‘Russian operation’ which is after all only alleged. The same can be said regarding the passage “[Officials] have greater uncertainty about how high in the Russian government the covert operation was authorized and what its aim may be”, which again presupposes the existence of the operation (“the covert operation”, “how high [it] was authorized”, “its aim”). Similarly, the sentences “Officials have also suggested that the Russians may have been trying to derail peace talks to keep the United States bogged down in Afghanistan. But the motivation remains murky” presuppose ‘motivation’ and therefore the existence of such an operation. What is particularly notable about these examples is the contradiction between the use of cautious language (“have suggested”, “may have been trying”) and existential presuppositions suggesting factuality (“the motivation”). On a minor note, it is also interesting to point out the use of the reporting verb with which the reaction of the Taliban is framed: “Zabihullah Mujahid, a spokesman for the Taliban, denied that the insurgents have ‘any such relations […]’”. ‘Deny’ means “to refuse to admit or accept something” (Oxford Learner’s Dictionary, 2025) and therefore may imply that the accusation is accurate.
The main gist of the story being that Russians use subterfuge and unethical means (paying bounties) to harm the US and its allies plays into propaganda principles 3 [evil leader], 5 [atrocities] and 6 [illegal weapons]. It portrays Russian leadership as bad players because they use nefarious means to create harm (principle 3). It also implies strong intent because the Russians are consciously paying money to the enemies of the US to assassinate troops for them (principle 5). And the very act of paying bounties rather than confronting US soldiers heads-on could be seen as unethical or at least dishonorable warfare (principle 6).
The story, therefore, has some typical hallmarks of a fake news story pushed by the intelligence community (anonymous officials) to promote or boost continuous engagement in a military conflict. But despite these signals, the story received broad uptake in the mainstream press. Within a few days, it was picked up by outlets like BBC , CNN , PBS , The Guardian , AP News , Sky News , Al Jazeera , Radio Free Europe , Politico , The Washington Post , ABC News and USA Today . These follow-up reports mainly adopted the story by the New York Times , some included additional information, but some also went a step further in that they framed the story to be based on a New York Times report, rather than an anonymous official’s claim, thereby naturalizing this claim.
The Guardian , for instance, introduces its article like this:
The Russian intelligence unit behind the attempted murder in Salisbury of the former double agent Sergei Skripal secretly offered to pay Taliban-linked fighters to kill British and American soldiers in Afghanistan, according to US reports.
[…]
The bombshell report in the New York Times says there has been a major escalation by Moscow to undermine the White House and its coalition allies, including the UK, as President Trump seeks a peace deal with the Taliban. (Doward, 2020)
It attributes the claim to ‘US reports’ and a ‘bombshell report in the New York Times ’. The reporting verb used to report the claim is “says,” which is a neutral verb suggesting factuality. The lack of any hedging or modality makes this pick-up article even more factual in the way it presents the story compared to the original, which is also reflected in the headline: “Russia offered bounty to kill UK soldiers”. Different from the NYT headline, it does not use any qualifiers, such as ‘sources say’.
The Washington Post also picked up on the story and imbued it with a higher degree of credibility than the NYT original. It titled “Russian operation targeted coalition troops in Afghanistan, intelligence finds”, thus framing the unevidenced claim as a factual finding. In the second paragraph of its article, the authors write: “The Russian operation, first reported by the New York Times , has generated an intense debate within the Trump administration about how best to respond to a troubling new tactic by a nation that most US officials regard as a potential foe” (Nakashima et al., 2020). Similar to the Guardian article, here, too, the original claim is backgrounded and its truth taken for granted by pinpointing the NYT report as its origin and by a lack of cautious language.
In the Wall Street Journal , the source of the claim features in the headline (“Russian Spy Unit Paid Taliban to Attack Americans, U.S. Intelligence Says”), but the subheading already endorses this narrative as fact: “Bounties paid by GRU are disclosed as US plans troops drawdown, Taliban peace plan” (Lubold and Strobel, 2020). Here, ‘disclose’ implies the factual nature of the claim.
The Times went even further and titled: “Putin paid Taliban in plot to kill US and British troops”. The original NYT article had said that officials “have greater uncertainty about how high in the Russian government the covert operation was authorized” (Savage et al., 2020) and had thus not identified the Russian president as the figure behind the alleged bounty operation. In the subheading as well, The Times went beyond the original story to present the claim as an undisputed fact by stating that “[a] secretive Russian intelligence unit has been offering the Taliban bounties to kill British and American soldiers in Afghanistan” (Evans, 2020).
This shows an interesting example of bandwagon journalism, where the original claim is taken for granted, and some news outlets incrementally add information or certainty that was not part of said claim.
3.3.2 Gaddafi troops supplied with Viagra, Libya (2011)
This story started to emerge in March and April 2011 and was picked up again by multiple outlets during June of the same year, as the UN-mandated NATO-intervention in Libya, resulting in the toppling of long-time leader Muammar Gaddafi, was entering its fourth month. It alleged that Libyan soldiers were given the drug Viagra to rape civilians. This was part of more general claims of mass rapes by Gaddafi forces. These were published in the midst of accusations by a number of countries that NATO was “going beyond the mandate given by the UN Security Council resolutions,” with “US ambassador Susan Rice highlight[ing] the use of Viagra in the closed-door meeting as the debate intensified” (AFP, 2011a). Allegations were made by people like the chief prosecutor for the International Criminal Court (ICC) that Gaddafi was buying “containers of Viagra-type drugs to carry out the policy [of using rape as a weapon]” (Lederer, 2011). These claims then contributed to NATO extending its operations in the country for another 90 days (BBC, 2011). Doubts regarding claims of mass rape and Viagra use began emerging in June, with SBS News being the first to publish a story quoting a UN human rights investigator who called the claims “mass hysteria” and criticized their unsubstantiated and contradictory nature (AFP, 2011b). This was followed by a report in The Independent discussing an investigation by Amnesty International in which it “failed to find evidence for these human rights violations and in many cases has discredited or cast doubt on them. It also found indications that on several occasions the rebels in Benghazi appeared to have knowingly made false claims or manufactured evidence” (Cockburn, 2011). In their 3 months investigation, they were unable to find “any evidence or a single victim of rape or a doctor who knew about somebody being raped” (ibid). While this does not disprove individual incidences of rape, it does not offer any evidence for a policy of mass rape. None of the articles viewed for this paper, however, included any update or follow-up on this rebuttal.
The claim clearly fits with the propaganda principles number 3 [evil leader], 5 [atrocities] and 6 [illegal weapons]. It feeds into principles 3 and 5 because the story alleges that mass rape did not happen spontaneously, but was purposeful and a matter of policy from the top. And it fits principle number 6 because the use of rape as a weapon of war is clearly a war crime and thus a pursuit of war by illegal means. Since the NATO intervention in Libya was argued in terms of the policy of responsibility to protect (R2P) (Terry, 2015), these claims offer additional gravitas and a need of urgency for NATO’s plans to stop Gaddafi.
The first appearances of the claims of mass rape occurred as almost casual mentions in the context of articles covering the turmoil in Libya. The Sunday Times on March 27, 2011 reported that “[m]obile phone footage of captured tanks and other vehicles appeared to show Gadaffi’s men had come supplied with condoms and Viagra.” It quoted an opposition fighter claiming that “[t]hey were intent on raping and abusing our women and girls” (Jaber and Amoore, 2011). It was Al Jazeera on the following day which first reported on rape as a weapon: “Several doctors say they have found Viagra tablets and condoms in the pockets of dead pro-Gaddafi fighters, alleging that they were using rape as a weapon of war” (Al Jazeera, 2011). On the same day, The Wire reported on the story of one Iman al-Obeidi, who claimed to have been raped by pro-government forces, noting that “[h]er story has not been verified, but […] her injuries, down to the rope burns,’ appeared to be consistent with what she said’” (Eichler, 2011). Another piece of the claim came from the Toronto Star on March 28, 2011, which published testimony by pro-government forces who had been taken prisoner by armed opposition groups: “These prisoners […] were clearly eager to repudiate the regime they served as recently as a week ago. And they were enthusiastic about detailing the orders they claim were imposed upon them […] Rape the women—here’s the Viagra to boost your sex drive” with another prisoner of war (POW) claiming, “Our instructions were to kill the men and rape the women. They handed us all this Viagra, said take as much as [you] need for all the women you can handle” (ibid). In essence, some of the early claims still contained a mix of definitive statements and hedging language (‘appeared to show’, ‘alleging’, ‘appeared to be consistent’), but the direct quotes of eyewitnesses close to the opposition with neutral reporting verbs (mostly ‘say’) imply a certain degree of credibility.
A second wave of articles on the topic took shape at the end of April, 2011. The Mirror reported that “[c]hildren as young as eight have been raped in front of their families by Viagra-fuelled troops loyal to Colonel Gaddafi, aid workers claim” (Daily Mirror, 2011). While this introductory line uses the verb ‘claim’, the remaining text recounts the statements of these aid workers neutrally using the reporting verb ‘say’. What is interesting to note is that in its headline (“Gaddafi thugs ‘rape children’”), the reporting verb is missing, but the claim is still put in quotation marks, a nuance that will gradually decline in future articles. Reuters reported on the issue on April 28, titling “US says Gaddafi troops raping, issued Viagra—envoys,” reporting the US claims as factual: “The U.S. envoy to the United Nations told the Security Council on Thursday that troops loyal to Libyan leader Muammar Gaddafi were increasingly engaging in sexual violence and some had been issued the impotency drug Viagra, diplomats said” (Charbonneau, 2011). Both the US statement as well as the diplomats’ reporting of it are recorded using neutral reporting verbs. And while the article does mention that the claims “remained unconfirmed,” it suggests credibility by connecting it to the “highly publicized case of Eman al-Obaidi [Iman al-Obeidi], the woman who burst into a journalists’ hotel in Tripoli last month saying she had been raped by pro-government militiamen.”
The story’s spread reached a peak in early June 2011, when the chief prosecutor at the ICC, Luis Moreno-Ocampo, informed the public that the ICC was “gathering evidence that the Libyan leadership is using rape as a tool of war and repression and had even acquired large quantities of drugs for its soldiers in an apparent bid to make them more likely to commit sexual violence” (UN News, 2011), without, however, providing any detail on what kind of evidence or information that was. Mainstream media outlets immediately picked up the story and repeatedly quoted the ICC prosecutor unquestioningly. While NBC News was more cautious in its phrasing, reporting that Moreno-Ocampo had “become ‘more convinced’ that Gadhafi decided to punish women by using rape as a weapon, which would be a new method in the Libya civil war” (Lederer, 2011), the BBC made this conditional into a factual. In their June 8 report, they wrote that “Luis Moreno-Ocampo said rape was a new aspect of Colonel Gaddafi’s repression” (BBC, 2011), making a claim that not even the ICC prosecutor had made. The claim was also endorsed as fact by the use of presuppositions. In the NBC article, for instance,Lederer (2011)paraphrases Moreno-Ocampo by writing: “He said it was difficult to know how widespread the use of rape is in Libya”. This presupposes the use of rape as a policy and shifts the question to its extent. In the BBC article, too, the discussion is not about whether mass rapes occurred or not, but about whether “the Libyan leader [Gaddafi] had decided to punish women by using rape as a weapon in the hope that it would instill fear and curb dissent” (2011).
The Times arguably employed the loosest interpretation of what Moreno-Ocampo had said. In their article “Gaddafi accused of ordering mass rape”, the first line transforms the prosecutor’s claim into an undisputed fact: “Investigators for the International Criminal Court have evidence that Colonel Muammur [sic!] Gaddafi ordered the mass rape of women and provided troops with Viagra and other drugs to increase their sex drive” (Barrowclough, 2011). The original statement had only mentioned “gathering evidence” and had only referred to whether mass rape occurred, not whether it had been ordered by Gaddafi (UN News, 2011). The Times article also claims that Moreno-Ocampo “said he would ask for a new charge of mass rape to be made against the Libyan leader” (original: “a rape charge is likely to be added”;UN News, 2011) and it states that “Mr Moreno-Ocampo said there was evidence the Libyan authorities bought Viagra-type medicines and gave them to troops as part of the official rape policy” (Barrowclough, 2011). The original spoke of “finding some elements confirming this issue of acquisition of Viagra-type medicaments” (UN News, 2011). The claim was also directly endorsed by the Australian Courier Mail . In their article titled “Gaddafi ‘ordered sex drugs for rapes’”, they write: “Investigators have evidence Libyan leader Muammar Gaddafi ordered mass rapes and bought containers of sex drugs to encourage troops to attack women, the chief ICC prosecutor says,” (Courier Mail, 2011) using a neutral reporting verb to suggest this is what the prosecutor said verbatim, which he did not. In a follow-up article the next day, the same newspaper doubled down on their interpretation of the prosecutor’s claim by employing even more definite language: “Prosecutors from the International Criminal Court said yesterday that the Libyan leader ordered mass rapes and bought containers of sex drugs to encourage troops to attack women” (Hadid, 2011), making the aforementioned statement even more categorical. France24 also stated the claim as a fact in the headline of their article “Gaddafi ordered mass rape, ICC prosecutor says” (France24, 2011), turning the gathering of evidence into a definite outcome.
Interestingly, even after a UN human rights investigator had criticized the lack of evidence for mass rapes (AFP, 2011b) and after Amnesty International had published their investigation concluding there was no evidence for mass rapes (Cockburn, 2011), media continued to push the same claims. On June 17, 2011, the BBC published a piece titled “Libya: Clinton condemns rape as a weapon of war”, in which then-US Secretary of State Hillary Clinton was uncritically quoted as saying she was “‘deeply concerned’ that Muammar Gaddafi’s troops were participating in widescale rape in Libya” and as calling for a “thorough investigation […] ‘to bring perpetrators to justice’” (BBC, 2011). And even though at that time Amnesty International’s investigation had already been published, Clinton is given space to insinuate that mass rapes did occur by talking about Iman al-Obeidi and the “‘other brave women [who] have come forward to tell of the horrible brutality they have experienced’” (ibid). The article also backs up this claim by referring to an anonymous source (“one major in the Libyan army who has now deserted”) who “told the BBC that reports of shipments of Viagra were widely known about”. None of these claims is contrasted with reports showing a complete lack of evidence. 1
3.3.3 Decapitated babies, Israel (2023)
The final example for fake news became viral after Hamas fighters had infiltrated Israeli border communities on October 7, 2023, killing up to 1,200 people in the process and taking many hostages. While the Israeli military was still clearing out these communities of the remaining militants, a story emerged on i24 News , an Israeli news channel, alleging that during their attack Hamas had killed 40 babies, decapitating at least some of them (Zedek, 2023). Journalist Nicole Zedek had been invited by the IDF (Israeli Defense Forces) to witness the aftermath of the Hamas attack in the kibbutz of Kfar Aza and reported the following: “Some soldiers say they found babies with their heads cut off, entire families gunned down in their beds. About 40 babies and young children have been taken out on gurneys—so far” (ibid).
Subsequently, the story received massive media coverage despite the fact that it lacked any forensic evidence. When asked to provide such, the IDF told Business Insider that “the military won’t seek further evidence for its claim that Hamas decapitated babies in Israel. He [the spokesperson] said it would be ‘disrespectful for the dead’ to do so” (Zitser, 2023). This admission notwithstanding, on October 11 then-US President Joe Biden said the following in an address to Jewish community leaders: “I never really thought that I would see, have confirmed, pictures of terrorists beheading children,” a claim that the White House later distanced itself from (Al Jazeera, 2023). A subsequent UN investigation found that up to 40 children were killed on October 7, though none were beheaded, amongst which one was under the age of 2 and 10 under the age of 10 years (Human Rights Council, 2024). It appears that the mere fact that children were killed was not seen as ‘horrible enough’, and so the claim continued to be spread even after it had been thoroughly deconstructed in an article by Le Monde in April of 2024 (Maad et al., 2024).
Reflecting on the political purpose of this claim, principles number 3 and 5 (principles evil leader & atrocities) appear to be a logical choice. Depicting the opponent as evil and less-than-human, as a barbaric entity that will stop at no act of brutality to inflict harm makes the reaction to said atrocities seem considerably easier to justify. In The Intercept , Jeremy Scahill makes the following argument: “It [the beheaded babies claim] was a detail that fueled the rage and quest for revenge, and was cited when Biden declared that Israel is fighting subhumans in Gaza. ‘They’re animals. They’re animals,’ Biden said on December 12 [2023] when he repeated the beheading claims. ‘They exceeded anything that any other terrorist group has done of late that I—in memory’” (Scahill, 2023). Stories such as the beheaded babies narrative therefore arguably served as a catalyst to be able to rationalize an even harsher response in the face of unimaginably inhuman and cruel acts.
Given the horrifying nature of the claim, it is perhaps unsurprising that major news outlets would take an interest in the story. The Times of Israel , another English-language Israeli news outlet, referenced the original story in their article entitled “‘At least 40 babies killed’: Foreign reporters taken to massacre site in Kfar Aza”: “A reporter from i24 News said that an IDF commander told her that they had found the bodies of some 40 babies, some of whom had been beheaded” (Berman, 2023). It also cited IDF general Itai Veruv who had been quoted in the original story as well: “It’s not a war […] It’s not a battlefield. You see the babies, the mothers, the fathers in their bedrooms, in their protection rooms, and how the terrorists kill them. It’s not a war… it’s a massacre” (ibid). 2 While both of these reports acknowledge that this information was “told” to them by Israeli soldiers, the British Telegraph newspaper omitted this caveat in their story “Babies killed in Hamas attacks on Kfar Aza kibbutz as death toll passes 1,000,” where the article opens with the following statement: “Babies have been killed in Hamas attacks on a kibbutz as the Israeli death toll passed 1,000, it has emerged,” stating that “Israeli forces discovered bodies of dead infants as they surveyed the Kfar Aza kibbutz” (Daily Telegraph, 2023). The claim is presented as a fact. And again it is accompanied by the direct quote by Major General Veruv.
Fox News , too, reported the claim without any hedging in their article titled “At least 40 babies, some beheaded, found by Israel soldiers in Hamas-attacked village” (Richard, 2023). Citing the original i24 News report, the author states that “Israel’s military has discovered unspeakable horrors in an Israeli community that was attacked by Hamas on Saturday, including dozens of dead babies, some with their heads chopped off” (ibid). The article extensively cites the i24 News , report, but never suggests that these reports were unverified.
The Daily Mail , not usually known for nuance, marked the IDFs reports as ‘claims’ at the beginning of their article: “Hamas terrorists massacred at least 40 babies and young children before beheading some of them and gunning down their families in a small kibbutz in Israel, horrified Israeli soldiers have claimed” (Bunyan and Tozer, 2023). Later-on in the article, however, it is strongly implied that the claim is accurate: “Outside the destroyed homes, the soldiers told i24News correspondent Nicole Zedek how they saw the bodies of babies next to their cots, their heads chopped off, in a sign of the depraved acts committed by the terrorists since they attacked Israel on Sunday. As many as 40 babies and small children were killed, IDF soldiers told i24News after taking them to the scene of the atrocity. Harrowing images from the scene show a baby’s cot covered with blood, her small dress lying next to it” (ibid). By linking the claims to pieces of purported evidence as well as by implying that the soldiers were eyewitnesses, the claim is framed more like evidence as the article proceeds.
The BBC article does not mention the concrete number of the killed toddlers that was claimed, but reports statements by Israeli soldiers with neutral reporting verbs: “Mr Ben Zion said Hamas gunmen who killed families, including babies were ‘just a jihad machine to kill everybody, [people] without weapons, without nothing, just normal citizens that want to take their breakfast and that’s all.’ Some of the victims, he said, were decapitated. ‘They killed them and cut some of their heads, it’s a dreadful thing to see’” (Bowen, 2023).
The New York Post also referenced the i24 News article and relayed the claims by Israeli soldiers in a factual manner, without using disclaimers: “Hamas terrorists slaughtered at least 40 babies and young children—decapitating some of them—at a kibbutz near the Gaza border, shaken Israeli officials and reporters at the scene said Tuesday. ‘It’s hard to even explain exactly just the mass casualties that happened right here,’ visibly distraught i24 News correspondent Nicole Zedek said during a broadcast from Kibbutz Kfar Aza […] ‘Babies with their heads cut off, that’s what [the soldiers] said’ (Land, 2023). Again citing Major General Veruv with the same quote as other outlets, the New York Post adds a further quote: “They killed babies in front of their parents, and then killed the parents. They killed parents, and we found babies between the dogs and the family killed before him. They cut heads of the people” (ibid). Referring to i24 News , Land adds, “The outlet said the bodies of about 40 babies and children had been recovered—so far” (ibid).
The British Times even put the quote of an Israeli soldier in their headline: “‘Babies had their throats cut’: how Kfar Aza kibbutz massacre unfolded” (Pfeffer, 2023). In the article, the author oscillates between quoting and paraphrasing Israeli soldiers’ experiences in the aftermath of the Hamas attack: “Every few minutes soldiers broke the silence to announce that more dead people had been discovered. Some said that up to 40 babies’ corpses had been found among entire families who were shot dead as they slept. The children in particular appeared to have suffered gruesome deaths: there were claims that some had had their throats cut” (ibid). While the article uses the word ‘claim’ to refer to the beheadings, the overall context of soldiers reporting their first-hand testimonies creates a compelling and emotional atmosphere that lends credence to these claims.
The Business Insider endorses the claims by means of neutral reporting verbs, presuppositions and explicit references to other media’s reporting. Its headline already sets the tone: “IDF Says Hamas Decapitated Babies in Israel” (Nelken-Zitser and Rebecca, 2023). In the article, this tendency continues: “Israel soldiers say they found dead babies at a kibbutz near the Israel-Gaza border;” “The IDF told Insider that some had been decapitated;” “A spokesperson for the Israel Defense Forces told Insider on Tuesday that its soldiers found the decapitated corpses of babies at Kfar Aza, a kibbutz near Gaza” (ibid). The use of ‘say’ or ‘tell’ in these paraphrases indicates a neutral attitude toward the claims and presents them as statement of fact. This impression is reinforced by the use of presupposition: “The spokesperson, Major Nir Dinar, did not say how many babies’ bodies had been found, nor how many had been beheaded” (ibid). The implication is that, indeed, bodies of babies had been found and that at least some had been beheaded. And while Business Insider does use a disclaimer, any potential doubt is mitigated by referring to either other media having covered the same claim or to soldiers on the ground: “Insider was unable to independently confirm what was found, but Israeli media outlets have also reported the claim, attributing it to soldiers. The spokesperson told Insider that although he hadn’t seen images or videos himself, ‘soldiers on the ground who are there told me this’” (ibid). The article seeks to further endorse the claim by adding the following passages: “Israeli officials appeared to confirm the beheadings, with Defense Minister Yoav Gallant telling soldiers ‘whoever comes here to decapitate—we will wipe out;’” “Nicole Zedek, reporting for the Israeli news station, i24News, said: ‘Babies, their heads cut off, that’s what they [the soldiers] said;” “In another clip, shared on X […] Zedek said a commander told her there, ‘about 40 babies, at least, were taken out on gurneys;’” “In a third i24News clip shared on X, an Israeli soldier at the kibbutz said Hamas is ‘very bad,’ adding that ‘the [sic] cut heads of children, cut heads off women” (ibid).
CNN , while still relaying the statements and claims by Israeli authorities and soldiers with neutral reporting verbs, was the only media outlet in this corpus which also gave the accused party their say: “Hamas has denied that its militants beheaded children or attacked women. The militant group’s spokesman and senior official Izzat al-Risheq on Wednesday described the accusation as ‘fabricated and baseless allegations’” (Darwish et al., 2023). The authors included Hamas’ response a second time when issuing a disclaimer: “CNN could not independently verify that report, and Hamas said media reports about attacking children were false” (ibid). Another example where a clear disclaimer was used is the British Independent newspaper: ‘When Hamas came here they cut the heads of women, they cut the heads of children,’ says Major David Ben Zion, while making a chopping motion toward his neck. ‘We saw dead babies, girls’ […] The Independent did not see evidence of his claims” (Trew, 2023).
In terms of corrections, all except for two of the articles mentioned above stand uncorrected. The only two articles that added an editor’s note to the original claim were, interestingly, the Israeli outlets i24 News (publishers of the original story) and the Times of Israel . In the i24 News note, dating from November 30, 2023, it is soberly stated that “[a]s the official numbers become clearer, we are correcting our initial report […] Nearly two full months after the massacre, the total figures are becoming clear. As journalists, our responsibility is to bring our audience the most accurate information, and we are updating our first report.” This updated report no longer includes the specific number of 40 babies, but retains the claim that there had been babies with their heads cut off (Zedek, 2023). The Times of Israel made a more explicit correction: “This article has been corrected and updated. An earlier version cited, in the headline and the text, a foreign press reporter who visited Kfar Aza saying she was told by an IDF commander that the bodies of 40 babies, some of them beheaded, had been found at the kibbutz. This claim has never been confirmed” (Berman, 2023). Accordingly, their updated article no longer includes a reference to 40 babies or the decapitation claim.
4 Discussion
The three case studies, while covering different events, exhibit a remarkable similarity in terms of how fake news operate and how their claims managed to spread as far and wide as they did. First and foremost, all of them bear all the hallmarks of disinformation, originating as they were from mostly anonymous and unaccountable sources with a vested interest in initiating/escalating an armed conflict and suggesting a strategic intent to prepare the ground for military action. Another aspect that suggests dis- rather than misinformation is the fact that false claims were repeated, often long after they were shown to be false, and that alternative explanations or critical voices were barely given space, if they were included at all. Finally, each served the purpose of facilitating/enabling military escalation.
Regarding the coding categories employed in this analysis, a number of other similarities show.
4.1 Sources
The articles discussed all employ similar types of sources, namely anonymous and/or eyewitnesses.
The use of anonymous sources is highly problematic: All three antagonists—Putin, Gaddafi, Hamas—had been, for a long time, official adversaries to US and other western governments. It is not transparent, therefore, what purpose anonymity could serve in these particular contexts. Hardly any of the sources would have had reason to fear any meaningful negative impact on their lives. On the contrary, if the stories are accurate, it would strengthen the narrative of an adversary to oppose, distrust and reckon with. If the story is inaccurate or false, however, giving intelligence sources, soldiers or other officials the opportunity to remain unnamed, in particular with regard to claims that boost, rather than challenge, the official narrative, this carries the risk of media being used to launder mis- or disinformation.
Other sources include (alleged) eyewitnesses, amongst them those who are also personally invested in the story they are commenting on. In the Libya story, these eyewitnesses include doctors, children and opposition fighters, but also POWs of the Libyan army. While opposition fighters have a natural impetus to portray the other side as evil, people living within the territory these fighters control are unlikely to go on record with narratives potentially antagonizing the fighters’ goals. It has been reported from other conflicts (e.g., Syria or Afghanistan) that the political realities and pressures on the ground affect the degree to which civilians are realistically able to verbalize opinions that run counter to the agenda of those in control of the territory (Cockburn, 2010;Cockburn, 2017). So, doctors or even more so children surrounded by armed fighters may provide information that they feel keeps them out of harm’s way. As far as POWs are concerned, even publishing testimonies likely to have been extracted under duress is considered a violation of the Geneva Convention, on the one hand to shield them from public curiosity, but on the other because there is a high risk of self-incriminating statements when testimony is extracted under pressure (Hyde, 2025). ICC prosecutor Luis Moreno-Ocampo, who is quoted as accusing Gaddafi of using mass rape as a policy, can also be seen as an eyewitness of sorts, as he claims to be collecting related evidence. Many years after the rape claims, the Guardian revealed that “[t]he former ICC chief prosecutor Luis Moreno Ocampo has been accused of being in league with a Libyan businessman close to military strongman Khalifa Haftar, the head of the so-called Libyan National Army” (Wintour, 2017), which was fighting Gaddafi at that time. It is therefore questionable whether he was as unbiased as the media portrayed him to be.
In the articles on beheaded babies in Israeli border communities, there is heavy use of eyewitnesses, namely of Israeli soldiers claiming to have seen the bodies of dozens of killed babies, some decapitated. The reference to these eyewitnesses in the media is mostly deferential, their claims are frequently directly quoted and—with very few exceptions—not challenged. However, in any given conflict, soldiers commanded to fight their enemies cannot be construed as an uninvested and unbiased source. While they may still provide valuable information, the fact that this ideological investment is not revealed and/or explained to the readers is problematic.
Overall, the news articles focused on in this paper show heavy use of either unaccountable official sources or of eyewitnesses who are either invested parties or under potential pressure to conform.
4.2 Emotional and biased language
In terms of language use, the majority of articles show elements of atrocity framing. There is a focus on rapes, beheadings and murdered infants as well as on moral insidiousness.
For the Russian bounty story, there is use of escalatory language to depict the alleged actions by the Russians in a nefarious way. There is talk of a “significant and provocative escalation”, of Russians “secretly offering bounties” and attempting to “derail peace talks” (a reprehensible behavior). The very term “bounty” carries archaic connotations of head hunters, of lawless killings without a trial.
In the case of the Libya story, words such as “rape” and “abuse” for the Libyan army’s actions as well as “pro-Gaddafi fighters” or “Gaddafi thugs” instead of ‘soldiers’ manage to paint an emotional picture in which vile and illegitimate forces conduct despicable, criminal acts. The POWs, whose testimony is cited as proof, are described as being “eager to repudiate the regime they served as recently as a week ago” (suggesting disloyalty) and as “enthusiastic about detailing [their] orders” (suggesting they enjoyed following them); this serves to highlight the inhumane nature of the enemy.
In the beheaded babies story, we encounter words with high emotional value. The incursion by Hamas forces is called a “massacre” and expressions such as “slaughtered,” “butchered,” “harrowing images,” “gruesome deaths”, “worse than ISIS” or “unspeakable horrors” evoke an image of excessive and savage violence. There is also a substantial amount of detail in describing what the IDF soldiers claim to have seen. Such detail makes the atrocities extremely visceral and the perpetrators viciously brutal, in particular as the violent acts were directed against the most vulnerable—infants.
Overall, there is considerable use of evaluative and/or visceral language which always suggests the righteousness of one side while implying the nefarious nature of the other. From the viewpoint of the audience, it is difficult to see the side portrayed as the enemy as anything but a condemnable and barbaric entity. Through linguistic means, the discourse creates a dichotomy between the forces of good and the forces of evil.
4.3 Reporting verbs, presuppositions and hedging
All three stories are overwhelmingly presented as factual—either explicitly or implicitly, and there is very little scrutiny regarding the veracity of the claims.
For the Russian bounty story, the original article by the NYT uses some degree of tentative language; however, at the same time it also uses verbs that indicate a high degree of certainty. Follow-up articles tend to gradually ascribe more certainty to the story. The dominant way to convey the claim is the reporting verb “say” or the phrase “according to.” There is barely any use of hedging. The alleged bounties are said to have been “disclosed” by the intelligence community—disclosing something necessitates that there is something to disclose.
The shift in the degree of certainty in the headlines is also noteworthy. While the NYT article indicated that this is a statement by intelligence, the Guardian omitted this information in the headline—the claim is framed as certified information. The Times does the same in their article, in which on top of endorsing the claim, they also add information that was not part of the original story. The Washington Post also included the source of the claim, but used the phrase “intelligence finds,” which would seem to indicate that the statement is based on solid evidence.
Credibility of the bounty claims is also suggested by means of presupposition. For instance, in one article it says “[t]he official did not describe the mechanics of the Russian operation, such as how targets were picked or how money changed hands”. This implies the existence of said operation, the factuality that targets were picked and that money did in fact change hands.
With regard to the Libya story, especially during the early phase of the claims, some hedging language can be found. However, even then the reporting verb that was by far most frequently used is “say,” with only the occasional use of “claim”. The veracity of the claim is treated as a conditional: “If it is true that Gaddafi’s troops are being issued Viagra […] it could indicate”, but at the same time it is implicitly validated through reference to previous rape cases that achieved publicity (cf. Iman al-Obeidi). When the ICC prosecutor went public with the fact that he was “gathering evidence” for the use of rape as a weapon of war, some media outlets such as the BBC rendered his tentative statements into a factual: “Luis Moreno-Ocampo said rape was a new aspect of Colonel Gaddafi’s repression” (BBC, 2011). The word “evidence” is occasionally used, though the nature of this evidence remains elusive except for some references to “cellphone videos of rapes” which “have been hard to locate—because, Colonel Qaddafi’s opponents assert, cellphone users quickly delete them to protect women” (Fahim, 2011). Considering the absence of concrete evidence, the frequent use of “say” to cite these claims appears like under-hedging. Despite this lack of evidence, some headlines frame the claim as rather certain, as France24 ’s headline shows: “Gaddafi ordered mass rape, ICC prosecutor says” (France24, 2011), a claim that is not supported by the article itself (“we are getting some information”). At times, presuppositions are also used to endorse the claim: “It was difficult to know how widespread the use of rape is in Libya”, which implies it is not a question of if but of how often . The only news article to show any scrutiny of the claims relatively early on was published in the Guardian on April 29, 2011. It consistently uses “claim” to cite the allegations and includes disclaimers such as “without offering any evidence” and “Foreign affairs specialists expressed scepticism about both claims” (MacAskill, 2011).
Of all three stories, the story on beheaded babies is reported in the most factual manner from the start. There is virtually no hedging in reporting the claims that dozens of babies were killed, with some cases of beheadings, and when it is, this is mostly in relation to the details of the event, for example: “The children in particular appeared to have suffered gruesome deaths” (Pfeffer, 2023). The dominant reporting verbs are “say”, “tell”, “add” and “describe” and there is extensive use of eyewitness testimony which remains unchallenged. Presuppositions are used to imply that bodies of beheaded babies were actually found. This credibility of the claim is also reflected in many of the headlines: “‘Babies had their throats cut’: how Kfar Aza kibbutz massacre unfolded” (Pfeffer, 2023), “Hamas kills 40 babies and children—beheading some of them—at Israeli kibbutz: report” (Land, 2023) or “At least 40 babies, some beheaded, found by Israel soldiers in Hamas-attacked village” (Richard, 2023). The degree of likelihood in relation to the scarcity of evidence is very high.
We can therefore identify two patterns: one is where hedging is frequently used while unfounded claims are repeatedly covered to ‘muddy the waters’; and another that shows an absence of hedging even though the evidence in the text does not support a high degree of likelihood. The first caters to the idea that “where there’s smoke, there’s fire”, i.e., not all might be substantial, but considering the amount of stories, something is bound to be true. The second betrays a problematic credulity with regard to information that comes from sources that the journalist—for one reason or another—considers trustworthy. It results in the audience being presented a claim as fact.
4.4 Principles of war propaganda
Each of the three stories incorporates instances of Morelli’s principles of war propaganda, in particular as it relates to atrocity stories of extreme brutality and moral debasement. We see principles 3 (the evil leader principle), 5 (the atrocities principle) and 6 (the illegal weapons principle) being especially prolific in the articles included in this paper.
By virtue of their use, news discourse presents the enemy leader(s) as ruthless and evil, to a degree that it seems useless to negotiate with them. The atrocities that are ascribed to the enemy are horrifying and/or insidious to such a degree that the adversary seems less like a human foe than a lecherous and bloodthirsty barbarian (in stories 2 and 3) or as a nefarious and cowardly, therefore untrustworthy, opponent (story 1). This is designed to increase popular indignation and support for retributive action.
The reason why these three principles are particularly eminent is because they are most efficient for conflict initiation or rekindled motivation, whereas the others oftentimes function to maintain the framing of a just and clean war.
4.5 Amplification/retraction and bandwagon effect
All three stories received immediate and broad amplification, also owing to the spectacular and/or abhorrent nature of their claims. Within hours, the stories spread across the mainstream media spectrum, with bandwagon journalism playing a considerable role. In the Russian bounty story, the original exposé by the New York Times was promulgated without any further vetting supposedly due to the high credibility that the ‘paper of record’ holds. Follow-up stories then repeatedly reference the NYT as the origin of the story. By pinpointing the origin of a claim to a newspaper report rather than an individual source, the fact that the story emerged from an anonymous figure is backgrounded and credibility is boosted. When the story fell apart, some news outlets published stories on that development, though these were not amplified to the same degree as the original story. The latter, at the time of writing, still stands uncorrected and without an editor’s note.
The Libya story was amplified in the course of consecutive waves of reporting, with the first wave receiving the least uptake. The story began with an individual accusation by the alleged rape victim Iman al-Obeidi and confessions by POWs, and then moved to US accusations of mass rape in wave 2 and climaxed in reports of the ICC prosecutor investigating these accusations. References back to the original claim by al-Obeidi or others serve as credibility markers, while statements calling for caution, such as those by Amnesty International, barely feature. Despite the fact that no evidence has ever emerged for the claims of mass rape, none of the articles features an update or an editor’s note.
The beheaded babies story spread as quickly as it was debunked. The story was broken on October 10, 2023 and had been essentially refuted by the Israeli government by October 12 (Chance et al., 2023). Originally published by the Israeli outlet i24News , the story quickly featured in European and US-American outlets. Most of the stories referred back to i24News ’ original reporting, the veracity of which remained mostly undisputed and unquestioned. According to a Washington Post article, posts on “40 murdered babies” on the social media platform X “garnered at least 44 million impressions, 300,000 likes and over 100,000 reposts within a day” (Chance et al., 2023). Even after the story had been officially refuted, the claim of beheaded and slaughtered babies continued to be used, most notably by then-US president Joe Biden (Kessler, 2023). None of the original articles has been retracted, though the i24News and Times of Israel articles had an editor’s note added that suggested the unconfirmed nature of the claims. The follow-up articles used in this corpus, however, had no such notes attached and still stand uncorrected.
4.6 Impact on conflict initiation/continuation
War propaganda is always purposeful because it intends to achieve a particular goal, such as preparing the ground for the initiation of a new conflict or for the continuation of an ongoing conflict which is affected by a lack of mobilization or legitimacy.
The Russian bounty story emerged at a time “when the Trump administration is negotiating with the Taliban and has committed to removing all troops from the country by next year [2021] (a move that is now being blocked by the House Armed Services Committee because of the bounty scandal)” (MacLeod, 2020). Seeing that it was based on artificial claims by unnamed officials, it could be argued that its intent was to create an atmosphere in which the withdrawal of troops from Afghanistan would be seen as a shameful defeat and thus very difficult to argue in favor of.
The Libya story on alleged mass rape was useful to argue for a continued and intensified NATO bombing campaign in the war-torn country. The campaign had increasingly faced criticism by UN Security Council members such as Russia and China. The claims reached great publicity when the US ambassador to the UN, Susan Rice, told diplomats that the US believed rape was officially sanctioned as a policy of war in Libya. This happened “as the UN Security Council wrangled over coalition attacks in Libya” (AFP, 2011a). According to diplomats present at the meeting, “she made the comment as part of a debate with another envoy to highlight that ‘the coalition is confronting an adversary doing reprehensible things’” (ibid). It resulted in “Nato extend[ing] its air operations for another 90 days and increased the scope of its UN-mandated campaign to protect civilians” (BBC, 2011). It seems clear that the claims here served as a direct catalyst to prolong NATO’s intervention in Libya.
The claim that Hamas beheaded babies emerged in the wake of October 7, 2023, and Hamas’ unprecedented attack on Israeli border communities and border patrols. The horrific and unheard-of nature of the claim shocked the world and alongside other unproven pieces of atrocity claims (such as that babies were burned in ovens or that pregnant women were cut open and impaled;Blumenthal, 2025) facilitated the argument for why Israel had to go into Gaza with full force, while cutting the enclave off from daily necessities. Israeli defense minister Yoav Gallant was quoted as saying: “I have ordered a complete siege on the Gaza Strip. There will be no electricity, no food, no fuel, everything is closed […] We are fighting human animals and we are acting accordingly” (Fabian, 2023). The rationale for pushing such atrocity claims beyond the documented atrocities committed on October 7 was arguably to ensure that Israel had maximum international support with minimal objections to its military reaction.
Across the three cases, however, the discursive patterns discussed cannot be explained exclusively in terms of ideological orientations but also have to be understood under the lens of crisis journalism’s structural pressures. In breaking-news environments characterized by speed, uncertainty and cross-platform competition, newsrooms are incentivized to privilege spectacular atrocity claims and emotionally charged visuals over time-consuming verification, thereby intensifying the effects identified byAllen (2006)andCottle (2011)for crisis reporting more generally.
5 Conclusion
This analysis has examined three widely publicized fake news stories and identified recurring patterns in how they operate, with sources emerging as a central factor. The frequent, and at times exclusive, reliance on unaccountable anonymous officials and invested eyewitnesses creates fertile ground for laundering fabricated or misleading claims through mainstream outlets.
Across all three cases, emotional and often visceral language, atrocity framing, and selective use of reporting verbs and presuppositions helped recast allegations into perceived facts, especially when amplified through bandwagon journalism. Sensational headlines and repeated coverage without adequate hedging or follow-up corrections further entrenched these narratives, while later debunkings received far less attention, if they were mentioned at all.
These practices are particularly dangerous in democratic societies, where public support for war must be manufactured rather than imposed. Applying principles of war propaganda that depict enemies as evil, barbaric, and beyond negotiation facilitates the initiation or escalation of armed conflict, while hiding the degree to which such consent rests on deception. When mainstream media act less as watchdogs than as transmission belts for atrocity stories that serve state and military agendas, citizens lose a crucial line of defense against manipulation and are pushed toward accepting policies antithetical to their own interests.
For media organizations, these findings represent a clear call for action. In the context of war reporting, but in particular when it comes to atrocity stories, source verification needs to trump the structural impulse for quick publication. This applies especially in the current age of crisis-driven ‘churnalism’ (Davies, 2008), where atrocities and emotional appeal maximize clicks and shares amid the next breaking-news frenzy. This is, above all, an ethical dilemma, but it is also a structural failure: verification yields to speed/competition, legitimizing propaganda despite uncertainty through the creation of moral outrage. Scrutiny is especially warranted when claims originate from anonymous sources or from invested eyewitnesses who oftentimes employ intense emotionality to manage perceptions. When a published story is shown to be based on false information, news organizations should tackle this heads-on and give prominent space to a debunker or correction article—not only to ensure that their audiences are able to correct wrongly held beliefs, but also to regain audience trust in the media’s integrity in times where trust in public institutions is hemorrhaging.
While this study suggests a consistent failure on the media’s part to act as a safeguard against war propaganda, it is limited by its small scope and lack of quantitative analysis. Future studies should examine other established cases of fake news in war contexts and strive to strengthen these findings by including a corpus-based quantitative analysis in order to systematically identify patterns in sourcing, amplification, and non-retraction. Another limitation lies in the fact that this study has exclusively investigated western media outlets. Further research could explore whether similar disinformation/verification patterns appear in non-western or alternative media systems as well.
