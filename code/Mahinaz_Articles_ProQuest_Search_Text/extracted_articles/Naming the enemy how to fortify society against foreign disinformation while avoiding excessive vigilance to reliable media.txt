Introduction
Governments and other organizations have launched various countermeasures to address the problem of disinformation disseminated by foreign adversaries. Paradoxically, research has shown that warnings and educational measures intended to fortify societies against disinformation can potentially erode trust in both democracy (Ross et al. 2022; Jungherr and Rauchfleisch, 2024) and in reliable information and trustworthy media (van der Meer et al. 2023; Clayton et al. 2020; Hameleers, 2023). This inadvertently serves the objectives of authoritarian states such as Russia, which disseminate disinformation in part to foster a general sense of suspicion, confusion, and cynicism that undermines societal stability long-term (Bennett and Livingston, 2018; Elswah and Howard, 2020; Pomerantsev, 2014).
This naturally leads to the question of how we can inoculate against disinformation without producing unintended consequences. In this study, we advance research on disinformation countermeasures by examining a novel link between media literacy, threat perceptions, and social cohesion. We propose that a media literacy education that highlights the foreign nature of certain disinformation campaigns may help citizens discern and resist unreliable information without losing trust in reliable media and governmental communication. We believe that awareness of an external threat, in tandem with a heightened national identity salience that strengthens social cohesion, is likely to move people from a default position of third-person bias (“it’s other people who are affected by disinformation”) to a position of “this concerns all of us”. This could increase motivation to successfully learn and apply what one is taught in media literacy training. To assess this idea, we examined the effect rates of two educational videos in a preregistered experiment in Sweden.
The study emerged in response to calls for additional research on how to inform about disinformation without causing negative side effects (Clayton et al. 2020; Jungherr and Rauchfleisch, 2024). Findings from previous experiments on excessive vigilance to reliable media are indecisive, with relatively small effect sizes and inconsistent results across test groups. Researching the balance between gullibility and skepticism, Hameleers (2023) tested the effects of disinformation interventions in the Netherlands and the U.S., finding statistically significant effects of reduced trust in reliable media only among U.S. participants. Furthermore, Humprecht et al. (2020, p. 507) highlight the need for more research on how resilience to disinformation can be sustained in countries where vulnerability to online disinformation has thus far been a lesser problem. Research on misleading information in general has primarily focused on the U.S. (Tucker et al. 2018; Humprecht et al. 2020), which, compared to Northern Europe, is a low-trust society (Ortiz-Ospina et al. 2016). Aiming to explain the varying occurrence of disinformation, Humprecht et al. (2020) found that the U.S.—characterized by low media trust, populist rhetoric, polarization, and a politicized and fragmented media environment—displayed low resilience to online disinformation, whereas countries in Western Europe and Canada—characterized by high media trust, less fragmented news consumption, stronger public service broadcasting, and less populist communication and polarization—displayed higher resilience. Being a high-trust society (Ortiz-Ospina et al. 2016), Sweden is in the ‘high resilience’ category. It is also a key target of Russian influence operations (Ramsay and Robertshaw, 2019, p. 80). This makes Sweden a suitable case for advancing research on potentially negative side effects of informing about unreliable information.
Research questions
We ask: Does education in source criticism and a heightened awareness of the strategic spread of unreliable information improve abilities to differentiate between trustworthy and non-trustworthy information (RQ1)? Does it risk reducing trust in domestic, trustworthy media and governmental communication, in addition to foreign untrustworthy media (RQ2)? And: Can emphasizing the foreign nature of non-trustworthy information, and priming national identity, reduce the risk of raising suspicion towards domestic, trustworthy, media (RQ3)?
Our results show that both versions of our media literacy education improved participants’ abilities to discern between foreign disinformation and legitimate information, without generating the negative side effects on trust expected from previous research. Furthermore, the group for which non-trustworthy information was presented as a foreign threat displayed significantly more trust in reliable media and governmental communication, while discounting foreign disinformation. These findings contrast with previous research in the U.S. setting. We end by problematizing the idea of designing media literacy trainings that amplify external threat perceptions, and discuss avenues for future research.
Why foreign disinformation?
In today’s digital media landscape, various forms of problematic information circulate, including misinformation—incorrect or misleading information shared unintentionally—and disinformation, which refers to deliberately false or misleading material spread with the intent to deceive (Armitage and Vaccari, 2021, p. 38). Both forms pose challenges for audiences seeking accurate information and for authorities striving to provide accurate information and knowledge to their citizens. Addressing these issues may involve solutions like promoting media and information literacy, as well as fostering critical evaluation of sources. However, disinformation—which is intentionally crafted to deceive—warrants particular concern.
Hameleers defines disinformation as a “context-bound deliberate act for which actors covertly deceive recipients by de-contextualizing, manipulating or fabricating information to maximize utility with the (targeted) outcome of misleading recipients” (Hameleers, 2022, p. 6). This kind of deliberate deception often amplifies lies and false narratives, especially concerning political issues, which can have severe societal consequences, such as influencing election outcomes or undermining democratic institutions.
Disinformation threatens to erode trust in media, politics, and democracy regardless of its origins, but not necessarily in the same way. While domestic disinformation can be spread by a whole cast of domestic actors—irresponsible politicians, lobbyists, conspiracy theorists, etc.—targeted foreign disinformation is usually spread by foreign states or state-adjacent actors. In Network Propaganda (2018), Benkler and Faris argue that domestic media is actually more likely than foreign states to succeed in disseminating (dis)information and influencing audiences. That said, foreign disinformation operates on a different scale than domestic efforts. Targeted foreign disinformation represents a de facto state-to-state issue with the potential to escalate into diplomatic disputes or even armed conflict. States engaging in cross-border disinformation campaigns typically have larger budgets than domestic media houses in small or medium-sized countries and can thus orchestrate operations on a far greater scale. Such campaigns may be part of hybrid warfare strategies aimed at destabilizing other nations and setting the stage for further escalation, potentially leading to war.
A variety of labels have been used for foreign disinformation projected by antagonistic actors, including sharp power (Walker, 2018; Glazunova et al. 2023), propaganda (Mader et al. 2022; Moore and Colley, 2024), disinformation (Erlich and Garner, 2023; Bennett and Livingston, 2018; Zilinsky et al. 2024), and malign information influence (Wagnsson, 2020). In policy circles, the problem is often labeled Foreign Information Manipulation and Interference (FIMI). The European Union External Action Service (2024) underscores that such communication is manipulative, coordinated, and intentional.1 We use Russian media outlets as an example of foreign disinformation, given Russia’s prominent role as a leading actor in the dissemination of unreliable information (Martin et al. 2023; Papada et al. 2023). Russia has conducted extensive influence operations in the U.S. and Europe (Dawson and Innes, 2019; Bastos and Farkas, 2019; Bradshaw et al. 2022; Elshehawy et al. 2022) and there is a growing influence of Russian disinformation in the Middle East and Africa (Dajani et al. 2021, pp. 442–443; Audinet and Dreyfus, 2023; Kachur, 2022). Furthermore, other states, including China, may be adopting or learning from Russian strategies (Charon and Vilmer, 2021; Brandt and Shafer 2020), which increases the generalizability of this study.
Russian disinformation and its effects
Our experiments used real excerpts from Russian state-sponsored platforms RT and Sputnik as examples of foreign disinformation. In September 2024, the U.S. Secretary of State accused RT of “functioning like a de facto arm of Russia’s intelligence apparatus”, referring to information operations, covert influence, and military procurement. RT specifically was described as the “weaponization of disinformation to subvert and polarize free and open societies extends to every part of the world” (The Guardian, 2024). Likewise, high-level European politicians have long described RT and Sputnik as propaganda outlets disseminating lies and disinformation (France 24, 2017; European Commission, 2022). Scholars in turn view RT as “one of the most important organizations in the global political economy of disinformation” and an “opportunist channel that is used as an instrument of state defense policy to meddle in the politics of other states” (Elswah and Howard, 2020, p. 623) and recent research describes RT and Sputnik as “key global sources of disinformation” (Yang et al. 2024, p. 1).
RT has operated in Russian, English, German, French, Spanish, and Arabic, with followers on platforms such as YouTube, Instagram, Facebook, and Twitter (Elshehawy et al. 2022, p. 863). Russian disinformation, including from RT, continues to reach European audiences even after the EU’s ban of RT and Sputnik in March 2022 (Council of the European Union, 2022). A 2023 survey of a representative sample of the Swedish population revealed that 5% of respondents reported reading RT or Sputnik after the ban (Wagnsson et al. 2024, p. 141). A recent study found that European communities on the political fringes reduce the effectiveness of the ban by sharing pro-Russian disinformation on alternative platforms such as Telegram and TikTok (Santos Okholm et al. 2024).
Russian disinformation also gains traction when disseminated and magnified by mainstream media (Watanabe, 2017; Lukito et al. 2020; Zhang et al. 2021). A recent study found that RT and Sputnik effectively lead coverage of domestic US news outlets of all political leanings on geopolitical reporting, thus setting the news agenda on key topics in international affairs (Yang et al. 2024). A European example is the faked French ‘bedbug scandal’ in 2023, when news about flees was amplified massively by Russian social media accounts, tapping into widespread scares about flees (Reuters, 2024). Russian disinformation also gains influence by collaborating with or posing as domestic alternative media, as shown by a social network analysis that detailed connections and content sharing across platforms between the European far-right and Russian actors (Innes et al. 2021). A recent study revealed that at least half of the leading German-language alternative media outlets are connected to the Kremlin (Beseler and Toepfl, 2024).
Reach is not the same as impact, and while the actual effects of Russian disinformation should not be overstated, there is evidence of measurable consequences. Russian disinformation often aims to sow distrust, doubt, and cynicism by putting pressure and spin on real schisms in targeted societies (Bradshaw et al. 2022). Studies demonstrate that Russian disinformation can trigger negative emotive responses and affective investment (Hoyle et al. 2023), breed polarization (Mader et al. 2022; Peisakhin and Rozenas, 2018), and manipulate political views (Fisher, 2020; Carter and Carter, 2021; Mader et al. 2022).
The erosion of trust—the specific focus of this article—has been a particular concern in research on the effects of the dissemination of foreign disinformation (Bennett and Livingston, 2018; Ross et al. 2022; Kondratov and Johansson-Nogués, 2022). Trust is key to the functioning of democracy (Inglehart, 1988; Putnam, 1993) but can be undermined through the strategic spread of disinformation (Ross et al. 2022; Zimmermann and Kohring, 2020). Disinformation can undermine several dimensions of trust, including trust in reliable information. In the worst-case scenario, low media trust can lead to media nihilism; a general distrust in news and governmental communication (Van Duyn and Collier, 2019, p. 43).
Hypothesis 1: Media literacy education and the risk of excessive vigilance
Research suggests that once someone has been misled by incorrect information, it is difficult to undo the harm by informing them that the information was incorrect (De Keersmaecker and Roets, 2017; Nyhan and Reifler, 2010). The ineffectiveness of post hoc corrections underscores the importance of preventive measures, such as education in media literacy. These often come in the form of various ‘inoculation’ methods.
In the early 20th century, following the widespread use of propaganda during World War I, the U.S. began its first efforts to counteract propaganda. In the 1930s, programs intended to ‘vaccinate’ against propaganda by enhancing people’s ability to recognize disinformation were introduced through school teaching and radio broadcasts (Hobbs and McGee, 2014). Following the vaccination metaphor, modern preemptive inoculation methods have gained increased relevance due to the spread of misinformation and disinformation in the digital sphere (Juhász, 2024). For example, the EU motivates its support of education in critical thinking and cyber and media literacy to novel threats in the information domain (Ördén, 2019, p. 426).
The notion that people need to become more critical of the information they encounter online is based on what can be called the ‘truth-default’ theory, which suggests that humans are naturally inclined to believe others and assume honest communication unless there are indications of deception (Levine, 2018). However, Bryanov’s and Vziatysheva’s (2021) review of research on fake news suggests the opposite, namely that people are primarily steered by ‘deception bias’, which signifies that the initial reaction to media information is distrust. Building on Pantazi et al. (2021), we describe this as excessive vigilance . Pantazi et al. (2021, p. 293) argue that people can be both disproportionally gullible and vigilant and that “…the two mechanisms appear to ‘misfire’ in the domain of political information, resulting in people appearing overly gullible towards inaccurate information and overly vigilant towards truthful information.”
This leads us to the focus of this article; that information about disinformation—which is a necessary component of inoculation methods—can yield negative side effects. Experimental studies in the U.S. (Clayton et al. 2020; Hameleers, 2023; van der Meer et al. 2023), Chile (Bachmann and Valenzuela, 2023), and South Korea (Lee and Jang, 2023) indicate that educating people about mis/disinformation through media literacy programs inadvertently increases their tendency to doubt legitimate information. Studies also show that indiscriminate warnings and careless elite discourse about fake news and disinformation can erode trust in reliable information (Van Duyn and Collier, 2019; Jungherr and Rauchfleisch, 2024). In addition, one study demonstrated that warning people about deepfakes (manipulated videos) did nothing to enhance their ability to spot fake videos. It did, however, lead them to accuse authentic videos of being fake (Ternovski et al. 2022). In line with this, Gray et al. (2020) warn that the discourse on disinformation, bots, trolls, deepfakes, and the attention economy can contribute to the emergence of an ‘infrastructural uncanny’ in which people are confused about what is real and fake. Additionally, research in the U.S. demonstrates that overemphasis on disinformation circulating in society can breed dissatisfaction with democracy (Tomz and Weeks, 2020; Ross et al. 2022; Jungherr and Rauchfleisch, 2024).
Further research is required to establish to what extent excessive vigilance poses a problem, whether it can be addressed, and how to find a balance between being appropriately aware of threats and being overly vigilant. Here, we begin by testing the following hypothesis:
H1 : Media literacy training improves discernment between unreliable and reliable information but also increases distrust towards reliable information [excessive vigilance] .
The next step is to assess whether negative side effects can be mitigated. How this could be done is discussed in the next section, which explores the connection between social cohesion, threat perceptions, and excessive vigilance.
Hypothesis 2—Strengthening social cohesion to avert excessive vigilance
We suggest that one way to mitigate some of the negative effects that may be associated with disinformation education (e.g., decreased trust) is to inform citizens that they are ‘under attack’. According to social identity theory, a perceived threat to the community should affect group identification (Hogg and Smith, 2007). Specifically, the prevalence of an external threat against the nation should heighten citizens’ national identity salience, i.e., make them identify more with their national belonging. This strengthened sense of national cohesion should increase prosocial intentions and trust within this group and might inhibit subgroup conflict along political divisions (Levendusky, 2018). The idea of priming national identity is thus not to nurture nationalism, but to strengthen a sense of social cohesion, making people put differences aside and trust each other in the face of a common challenge.
How may such an effect reduce the risk of excessive vigilance? Our study measures vigilance on two levels. It features a survey in which participants rate their trust in various media sources, intended to measure outcomes at the attitudinal level. It also features a ‘truth evaluation exercise’ in which participants are tasked to differentiate between news text items from either disinformation outlets or trustworthy news sources, thus getting at the behavioral level. Concerning attitudes, it is conceivable that heightened perceptions of the prevalence of an external threat (Russian disinformation) will decrease trust in media sources associated with said threat (Russian disinformation outlets). On the flip side, the increased sense of national cohesion that comes from being ‘under attack’ seems likely to increase trust in media sources associated with the national identity group in question (Swedish public service broadcasting, Swedish qualitative news outlets, international news outlets of close allies, and information from Swedish government agencies). Concerning the behavioral level (evaluation exercise), the connection between national identity salience and distrust of unreliable media is less clear, as attitudes by themselves are insufficient to successfully differentiate between foreign disinformation and trustworthy content (which requires media literacy skills). However, to the extent that some participants are familiar with RT and Sputnik (the sources are visible to the participants), they may rank items from these outlets as untrustworthy, for the same reasons as outlined above. Furthermore, it is conceivable that a heightened national identity salience could move people from a default position of third-person bias (“it’s other people who are affected”) to a position of “we’re in this together”, which could increase motivation to successfully learn and use what one is taught in the media literacy training. We thus test the following hypothesis:
H2 : Media literacy training that emphasizes disinformation as a foreign threat—together with a national identity prime—improves discernment between unreliable and reliable information and strengthens trust in reliable information .
Methods
The survey experiment took place online from 5 to 21 March 2024.2 We used the research agency Novus to recruit a random sample of Swedes over the age of 18 from a national web panel (no opportunity for self-recruitment) representative of the Swedish population. Novus invited 3181 people in total. 1068 opted to participate and 14 were lost due to non-consent, technical errors, or failing the attention check, leaving a final N of 1054. The sample was 54.1% male; the median age was 53 ( M  = 51.95, SD = 17.19); and 61.8% reported finishing some form of tertiary education.
After giving informed consent participants were randomly divided into three groups. Treatment group 1 (T1, n  = 345) partook in a short reflection exercise asking them to consider why people consume news. They then watched an educational video (“Video 1”, length: 3:37 min) about disinformation and media literacy.3 The video provided a general overview of disinformation and emphasized the importance of source criticism. It began by stating that democracy relies on both trust and the free exchange of ideas, but that recent changes in how information is consumed have introduced tensions between these values. In light of this, the video stressed the necessity of practicing source criticism. Viewers were informed that “a critical approach to source evaluation begins with careful reading and interpretation.” They were encouraged to question the sender’s identity, consider who might benefit from the message, and examine the values it promotes. The video also pointed out that the reporting style can reveal the sender’s intentions, observing that credible sources often strive for objectivity and a neutral tone that avoids excessive bias or emotional appeals. Notably, Video 1 did not address specific external actors or threats but concluded with a general reminder of the critical role source criticism plays in sustaining democracy. Great care was also taken to edit Video 1 to avoid introducing unintentional primes related to national identity. The control group ( n  = 353) did the same reflection exercise as T1. They then watched an educational video about the ‘Internet of Things’ (“Video 3”, 3:52 min).
Treatment group 2 (T2, n  = 356) partook in a short reflection exercise in which they were asked to reflect on and write down reasons why people like Sweden. A similar exercise successfully heightened national identity salience in Levendusky (2018). They then watched an educational video (“Video 2”, 4:05 min) about disinformation and media literacy. Video 2 began with the same introduction as Video 1, providing identical recommendations on how to practice source criticism. However, this video also highlighted how foreign actors use unreliable information to sow division within Sweden. Russian disinformation was specifically addressed, with eight named outlets, including Sputnik and RT, briefly shown in the graphics (though not mentioned by the narrator). Instead of concluding with a generic statement about the value of source criticism, this video ended with a more patriotic call to action: “If we all practice source criticism and remain alert to information influence, we can protect our society from foreign threats and preserve a free democracy. This requires that you, I, and everyone else in Sweden work together to avoid being deceived.” Drawing on Fischer et al. (2010)’s experimental study of British national identity and the 2005 London bombings, we expected the initial priming of national identity, paired with the video cues of external threat prevalence, to create stronger in-group (national) identification for T2.
All participants had to spend at least 1 min on the reflection exercises. The median time spent on all parts of the experiment was 18 min. We created the two media literacy trainings (Videos 1 and 2) by editing an authentic training video from the Swedish Civil Contingencies Agency (a government agency). This ensured that the videos felt realistic and professionally produced, while also increasing the validity and extra-scientific relevance of the study. All videos were similar in style and featured the same narrator. The videos were embedded on the same platform (designed by the sample and survey provider Novus) as the truth evaluation exercise and the questionnaire. The videos, the exercise, and the questionnaire were all unskippable. To illustrate the appearance of the videos, a few screenshots are provided (Fig. 1).

After watching the respective videos, all participants completed the truth evaluation exercise, similar to how previous experiments evaluate the effectiveness of inoculation interventions (e.g. Hameleers, 2023). The participants were asked to rate the plausibility of ten authentic news excerpts, ranging from 28 to 68 words each.4 Five excerpts were taken from trustworthy media outlets—SVT (Swedish public service), DN (major daily in Sweden), and the BBC—and five came from Russian state media known to spread disinformation (Sputnik and RT, hereafter referred to as ‘disinformation outlets’). The excerpts from BBC and Swedish outlets contained factual and correct reporting on international issues. Russian excerpts consisted of content on similar topics that could be deemed as disinformation targeting Sweden or the West more broadly. We chose to include only Russian disinformation excerpts to maintain a coherent tone and consistent, realistic, themes, ensuring they aligned with the real threat depicted in the training videos. The messaging in these excerpts was either entirely false or heavily skewed and/or exaggerated, and sometimes written in a sensationalist or mocking style. We selected the excerpts to represent broader themes found in RT and Sputnik. For example, one excerpt frames NATO as an aggressor; three frames Sweden and other EU countries as puppets for the U.S./NATO; and one paints an exaggerated picture of a benevolent and friendly Russia. We also wanted it to be possible for an attentive reader to pick up on stylistic cues that may signal the intent of the sender, as they were taught in the media literacy training. Examples of such cues include terms like “‘the Russian threat’” (in scarecrows), partisan and value-laden language (“The EU blindly followed the USA”, or “the friendship between Russia and Africa is stronger than ever”); and a slightly mocking tone (“Sweden and Finland willingly allowed themselves to be pushed into NATO without substantive reason”). English language excerpts were translated into Swedish, and some excerpts were shortened. The sources were visible below each excerpt and the excerpts were presented in a randomized order (see our supplementary material for a more detailed discussion and English-language versions of all excerpts).5
In addition to the truth evaluation exercise, all participants filled out a questionnaire measuring their trust in various media and government communications, and their attitudes concerning the threat of disinformation. The questions closely mirror those used in common national surveys in Sweden, such as the SOM surveys, with minor adaptations. Responses were recorded using traditional 7-point Likert scales. The order of the questionnaire items was randomized, as was the order of the exercise vs. the questionnaire (some filled out the questionnaire before the exercise, some after) to avoid undesired order effects. At the end of the experiment, the participants were debriefed and told of the nature of the excerpts. The statistical analysis was carried out in SPSS and Stata, following a preregistered analysis plan.
We believe this design gets at the heart of the empirical questions in a methodologically sound manner. The combination of control group comparisons, random sampling, and a representative sample facilitates a clear and generalizable (at least at the country level) effect measure while maintaining statistical simplicity. The between-subjects design (each participant experiences only one condition) along with random assignment to treatment groups and the randomization of both question order and the sequence of the questionnaire and exercise, minimizes the risk of carryover or order effects, thereby enhancing internal validity. Additionally, the study’s preregistration ensures transparency, reduces bias, and supports easy replication.
Results
Manipulation checks
We measured national identity salience by asking participants (i) to which degree they feel Swedish and (ii) whether they feel similar to other Swedes ( α  = 0.62). The average national identity salience increased from 5.64 in the control condition to 5.80 in T2 (on a 1–7 scale). The treatment thus had a noticeable effect (we see practically no change in T1, who were not primed), with a comparable effect size as in Levendusky (2018). While the difference between the control condition and T2 was not statistically significant at the predetermined level ( p  < 0.05), it approached significance ( p  = 0.053), and the experimental design and null result for T1 suggest that the priming was effective.
The effects from the stimuli (the videos) were stronger than that of the priming. As compared to the control condition, T2’s average ‘perceived prevalence of external attack’ (“I believe that foreign actors are trying to harm Sweden through information influence”, 1–7) increased by 0.42 ( p  < 0.001) and T2’s ‘perceived threat of disinformation’ (“I perceive it as a societal threat that foreign actors are using information influence”, 1–7) increased by 0.26 ( p  < 0.05).67
Abilities to discern foreign disinformation
The initial part of Hypothesis 1 states that media literacy training improves discernment between dubious and legitimate information. So, did the treatments improve discernment? To increase validity, reliability, and robustness, we tested this using five different operationalizations.8 We used the truth evaluation exercise (behavioral level) to measure distrust in disinformation excerpts (more distrust towards disinformation excerpts indicating better discernment) and two types of accuracy (placing trust in trustworthy excerpts while distrusting untrustworthy ones + identifying deceptive intentions in disinformation excerpts but not in regular media excerpts). We used the questionnaire (attitudinal level) to measure trust in media more generally. Specifically, we looked at distrust towards disinformation outlets and ‘critical media assessment skills’ (trusting trustworthy sources while expressing skepticism towards less trustworthy ones). It follows from H1 that T1 and T2 would display more distrust towards the disinformation excerpts; higher accuracy; more distrust towards disinformation outlets; and higher levels of critical media assessment skills than the control group, indicating increased discernment.
As seen in Table 1, both T1 and T2 improved their discernment across most metrics. Both performed better than the control group in the truth evaluation game: They were significantly more distrustful of the disinformation excerpts and showcased significantly higher accuracy (on both accuracy measurements). The difference was the starkest for T2. On the attitudinal level, only T2 showed a statistically significant increase in critical media assessment skills (‘CMAS’). Perhaps surprisingly, neither group showed any statistically significant increase in distrust toward disinformation outlets. This is probably due to a ceiling effect, as the average distrust in the control condition was already 6.36 on a 1–7 scale. Apart from getting more answers ‘right’, we also find that the trainings made T1 and T2 slightly more confident in their discernment capabilities (T1: B  = 1.206, p  = 0.097; T2: B  = 2.396, p  = 0.001; Scale: 7–70). Excessive vigilance?
We now turn to the second part of Hypothesis 1, which states that media literacy training increases distrust toward legitimate information. In other words, the claim being tested is that interventions intended to enhance vigilance against disinformation may cause a reduction of trust in legitimate communication. If true, we should expect our two treatment groups to become more distrustful of trustworthy media. We investigated whether this was the case by measuring the participants’ distrust of the regular (trustworthy) news excerpts in the truth evaluation game, and with the questionnaire, which asked about their trust in mainstream media outlets, public service media, and governmental communication. We also asked the participants to estimate the total percentage of disinformation, propaganda, and false claims in the media they consume. An increase in such a measurement would be a potential indication of excessive vigilance.
It follows from H1 that T1, who had been warned of disinformation without receiving any potential boost to their sense of social cohesion, would show signs of excessive vigilance. Counter to this hypothesis, neither T1 nor T2 showed increased distrust toward the regular media excerpts. In fact, they became more trusting (dependent variable ‘distrust towards regular media excerpts’, T1: B  = −0.15, p  = 0.052; T2: B  = −0.22, p  = 0.005; 1–7 scale). Furthermore, they showed increased trust in mainstream media outlets, public service media, and governmental communication, as seen in Table 2. T1 and T2 also estimated a lower percentage of perceived disinformation in the media they consumed compared to the control group (statistically significant for T2). Overall, Hypothesis 1—media literacy training improves discernment between dubious and legitimate information but also increases distrust towards legitimate information—was partially supported. Our data agrees with the first part of the hypothesis; media literacy training does indeed improve discernment capabilities. However, counter to the second claim, this does not seem to come at the cost of excessive vigilance.
Consequences of emphasizing external threat and national cohesion
Hypothesis 2 states that media literacy training incorporating national identity priming and emphasizing the foreign nature of the disinformation threat increases discernment capabilities and strengthens trust. The idea is that an emphasis on external attack and national cohesion could boost domestic trust levels (both horizontal and vertical) and heighten vigilance to foreign unreliable messaging. As such, the interesting difference to examine is that between T1 and T2. If the hypothesis is true, T2 should showcase better discernment capabilities and more trust in legitimate sources than T1.

Given the experimental design of our study, it is likely that these differences are due to the differences in treatment, but given the lack of significance, it is not unthinkable that they could be due to chance. It thus seems likely (but only partially supported) that media literacy training emphasizing the (foreign) threat aspect of disinformation does increase trust in trustworthy sources. This seems especially true regarding government communication (the strongest and only significant relationship in the comparison of T1 and T2, with a noticeably higher adjusted R 2 value).
This study has examined new ways to inform and educate about disinformation. The experiment was set up to test the idea that emphasizing foreign threats and national identity can improve the results of media literacy training and reduce the risk of excessive vigilance to reliable information. In contrast to several previous studies, we did not identify excessive vigilance after warning participants about disinformation.
According to our experiment, media literacy education works. Our results also indicate that an awareness of the threat of foreign disinformation can increase the capacity to discern between reliable and unreliable information and strengthen trust in reliable media and governmental communication. This summary of our findings will be developed and problematized below.
Discussion and conclusion
Improved awareness → improved discernment
Turning to our first research question (RQ1), we found that education in source criticism and a heightened awareness of the strategic spread of unreliable information improved participants’ abilities to differentiate between trustworthy and non-trustworthy information. The improvements were the strongest in T2 who had been informed about the threat of foreign disinformation.
No evidence for excessive vigilance
The education did not reduce trust in domestic, trustworthy media and governmental communication (RQ2). Neither T1 nor T2 became excessively vigilant. In actuality, both treatment groups became more trusting of the regular media excerpts, and T2 also displayed increased trust in governmental communication, mainstream media outlets, and public service media. T2 also estimated a lower share of perceived disinformation in their media consumption compared to the control group. We see this as a rough estimate of media trust, indicating that the education did not decrease the participants’ trust in the media they consume on a daily basis.
A ‘foreign framing’ increases domestic trust
Furthermore, an emphasis on disinformation as a foreign threat, and the priming of national identity, did reduce the risk of excessive vigilance toward domestic, trustworthy media (RQ3). T2 displayed less distrust towards the regular media excerpts, mainstream media outlets, public service, and governmental communication, although only the latter reached significance at the predetermined level. T2 also displayed significantly more distrust in disinformation excerpts, but this was not simply due to increased overall skepticism, as T2 also showcased significantly higher discernment accuracy than T1. T2 also displayed more distrust of disinformation outlets and better critical media assessment skills (trusting trustworthy sources), although these results were not significant. These results indicate that the media literacy education that emphasized the threat of disinformation increased trust in trustworthy sources.9
Our contribution
This study advances previous research in several ways. First, experimental studies in the U.S. suggest that warnings of disinformation must be carefully crafted and precise to minimize or avoid negative side effects (Freeze et al. 2021; Jungherr and Rauchfleisch, 2024). We advance this line of research by crafting and assessing two slightly different versions of education that yielded different effects.
Theoretically, the study advances recent research on countermeasures to foreign disinformation, showing that informing people about the dangers of disinformation projected by foreign powers was the most effective way of sustaining trust in reliable communication. The strongest (and only significant) effect in the comparison between the trust of T1 and T2 concerned trust in governmental communication. Compared to the control condition, T1’s distrust in governmental communication actually increased (+0.17). For T2, it decreased (−0.15). This could indicate that the effect is of most importance regarding governmental communications (which may suggest that a so-called ‘rally round the flag effect is at play).
Future studies in different contexts
Did the positive effects for T2 stem from these participants’ heightened sense of threat or an increased sense of national cohesion? Our manipulation checks and a further exploratory mediation analysis indicated that the differences between treatment groups are primarily explained by varying threat perceptions, with an increased sense of national cohesion playing a lesser role. A suggestion for future research is to examine whether a heightened sense of national cohesion makes a bigger difference in more polarized states, such as the U.S. Levendusky (2018) demonstrated that priming of national identity in the U.S. decreased polarization, which could boost trust in institutions, including domestic media and governmental communication. Sweden is not as polarized as the U.S., and institutional trust is already high, which may make efforts to increase trust through decreased polarization less relevant. This suggests that media literacy educations are not ‘one-size-fits-all’, and future educational initiatives should take national, cultural, political, and other differences (e.g., digital literacy and language skills) among target audiences into account. An excellent example of such an approach is the prebunking game Gali Fakta by Facciani et al. (2024), which is tailored to an Indonesian audience. In Gali Fakta, players are tasked with helping fictional friends and family members avoid hoaxes in a WhatsApp-inspired setting. Like our two training videos, Gali Fakta significantly improved the players’ discernment capabilities without causing excessive vigilance (Facciani et al. 2024).
Our results also mirror those of Hameleers (2023), who found no indicators of excessive vigilance to reliable media in the Netherlands. Like Sweden, the Netherlands is a high-trust society. Taken together, these results are reason for optimism about the prospects of educational efforts against disinformation in high-trust societies. Additional research is required to test the effects of education highlighting foreign disinformation in low-trust societies with a higher degree of polarization, such as the U.S. An emphasis on a foreign threat and trust promotion (“we’re in this together mindset”/appealing to shared identities) might be even more effective in rendering an effect in polarized societies with lower base levels of trust.
Limitations and potential issues with this approach
A few limitations of the present study, that may also serve as suggestions for future research, need to be addressed. First, a potential objection to our results is that the increased performance of T2 (e.g. increased accuracy) compared to T1 is merely a result of T2 being given more information. After all, Russia was singled out in T2’s stimuli material (Video 2) and RT and Sputnik appeared briefly in the video’s graphics. To check for this, we ran an exploratory regression analysis in which we used treatment status, ‘perceived prevalence of external attack’, ‘perceived threat of disinformation’ and national identity salience as predictor variables. This model showed that differences in accuracy were best predicted by the participant’s sense of threat (both threat variables were significant at the highest level), and not their treatment status per se. In other words, T1 participants who perceived the threat similarly to T2 participants performed comparably on our accuracy measures, meaning that the outcome differentials between T1 and T2 are best explained by T2’s heightened awareness of the threat of foreign disinformation.
Secondly, recent research indicates that a “one-size-fits-all” approach to education is inadequate, and interventions against disinformation must be adapted to the diverse national contexts across Europe (Dragomir et al. 2024). As noted above, Sweden is a state in the ‘high-trust, high resilience category’ and a key target of Russian disinformation. Future research is needed to explore to what extent our results are generalizable in states where trust and resilience to disinformation are weaker. Scholars have identified variations in susceptibility to disinformation, particularly in relation to strengths and weaknesses of the media system, media use, and levels of trust (Dragomir et al. 2024). There is thus a need for replication studies in different national and cultural contexts. The robustness of our results should also be tested in societies with lower awareness and readiness to accept the idea of disinformation as a threat to national security. Swedes are already highly attentive to the problem of foreign disinformation (Wagnsson, 2020), which might have suppressed the effects of our treatments. A lower base level of awareness and less elevated threat perceptions could imply a stronger effect of education in media literacy based on foreign threat perceptions. Alternatively, people might oppose the framing of a specific foreign actor as a threat, which could weaken the effect of the educational effort.
Thirdly, our treatments did not only aim to inform participants about foreign disinformation (and participants in T1 were not informed about the problem with foreign disinformation at all), but also included general recommendations, such as placing more trust in information presented factually and neutrally while being more skeptical of information conveyed in a sensationalist manner. This education improved both groups’ ability to discern foreign disinformation. Future research is however required to determine whether media literacy education designed in this way also improves participants’ abilities to discern domestic disinformation. This includes methods to warn about the spread of disinformation by domestic media outlets, including alternative media, that either collaborate directly with foreign governments who use disinformation as a tool, or align their messaging and content with such entities (Beseler and Toepfl, 2024; Innes et al. 2021).
Fourthly, our educational intervention was a one-time experience. In reality, information consumption is more akin to a constant drip feed of a slush of mixed bits of information, rather than a one-time meal. Additionally, people seldom engage in deliberate truth-evaluation practices while consuming information. It is therefore essential to assess the differences in outcomes between one-time interventions and repeated efforts. Research presents mixed evidence regarding the duration of effectiveness of inoculation against mis/disinformation (Maertens et al. 2021, Zerback et al. 2021; Pfau et al. 2006). Duration of effects can partly depend on decreasing levels of perceived “threat” from disinformation (Maertens et al. 2021, p. 12), which highlights the need for multi-wave survey experiments to track the long-term effects of education, in particular regarding threat perceptions. In line with Maertens et al. (2021, p. 12) we recommend further research on long-term effects and how repeated educational interventions—booster shots—might reduce the risk of diminishing longitudinal effects.
Fifthly, singling out foreign disinformation may have negative side effects. Media literacy education on foreign disinformation threats could (mis)lead people to overlook problems with disinformation in the domestic media ecosystem, and this risk should be assessed in future research. Furthermore, warnings of foreign disinformation can contribute to securitization of information. Informational securitization may challenge liberal values in the information domain, including freedom of expression and the free flow of information. Such an escalation of information warfare can be seen in the EU’s decision to block the Russian outlets RT/Sputnik from broadcasting until the aggression against Ukraine ceases and Russia discontinues the dissemination of “disinformation” and engagement in “information manipulation” against the EU and its member states (Council of the European Union, 2022).
Additionally, naming enemies is indeed customary in international politics. Among scholars of international relations, there is a long tradition of viewing ‘othering’ as a common and even inevitable practice that glues societies together and strengthens national identity (Engels, 2009; Campbell, 1998; Neumann, 1996). Yet, naming and dishonoring specific states when informing about disinformation may lead to the dismissal of valid criticism from non-nationals, foster excessive fear within society, exacerbate the ‘othering’ of certain nationalities and countries, and, in the worst-case scenario, be exploited by actors with malicious intent. Educating citizens about the dangers of foreign disinformation in this way could inadvertently fuel xenophobia and general suspicion of external influences. Singling out specific states could also contribute to polarization in societies where certain segments sympathize with those states. Accusing other states of spreading disinformation is furthermore contrary to liberal ideals of treating others in non-antagonistic, humble ways and avoiding portraying others in ways that sustain hierarchies that can deteriorate the international security situation (Diez and Manners, 2014, pp. 65–67; Wagnsson and Hellman, 2018). On the other hand, one might argue that citizens are entitled to knowledge about the risk of encountering disinformation projected by foreign regimes and antagonistic actors, both in connection with elections and in everyday life. Negative side effects might to some extent be diminished by carefully considering how media literacy education is designed, such as conveying information in factual—not sensationalist and emotional—language and acknowledging that many different actors distribute disinformation in today’s digital media sphere, focusing not only on one single state but presenting several examples. Furthermore, such education could emphasize the importance of distinguishing between a state or a regime, such as Russia, engaging in disinformation, and its citizens. Additionally, it would be valuable to conduct experimental research to examine the potential risks, including the extent to which education on foreign disinformation might inadvertently contribute to fostering xenophobia.
Final conclusions
This article adds to previous research by showing that in contrast to findings in the U.S., boosting awareness of unreliable information among participants in Sweden, a high-trust society, does not generate negative side effects related to trust in media and governmental communications. Furthermore, even though neither of the two trainings yielded excessive vigilance to reliable information, the one highlighting an external threat generated better overall results. Nevertheless, we have highlighted risks connected to the ‘othering’ of foreign regimes and antagonistic actors. Additional research is needed to reach firm conclusions about how to inform about unreliable content in a nuanced way, steering away from exaggerated threat perceptions, while at the same time raising awareness of and clarifying the problem with foreign disinformation.
