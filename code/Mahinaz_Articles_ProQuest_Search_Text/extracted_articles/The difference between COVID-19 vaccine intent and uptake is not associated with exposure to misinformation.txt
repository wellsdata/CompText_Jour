Introduction
In 2020, as COVID-19 spread across the globe without any specific treatment, vaccines emerged as the most promising solution to control the pandemic. Public health authorities worldwide eagerly anticipated their development. Early in the year, academic teams and polling institutes began evaluating the potential uptake of a COVID-19 vaccine upon its availability. Most studies focused on either “vaccine acceptance” or “intent to vaccinate.” For our purposes, we consider these terms synonymous and use the term “intent to vaccinate” throughout this paper.
The Pfizer-BioNTech, Moderna, and AstraZeneca-Oxford vaccines were the first to receive emergency use authorization in December 2020. By January 31, 2021, approximately 64.68 million people globally had received at least one vaccine dose: 31.46 million in North America, 22.1 million in Europe, 8.65 million in Asia, 2.44 million in South America, and 32,206 in Africa. By this time, 21 countries had vaccinated more than 1 million of their populations [1].
The World Health Organization Director-General Tedros Adhanom Ghebreyesus's statement on February 15, 2020—"We are not just fighting an epidemic; we are fighting an infodemic"—highlighted the growing concern over the COVID-19 "infodemic." This term describes the rapid and widespread dissemination of information, especially via social networks, but typically pertains to the spread of misinformation, which was broadly assumed to decrease vaccine intent. Vaccine hesitancy was reportedly on the rise worldwide, potentially hindering the achievement of the vaccination coverage necessary for herd immunity [2, 3, 4, 5–6].
Three years into the COVID-19 vaccination campaign, we can assess how initial vaccine intent translated into actual vaccine uptake.
Materials and methods
We conducted a search on Google and PubMed using the terms “COVID-19 vaccine” and “intent,” and “COVID-19 vaccine” and “acceptance,” for studies conducted in 2020, before widespread vaccination and the release of initial data on vaccine side effects and efficacy [7]. All the secondary data are public.
We found 132 intent estimates from 47 countries. After excluding 6 studies due to specific population focus or biased methodology, we analyzed 125 intent estimates from 29 articles or reports covering 46 countries (supplementary material, Table 1). When multiple intent estimates existed for a single country, we calculated an average to derive a single figure per country.
We then compared these figures to the percentages of people in these 46 countries who had received at least one dose of a COVID-19 vaccine, using data from the Our World in Data (Online Resource 1, Table 2) [8]. The end of the data collection period ranged from October 2022 to December 2023. To gauge misinformation exposure, we used two existing indicators. The first was the monthly Infodemic Risk Index (IRI) from the COVID-19 Infodemics Observatory, which analyzes Twitter messages [9]. The IRI represents the “likelihood that a user receives messages from potentially misleading sources.” We downloaded the IRI data for the 46 countries from July 2020 to December 2021 and calculated the average for each country (Online Resource 1, Table 3). The IRI only analyzes Twitter messages and would not be sufficient to study the overall exposure to misinformation in a country. However, it is an appropriate tool to conduct a comparison between different countries. Misinformation on Twitter can be considered as representative of misinformation on social networks. The second indicator was a ranking of 138 countries based on the prevalence of COVID-19 misinformation, as determined by S. Al-Zaman (Table 1) [10].
Results
In 13 countries, vaccine uptake surpassed vaccine intent, ranging from a 5.98 percentage point increase in South Korea to a 23.61 point increase in Singapore. In 9 countries, the difference falls within a ± 5 percentage point margin of error, suggesting that uptake is equivalent to intent. However, in 24 countries, vaccine uptake was lower than estimated, varying from -5.30 points in Nepal to -69.20 points in Niger.
A distinct pattern is evident when considering different world regions (as illustrated in Fig. 1). In Europe, the Americas, and several countries in Asia and Oceania — specifically Australia, Singapore, Japan, South Korea, China, and Pakistan — vaccine uptake is equal to or greater than the vaccine intent. Conversely, in Africa, vaccine uptake generally falls below intent, with Morocco as an exception at -3.20 percentage points, and this trend is also observed in Nepal, India, Malaysia, and Indonesia. In the 13 countries where vaccine uptake exceeded initial intent, the average intent to vaccinate was somewhat lower, at 67.56% (ranging from 58.58% to 80.45%). This contrasts with the 24 countries where vaccine uptake was less than the initial intent, where the average intent to vaccinate was greater, at 79.14% (ranging from 54.10% to 96.60%). However, the actual vaccine uptake was significantly greater in the first group of 13 countries, averaging 82.23% (with a range of 61.56% to 91.55%), compared to an average of 45.29% (ranging from 13.00% to 91.30%) in the latter group of 24 countries.
The infodemic risk index (IRI) was above the median value of 0.2049 in four of the 13 countries where uptake surpassed intent (Russia, the United States, Sweden, and Colombia). Conversely, in 11 of the 24 countries where uptake was lower than intent (Nepal, Rwanda, Malaysia, Sierra Leone, Indonesia, Nigeria, Democratic Republic of Congo, Uganda, Ethiopia, Kenya, and Niger), the IRI was below the median (Fig. 2). Among the 13 countries in which vaccine uptake was greater than intent, five ranked among the top 10% of the 138 countries assessed for exposure to COVID-19 misinformation. These include the United States (2nd), Spain (4th), Colombia (5th), France (6th), and Italy (12th). In the group where uptake is considered equivalent to intent, we find Brazil (3rd) and Morocco (70th). In countries where uptake was lower than intent, we find Sudan (133rd), Rwanda (131st), Burkina Faso (117st), Libya (97th), Gabon (96th), and Senegal (87th). The average misinformation exposure rank for the 13 countries with higher uptake than intent is 28.7, while it is 65.4 for the 19 countries where uptake is lower (five of the 46 countries studied are not included in the ranking).
Our study primarily investigated the shift from vaccine intent in 2020 to actual vaccine uptake. A comparison between the ranking of exposure to COVID-19 misinformation and vaccine uptake yields similar findings (Online Resource 1, Fig. 1). In the 11 countries with the highest misinformation exposure, vaccine uptake (at least one dose) exceeds 70%. Among the 11 countries with the lowest exposure, uptake is greater than 70% in three countries, between 50 and 30% in four countries, and less than 30% in four countries. The average percentage of vaccine uptake in the 20 countries most exposed to misinformation was 73.08%, whereas it was 53.47% in the 19 least exposed countries.
Discussion
The organization of the 46 countries we studied according to the discrepancy between actual COVID-19 vaccine uptake and the intent to receive a vaccine measured in 2020 revealed a distinct pattern. This difference is positive in a group of 13 countries, which includes 6 European, 3 American, 3 Asian countries and Australia (ranging from + 5.98 to + 23.61 percentage points). It is nonsignificant in a second group of 9 countries, encompassing 3 American, 3 European, 2 Asian, and one African country. However, it is negative in a third group of 24 countries, comprising 20 African and 4 Asian countries (ranging from − 5.30 to − 69.20).
The average intent to vaccinate in 2020 was lower in countries where the uptake exceeded the intent (67.56%) than in countries where the uptake was lower than the intent (79.14%). In contrast, the average vaccine uptake was significantly greater in the first group (82.23%) than in the second group (45.29%). For instance, the intent was 45.56% in Russia, 58.58% in France, and 65.97% in Italy, where the uptake reached 61.56%, 80.63%, and 83.28%, respectively. Conversely, the intent was 81.35% in Malawi, 90.50% in Kenya, and 93.00% in Niger, where the uptake was only 26.26%, 26.83%, and 23.80%, respectively. This indicates a shift in the first group of countries from a low or moderate intent to a high uptake, while the second group showed the reverse trend, moving from a high intent to a low uptake. What might explain these divergent trends?
Various factors, such as fear of vaccine side effects, have been suggested to be drivers of vaccine refusal. However, since these side effects are globally uniform, they cannot fully account for the divergent trends observed. Another factor considered is trust in the government, but it seems unlikely that this trust would have increased significantly over two years in the first group of countries and decreased in the second [4].
Is the impact of the infodemic, or misinformation on social networks, the explanation for this dual, opposite evolution?
The average infodemic risk index (IRI) from July 2020 to December 2021 was greater in countries such as France, Russia, Sweden, Colombia, and South Korea, where the increase between intent and uptake ranged from 5.98 to 22.05, than in Niger and Kenya, which experienced the greatest decreases in intent versus uptake (63.67 and 69.20, respectively). The average IRI is below the median of the 46 studied countries in 11 of the 24 countries where a decrease in uptake relative to intent was observed. If the infodemic was a major influence on this decrease, one would expect most of these countries to have an IRI above the median. Conversely, the IRI in the 13 countries with an increase between intent and uptake would be expected to be below the median.
In addition, the ranking of countries according to their exposure to COVID-19 misinformation did not correlate with the increase or decrease between vaccine intent and uptake. For example, there was an increase in countries highly exposed to misinformation, such as the United States, Colombia, Spain, France, and Italy, all of which rank among the top 10% most exposed countries in a study. Conversely, a decrease was observed in countries least exposed to misinformation, such as Sudan, Rwanda, Burkina Faso, Libya, and Gabon. These findings are consistent with a study by Islam et al. [11] conducted in the first three months of 2020, which revealed that the frequency of COVID-19 misinformation on social media was highest in countries such as India and the United States, high in Spain and the United Kingdom, but very low in countries such as Senegal, Sudan, Ethiopia, and Mali.
IRI analyses and the ranking of exposure to misinformation did not show any correlation between misinformation and a decrease between vaccine intent and uptake. Moreover, the average ranking of exposure to misinformation is 2.3 times greater in countries where uptake exceeds intent than in countries where uptake is lower.
The comparison of COVID-19 vaccine uptake levels with countries’ rankings in terms of exposure to COVID-19 misinformation reveals that uptake is greater in countries with more exposure to misinformation: 73.08% in the half-most exposed countries versus 53.47% in the half-least exposed countries.
As reported by numerous studies, misinformation has likely influenced some individuals to refuse vaccination. However, on social networks people are more exposed to accurate information about COVID-19 than to misinformation [12]. Studies conducted from December 2019 to May 2020 showed that the proportion of COVID-19-related posts containing misinformation on social networks varied from 0.2% to 14.2%, depending on the network (one study found 28.8% on Twitter, including inappropriate humor) [13]. A study from March–April 2020 revealed that “more credible,” “less credible,” and “not credible” information about COVID-19 constituted 72.4%, 23.6%, and 4.0% on Twitter, 83.6%, 14.8%, and 1.7% on Facebook pages, and 70.1%, 25.7%, and 4.3% on Facebook groups, respectively [14]. A 2021 study analyzing 300 million English-language tweets related to COVID-19 vaccines concluded that there was “a relatively low prevalence of low-credibility information compared to the entirety of mainstream news” [15]. Furthermore, a study conducted in Pakistan showed that using social media was associated with an increased probability of being vaccinated by 1.61 units [16].
In addition to the internet, people are also exposed to traditional media, which have largely echoed information from health ministries and scientists. It is plausible that, influenced by information on the efficacy and safety of the vaccines postlaunch in December 2020, some people decided to get vaccinated despite initially being hesitant. In countries where uptake exceeded intent, vaccine refusal driven by misinformation was likely outweighed by acceptance driven by accurate information.
However, does misinformation account for the drop between intent and uptake in 24 of the 46 countries we studied, including all African countries except Morocco?
As noted earlier, exposure to COVID-19 misinformation tends to be lower in these countries than in those where uptake exceeds intent. Studies indicate that people in West Africa generally have a positive opinion of COVID-19 vaccines. A study conducted in Niger in March 2023 showed that 73.3% of people believe that COVID-19 vaccines are “a good thing,” with 83% of those who had heard messages promoting COVID-19 vaccination approving these messages [17]. Another study reported a COVID-19 vaccine acceptance rate ranging from 63.3% to 95.1% in 6 West African countries in 2021–2022 [18].
Some African countries experienced occasional vaccine shortages in the first half of 2021. However, according to a WHO team study from April 2021 to June 2022, each month the number of doses administered in the WHO African Region was approximately one-third lower than the available doses [19]. As of June 2022, 58.40% only of the received doses had been administered, while 1.7% had expired. The authors noted that “the attendance at fixed sites was dropping over time as the result of reduced risk perception.” Outreach and mobile vaccination teams in communities to bring vaccines closer to the people were not very successful.
The WHO estimated that in sub-Saharan Africa 80% of SARS-CoV-2 infections, the virus that causes COVID-19, are asymptomatic [20]. In addition, the actual number of cases is greatly underestimated; for example, the estimated COVID-19 mortality rate is 4 times greater than the reported mortality in Ghana, 7 times greater in Guinea, 15 times greater in Nigeria, and 52 times greater in Niger [21]. According to a study conducted in Burkina Faso, Ivory Coast, and Sierra Leone in January 2021, and the aforementioned study in Niger, people in these countries saw images of sick and deceased individuals on TV channels from industrialized countries, such as French TV24 or British BBC, but not on national channels [22]. Notably, 82.9% of Niger’s population thinks that COVID-19 exists in the world, but only 43.8% believe that it exists in their country. Our two studies also revealed that a vast majority of the population thinks that only sick individuals transmit the coronavirus that causes COVID-19, and our work in Niger, along with other studies, showed that very few people believe they have ever encountered someone with COVID-19 [23].
Unsurprisingly, the combination of a low number of reported COVID-19 cases, scarce news reports on the local COVID-19 toll, and the belief that only sick people transmit the disease led people to adopt a “wait-and-watch approach” for COVID-19 vaccination [24]. The perceptions of the COVID-19 pandemic in sub-Saharan Africa evolved from “general psychosis” in early 2020 to “adaptation” and “relief” [25]. By the time the vaccines became available, COVID-19 was no longer seen as a threat by a large majority of the population, leading many to deem vaccination unnecessary [19].
Limitations
The 125 estimates of intent to vaccinate were obtained at different dates from April to December 2020 and this intent is likely to have changed during this time span. However, as Supplementary Table 1 shows when various estimates are available for the same countries they don’t vary much over time, except for a decrease between 10 to 13 percentage points in Brazil, China, Ecuador, India, Spain and the United States, and over 20 points in France. Except for India, all these countries are on the left side of Fig. 1. Taking in consideration only the latest estimates of intent in these countries would have increased the difference between uptake and intent, which is already the highest in these countries.
The IRI index only analyzes Twitter messages and the criteria to label an information as “misinformation” could be discussed. In addition, Twitter use is different across countries, with a lower usage in Africa than in Europe or the Americas. However, this index is appropriate for a comparison between countries. Twitter misinformation can be considered representative of social media misinformation in each country and the labelling criteria of the index being consistent.
The main limitation is that we used broad, generalized data sets that only allow to draw broad conclusions and to highlight a geographical pattern. Although numerous articles have been published on the spread of misinformation on the internet and the social networks, and some articles on the impact of misinformation on selected panels of people, there is a lack of studies on the actual impact of COVID-19 misinformation at the population level.
Conclusion
The determinants of vaccine uptake are complex and multifactorial, and our study doesn’t provide a unique, causal explanation of high or low vaccine COVID-19 vaccine uptake. However, our results do indicate that the 2020 estimates of COVID-19 vaccine intent, made before vaccine rollout, were not accurate predictors of actual vaccine uptake. They also demonstrate that there is no association between the exposure to misinformation and the decision to vaccinate. The potential negative impact of misinformation on vaccine uptake is probably outweighed by the impact of accurate information regarding the vaccine's efficacy and safety.
Individual decisions to get vaccinated are largely based on personal assessments of the benefit/risk balance of vaccination. This evaluation is shaped by both accurate and erroneous information, as well as personal experiences of the pandemic, including knowledge of acquaintances and family members who fell ill or passed away, news coverage, and statements from health authorities.
In European and American countries, the perception of COVID-19 as a significant threat remained prevalent among most of the population in 2021 and 2022. Additionally, the widespread administration of vaccines demonstrated their safety and efficacy, leading to vaccine uptake exceeding the 2020 vaccine intent levels. In contrast, in African countries, where the initial vaccine intent in 2020 was greater than that in Europe and the Americas, the fear associated with the disease diminished significantly after 2020. Consequently, most of the population no longer views COVID-19 as a serious threat, resulting in a vaccine uptake that is lower than the original intent.
