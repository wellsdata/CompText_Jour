Introduction The global projections suggest that total global food demand will increase by 35%–56% between 2010 and 2050, while the population at risk of hunger is expected to change by −91% to +8% over the same period. These ranges alter slightly, when the climate change is taken into consideration (van Dijk et al ., 2021).
Food safety is influenced by factors such as demographic ageing, globalisation, geopolitical risks, and food fraud, with economic motives significantly affecting public health. Consumers face information asymmetry, leading to demands for greater transparency in the food system (Miller, 2021). Mistrust is exacerbated by disinformation spread via social networks and media (Diekman et al ., 2023).
The Czech Republic, a post-communist nation, presents a unique case. Rapid privatization and oligopolisation in its food production sector have led to market concentration and food safety challenges. The food retail sector often prioritizes lower costs over safety. Czechia’s position between Eastern and Western Europe complicates its regulatory landscape.
The food safety issue in the Czech Republic has recently become a serious social and political topic that is also related to the war in Ukraine. The evidence is represented by many disinformation activities with a negative impact on society, population’s concerns about their health, as well as its trust in the regulatory institutions. For instance, disinformation regarding grain from Ukraine has become one of the main propaganda topics directed against Ukraine (Čeští elfové, 2023). A group called “Flour from Ukraine and other goods” was created on Facebook, calling for a boycott of food chains and stores, where goods from Ukraine appear (České noviny, 2023). A disinformation email was also spread, which warns, which the goods with the number 482 included in the barcode, contain poisonous raw materials from Ukraine, but the control authorities of the Czech Republic have to remain silent about this and the warning must not be broadcast even on television (iRozhlas, 2024). The examples given strongly confirm the fact that food security has become part of a hybrid war (Briggs, 2020; Wigell et al ., 2021; Baumgartner, 2023) and its solution requires an institutional approach in relation to the regulatory bodies as well as the literacy systems (Mokhtar et al ., 2022). Public trust in regulatory frameworks is strained by historical and economic factors.
It has an impact on the processes of creation of the regulatory and safety systems related to food health in the individual countries. The pandemic crisis caused by COVID 19 and the worsening geopolitical situation related to the war in Ukraine (Rabbi et al ., 2023; van Meijl et al ., 2022), later also related to the Iranian conflict, and the growing hybrid threats and influences of the great powers have also been raised via increasing threats about food safety (Maqbool et al ., 2024; Han et al ., 2021; Petetin, 2020). Only a few research studies examine these causal relationships among the individual aspects of food safety. The available studies have considerable comparative limitations from a geographical perspective, as well as from the missing effects of political-economic relationships (Manning, 2019; Kvasha et al ., 2024). Also, food safety risks related to the war in Ukraine have not the same impact on the particular countries (Kovács et al ., 2022; Leal et al ., 2023). In the impacted countries, food safety risks can be dominant and thus, they are able to affect not only the population quality of life, but also shape their perception of food risks and decision-making (Holmberg, 2024; Abay et al ., 2023). Investigating this issue has currently become gradually important and desirable, especially for the creation of many policies and government strategies that should help the population (El Bilali and Ben Hassen, 2024). Hence, systematic research is needed focused on conceptualising tools for the active food safety strategies related to food health, which is threatened by hybrid threats (Nikolov, 2018), and limited public trust in the government institutions that guarantee food safety (Borda et al ., 2021; Franc-Dąbrowska et al ., 2021). This study addresses this gap by analysing food safety perceptions and regulatory trust in Czechia, offering insights for policymakers and contributing to broader discussions on improving media and scientific literacy in food safety across Europe.
Literature review Countries have developed robust food security strategies involving various actors. Zhu et al . (2019) argue that government agencies excel at detecting food safety incidents, with media playing a complementary role. Such incidents can trigger consumer concern and harm the food industry’s reputation (McDowell, 2017; Cumming et al ., 2020). Communication gaps between stakeholders and practitioners in food safety persist. Experts focus on public welfare, while stakeholders may prioritize their interests (Rutsaert et al ., 2014). The public also plays a role in verifying information and notifying authorities. Media outlets often use unreliable sources, necessitating careful interpretation. Trust in social media as a source of food safety information depends on the provider’s trustworthiness (Hobbs and Goddard, 2015). Trust in information from media, consumer organizations, or regulatory bodies reduces the likelihood of purchasing affected products (Lobb et al ., 2007). Social media empower consumers to share food safety information, with trust mainly placed in scientists (67.5%) and family members (33%) (Soon, 2020).
Early notification of food safety risks is crucial, and social media play a significant role (Lam et al ., 2020). Public perception of food safety risks is linked to social media attention and trustworthiness (Yang et al ., 2016). Social media can enhance consumer knowledge but also lead to unhealthy behaviours (Ventura et al ., 2021). Platforms like Facebook, WhatsApp, and YouTube are commonly used for food safety information, though awareness of their potential varies. Despite this, the reliability of online social capital requires further exploration (Soon, 2020). Wu (2015) identified risk perception, emotions, and social trust as key factors influencing the use of Facebook for risk communication. Long-term studies comparing platforms are needed to understand better their impact on health behaviours. Social media platforms like WhatsApp, Snapchat, and Instagram are influential in disseminating food safety information, but influencers’ role remains underexplored. While influencers can positively impact, misinformation poses a public health risk. Timely and transparent information publication is essential to boost consumer confidence (Cambier and Poncin, 2020).
Investigating food safety incidents revealed by governments and media is crucial, advocating for comparative analysis to assess their roles in national food security systems (Zhu et al ., 2019). Emotional responses to food safety incidents are stronger predictors of risk perception and preventive measures than awareness (Mou and Lin, 2014). Negative comments elicit negative responses, regardless of source credibility, while positive or mixed comments result in less negative reactions when associated with trustworthy sources (Seo et al ., 2015). Social media’s role in food safety communication offers rapid response and broad population reach but may overlook certain demographic groups (Overbey et al ., 2017). Trust, personal interests, and beliefs drive social network users to seek food safety information online (Zhu et al ., 2019). Emotions also play a role in whether individuals take preventive actions, with extreme emotional reactions potentially escalating crises (Rutsaert et al ., 2014). Targeted dissemination of food safety information via social media can enhance media literacy, improve consumer awareness, and encourage verification before sharing.
Institutions, journalists, regulators, and industry representatives play crucial roles in food risk communication (Henderson et al ., 2017). Exploring how journalists use social media to create informed content about food risks is essential. Regulatory authorities must engage with social networks to ensure accurate information dissemination (Havinga, 2018). While facilitating two-way communication and rapid information transfer, these institutions face challenges in controlling information due to social media sensationalism. Therefore, monitoring media actors’ use of social platforms is necessary to maintain consumer and social media trust. Social media’s growing importance in future food safety communication can significantly influence consumer behaviour (Garcia et al ., 2020). Recommendations and perceived risks directly impact purchase intentions, while information trustworthiness indirectly influences intentions through social media (Cui et al ., 2022). However, political, financial, and technological constraints limit social media’s role in raising public awareness, particularly in developing countries (Hama Kareem et al ., 2016). Emerging technologies like mobile phones as detection devices hold promise for enhancing food safety by linking findings with social media, potentially serving as early warning systems (Jin et al ., 2020).
Despite the Internet and social networks' growing importance, a segment of the population still resists seeking information online, necessitating a multi-channel communication strategy (Ma et al ., 2017). Although social media offers significant educational potential, not everyone prefers to receive food safety information via these platforms (Burke et al ., 2016). Therefore, developing multi-platform educational programmes that include traditional and digital approaches is essential. Social media also present risks for spreading disinformation in health, food safety, and other areas. However, they can counteract disinformation by disseminating corrective messages with strong social support. Correcting disinformation about genetically modified foods is most effective when provided by expert sources with high social endorsement (Wang, 2021). Less than a third of respondents could identify food safety disinformation, with most less likely to purchase foods flagged as problematic on social media (Soon, 2020). Although social media holds potential, it is not a definitive solution for communicating health risks related to food products (Park and Lee, 2018; Afful-Dadzie et al ., 2023). Alongside food labels and social media, traceability systems are crucial sources of food safety information (Lam et al ., 2020). Understanding consumers’ information needs is vital for developing effective communication tools. Food manufacturers can create internal and external information systems to build trust and support food safety management policies (Panghal et al ., 2018). Social media’s strengths in communicating food risks include availability, interaction, speed, and crisis communication efficacy, but weaknesses include low trust, information overload, and preference for traditional media (Rutsaert et al ., 2014; Kasza et al ., 2022).
Data and methodology A nationwide study of the Czech population was conducted by CEDMO NPO in collaboration with Charles University, with data collection supported by MEDIAN. The initial sample consisted of over 4,000 participants, recruited via quota sampling to reflect the Czech Republic’s demographics. After exclusions, the final analysis included data from 2,729 individuals.
The research sample comprises 2,729 participants, with a nearly equal gender distribution: 48.9% male ( N  = 1,334) and 51.1% female ( N  = 1,395). In terms of education, the largest group completed high school with graduation (35.0%), followed by those without graduation (33.3%), while 18.9% hold a university degree, and 12.8% completed only primary school. Geographically, the sample is distributed across Prague and Central Czechia (25.6%), the rest of Czechia (35.8%), and Moravia (38.5%). Regarding the size of residence, 39.2% live in areas with fewer than 4,999 inhabitants, 38.1% in towns with 5,000–99,999 residents, and 22.7% in cities exceeding 100,000 residents. The economic status shows that 46.0% are employed, 12.4% are entrepreneurs or freelancers, and 3.2% are unemployed, with pensioners (26.5%) and students (7.7%) also represented. Age distribution reveals a balanced spread, with the largest group being 65+ (24.8%), followed by 35–44 (18.0%) and 45–54 (17.9%), while the younger age brackets (16–24 and 25–34) comprise 9.8% and 14.7%, respectively.
This research examines the intersection of disinformation, misinformation, and public perception of food safety in the Czech Republic. It explores how false information affects trust in food safety, the role of state intervention in media control, and the impact of misinformation on support for EU policies. The study also investigates the sources used by the public to verify news and food safety details, as well as the factors driving interest in food packaging information. Key research questions include:
- What influences individuals' choice of information sources when verifying news or seeking information about food safety and quality?
- How do different age groups perceive the reliability of food safety information, and what drives these perceptions?
- What is the impact of disinformation exposure on public opinion regarding EU food safety regulations?
- How does educational attainment affect attitudes towards the safety and benefits of importing agricultural products and food?
- Which socioeconomic factors shape opinions on the simplified import of agricultural products and food as a health threat to the Czech population? This question is explored using logistic regression analysis in a dedicated subchapter.


Results Our primary focus is on the sources respondents use to verify news accuracy or find food-related information, particularly regarding quality and safety. Table 1 outlines responses to the question: “When you want to verify the truth of a news story or find information about food, its quality, or safety, which information source do you use?” The table highlights key trends in information sources. Nearly half of the respondents (49.6%) rely on search engines and news aggregation apps, showing a preference for accessible and varied sources. Family members are a key source, trusted by 31.2% of respondents, emphasising the role of personal networks. Public television (24.7%) and specialized state institution websites (21.7%) are significant trusted sources, indicating reliance on authoritative channels. In contrast, private television (10.4%), public radio (12.9%), and mainstream news websites (15.6%) are less frequently used. Alternative news sites (2.9%) and private radio stations (3.6%) are among the least trusted. Social media platforms and fact-checking organizations are consulted by 7.9% of respondents, indicating moderate trust. Overall, the data reveals a diverse range of trusted sources, with digital platforms and personal relationships playing significant roles.
In this analysis, we explore how different age groups perceive the growing interest in food packaging information, revealing significant age-related differences. Table 2 shows older adults (65+) are more likely to link this interest to mistrust in health safety assurances from regulatory institutions, with 30.6% responding “Definitely yes” and 34.4% “Yes,” compared to 11.4% and 16.3% among the youngest group (16–24). This suggests declining confidence in regulatory bodies among older generations. The belief that younger people’s concern about food quality drives this interest is more evenly spread across age groups, slightly higher among those aged 65+ (27.2% “Definitely yes”) and 16–24 (21.0%). The view that inflation and rising costs have degraded food quality is most strongly held by those aged 65+ (38.5% “Definitely yes”), indicating heightened economic sensitivity among older adults. Political and expert/educational campaigns are less influential overall, with neutral responses common across all age groups. However, older adults show more agreement with expert campaigns, particularly regarding educational efforts to enhance food quality understanding. These trends highlight varying levels of trust and concern among age groups, likely reflecting broader societal and economic shifts.
Next, we assess the influence of unfounded or false claims about food safety and EU regulations on public support for the EU, focusing on respondents' exposure to disinformation over the past month. Table 3 reveals the perceived impact of misinformation on EU support, segmented by disinformation exposure. Among those heavily exposed to disinformation, 22.1% view the influence as absolutely essential, with 19.2% considering it essential. In contrast, only 8.2% of those not exposed to disinformation see it as absolutely essential, and 19.6% view it as essential. Notably, 29.9% of those with no disinformation exposure remain neutral, while 4.2% perceive no influence at all. These results indicate that exposure to disinformation plays a significant role in shaping perceptions of the EU, stressing the impact of media exposure on public opinion.
The response distribution shows a clear trend: as disinformation exposure decreases, the perceived importance of misinformation’s influence on EU support diminishes. Those highly exposed to disinformation are more likely to view it as crucial, suggesting repeated exposure amplifies its perceived impact. Conversely, those with lower or no exposure see its influence as less significant. For example, 39.4% of the rather unexposed and 34.1% of the not at all exposed groups view its influence as neutral or negligible, compared to just 25.8% of the highly exposed group. This suggests that less exposure leads to a more critical attitude towards misinformation, highlighting the role of media literacy. Interestingly, even moderate exposure to disinformation shapes perceptions, with 24.5% and 26.3% of the “rather exposed” and “rather unexposed” finding misinformation rather essential. This data highlights the strong link between disinformation exposure and its perceived influence on EU support, emphasizing the need to address disinformation and enhance media literacy.
In the final analysis, public opinion is divided on whether simplified import of agricultural products poses a major health threat. Of the population, 55.6% express concern, with 16.8% seeing it as a major threat and 38.8% as somewhat risky. Meanwhile, 17.5% are undecided, and 22.6% perceive minimal or no risk, indicating some trust in existing safety measures. Table 4 reveals that older respondents (65+) are the most concerned, with 25.3% saying “Yes” and 21.8% “Definitely yes.” This concern may stem from increased health vulnerability or past food safety issues. Younger respondents (16–24) are less concerned, with only 4.7% saying “Definitely yes” and 9.5% “Definitely no,” possibly due to greater exposure to global food markets or confidence in modern safety standards. A significant portion, particularly those aged 25–34, are neutral, suggesting either a lack of information or ambivalence about the risks and benefits of imports, indicating a need for public health education. Overall, responses reflect varying risk perceptions influenced by experience, media, and cultural attitudes.
After respondents indicated they did not perceive simplified imports of agricultural products and food as a major health threat, they were asked to identify the main reasons for this belief. Of the 2,729 respondents, 1,212 selected reasons. Table 5 shows the reasons, segmented by educational attainment, for believing that simplified imports are not a major health threat. The data reveal distinct motivations across educational levels, highlighting attitudes toward imported food. Higher-educated respondents often cited trust in international food safety standards, while those with lower education were more likely to trust local food safety regulations. This suggests that educational background significantly shapes how individuals assess the safety of imported foods.
Among individuals with primary school education, the dominant reason for not viewing food imports as a major health threat is their lower price, cited by 47.8%. This group also values the higher quality of imports (35.8%) and the broader food selection (25.0%). A minor percentage (9.7%) mentioned other reasons. High school graduates without further qualifications also highlight lower prices (46.6%) and higher quality (36.5%) but place more importance on the increased variety of food (45.3%) and the need for domestic agricultural efficiency (29.4%). High school graduates with diplomas show a strong preference for greater food choice (52.1%) and maintain a focus on lower prices (42.1%) and quality (43.9%). They consistently recognise the need for improved local agricultural efficiency (29.2%). For those with a university degree, the key reason is the wider food variety (61.6%). They place less emphasis on lower prices (35.9%) and note the pressure on local agriculture (37.9%). Notably, 15.6% cited “Other” reasons, indicating a wider range of motivations among this group. These patterns suggest that educational attainment affects perceptions of imported food benefits, with higher education correlating with a greater appreciation for diverse food choices and efficiency concerns for local industries, while lower education levels prioritise economic factors like price.
Regression analysis To better understand the factors shaping respondents' opinions on the potential health threat posed by simplified imports of agricultural products and food into the Czech Republic, we applied logistic regression (Azanaw et al ., 2019; Field, 2013). This method is well-suited for predicting the relationship between a binary dependent variable - whether respondents perceive imports as a major health threat - and a set of independent variables. Logistic regression estimates the likelihood of a specific outcome based on the explanatory variables. The dependent variable was derived from the question: “Do you consider the simplified import of agricultural products and food from other countries into your country a major health threat to the local population?” Respondents could choose from: “Definitely yes” (459 observations), “Yes” (519), “Rather yes” (539), “Neutral” (478), “Rather no” (306), “No” (311), and “Definitely no” (117). We excluded neutral responses (478) as they did not express a definitive opinion, leaving 2,251 observations for analysis. For clarity, we grouped the responses “Definitely yes,” “Yes,” and “Rather yes” into a single supportive category (Yes), and “Rather no,” “No,” and “Definitely no” into a negative category (No). This binary grouping enabled a more focused analysis of the factors influencing whether respondents viewed food imports as a health threat. The binary logistic regression model evaluated whether respondents perceive simplified import of agricultural products and food from other countries as a major health threat. The positive intercept (B = 0.431, SE = 0.100, p  < 0.001) indicates a general tendency to view such imports as a health threat. Age is a significant predictor (B = 0.016, SE = 0.003, p  < 0.001), with an odds ratio (Exp(B)) of 1.016. This suggests that each additional year of age increases the likelihood of viewing imports as a health threat by 1.6%, possibly due to heightened health concerns. Gender also influences perceptions, with males significantly less likely than females to see imports as a threat (B = −0.587, SE = 0.095, p  < 0.001, Exp(B) = 0.556). Males’ odds of perceiving imports as a threat are about 44.4% lower than those of females. Education level impacts perceptions as well. High school graduates without a degree are more likely to view imports as a health threat compared to university graduates (B = 0.725, SE = 0.135, p  < 0.001, Exp(B) = 2.065). Individuals with only primary education also show a higher likelihood of concern (B = 0.314, SE = 0.164, p  = 0.056, Exp(B) = 1.368). Residential area size is another significant factor. Those in areas with fewer than 4,000 inhabitants are 41.1% more likely to view imports as a health threat (B = 0.344, SE = 0.123, p  = 0.005, Exp(B) = 1.411), with similar trends seen in medium-sized areas. Internet usage intensity affects perceptions, with frequent users less likely to see imports as a threat (B = −0.436, SE = 0.129, p  = 0.001, Exp(B) = 0.646). However, those who use the internet once or almost daily are more likely to view imports as a threat (B = 0.398, SE = 0.157, p  = 0.011, Exp(B) = 1.489). Exposure to misinformation is a significant predictor. Those highly exposed to misinformation are 92.7% more likely to see imports as a health threat (B = 0.656, SE = 0.253, p  = 0.009, Exp(B) = 1.927), stressing the impact of misinformation on public perceptions.
Discussion This study examines the sources used to verify news and gather information on food quality and safety, highlighting key trends. Nearly half (49.6%) prefer search engines and news apps, reflecting a need for accessible sources. Social networks are important, with 31.2% trusting family members and 25.3% consulting friends, pointing out personal connections. Public television (24.7%) and state institution websites (21.7%) are also trusted, indicating a reliance on authoritative outlets. Less frequently used are private television (10.4%), public radio (12.9%), and mainstream news sites (15.6%), with alternative news sites (2.9%) and private radio stations (3.6%) least trusted. Social media platforms and fact-checking organisations are moderately trusted, used by 7.9% of respondents. Trust and personal attitude drive social media use in food risk contexts, with its reach and immediacy as key benefits (Overbey et al ., 2017). Psychometric factors and media perception also shape food risk views (You and Ju, 2017). The public expects clear information from government agencies, which requires a strong presence in a coordinated regulatory system (Papadopoulos et al ., 2012). Credibility in food safety information depends on independence from special interest groups and transparent reporting. Evaluating media credibility involves considering broader social issues and the media’s role in public protection.
Age-related differences in food packaging interest are notable. Older adults (65+) are more likely to mistrust health safety assurances, with 30.6% responding “Definitely yes” and 34.4% “Yes,” compared to 11.4% and 16.3% among younger respondents (16–24). While younger generations consistently show concern for food quality, older adults worry more about inflation’s impact on food quality and support educational campaigns, indicating a preference for expert guidance. Consumers increasingly seek details on origin, applied technologies, and food fraud. Regional differences are evident: Europeans prioritize the country of origin in assessing food safety, while Asians consider health and producer information (Wu et al ., 2021). Perceptions of food fraud vary, with Europeans viewing it as a lesser risk compared to other regions. Tools for food traceability are vital for consumer safety verification (Bitzios et al ., 2017). Giacalone et al . (2016) highlight the need for food that supports healthy ageing, focusing on nutrition, sensory quality, and social aspects. For the elderly, food security links to personalised products and social technologies. Briazu et al. (2024) support personalised dietary advice but recommend considering socioeconomic and cognitive factors over genetic profiles.
Disinformation significantly impacts perceptions of its effect on EU support. Among those heavily exposed, 22.1% consider misinformation crucial, compared to just 8.2% among those unexposed. This highlights the role of media exposure in shaping opinions and the importance of media literacy in mitigating disinformation’s effects. Less exposure correlates with a reduced perception of its significance, indicating that media literacy can lessen the impact of false narratives. Disinformation also harms food companies, reducing trust in both local and international brands (Fârte and Obadă, 2021). The agri-food sector is especially vulnerable due to its social importance (Čechmánek, 2024). Despite this, research on agri-food disinformation in social media is lacking. It’s crucial to study not just disinformation but its sources, spread, stakeholder influences, detection methods, and protective strategies (Chowdhury et al ., 2023).
Public concern about the health risks of importing agricultural products varies significantly by age. Older respondents, particularly those aged 65+, show greater concern, with 47.1% viewing imports as a major health threat. Younger individuals, especially those aged 16–24, are less worried, likely due to their exposure to global food markets and confidence in food safety standards. Education also influences views, with higher education linked to greater appreciation for diverse food options and understanding efficiency pressures on local industries. Baumann et al . (2019) note that socioeconomic status impacts food choices and safety perceptions. Those with lower socioeconomic status face economic constraints, emphasizing the importance of context in studying food risks across social groups. Interest in food safety often aligns with personal health concerns (Saba et al ., 2019). Rising food prices, driven by inflation, increase uncertainty and can support disinformation (Van Lenthe et al ., 2015). These factors negatively affect food safety decisions, with countries adopting various monetary policies to manage food inflation (Kaur, 2023).
Food safety risk perception varies based on experiences with imported foods and social media influence (Choi, 2024). International differences are also notable, with Northern Europeans less concerned about food risks than Southern Europeans (Hohl and Gaskell, 2008). Trust, integrity, and reputation shape perceptions of a country’s food safety, affecting perceived import risks (Knight et al ., 2007). Confidence in food safety often depends on effective control measures like inspection and certification, which can sometimes impede international trade (Attrey, 2017). Even in developed countries, there is a preference for domestic over imported products, despite domestic food safety issues (Wu et al ., 2021). Additionally, food fraud undermines consumer trust in the food industry and regulatory systems (Kendall et al ., 2019).
Regression analysis shows that age, gender, education, residential area size, internet use, and misinformation exposure strongly shape perceptions of health risks from simplified food imports. Older adults, women, those with less education, and small-town residents are more likely to see these imports as a health threat. Internet use and misinformation exposure also play significant roles in shaping these views. The study highlights the importance of varied information sources, age-related perception differences, and misinformation’s impact on EU support. Enhancing media literacy and targeted public health education could create a more informed public. Zanetta et al . (2022) support these findings, showing that factors like age, gender, education, and income, along with risk perception and incident experience, influence consumer food safety views. Papadopoulos et al . (2012) also emphasize the importance of demographics and public trust in effective food safety communication.
Maintaining transparency and effectively managing food safety risks are essential for public trust. Many individuals are not well-versed in food standards and safety programmes, leading to a growing dependence on regulatory agencies for ensuring food safety and clear communication (Franc-Dąbrowska et al ., 2021). While food producers are crucial, they cannot bear the entire responsibility; government institutions must also play a key role. Public distrust emerges when regulatory bodies fail to provide timely, clear information. To bolster confidence, governments must ensure consistent, high-quality food safety programmes and transparent communication.
Conclusion Global megatrends, including climate change, population growth and ageing, urbanization, and rising consumer demands, posed significant challenges in the field of food safety. These trends created new demands on manufacturers, traders, and regulatory authorities. Technological advancements impacted food production and distribution, introducing various risks and challenges. This research comprehensively examined the factors shaping public perceptions of food safety and trust in regulatory frameworks in the Czech Republic. The study identified key insights, particularly the complex interactions between information sources, exposure to disinformation, and demographic factors influencing public opinion. A significant finding was the prominent role that search engines and news aggregation apps played in verifying food safety information, with nearly half of respondents relying on these digital tools. This highlighted the growing reliance on accessible, diverse information sources in shaping public decision-making.
The research also revealed that disinformation significantly impacted public attitudes towards the European Union’s food safety regulations. Those exposed to higher levels of disinformation were more likely to see misinformation as critically influencing their support for EU policies, stressing the public’s vulnerability to false narratives. This highlighted the need for initiatives aimed at improving media literacy and critical thinking to combat the effects of disinformation. Furthermore, the study demonstrated age-related differences in food safety perceptions, with older individuals showing greater concern about the health risks of simplified agricultural imports. In contrast, younger demographics displayed more relaxed attitudes, possibly due to greater exposure to global food markets and trust in modern safety standards. Logistic regression analysis further emphasized that age, gender, education, residential area size, internet usage, and misinformation exposure significantly influenced perceptions of food import risks. Older age, lower education levels, rural residency, and high misinformation exposure were all linked to increased concern about imported food products.
Overall, the study demonstrated the importance of targeted public health communication and education efforts to address the diverse concerns of different demographic groups and mitigate the influence of disinformation on public perceptions of food safety and policy.
