Introduction
On May 12, 2008, a destructive earthquake struck Wenchuan County in Sichuan Province, China, resulting in extensive human casualties, with thousands of deaths, widespread destruction, and significant economic losses exceeding $150 billion (Fan et al., 2018). Despite over a decade having passed since this disaster, few studies have investigated the psychological health and coping mechanisms of Wenchuan earthquake survivors during subsequent public health emergencies, particularly in the face of overwhelming information on social media. The popularity of social media platforms has skyrocketed in recent years, and despite their benefits, concerns are growing about the adverse effects of social media on emotions and cognition. During the COVID-19 pandemic in particular, there was a significant increase in physical isolation and the spread of misinformation/disinformation online, which had the potential to cause widespread panic. Research addressing the relationship between post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) and other potential influencing psychological factors in the social media era is scarce. The present study investigated these relationships among survivors of the 2008 Wenchuan earthquake (Fan et al., 2018; Yang et al., 2022).
PTSD, especially when co-occurring with social anxiety, can impede an individual’s social, academic, professional, familial, and legal progress (Swan and Kendall, 2016). Natural disasters or global public health emergencies, such as the COVID-19 pandemic, can result in individuals being physically isolated from their social milieu, leading to the restriction of interactions with their friends and family members (Düzce University Turkey et al., 2021). This can induce the fear of missing out (FoMO), which is characterised by an individual’s apprehension of missing out on the enjoyable experiences of others and a persistent desire to seek out information about and remain connected to such experiences (Przybylski et al., 2013). Research has found that either active or passive social isolation may lead to intensive Internet use, potentially resulting in greater exposure to misinformation and more frequent retweeting behaviour (Düzce University Turkey et al., 2021; Yang et al., 2022).
It can be argued that social media facilitates communication within individuals’ social networks and thus increases the likelihood of experiencing the feeling of missing out, which is particularly pronounced in scenarios where physical isolation results from the outbreak of a natural disaster. Therefore, it is plausible that the disturbance of social networks caused by an earthquake could contribute to the occurrence of FoMO. Additionally, PTSD has been identified as a mediating factor in studies on FoMO, expanding the range of psychopathology-related concepts. Elhai et al. (2020) study was one of the first to examine the relationship between FoMO and PTSD intensity in Asian participants. They addressed concerns about the lack of studies examining the correlation between non-social smartphone usage and information sharing, as well as mental health factors. Their work offered novel insights into the role of health anxiety in FoMO from unmet social needs during public health emergencies and consequential problematic sharing behaviour online. Additionally, one study (Fitzgerald et al., 2023) found that married individuals had lower levels of FoMO and ruminative thinking compared to unmarried individuals.
PTSD tends to manifest soon after an individual experiences a traumatic event and may persist for a long time. Previous research found that PTSD developing shortly after an earthquake has a positive effect on continued FoMO and results in other negative psychological symptoms such as rumination. Baseline PTSD can likely predict FoMO and rumination, in turn leading to posttraumatic psychological reactions. However, due to the limitations of their cross-sectional designs, such studies cannot establish causality or a temporal sequence. Furthermore, prior studies have identified different predictors of PTSD and FoMO, leading to the conclusion that their developmental pathways are distinct (Zhou and Wu 2016). Although this conclusion may aid our understanding of the difference in predictors, establishing the differences between the developmental process of PTSD and other psychological variables is challenging due to the lack of longitudinal and developmental perspectives in existing research.
Researchers have investigated the correlations between PTSD and various facets of maladaptive technology utilisation (Ehring & Ehlers, 2014). FoMO involves the behaviour of constantly tracking social networking sites (SNS) and messaging applications to stay informed about and avoid feeling left out of others’ experiences (Przybylski et al., 2013). Talwar et al. (2019) investigated the relationship between the adverse effects of social media usage and the tendency to believe misinformation among active users of social media platforms. Another study found a positive correlation between excessive social media use and FoMO (Leung et al., 2021), observing that individuals with low self-esteem experience greater FoMO, which in turn contributes to the development of depressive symptoms. Intentional sharing of deepfake content, the experience of FoMO, and social media news consumption are all positively correlated (Ahmed 2022b), indicating that individuals with lower cognitive abilities are more likely to engage in frequent sharing behaviours and experience higher FoMO. This suggests that the association between FoMO and news sharing behaviour provides an empirical basis for the formulation, arrangement, and mitigation of potentially harmful actions (Dhir et al., 2018). The present study endeavours to examine the relationship between psychological factors, outcomes, and beliefs regarding information disseminated through social media platforms.
While research suggests an association between PTSD and FoMO and problematic internet usage, the specific mechanism underlying this process has not been explored. According to the “shattered world” hypothesis put forward by Janoff-Bulman (2010), traumatic experiences can dismantle individuals’ fundamental beliefs and assumptions regarding themselves, others, and the world, potentially resulting in the formation of profoundly negative assumptions and emotions, ultimately leading to the manifestation of PTSD. Between the initial challenge to their beliefs and the ensuing posttraumatic reactions, individuals often feel compelled to engage in recurrent contemplation of the traumatic events. This recurrent contemplation, referred to as rumination, is thus an equally important cognitive factor relevant to both PTSD and misinformation studies.
Rumination is a complex process involving behaviours and thoughts that focus on negative mental states and their consequences. It is recognised as a psychological vulnerability factor for depression and anxiety (Roelofs et al., 2008). One study found that individuals with PTSD frequently ruminate without resolution, and that ruminating increases with social media use (Astuti, 2022). Individuals’ social media usage was demonstrated to be considerably influenced by the ideas shared by their immediate social network, including close friends and family members (Lannin et al., 2021). Previously, rumination was thought to be related to negative trauma outcomes because those who ruminate tend to focus their attention on the traumatic experience and its associated feelings rather than on taking proactive action. However, recent research by Zhou and Wu (2016) indicates that rumination comprises three components, namely rumination, rumination-brooding, and rumination-reflection. The authors argued that although intrusive and deliberate rumination are positively correlated, it is important to recognise that they serve distinct functions and may make different contributions to posttraumatic psychological responses. Chang et al., (2019) study emphasised that the frequency and consistency of unverified and potentially misleading information being disseminated within society require rectification. Rumination can lead to mistakes during information processing and intensify PTSD-associated symptoms. The activation of ruminative memories can impact the capacity to retrieve information online, suggesting a potential association between ruminating and believing misinformation (Hertel et al., 2023). However, there is a lack of research examining the effect of social media messages on specific ruminant populations (such as the PTSD-rumination population).
Ongoing research is investigating the relationship between excessive consumption of online information, FoMO and rumination; however, our understanding of the precise nature of the connection between these phenomena remains incomplete. To enhance the efficacy of addressing this research topic, we specifically focus on the PTSD demographic, given the instability of their developmental psychological characteristics and the established significant correlation between rumination and PTSD-related mental health variables (Schumm et al., 2022). Further research is required to unravel the complex relationship between these variables in the social media era.
The rapid evolution of the network approach in recent years provides a promising opportunity to clarify this complex puzzle (Epskamp et al., 2018). The network perspective conceptualises mental disorders resulting from causal interactions among a multitude of symptoms, rather than interpreting them as latent entities. Because network analysis enables the examination of relationships at the individual symptom level, providing valuable insights into the interplay and mutual influences among a variety of mental disorders, it is an appropriate approach to employ for our investigation of the associations between PTSD and misinformation (Epskamp et al., 2018; J. Li et al., 2023).
In network analysis, a network comprises nodes and edges, where nodes represent observable variables, such as symptoms, and edges represent the associations between them (Epskamp et al., 2012). The importance of each node in the network can be measured using the metric of expected influence (EI), which calculates the total sum of the weights (positive and negative) of all edges connected to a specific node. Nodes with higher EI values are considered more significant within the whole network. Nodes can correspond to various mental disorders within a network. The bridge expected influence (BEI) is the total of the bridge weights linked to a specific node. Nodes with a high BEI are more likely to activate or be activated by neighbouring nodes in a nearby community (Heeren, 2018). It is therefore vital to focus on these bridge nodes during interventions since inhibiting them is likely to have a cascading impact, potentially preventing the occurrence of subsequent symptoms (Ren et al., 2023).
Rhemtulla et al. (2016) introduced the cross-lagged panel network (CLPN) method, which combines network analysis models with the cross-lagged panel model. This approach enables the examination of the longitudinal dynamics that occur within and between psychological factors over time. In CLPN, a cross-lagged effect occurs when a node at a given time point predicts that it will be a different node at the next time point, even after considering all other nodes at that time point. Each of these effects can be predicted in a directed network. The network centralities for both in-expected influence (IEI) and out-of-expected influence (OEI) can be established because the cross-lagged edges are directed. OEI indicates the influence that a given node at T1 has on factors at T2, while IEI refers to the degree of variance for a given node at T2 that is explained by variables at T1. CLPN can therefore make substantial contributions to addressing longstanding issues and offer valuable insights relevant to the aims of the present study. Despite the potential confounding of autoregressive paths in the CLPN by stable individual differences (i.e., trait-like and time-invariant characteristics), this methodology is particularly appropriate for longitudinal panel data analysis. It enables the modelling of temporal effects among individual components of a construct across various PTSD, rumination and FoMO symptoms.
This research is significant for the following reasons. First, given the increasing importance of maintaining information authenticity on social platforms, it has become crucial to address the simultaneous occurrence of negative emotions and the belief in misinformation. While some studies have offered valuable insights into the mechanisms underlying misinformation sharing/beliefs over time, a comprehensive understanding is still incomplete, especially for certain populations, such as individuals with PTSD. This research intends to bridge this gap.
Second, our study proposes a network-based methodology with multiple objectives. This approach enables the identification of various OEI behaviours and calculates their predictive relationships. Additionally, it can determine the IEI that contributes to the intricate complexity of misinformation beliefs. Third, research examining Chinese PTSD populations and their Internet usage patterns is scarce; this topic is particularly salient in recent years as China has entered the social media age. How these populations engage with communities amid a surge of disinformation and how their behaviours develop and transform over time remains an insufficiently investigated area. Furthermore, our research yields important results that can inform mental health practice and public policy. Consequently, the work can aid in the development and execution of more efficient preventive intervention measures to eliminate excessive belief in misinformation on social media platforms.
In this context, we aim to use CLPN analysis to elucidate time-variant relationships among PTSD, FoMO, and misinformation sharing. By computing symptom centrality indices, the first objective is to identify which symptoms were most central to prospective prediction across those psychological factors. Prior research suggests that PTSD caused by an earthquake may trigger FoMO, which could subsequently induce a strong desire to interact with online fake news as the original trauma did (Gong and Ren, 2023; Zhou and Wu, 2016). The second objective is to understand the dynamics between PTSD and FoMO symptoms and how they are triggered by misinformation sharing behaviour is crucial. We therefore examined the distinct trajectories of these three psychological factors using a longitudinal survey.
Methods
Design of the study
We employed a survey research design to gather the study data, choosing to use a questionnaire survey because of its capacity to produce substantial volumes of data compared to experimental studies. The questionnaire survey enabled us to collect valuable information from Chinese earthquake survivors in a short period of time. To reduce the bias of cross-sectional data and to examine the temporal relationships between variables, we employed a longitudinal questionnaire experimental design, collecting multiple questionnaires from the same population.
Participants
Participants were recruited from Beichuan County, Sichuan Province, China, a region that experienced significant devastation during the Wenchuan earthquake. None of the participants had experienced a concussion in the week before completing the survey, and all abstained from using psychostimulants or any other substances affecting the central nervous system. Although some prior research has suggested that administering questionnaires through digital media platforms generates more responses (Gever, 2025; Monday and Gever, 2025), we administered the questionnaires face-to-face in Beichuan County. This was because of the specificity of some variables in this study (e.g., particular questions in the PTSD scale may elicit negative memories from participants) and the experience of one author working in Beichuan, who noted that some middle-aged participants would belong to an ethnic minority with a distinct language and therefore lack proficiency in Chinese, rendering a direct web-based questionnaire unsuitable. Thus, it was necessary for the researcher to translate and paraphrase the questionnaire for this population. Data was collected in early 2023 and November 2023, with a 6-month interval between the two collection waves. We eliminated questionnaires that had consecutive unchanged responses and an excessive number of missing answers. This resulted in a final sample of 331 valid responses for inclusion in the longitudinal analysis, including 168 males and 163 females aged between 20 and 47 years (M age  = 30.465, SD age  = 5.369). Considering potential earthquake re-exposure from the scales, individuals who exhibited a high level of PTSD symptoms or rumination were provided with professional psychological care by the research team with the assistance of the local government.
Ethics
The study was approved by Ethics Committee of Mianyang College of Administration (Grant Number: MY23010) on April 1, 2023. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and all participants gave online informed consent and were compensated for their time.
Measurements
PTSD scale
We referred to previous studies that utilized Chinese questionnaires(Xie et al., 2018), which demonstrated excellent levels of validity and reliability in Chinese population. This scale comprises 3 subscales (reexperience, avoidance, and hypervigilance) with total of 17 items, and each item is rated on five-point scale ranging from “1 = strongly disagree” to “5 = strongly agree”. An example item is “Even if nothing reminds you of it, you remember or have mental images of the traumatic event”. Higher total scores indicate more severe PTSD. The Cronbach’s alphas of this scale were satisfactory at T1 (α = 0.95) and T2 (α = 0.90).
Fear of missing out scale
We used Przybylski’s et al. (2013) scale, and it includes 10 items scored on a five-point Likert-type scale(1= “Not at all true of me”, 5 = “Extremely true of me”). An example item is “I keep checking my smartphone so I would not miss anything”. Higher total scores indicate higher FOMO. The Cronbach ’s alpha coefficients were adequate at T1 (α = 0.94) and T2 (α = 0.90).
Rumination scale
The assessment of rumination was performed using the revised Chinese version developed by Zhou et al. (2016), which shows good reliability as well as acceptable internal consistency in Chinese samples. This is a 22-item scale (3 subscales: brooding, e.g., “I often wonder what I did to cause this”; reflection, e.g., “I often analyze recent events to understand why I feel depressed”; depression-related rumination, e.g., “I often think about how lonely I am”) to evaluate various cognitive coping techniques. All items are rated on a 5-point Likert scale that ranges from 0 = “completely disagree” to 4 = “completely agree”. Higher total scores indicate more severe rumination. The Cronbach’s alpha was 0.90 and 0.88 for the two-time point.
Misinformation beliefs scale
We used Gong and Ren (2023’s) scale, which was based on previous studies of social media use (Ahmed, 2022a; Hou et al., 2020; Yang et al., 2022) and showed adequate reliability in misinformation studies. An example item is “I will disseminate any information I find useful to others”. Responses were given on a 5-point scale ranging from 1= “not at all true of me” to 5 = “extremely true of me”. Cronbach’s alpha was 0.86 and 0.80, respectively.
Statistical analysis
Descriptive statistics were performed using JASP. The statistical analyses were conducted using the R language (Version 4.1). The network analysis and visualisation were performed using R packages, including “glmnet” (Friedman et al., 2010) for calculating regressions, “qgraph” for plotting all graphs and “bootnet” for calculating node stability. Age and sex were included as demographic characteristics. Some scholars have suggested combining the benefits of both digital software and manual methods to provide thorough and complex research results (Asogwa, 2024); however, since this study converted qualitative data into quantitative data (e.g., 0 = female and 1 = male), and in accordance with the conventional methodology for analysing questionnaire data, we primarily employed digital software for analysis in this study.
To estimate the CLPN model, we utilised regularised regressions and the penalised maximum likelihood with the LASSO penalty to generate autoregressive and cross-lagged coefficients. To enhance graph sparsity and minimise the risk of overfitting, the tuning parameter (λ = 0.5) was implemented, consistent with Friedman et al. (2010). Unlike cross-sectional data-based network analysis, expected influence is more important than node centrality in CLPN results. Rhemtull et al. (2022) created CLPN by combining network analysis models with the cross-lagged panel model to reveal longitudinal dynamics within and between psychological constructs. In a directed network, CLPN can predict the contributions of variables, including autoregressive and cross-lagged effects. The directed cross-lagged edges enable the calculation of network centralities for in-expected and out-expected influence (Robinaugh et al., 2016). IEI measures the proportion of variance for a given node at T2 accounted for by variables at T1, while OEI measures the effect of T1 on T2 variables (Rhemtulla et al., 2016). These analytical strategies have provided valuable insights and guided the current study in addressing this longstanding issue.
Given that edges in CLPN are directed, there exists an in-degree and an out-degree for each node; therefore, by estimating IEI and OEI, the directionality between items can be determined. Estimates were made for the one-step and two-step bridge EIs (BEI1; BEI2) to establish which items were connected with the four main psychological clusters (PTSD, rumination, FoMO, misinformation beliefs). In CLPN results, blue edges represent positive relationships and red edges represent negative relationships. Edge thickness reflects the strength of edge weights, with thicker edges indicating stronger relationships (Wysocki et al., 2022).
For the stability and accuracy analysis of the network, we used the “bootnet” package, computing 95% confidence intervals (CI) using 1000 bootstraps of nonparametric bootstrapping for each network to calculate the network centrality and stability. According to Epskamp et al. (2018), wide CIs can make it challenging to ensure the stability of an edge. To assess the stability of node centrality, we used a correlation stability coefficient (CS-coefficient), which quantifies the maximum percentage of cases that can be removed while still maintaining a correlation greater than 0.70 between the centrality calculated using the original network and those obtained from the new network (excluding the dropped cases). Epskamp et al. (2018) recommended that the CS-coefficient be higher than 0.25 and preferably above 0.5 to ensure the reliability and interpretability of results.

Discussion
By applying the CLPN approach, we successfully determined the precise and directional connections between these behaviours and established their chronological order. Our findings highlight that some nodes in misinformation beliefs (e.g., Mis3: If a news report contradicts your expected outcome, you tend to believe it is fabricated/fake) and FoMO (e.g., FoMO5: It is important that I understand my friends “in jokes”) emerged as high OEI symptoms, whereas avoidance and reexperience PTSD subscales were identified as high IEI symptoms. Furthermore, Mis3 and two rumination subscales (depression-rumination and brooding) were found to act as core bridging symptoms, connecting the abovementioned mental health issues.
Specifically, the symptom ‘I fear my friends have more rewarding experiences than me’ (FoMO2) and Mis3 were identified as key symptoms leading to the development of other PTSD-related symptoms over time. Notably, these two symptoms exhibited strong connections with the PTSD symptom of avoidance over time. In other words, the emergence of specific symptoms within FoMO and misinformation beliefs can catalyse the development of PTSD symptoms. These symptoms, in turn, exacerbate and intensify PTSD over time. As found by prior research on the effects of other forms of media on earthquake survivors, FoMO is a key factor in maintaining both the loop of rumination and PTSD, and could be seen as the primary requirement for diagnosis (Gong and Ren, 2023). Our research broadens the current findings by illuminating the potential significance of FoMO and misinformation beliefs as the basis for successive responses and heightened dependency within negative reinforcement models of PTSD and rumination.
The core feature of FoMO, ‘It bothers me when I miss an opportunity to meet up with friends’ (FoMO7), was relatively predictive for the subsequent development of other features within the FoMO cluster, such as ‘I get worried when I find out my friends are having fun without me’ (FoMO3) and ‘When I miss out on a planned get-together it bothers me’ (FoMO9). A co-occurrence network analysis of the relationships between smartphone addiction, social network use, and FoMO among Chinese students yielded similar results (L. Li et al., 2022). Considering the established correlation between FoMO and the emergence of ruminative thoughts with social media use, our study may potentially heighten our understanding of this relationship.
The current research yielded some unexpected, yet interesting, results. First, it was observed that the presence of FoMO2 negatively predicted the occurrence of a specific rumination symptom (avoidance) and some embodiment misinformation beliefs over time, including ‘Mis2’ and ‘Mis4’. In line with the theory of compensatory Internet use, we speculated that one possible explanation for this is that individuals with higher FoMO2 scores would exhibit greater concern regarding their friends’ experiences, particularly those that are more successful and more appealing. This concern is evident in their questionnaire responses. Consequently, these individuals tend to spend more time on social media platforms, searching for updates on their friends’ activities, indicating a higher likelihood of engaging in fact-checking behaviour, which results in decreased negative psychological symptoms (i.e., PTSD and beliefs about misinformation).
A recent study provided strong evidence that limiting one’s social media use can function as a method for regulating loneliness and depression, which is consistent with our own research findings. The study employed baseline monitoring for a week, revealing significant reductions in loneliness and depression in the group who limited their social media use, as well as an elimination of FoMO (Hunt et al., 2018). Additionally, some research has demonstrated a positive correlation between increases in fact checking and FoMO (Scheinfeld and Voorhees, 2022); individuals who browse or search for news on social media because their friends have shared it may feel more motivated to stay well-informed about current events as part of increasing opportunities to socialise (Ahmed et al., 2024). The association between frequent media usage and FoMO may be attributed to the desire to stay informed about current events to appear knowledgeable among acquaintances.
One of the most significant findings from our study was the positive association between FoMO5 and misinformation beliefs. This result could partly be explained by the theory of the spiral of silence (Noelle-Neumann, 1974), which would suggest that while individuals can express themselves freely and openly through media platforms, there remains an inevitable spiral of silence; i.e., the motivation for social isolation has not disappeared with the development of the Internet, and online communities continue to exert pressure on individual opinions (which is also a figurative explanation for FoMO5). Additionally, Mis3 is positively correlated with PTSD avoidance, which also aligns with a previous experimental study (Monds et al., 2013). This study examined several crucial factors that may contribute to an individual’s susceptibility to misinformation about distressing events, finding that avoidance scores were related to increased recall of misinformation items. Furthermore, in the present study, the PTSD symptom of “hypervigilance” was the key bridge symptom in the network among individuals, since it has relatively high bridge EI. In our study, an increase in FoMO within T1- > T2 leads to increased hypervigilance, consistent with prior cross-sectional and longitudinal studies on PTSD and FoMO (Shi et al., 2022; Yuan et al., 2021). Specifically, a three-wave longitudinal design study found that FoMO partially mediated the relationship between depressive symptoms (like PTSD) and problematic Internet use severity (Yuan et al., 2021), and an online survey of children revealed a positive association between FoMO and both childhood trauma and PTSD (Shi et al., 2022).
Simultaneously, ruminative symptoms, such as ‘brooding’, trigger the development of FoMO9 at a later stage, indicating the existence of a negative cycle. This confirms the findings from many previous cross-sectional studies on FoMO and rumination. For instance, a study examining the use of Facebook among college students revealed a positive correlation between rumination and FoMO (Dempsey et al., 2019). Another study proposing to explain the variance in FoMO within two independent samples in America revealed that rumination significantly predicted FoMO (Fitzgerald et al., 2023). Based on these highlights, we can reasonably speculate that excessive engagement in ruminative thoughts among the PTSD population can result in high FoMO, leading to a greater belief in misinformation. Conversely, individuals experiencing PTSD may turn to social media platforms as a means of seeking emotional relief, where access to misinformation may aggravate their FoMO and rumination.
Theoretical and practical implications
Our research not only enhances the theoretical significance of related research on misinformation and fake news but emphasises the practical value of considering bridge symptoms in the exploration of media exposure effects, especially the impact of misinformation access. Using network analysis, we demonstrated how misinformation beliefs could cause participants to experience PTSD symptoms and further impair their social functioning. This personal dysfunction subsequently initiates a series of emotional and behavioural issues, such as rumination and FoMO, which may create a negative cycle between them.
There are some unique contributions in practical implications. In practice, disrupting the relationships between bridge symptoms is crucial to prevent the exacerbation of intense correlations among coexisting mental health issues. As part of the treatment approach when working with disaster survivors who exhibit excessive beliefs in online information, it is important to consider the presence of concurrent negative symptoms, particularly symptoms of PTSD and rumination. This study emphasises the significant implications for individuals affected by natural disasters in terms of intervention and enhancing their healthcare. Clinical research should prioritise the prevention of excessive and ruminative thinking and the redirection of attention among people with PTSD, particularly in relation to the adverse effects of earthquakes. As an emerging subject of interest in both theoretical and empirical research, it is recommended that future studies examine the impact of FoMO and rumination on personality traits and social media usage, particularly focusing on specific populations, as suggested by Ashiru et al. (2022). Public policymakers should adopt proactive strategies and interventions to help individuals access essential resources to effectively manage their social media usage before it progresses to become more serious problematic behaviour necessitating intervention.
Limitations and future research
This study explored the mechanisms underlying the simultaneous presence of rumination, FoMO, and misinformation beliefs among the Wenchuan earthquake PTSD population. However, this research has some limitations. First, the DSM-5 PTSD scales (Contractor et al., 2017) have been recently updated and now include four clusters, with the addition of a ‘negative alterations in cognition/mood’ subtype. This differs to the PTSD scale used in the present research. Future studies should therefore focus on examining how this subtype may interact with other mental health issues (e.g., symptoms of anxiety). Second, this research was conducted post-COVID-19, which may, as a public health emergency, also trigger PTSD. Future research should distinguish between PTSD caused by a single disaster and complex PTSD (CPTSD), which results from multiple events. Third, while this research offers important perspectives on the correlation between negative emotions and misinformation beliefs, it is also advisable to consider alternative theories that emphasise the significance of cognitive biases (Sun and Zhang, 2021)—further study is necessary to elucidate these complex mechanisms. Fourth, considering the moderate accuracy of bridge EI, bridge nodes in our network model should be interpreted with caution. Finally, there is a notable imbalance in the distributions of both gender and age in our sample. To address this, we applied appropriate statistical controls; however, it is essential to verify these findings in various genders and age groups, as different mechanisms may appear Ahmed 2022b. Additionally, given that our study primarily focuses on middle-aged Chinese individuals, it may be necessary to validate these findings in more diverse populations and different cultural contexts.
Meanwhile, although the analytical methodology we employed offers superior interpretative strength to structural equation modelling, there are several different methods for analysing longitudinal networks, and CLPN analysis may be considered a fundamental approach. Further research could expand upon this foundation by incorporating advanced analysis methods, such as time-series network analysis (Badal et al., 2023), to better capture dynamic changes. In summary, these limitations highlight the necessity for further investigation in this field. Such future studies should involve a comprehensive analysis of the relationships between different areas of mental health, the integration of alternative theoretical frameworks, the validation of results across diverse populations, and a thorough examination of the distinctive associations discovered.
Conclusion
This study holds considerable implications as it represents the inaugural exploration of the longitudinal relationships between rumination, FoMO, and belief in misinformation among the Chinese earthquake PTSD population using cross-lagged panel network analysis. Our findings reveal that specific FoMO symptoms like ‘I fear my friends have more rewarding experiences than me’ (FoMO2) and ruminative symptoms such as ‘depression-rumination’ and ‘brooding’ can predict belief in misinformation over time. Additionally, Mis3 (‘If a news report contradicts your expected outcome, you tend to believe it is fabricated/fake’), ‘Conflict’ (A6), and negative emotional states like ‘depression-rumination’ may act as bridge symptoms linking the co-occurrence of these conditions. Multilevel and timely interventions that target bridge symptoms, such as cognitive behavioural therapy and efforts from policymakers and medical practitioners, may help to alleviate PTSD and reduce misinformation sharing behaviour among earthquake survivors.
