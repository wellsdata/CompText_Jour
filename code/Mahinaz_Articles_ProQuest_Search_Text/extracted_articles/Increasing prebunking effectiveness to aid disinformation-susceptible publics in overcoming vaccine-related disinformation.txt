1 Introduction
As the digital landscape floods the public with information, disinformation has emerged as a formidable threat to health decision-making. Nowhere is this more visible than in public responses to vaccine messaging, where false claims, particularly surrounding COVID-19, have undermined trust and fueled hesitancy [1,2] .
For instance, recent evidence from the Tough Talks-COVID study highlights that medical mistrust serves as a primary barrier to vaccination; among historically marginalized groups, such as Black communities, this deep-seated mistrust significantly reduces the likelihood of initiating the first vaccine dose [3] . Effectively addressing this challenge requires more than just broad messaging; it demands strategic segmentation of the public and tailored interventions that directly engage with the psychological vulnerabilities and belief systems underlying health information processing.
Understanding the diversity in how individuals engage with health information is critical for designing effective countermeasures [4] . The motivation-knowledge-attitude framework [5] provides a robust basis for classifying publics by their levels of motivation, attitude strength, and knowledge gaps. One notable group within this framework, lacuna publics, is defined as highly motivated actors who exhibit strong opinions but hold limited knowledge about controversial health issues [5] . These publics are not merely passive consumers of disinformation; they actively disseminate it, complicating public health efforts [6] .
Although existing research has mapped susceptibility to disinformation across public types, empirical studies that link such segmentation to message strategies remain limited. To address this gap, the present study explores the use of inoculation-based messages, a preventative strategy that equips individuals with cognitive resistance to disinformation before exposure. While inoculation theory has demonstrated effectiveness in health communication [7] , its integration with public segmentation frameworks like the MKA model remains underexplored.
In light of growing calls for message design that supports psychological autonomy, this study also draws on self-determination theory [8] to assess whether autonomy-supportive or autonomy-controlling messages are more effective across different public types. Using a between-subjects online experiment ( N  = 261), we investigate how four segments, disinformation-amplifying, receptive, vulnerable, and immune publics, respond to inoculation messages differing in their support for audience autonomy.
By bridging theoretical frameworks with experimental testing, this research offers new insights into the design of targeted prebunking strategies. It contributes to both theory and practice by informing how public segmentation and message framing can work synergistically to limit the spread and influence of health-related disinformation.
2 Literature review
To better understand how to design strategic disinformation-countering messages, this study introduces an integrative framework that brings together three complementary theoretical perspectives. First, the Motivation–Knowledge–Attitude (MKA) framework is utilized to diagnose public heterogeneity, particularly for identifying vulnerable “lacuna publics” who may be especially susceptible to misinformation. Second, Inoculation Theory elucidates the core psychological mechanism underlying prebunking—namely, “motivational threat”—which functions as a necessary trigger for building resistance against persuasive misinformation. Third, Self-Determination Theory (SDT) informs the design of these inoculation messages by highlighting how variations in linguistic tone (i.e., autonomy-supportive versus autonomy-controlling) shape audience receptivity.
2.1 Adapting public segmentation frameworks
Rooted in Grunig's earlier work on communication behavior [9] , the Situational Theory of Problem Solving (STOPS) was developed by Kim and Grunig [10] to explain when and why individuals seek information and engage in communication. While initially focused on individual-level behavior, the theory has since been extended to help organizations segment publics for strategic communication planning [10] . Drawing on Grunig and Hunt's foundational model of public typologies [11] , publics are classified into non-public, latent, aware, and active segments based on their levels of involvement and perceived constraints [12] . Building on this segmentation, Kim and Krishna [5] introduced the concept of lacuna publics — a subset of active publics defined by strong attitudes but deficient knowledge on controversial issues. Such publics pose particular challenges due to their conviction-driven discourse and resistance to counterperspectives [13] . To further understand public susceptibility to disinformation, Krishna [13] proposed the motivation-attitude-knowledge deficiency framework, which segments audiences into four types: disinformation-immune, disinformation-vulnerable, disinformation-receptive, and disinformation-amplifying ( Table 1 ).
Lacuna publics, also referred to as disinformation-amplifying publics, are marked by high motivation and communicative engagement but low knowledge and low constraint recognition. These individuals actively seek, interpret, and disseminate information, often reinforcing disinformation due to their ideological certainty and low tolerance for dissenting views [6] .
Using the Motivation-knowledge-attitude Framework to understand the effects of disinformation.
Disinformation refers to inaccurate or manipulated content that is deliberately disseminated [14] . Such misleading information can cause significant harm, whether originating from intentional deception or honest error [15] . In the context of a social media–saturated society, lacuna publics, also termed disinformation-amplifying publics, are especially vulnerable. Defined by Krishna [6] , these individuals not only accept but actively circulate disinformation. Their high motivation, frequent information sharing, and strong ideological alignment make them key agents in the spread of false narratives.
Critically, their limited knowledge base often results in engagement driven more by emotional conviction than informed reasoning, heightening the risk of social fragmentation [16] . Kim and Krishna [5] emphasize that the core concern lies not in their disengagement, but in their fervent belief systems and rejection of alternative viewpoints. These characteristics also make lacuna publics notably resistant to corrective efforts such as inoculation messages [6] . Consequently, they pose a formidable challenge to public health communication. Research in inoculation theory and public segmentation highlights the importance of message tailoring and sensitivity to psychological reactance when addressing disinformation-susceptible publics [6,17–19] .
2.2 Combating COVID-19 vaccine disinformation with prebunking
(Mis/dis)information about vaccines has surged in recent years, a trend intensified by the COVID-19 pandemic [20] . This has underscored the urgent need for effective strategies to promote vaccine acceptance and counter misinformation [21] . Among these, prebunking, a proactive approach rooted in inoculation theory, has shown particular promise, especially when combined with message designs that incorporate autonomy-supportive or autonomy-controlling language.
While debunking, the correction of misinformation after exposure, can be effective [22] , it often triggers unintended consequences. These include the continued influence effect, where false information persists in memory even after correction, and psychological resistance, particularly among hesitant individuals [7,23] . In contrast, prebunking preemptively fortifies individuals by presenting weakened versions of misinformation alongside refutations, thereby stimulating “cognitive antibodies” that protect against future persuasive attacks [7,24] . The practical utility of this approach is exemplified by Miller et al. [25] , who highlight the critical mediatory role of healthcare workers in countering vaccine misinformation. Their study demonstrates that integrating Problem-Based Learning with prebunking strategies significantly bolsters the confidence of healthcare professionals in identifying and refuting misleading information.
Inoculation theory posits that individuals are typically unmotivated and unpracticed in defending their attitudes [26] . However, forewarning them of potential attacks, threat induction, and offering refutational preemption can prompt active resistance. These mechanisms not only enhance message processing but also increase recognition of manipulative rhetorical strategies [27,28] . Recent experimental studies support this view. Piltch-Loeb et al. [20] found that brief inoculation videos significantly increased participants' resistance to misinformation, reduced their willingness to share false content, and improved vaccine intentions.
In the context of prebunking, motivational threat acts as the trigger for psychological resistance [29] . Unlike apprehensive threat, which is grounded in fear, motivational threat stems from the recognition that one's mental autonomy is at risk. This awareness creates a proactive state where the individual is motivated to scrutinize and counter-argue incoming misinformation [30,31] . Studies suggest that motivational threat more effectively activates resistance than fear-based appeals ( [32] ; anonymised for peer review). It is therefore critical to evaluate how inoculation messages elicit such threat responses, particularly among publics susceptible to disinformation. Given this theoretical foundation, the present study tests the following hypothesis: H1 Inoculation message exposure will elicit higher levels of motivational threat compared to a control condition.

2.3 Enhancing mechanisms within inoculation messages to combat disinformation about vaccines
One promising avenue for enhancing the effectiveness of inoculation messages involves incorporating principles from self-determination theory (SDT) (anonymised for peer review). SDT posits that individuals are inherently motivated to act proactively, but when basic psychological needs are unmet, motivation diminishes. The theory identifies three fundamental needs, competence, relatedness, and autonomy, that underpin psychological well-being and influence individuals' perceptions of communication [33] .
Research shows that controlling environments can undermine motivation and well-being [34] . Directive messages, such as “you will not make good grades if you do not study,” may be interpreted as pressure, leading recipients to feel coerced rather than supported [35] . Similarly, overly prescriptive solutions can restrict autonomy. In contrast, autonomy-supportive communication, offering meaningful choices, validating anxieties, and enabling exploration, fosters motivation and is perceived as less threatening and more legitimate [35] .
Despite growing interest in SDT, few empirical studies have linked autonomy-based message strategies to segmented publics' responses to disinformation. Understanding the interaction between message format and audience characteristics remains an important research frontier. This study addresses this gap by examining the perceived motivational threat and autonomy support elicited by inoculation messages across distinct public segments.
Specifically, the study compares responses from disinformation-susceptible groups, including disinformation-amplifying (lacuna publics), disinformation-vulnerable, and disinformation-receptive segments, to those from disinformation-immune individuals. The aim is to assess whether susceptibility influences perceptions of inoculation message mechanisms.

2.4 Examining message effectiveness between disinformation-susceptible publics
This study employed perceived message effectiveness as the primary outcome to assess the impact of the intervention. Prior research on COVID-19 vaccine communication has consistently highlighted the role of message framing. For example, loss-framed messages have been shown to reduce risk perceptions and promote vaccine uptake more effectively than gain-framed or emotionally rational appeals [36] . Messages emphasizing vaccine efficacy, safety, and infection risk have also been associated with increased vaccination intentions [37,38] .
While these strategies can be effective, their impact often depends on individual factors such as prior attitudes and knowledge. This study, therefore, examines both the effects of inoculation message design and how different audience segments interpret these messages. Particular attention is given to disinformation-susceptible groups such as lacuna publics. The objective is to understand how message mechanisms interact with audience characteristics to influence perceived effectiveness.

These mechanisms enhance resistance by improving attitudes and behavioral intentions. Building on this, the present study investigates how distinct inoculation message formats, including those that emphasize autonomy support, autonomy control, or provide no motivational cues, affect perceived message effectiveness within the context of the motivation-knowledge-attitude framework.
Beyond main effects, prior work suggests that message type may interact with audience characteristics. Autonomy-supportive messages, which provide choice and affirm personal agency, are typically viewed as more persuasive. In contrast, autonomy-controlling messages may provoke resistance, particularly among publics already predisposed to strong opinions and low trust in corrective efforts. For example, lacuna publics may react defensively when told what to believe, interpreting such messages as controlling or condescending. This psychological reactance may not only reduce message effectiveness but also undermine prior inoculation efforts, potentially increasing disinformation sharing. Given this, the study further explores whether the effectiveness of message mechanisms varies by audience type.

3 Methods
This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of [university name blinded for review] (IRB No. [21-05-4676]). Drawing on Cohen's [39,40] and Kang's [41] benchmark parameters, an a priori power analysis conducted via G*Power determined that a minimum of 251 participants would be required to detect a small to medium effect size ( f  = 0.25) with an alpha of 0.05 and a statistical power of 0.95. The survey was conducted in April 2022 with a total of 311 individuals recruited via Amazon's Mechanical Turk (MTurk) to participate in this study. Previous research has shown that MTurk samples closely reflect the broader U.S. population, offering access to demographically diverse and representative samples [42] . Furthermore, data quality from MTurk participants has been found to be comparable to that collected through social media recruitment and traditional face-to-face behavioral testing [43] . Only participants who were at least 18 years old, resided in the United States, and had ≥95% Human Intelligence Tasks (HIT) approval rate were able to participate. On average it took participants 10.22 min to complete the study. After data cleaning and the removal of incomplete samples ( n  = 14) or participants who failed at least one of two attention checks ( n  = 49), the effective sample size was 261. Of those, 58.2% were males, 41.0% were females, and 0.8% other. The average age of participants was 36.55 ( SD  = 9.89).
3.1 Procedures
After providing consent, participants were asked to complete a series of questions to determine public segmentations including measurements regarding their knowledge, prior held attitudes, and motivation toward the topic of the COVID-19 vaccine. In addition, a question regarding vaccine skepticism was asked to be used as a control variable in the analysis. After the variables needed to determine lucana publics were captured, participants were randomized into one of three messages (i.e., inoculation with autonomy support, inoculation with autonomy control, control message). The inoculation messages were developed based on work by [author name redacted] [44,45] that were found to induce mechanisms of inoculation and/or autonomy support. Both conditions contained a forewarning that the participant may receive disinformation regarding the COVID-19 vaccine safety (i.e., “use of the vaccine has been attacked by people who hope to spread false information to manipulate you”), along with counterarguing points that demonstrate its safety (i.e., “the vaccines were tested in clinical trials involving tens of thousands of people to make sure they met safety standards” (exemplar of stimuli can be found in Table 2 ). The first group ( n  = 91) was randomized to see the inoculation message containing autonomy supportive messaging that emphasized that the participant had the choice to choose whether to believe disinformation regarding the vaccine.
The second group ( n  = 92) was randomized to see the inoculation message containing autonomy control messaging that emphasized the participant had to believe the disinformation was not true and then they must receive the COVID-19 vaccine. The last group received a message control ( n  = 78), seeing only a control message commonly used in inoculation research [17] . Once the stimuli were viewed, participants received an attention check to ensure content was read, along with manipulation checks and the remaining dependent variables. Lastly, demographics were collected, and a randomized code was generated for participants to receive payment.
3.2 Dependent variables
An exploratory factor analysis using principal component factoring with a Oblimin with Kaiser Normalization rotation was conducted on all six dependent variables separately to confirm that items loaded properly. The factor variance and Eignvalues are reported below, along with cronbach's alpha, means, and standard deviations.
3.2.1 Motivational threat
Adapted from previous inoculation research [46,47] participants were asked four questions to ensure that motivational threat was induced. Statements such as “After reading the message, I want to counterargue disinformation about the COVID-19 vaccine” were measured on a seven-point strongly disagree-strongly agree Likert-type scale ( a  = 0.85, M  = 5.08, SD  = 0.96, 68.78% of variance explained, Eigenvalue = 2.75).
3.2.2 Autonomy support
To determine if the autonomy manipulation was perceived by participants, autonomy support was measured. Adapted from Sheldon & Filak [48] , participants were asked five items on a seven-point strongly disagree-strongly agree Likert-type scale including statements such as “While reading the message, I felt like I had a choice on what I should support regarding COVID-19 vaccine information” ( a  = 0.82, M  = 5.33, SD  = 1.04, 58.49% of variance explained, Eigenvalue = 2.93).
3.2.3 Motivation
Participants received a three-item scale adapted from Kim and Grunig [10] to measure motivation. The items were measured on a seven-point, strongly disagree-strongly agree Likert-type scale, including statements such as “I want to better understand the COVID-19 vaccine” ( a  = 0.85, M  = 5.09, SD = 1.11, 77.05% of variance explained, Eigenvalue = 2.31).
3.2.4 Attitude
Attitude toward the COVID-19 vaccine was measured on a seven-item, seven-point strongly disagree-strongly agree Likert-type scale adapted from Krishna [13] , which included statements such as “I believe that mandatory COVID-19 vaccines to be an infringement on my rights” ( a  = 0.83, M  = 4.22, = 1.29, 54.85% of variance explained, Eigenvalue = 3.84).
3.2.5 Knowledge deficiency
Adapted from Krishna [13] , knowledge about COVID-19 vaccines was tested with ten true/false statements based on common misconceptions about each of the issues. Items included statements such as, “The natural immunity I get from being sick with COVID-19 is better than the immunity I get from COVID-19 vaccination” ( M  = 5.80, SD  = 1.06).
3.2.6 Perceived message effectiveness
Perceived message effectiveness of the two inoculation messages was measured with a seven-point semantic differential scale of four items including if the inoculation message was persuasive, effective, convincing, or compelling [49] ( a  = 0.82, M  = 5.19, SD  = 0.93, 64.67% of variance explained, Eigenvalue = 2.59).
3.3 Data analysis
All data analyses were conducted using SPSS. To test the manipulation checks and hypothesis 1, one-way ANOVAs were used to determine if there were significant differences between the message manipulations on motivational threat and autonomy support. For all research questions, ANCOVA was used to control for if the initial COVID-19 vaccine had been received.
Following procedures based on Krishna's [6] research prior to testing the hypothesis and research questions, participants were segmented into four publics within the motivation-attitude-knowledge framework ( Table 3 ). Specifically, a mean-split procedure was applied to scores measuring problem recognition, involvement recognition, and constraint recognition — the three situational perceptions central to STOPS — alongside attitude measures toward the issue [12] . To do so, participants were scored out of 10 on their knowledge deficiency, and those above the mean ( M  = 5.79, SD  = 1.06) were identified as being knowledge-deficient, and were recoded as 1 ( n  = 119). Similarly, those reporting higher-than-average levels of motivation in problem solving ( M  = 5.09, SD  = 1.37) were recoded as 1 ( n  = 158). Individuals reporting a mean of 4.22 or below for COVID-19 vaccine acceptance were classified as holding low extreme attitudes, and their scores were recoded as 1 ( n  = 75). This resulted in 67 individuals with high extreme attitudes in the sample. Following the summation procedure, 65 individuals were identified as being lacuna (i.e., disinformation-amplifying), while 196 individuals were classified as non-lacuna. Additionally, participants were broken into four segments including disinformation-immune ( n  = 75, 28.7%), disinformation-vulnerable ( n  = 78, 29.9%), disinformation-receptive ( n  = 43, 16.5%), and disinformation-amplifying ( n  = 65, 24.9%).
3.4 Priori analysis
Prior to testing the research questions put forth regarding lucana publics and perceived message effectiveness, a manipulation check was conducted to ensure that the appropriate levels of inoculation and autonomy mechanisms were present. Four statements were used to measure levels of motivational threat, such as “After reading the message, I feel motivated to think about why I hold the beliefs I do about the COVID-19 vaccine.” Additionally, seven statements assessed levels of autonomy support, for example, “While reading the message, I felt like I had a choice on what I should support regarding COVID-19 vaccine information.” Responses to these statements were evaluated on a seven-point Likert-type scale, ranging from strongly disagree to strongly agree.
Two ANCOVAs, controlling for if the initial COVID-19 vaccine was received, were conducted to ensure the manipulation of inoculation and autonomy support were successful. It was found that when examining levels of motivational threat the manipulation for both inoculation messages (i.e., autonomy support vs. autonomy control) were significantly different from the control message, F (2, 257) = 8.13, p  < .001, Power = 0.98, η 2  = 0.06. Post-hoc comparisons using Bonferonni indicated that individuals who received either the inoculation message using autonomy support ( M  = 5.18, se  = 0.12, p  = .01) or the inoculation message using autonomy control ( M  = 5.33, se  = 0.12, p  < .001) experienced statistically significantly higher levels of motivational threat than those who were in the control condition ( M  = 4.65, se  = 0.13). Thus, the inoculation treatment was successful and H1 is supported.
When examining autonomy support, an ANCOVA found that there were significant differences between the conditions, F (2, 257) = 3.09, p  = .03, Power = 0.65, η 2  = 0.03. The condition containing autonomy supportive language had the highest levels of autonomy support ( M  = 5.46, se  = 0.10), followed by autonomy control ( M  = 5.39, se  = 0.10), and the control message ( M  = 5.10, se  = 0.11).
4 Results
To address RQ1, which examines how public types differ in their levels of motivational threat and perceived autonomy support following inoculation, an ANCOVA was conducted ( Table 4 ). Vaccination status (whether the initial COVID-19 vaccine was received) was included as a covariate.
It was found that there was a statistically significant main effect of public segments on motivational threat, F (3, 256) = 17.66, p  < .001, Power = 1.00, η 2  = 0.17. The large effect size indicates that public segment membership accounts for approximately 17% of the variance in motivational threat, suggesting that audience susceptibility plays a substantively meaningful role in how inoculation messages are experienced. Post-hoc comparisons (Bonferroni) revealed several significant differences.Disinformation-vulnerable publics ( M  = 5.36, se  = 0.12) experienced significantly higher motivational threat than disinformation-immune publics (M = 4.44, se  = 0.12, p  < .001). Disinformation-amplifying publics ( M  = 5.65, se  = 0.13) also experienced significantly higher motivational threat than disinformation-immune publics ( p  < .001), and reported the highest motivational threat of all four segments. Additionally, there was a significant difference between vulnerable and receptive publics ( M  = 4.79, se  = 0.16, p  = .03), such that vulnerable publics experienced greater motivational threat. Taken together, these findings indicate that publics with higher disinformation susceptibility, particularly vulnerable and amplifying segments, are more strongly activated by inoculation messages in terms of threat perception, while immune publics, who already possess established resistance, respond with comparatively lower threat.
It was also found that there was a statistically significant main effect of public segments on the inoculation message autonomy support, F (3, 256) = 7.43, p  < .001, Power = 0.98, η 2  = 0.08. Post-hoc analysis (Bonferroni) again showed that there was statistically significant differences ( p  = .002) between disinformation-immune publics ( M  = 4.96, se  = 0.11) and disinformation-vulnerable publics ( M  = 5.50, se  = 0.10), such that the vulnerable publics perceived more support. Additionally, there was statistically significant differences ( p  < .001) which showed that disinformation-vulnerable publics perceived the inoculation messages to be less supportive than disinformation-amplifying publics ( M  = 5.62, se  = 0.11). This pattern is practically meaningful and somewhat counterintuitive: the audiences most resistant to disinformation perceived the inoculation messages as least autonomy supportive, while those most engaged in spreading disinformation perceived them as most supportive. These findings suggest that inoculation messages may need to be more intentionally designed to signal genuine autonomy support to already-resistant audiences, while also highlighting the potential receptivity of amplifying publics as an underexplored intervention target. There were no significant differences between the other public types.
RQ 2a-c used a series of ANCOVAs controlling for if the initial COVID-19 vaccine had been received. RQ 2a tested the main effects of public segments on PME of the mechanisms within the inoculation messages ( Table 5 ). It was found that there was a statistically significant main effect of public segments on the inoculation message PME, F (3, 256) = 4.05, p  < .008, Power = 0.84, η 2  = 0.04. The small-to-moderate effect size indicates that message type accounts for approximately 4% of the variance in PME. Overall, when examining the average mean scores, disinformation-vulnerable publics ( M  = 5.41, se  = 0.12) had the highest PME, followed by amplifying publics ( M  = 5.38, se  = 0.13), receptive publics ( M  = 4.97, se  = 0.16), and immune publics ( M  = 4.92, se  = 0.12). Post-hoc analysis (Bonferroni) showed that disinformation-vulnerable publics had statistically significantly higher PME than disinformation-immune publics ( p  = .03), a mean difference of approximately 0.49 scale points. No other pairwise differences were statistically significant.
RQ 2b examined if the enhanced inoculation message mechanism type (i.e., autonomy supportive, autonomy control, no message - control) influenced PME. There was a statistically significant main effect of message type on PME, F (3, 257) = 7.46, p  < .001, Power = 0.94, η 2  = 0.05 ( Table 6 ). The small-to-moderate effect size indicates that message type accounts for approximately 5% of the variance in PME. Post-hoc analysis (Bonferroni) showed statistically significant differences when using either autonomy supportive ( M  = 5.31, se  = 0.11, p  = .007) or autonomy controlling ( M  = 5.39, se  = 0.11, p  < .001) messages compared to the control condition ( M  = 4.81, se  = 0.12). The mean differences between each inoculation condition and the control were approximately 0.50 and 0.58 scale points respectively, indicating that both inoculation approaches consistently and meaningfully outperformed the absence of any motivational framing. There was no statistically significant difference between the autonomy supportive and autonomy controlling conditions, suggesting that the presence of motivational framing, rather than its specific orientation, drives the effectiveness advantage over the control.
RQ2c examined the interaction between different public segments and the type of enhanced inoculation message. A statistically significant interaction was found between public segments and message type when assessing perceived message effectiveness (PME), F (6, 248) = 3.17, p  = .005, Power = 0.92, η 2  = 0.07 ( Table 7 ). The effect size ( η 2  = 0.07) indicates a moderate practical effect, suggesting that the relationship between message type and perceived effectiveness meaningfully varies across audience segments beyond what would be expected by chance alone.
When examining the interaction effects in greater detail, the pattern of results reveals how each message type operates differently depending on a public segment's preexisting susceptibility to disinformation. There was statistically significant difference for PME between autonomy support ( M  = 5.46, se  = 0.20) and autonomy control ( M  = 5.35, se  = 0.21) for disinformation-immune segments when compared to the control condition ( M  = 4.06, se  = 0.18, p  < .001). Meaning disinformation-immune publics, those already resistant to misinformation, responded substantially more favorably to both inoculation message types than to the control, suggesting that inoculation messages may reinforce and validate preexisting resistance rather than simply introducing new defenses. The meaningful gap between the inoculation conditions and the control for this segment underscores that even among already-resistant audiences, message framing carries practical weight.
While no other interactions were statistically significant, when looking at the interaction between autonomy supportive inoculation and public segments, disinformation-immune publics had the highest PME average ( M  = 5.46, se  = 0.20), with receptive publics having the lowest ( M  = 4.99, se  = 0.26). When examining autonomy controlling inoculation, disinformation-vulnerable publics had the highest PME ( M  = 5.58, se  = 0.20), with receptive publics having the lowest ( M  = 5.04, se  = 0.26). For the control condition, PME was highest for disinformation-vulnerable publics ( M  = 5.38, se  = 0.21) and lowest for immune publics ( M  = 4.06, se  = 0.20). Taken together, these findings suggest that inoculation messaging, regardless of autonomy framing, meaningfully enhances perceived effectiveness only among publics already resistant to disinformation, while audiences more susceptible to or engaged in disinformation spreading perceive all message types similarly. These results underscore the importance of audience segmentation in designing and evaluating inoculation interventions.
5 Discussion
This study aimed to (1) identify disinformation-susceptible public segments regarding the COVID-19 vaccine and (2) examine how these segments perceive intervention messaging, specifically inoculation. A two-phase design, using a survey and experiment, was conducted with American adults to analyze the characteristics of four conceptualized publics. The study further assessed the message effectiveness of inoculation strategies among lacuna publics compared to non-lacuna counterparts. By segmenting individuals based on motivation, attitude, and knowledge deficiencies, this research advances the typology framework of disinformation-susceptible publics. Key insights emerged regarding motivational threat, autonomy support, and perceived message effectiveness (PME).
To establish baseline segmentation, the study first identified lacuna (disinformation-amplifying) and non-lacuna publics (disinformation-immune, disinformation-receptive, and disinformation-vulnerable). Following segmentation, the experiment tested inoculation messages varying in autonomy support. Results revealed that both autonomy supportive and autonomy controlling messages elicited significantly higher motivational threat compared to the control, with no significant differences emerging between the two inoculation types. Public segments differed significantly in their experience of motivational threat and perceived autonomy support, with disinformation-vulnerable publics reporting the highest levels of both. Both inoculation message types were perceived as significantly more effective than the control condition overall, and the interaction between message type and public segment revealed that perceived effectiveness differences across conditions were driven primarily by the disinformation-immune segment. For all other segments, message type did not significantly influence PME.
5.1 Inoculation message manipulation and motivational threat
H1 predicted that both inoculation message types would produce higher motivational threat than the control condition, and this hypothesis was supported. Individuals who received either the autonomy supportive or autonomy controlling inoculation message experienced significantly higher levels of motivational threat than those in the control condition, with no significant difference between the two inoculation conditions. This confirms that the manipulation was successful and that motivational framing, whether autonomy affirming or directive, is sufficient to activate the threat component central to inoculation theory, consistent with prior work demonstrating that forewarning and refutational content reliably elevate threat perceptions in inoculation contexts [45,50,51] .
5.2 Motivational threat, autonomy support, and public segmentation
Beyond the manipulation check, RQ1 findings reveal that public segments differ significantly in how they experience inoculation messages in terms of both motivational threat and perceived autonomy support. Disinformation-vulnerable publics reported the highest levels of motivational threat, while disinformation-immune publics reported the lowest. This pattern is theoretically consistent with inoculation theory: vulnerable publics, lacking established resistance to disinformation, may be more cognitively responsive to the threat cues embedded in the message, recognizing the warning as personally relevant to their own informational environment [13] . Immune publics, by contrast, may perceive less novelty or urgency in the threat given that resistance is already well established.
The autonomy support findings further extend this pattern. Disinformation-vulnerable publics perceived the inoculation messages as less autonomy supportive than disinformation-amplifying publics. From an SDT perspective, this is worth careful attention. If vulnerable publics perceive less autonomy support in inoculation messages, this could undermine the intrinsic motivation needed to engage with and act on message content [52] . Health communicators should therefore consider how inoculation message design can more explicitly affirm the agency of audiences most susceptible to disinformation, particularly given that this group simultaneously experiences the highest motivational threat.
5.3 Perceived message effectiveness and public segmentation
RQ2a findings indicate that public segments differ in overall PME, with disinformation-vulnerable publics reporting the highest perceived effectiveness and disinformation-immune publics reporting the lowest. This pattern aligns with STP's proposition that problem recognition and involvement shape how publics engage with and evaluate messages [9] . Vulnerable publics, by virtue of their greater exposure to and engagement with disinformation, may perceive inoculation messages as more personally relevant, thereby rating them as more effective. The comparatively lower PME among immune publics may reflect a form of informational saturation, whereby audiences who have already internalized resistance find little added value in the message content [13] . Notably, lacuna publics reported the second highest PME, suggesting that disinformation-amplifying audiences may also exhibit meaningful receptivity to inoculation messaging, a finding that warrants further investigation.
5.4 Message type and perceived effectiveness
RQ2b findings demonstrate that both autonomy supportive and autonomy controlling inoculation messages were perceived as significantly more effective than the control condition, with no significant difference between the two inoculation message types. This finding supports the utility of enhanced inoculation approaches that incorporate motivational language, consistent with prior work integrating SDT principles into persuasive health communication [53,54] . The equivalence of the two inoculation conditions at the overall level suggests that the presence of motivational framing, whether autonomy affirming or directive, may be sufficient to enhance perceived effectiveness relative to a message with no such framing. This raises an important question about whether the theoretical distinction between autonomy supportive and autonomy controlling approaches carries practical significance at the overall population level, or whether its relevance emerges primarily at the level of specific audience segments.
5.5 The interaction between message type and public segments
The most theoretically rich findings emerge from RQ2c, where the interaction between message type and public segmentation revealed that the significant interaction effect was driven primarily by the disinformation-immune segment. Post-hoc comparisons showed that for immune publics, both inoculation message types were rated significantly higher in PME than the control condition. Critically, however, the two inoculation conditions did not significantly differ from one another for this segment, indicating that immune publics responded more favorably to any inoculation message relative to the control, rather than showing a preference for one motivational framing over the other. For disinformation-vulnerable, receptive, and amplifying publics, no significant differences in PME were found across any message conditions, meaning that message type did not meaningfully differentiate perceived effectiveness for these groups.
This pattern carries important implications. Inoculation messages appear to function most effectively as reinforcement for audiences who already possess critical orientations toward disinformation, consistent with McGuire's [24] original conceptualization of inoculation as a process of strengthening existing attitudinal defenses. The absence of significant message type differences among vulnerable, receptive, and amplifying publics suggests that vulnerable and amplifying publics require more foundational need-supportive communication before motivational framing within inoculation messages can take effect. From a public health standpoint, these findings suggest that inoculation campaigns may yield stronger returns in perceived effectiveness among already-resistant populations, and that reaching the most at-risk audiences may require strategies that extend beyond message framing alone.
5.6 Theoretical implications
The theoretical implications of this research extend across inoculation theory, self-determination theory, and attitude-motivation-knowledge deficiency framework, and point toward greater integration of these frameworks in health communication scholarship. This study takes a meaningful step forward by examining how inoculation mechanisms operate among individuals with varying degrees of susceptibility to disinformation, rather than treating the audience as a uniform population with pre-existing attitudes. This expansion allows researchers to explore not only how people resist persuasive messages when they hold prior beliefs but also how they engage with protective messaging when they are actively vulnerable to disinformation.
First, the successful manipulation of motivational threat across both inoculation message types reinforces the theoretical centrality of threat as a mechanism of inoculation. However, the RQ1 findings complicate this picture by demonstrating that baseline levels of motivational threat vary meaningfully across public segments. This suggests that threat elicitation is not a uniform process but is instead conditioned by audience members' preexisting susceptibility and informational engagement. Future theoretical development of inoculation theory should account for how individual and audience-level characteristics moderate the magnitude and nature of threat responses, rather than treating threat as a fixed outcome of message exposure.
Second, the SDT-informed autonomy framing manipulation produced equivalent effects on both motivational threat and PME regardless of whether the framing was supportive or controlling. This null difference between conditions is itself theoretically informative. It suggests that in the context of inoculation messaging, the motivational orientation of the framing may matter less than the simple presence of motivationally relevant language. This finding invites further theoretical refinement of how SDT constructs translate into persuasive message design, particularly in health contexts where both autonomy support and structured guidance have established roles ( [52,53] ). That said, the equivalence of conditions at the overall level should not be interpreted as evidence that autonomy framing is inconsequential; rather, its relevance may be more precisely located at the level of audience segment than at the population level.
The equivalence of autonomy supportive and autonomy controlling conditions in terms of PME, while practically convenient, raises important ethical considerations for health communication practice. SDT research consistently demonstrates that autonomy controlling communication, despite its potential short-term persuasive utility, can undermine psychological need satisfaction, erode intrinsic motivation, and generate reactance over time [52,55] . In the context of vaccine communication, where public trust is already fragile and long-term behavioral engagement is the ultimate goal, the use of directive and controlling message framing warrants careful scrutiny. The fact that both conditions performed equivalently in terms of perceived effectiveness suggests that communicators need not sacrifice autonomy supportive principles in pursuit of persuasive impact. Where possible, autonomy supportive framing should be prioritized not only on ethical grounds but also because it is more likely to foster the sustained engagement and internalized motivation that durable health behavior change requires.
Third, the observed interaction effects contribute directly to the theoretical integration of the Situational Theory of Problem Solving (STOPS) and the Attitude-Motivation-Knowledge (AMK) deficiency framework (lacuna framework) with Inoculation Theory. While the lacuna framework categorically delineates publics based on their cognitive and attitudinal deficits [13] , STOPS posits that communicative behaviors are contingent upon situational perceptions, which include problem recognition, constraint recognition, and level of involvement [10] . Merging these perspectives provides a more nuanced lens through which to examine how different publics receive and evaluate inoculation messages. Disinformation-immune publics, who by definition have high involvement with and recognition of disinformation as a problem, appear uniquely positioned to perceive inoculation messages as effective relative to a control. This has theoretical implications for how inoculation researchers conceptualize their target audiences: if the perceived effectiveness of inoculation is strongest among already-resistant publics, then the theory's practical reach for vulnerable populations may require supplementation with additional frameworks that address foundational engagement and involvement.
Fourth, the study advances the typology framework of disinformation-susceptible publics by demonstrating that segmentation based on motivation, attitude, and knowledge deficiencies produces theoretically coherent and empirically distinguishable differences in message processing. The study also considers the dichotomy of pro- and anti-attitudes and incorporates individuals' dispositions in favor of or against a particular issue, recognizing that disinformation susceptibility is a multifaceted construct influenced by factors beyond mere positive or negative sentiment. By applying inoculation theory across these varied public types, this research broadens understanding of how people interact with and respond to health information in a disinformation-rich environment, and lays groundwork for future research into the complexities of persuasion and resistance across diverse audience segments.
5.7 Practical implications
The practical implications of this study are particularly significant in light of COVID-19 challenges and widespread vaccine disinformation. First, the results underscore the value of audience segmentation as a foundational step in inoculation campaign planning. Rather than designing a single inoculation message for broad public consumption, communicators should invest in identifying where their target audiences fall along the disinformation susceptibility continuum. The divergent response patterns observed across public segments demonstrate that the same message can produce meaningfully different effects depending on who receives it, and that a one-size-fits-all approach risks misallocating resources toward audiences who may not benefit most from inoculation strategies.
Interestingly it was found that the audiences most resistant to disinformation perceived the inoculation messages as least autonomy supportive, while those most engaged in spreading disinformation perceived them as most supportive. This could reflect differences in the audiences' prior knowledge and psychological needs. Because disinformation-susceptible publics have a more limited capacity to detect misinformation, they may be more open to external guidance, making the message's autonomy-supportive elements highly noticeable. Conversely, disinformation-immune publics already possess a strong critical thinking framework; for them, autonomy-oriented cues may feel redundant, actually diminishing their sense of autonomy support. This observation suggests that inoculation messages may need to be more intentionally designed to signal genuine autonomy support to already-resistant audiences, while also highlighting the potential receptivity of amplifying publics as an underexplored intervention target. Ultimately, these results support SDT's premise that individual differences dictate the perception of autonomy support, highlighting the necessity of message–audience fit in inoculation communications [53] .
The absence of significant message type effects among disinformation-vulnerable, receptive, and amplifying publics points to an important gap in the current inoculation toolkit. For these audiences, inoculation messaging alone may be insufficient, and communicators should consider complementary strategies. These might include trusted messenger approaches that leverage credible community voices, narrative-based messaging that builds emotional connection and relevance, or need-supportive communication strategies grounded in SDT that address foundational barriers to engagement before introducing inoculation content. Reaching lacuna publics in particular may require campaign strategies that first build informational trust and reduce constraint recognition before persuasive inoculation messaging can take effect.
Finally, these findings have direct relevance for public health organizations, government agencies, and nonprofit communicators developing vaccine campaigns and, more broadly, for any health communication effort operating in an environment where disinformation poses a significant barrier to public engagement. The integration of public segmentation frameworks into campaign design, message testing, and evaluation offers a practical path toward more targeted, efficient, and ethically grounded health communication.
5.8 Limitations and future research
This study worked to further conceptualize the motivation-knowledge-attitude framework in regards to disinformation regarding the COVID-19 vaccine. Despite contributions of this study, certain limitations must be acknowledged to help propel future research. Particularly, the data collection strategy adopted in this study deserves attention. This sample was limited to MTurk users and is not representative of the U.S. population. Additional work must be done to examine the potential differing effects of both autonomy support and inoculation strategies across different cultures. In this same vein, participants were segmented using a mean-split approach to identify lacuna publics, a method consistent with established STOPS research [6,12] . While this approach facilitates the comparison of distinct audience segments, future research might utilize continuous modeling or latent class analysis to further explore the granular nuances of knowledge deficiency without the loss of statistical power associated with dichotomization.
Additionally, prior to exposing participants to the experimental conditions, they were asked issue-specific questions for segmentation purposes to evaluate their motivation, knowledge, and attitudes toward a specific topic (i.e., Covid-19 in this study). These questions were designed to minimize the likelihood that participants would form anticipatory thoughts or biases related to the experimental conditions. For instance, the motivation question, “I want to better understand the COVID-19 vaccine,” utilized a seven-point Likert-type scale, which decreases the possibility of priming participants. However, it remains challenging to entirely eliminate potential concerns about priming. Future research should consider additional strategies to further mitigate any priming effects and ensure the robustness of the findings.
This study represents an initial application of inoculation mechanisms to disinformation-susceptible publics, and several directions merit further investigation. Future research should expand the scope, for example by examining climate change misinformation or emerging crisis topics. It should also investigate how varying disinformation attack scenarios affect different public segments, exploring diverse topical domains, and evaluating the differential impact of message framing strategies across populations. Such efforts would collectively advance a more comprehensive and refined understanding of prebunking effectiveness. As this study is an initial examination of the differing effects of inoculation strategies on public segments, emphasis was put on measuring perceived message effectiveness. Future research should build from these findings to apply theoretical models such as theory of planned behavior, shifting the focus toward the longitudinal impact of inoculation on cognitive attitude formation and behavioral trajectories of different public segments. As one of the core segments of this framework is disinformation-amplifying, it is important to continue the effect to examine potential ways to thwart the spread of disinformation.
Moreover, future research should broaden this scope by incorporating additional dimensions of SDT, specifically competence, defined as the mastery of personally significant skills, and relatedness, which refers to the sense of connection to others [52] . Examining these constructs alongside autonomy would provide a more holistic understanding of the psychological drivers essential to sustained behavior change in the context of disinformation resistance. While the autonomy-controlling messages used in this study utilized highly directive phrasing to ensure strong experimental manipulation, we acknowledge the potential impact on ecological validity. Such explicit language was chosen to clearly elicit psychological reactance in a laboratory setting; however, future studies should explore whether more subtle linguistic variations produce similar effects in real-world health communication contexts.
6 Conclusion
Building upon Krishna's [6] seminal insight that “disinformation audiences not as mere audiences receiving information, but as problem solvers searching for and transmitting information” (p. 16), this study underscores the paramount importance of pre-emptive measures, such as forewarning and the provision of accurate information to counteract the transmission of disinformation. The critical role of message effectiveness (PME) is brought to the forefront, as this study represents the initial step in understanding how inoculation strategies influence public perception and response to disinformation with differing publics. Enhancing mechanisms within inoculation messages offer a promising approach for boosting public resistance to disinformation, especially among those most vulnerable. The significance of classifying disinformation-susceptible publics was highlighted in this study, even though the term lacuna publics is relatively novel. Their distinct characteristics identify them as the primary audience for interventions aimed at inoculating against disinformation. These practical and theoretical insights are invaluable for public health practitioners, communicators, and policymakers seeking to mitigate the harm caused by disinformation and safeguard public well-being.
