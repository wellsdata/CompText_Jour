Introduction
The spread of online misinformation poses a significant global challenge, with the potential to fuel social disputes, normalize prejudice, and skew public perceptions of various societal groups. In Israel, where social and political divisions run deep, the proliferation of misinformation may intensify existing tensions, especially between the ultra-Orthodox (Haredi) and non-Haredi populations.
Haredi Jews account for 12.3% of the population of Israel (Regev and Miletzky 2024) and form a distinct fundamentalist religious and cultural group characterized by strict adherence to traditional practices, separation from secular society, and rapid population growth. Haredi’s daily life revolves around prayer (three times daily), Torah study for men, and strict Sabbath observance prohibiting technology use, driving, or handling money. Haredim primarily reside in enclaves, maintain Yiddish as their primary language, and practice strict gender segregation in educational and social settings (Chen and Li 2024). The tension between Haredi and non-Haredi Jews involves disputes around military service exemptions and economic burdens caused by the limited workforce participation of Haredi men (the Jewish People Policy Institute 2024; Malach and Cahaner 2024; US Department of State 2023). Although they are a relatively small minority segregated from the general population, Haredi parties wield significant, sometimes disproportionate, power in the political landscape (Chen and Li 2024), which increases the existing intergroup tension.
Cultural ghettos or walls are central in Haredi communities, as they help to push away non-religious influences and preserve purist religious life. Consequently, rabbis and community leaders generally reject the mass media and the Internet, as these are viewed as threats “from outside” to family and community values. As a result, most Haredi communities limit their use of the Internet and consistently show significantly lower levels of digital literacy than the non-Haredi population (Malach and Cahaner 2024). Use of the Internet is nonetheless expanding and is estimated to be around two-thirds; however, among those who access the Internet, a large majority use strict content filters and limit Internet use mostly for work or news consumption (Cohen 2023). These differing norms around technology use produce discrepancies in digital literacy levels and, as a result, may lead to variations in vulnerability to online misinformation. Although previous studies have examined how different demographic groups respond to false or misleading online content, the Haredi community remains underexplored. This lack of attention is striking, given the lower levels of Internet use and digital literacy in Haredi populations, and considering the possible influence of misinformation on political attitudes and intergroup tension in the context of the tension in Israeli society and the increasing political power of Haredi groups (Dödtmann 2023, Malach and Cahaner 2024; US Department of State 2023). Understanding how Haredi and non-Haredi populations scan and process online news can shed light on broader patterns of susceptibility to misinformation, offering valuable insights for policymakers, educators, and community leaders seeking to promote responsible online engagement.
This study compares Haredi and non-Haredi Jews in Israel in terms of their ability to detect misinformation and the ways in which they visually scan online content. Ultimately, we sought to determine whether differences in digital literacy and knowledge predict disparities in misinformation detection between these two populations. These findings have implications for designing targeted interventions, training programs, and policies that can bolster digital literacy and mitigate the risks posed by misinformation in a diverse society.
Literature Review
Misinformation (Fake News)
There are disagreements regarding the exact definition of the terms misinformation and fake news . Some definitions of fake news refer to inaccurate information that imitates other news media in format (Lazer et al. 2018; Quandt et al. 2019). Misinformation is often defined as the unintentional dissemination of false information (Cooke, 2018; Lazer et al. 2018), or spreading information in the form of satire and rumors (Tandoc Jr. et al. 2018). The literature distinguishes between “disinformation” which is deliberately spread by the sender, and “misinformation,” which is considered unintentional. For the purposes of this study, we adopted the term misinformation to include fake or made-up information that appears as authentic messages, to include messages that do not necessarily appear as news items, and that we cannot determine were created or disseminated with the intention to mislead.
The dissemination of misinformation to serve social, political, and economic interests is not a new phenomenon. Misinformation has influenced the processes and revolutions that took place in Europe as early as the 16 th and 18 th centuries, primarily during the French Revolution (Elyada 1991; Spencer 2007). Misinformation spreads more frequently and to a greater extent than “regular” news (DiFonzo et al. 2012; Vosoughi et al. 2018). The extent to which fake news spreads also affects how convincing these news items are, and studies have found that repeated exposure to a news item makes it more credible to viewers, even if it is baseless (Fazio et al. 2015; Pennycook et al. 2018). Changes in the media landscape, especially the introduction of social networks, have created opportunities for groups and individuals to easily spread misinformation to advance their objectives. Political and social actors spread misinformation to skew public opinion and influence political systems (Lees 2018; Levinson 2019; Marwick 2018; McNair 2017; Weimann 2019).
Various studies have examined the sociodemographic factors related to the spread of misinformation. Age was found to be a factor, with older people more likely to spread misinformation online. Other variables were studied but were not found to predict the spread of misinformation, including education, income (Guess et al. 2019), and gender (Guess et al. 2019; Rainie et al. 2017; Tripodi 2018). However, in another study, participants with lower levels of education were more likely to disseminate misinformation unintentionally (Steinfeld 2022a). A recent study examined the practices of intentionally and unintentionally disseminating misinformation and engagement in the prevention of misinformation among Israeli citizens. The study found a clear connection between spreading misinformation and combating it, both directly related to political activism. Political activists, especially those who participated in violent or extreme political activities, wage a disinformation war online, in which they implement practices of either spreading misinformation or fighting the spread of misinformation, depending on the alignment of the particular news item with their political views (Steinfeld 2022a).
The effects of misinformation
Misinformation is a cause for concern because of its real and potential consequences. Due to the increasing tendency of news consumers to rely on social media as their primary source of news (Newman et al. 2022; Steinfeld 2022b) and the increasing spread of false messages in social media, users are subject to persuasive manipulation, which may lead to behavioral changes. The global decline in institutional trust, especially in mainstream media (Strömbäck et al. 2020), is arguably an outcome of the increasing use of misinformation by politicians and public figures to discredit legitimate media channels (Das and Schroeder 2021; Lees 2018; McNair 2017) or public institutions (Levi 2018; Morgan 2018). These processes may threaten democratic systems (Morgan 2018; Reuter et al. 2022). A recent study shows that in Israel, a clear majority agrees that “false messages leave the public confused about basic facts” and that “the consequences of the phenomenon are serious” to a large or very large extent (Steinfeld 2022b). Similar results were reported in studies conducted in the US (Barthel et al. 2016), the UK, and Australia (Leung et al. 2021), as well as in various other countries worldwide (Newman et al. 2022).
Digital literacy and misinformation identification
The premise of this study is that spreading misinformation involves misconceptions regarding the credibility of the message among those who disseminate it. Therefore, it is important to examine the predictors of an individual’s ability to successfully identify misinformation. Research indicates that the most influential predictors of successfully recognizing misinformation are media literacy, or more specifically, digital literacy (Bulger and Davison 2018; Cooke 2018; Kahne and Bowyer 2017; Levi 2018; Vraga and Tully 2021). Although these two literacies are closely related, they have distinct emphases. Media literacy is described as the “ability of a citizen to access, analyze, and produce information for specific outcomes” (Aufderheide, 1993, p. 6), stressing critical thinking and the informed consumption of media content. Digital literacy extends media literacy by highlighting the ever-evolving nature of digital communication technologies and the participatory, interactive aspects of digital media consumption and production (Jones-Jang et al. 2021). Digital literacy has various definitions in the literature, although most include common components, such as knowledge of computers and the Internet, as well as technological skills enabling users to create, share, locate, evaluate, and use digital information (digital skill literacy or digital literacy skills; see Kim and Yang 2016; Reddy et al. 2020; Walton 2016). In a post-factual age, scholars recommend digital literacy education to integrate critical reading, thinking, and fact-checking practices, as well as awareness of data misuse (Breakstone et al. 2018; Cooke 2018; Engel 2017).
In recent years, the concept of data literacy has emerged as a specific offshoot of digital literacy, focusing on individuals’ ability to use data for everyday reasoning and problem-solving. Data literacy has increasingly been recognized as essential in today’s data-rich world, in which people frequently interact with data, make data-driven decisions, and consider how to use their personal data (Wolff et al. 2016). Carmi et al. (2020) argue that in an era marked by “information distortion,” it is necessary to revisit the meaning of data literacy and identify which skills, thought processes, and actions are required to handle misinformation and its impacts on citizens’ perceptions, decisions, and actions. Their proposed framework of Data Citizenship emphasizes critical thinking and personal agency in interpreting, using and giving meaning to data and data-related processes.
Media and digital literacy have thus emerged as central solutions for combating misinformation (Bulger and Davison 2018; Cooke 2018; Levi 2018). As this study examines how individuals view and scrutinize digital metadata, considered a literacy practice that is highly specific to the online environment- it primarily focuses on digital literacy, which is best suited for examining how people consume, create, and engage with digital information.
In the context of misinformation identification, digital literacy is operationalized through several key components: technical competence, such as familiarity with Internet-related terms, attitudes toward technology, and knowledge of how social media platforms function (Anthonysamy and Sivakumar 2024; Sirlin et al. 2021); cognitive literacy, such as the ability to understand, analyze, and critically evaluate information and source credibility (Anthonysamy and Sivakumar 2024; Qian et al. 2023); and information verification practices, such as using fact-checking tools and techniques (Qian et al. 2023).
The ultra-orthodox and digital literacy
This study examines digital literacy gaps between the ultra-Orthodox (Haredi) and non-Haredi Jewish populations in Israel as predictors of differences in successfully identifying misinformation. The premise of this study is that the digital divide between populations, expressed among others in the different levels of digital literacy between the Haredi and non-Haredi populations in Israel, leads to differences in the ability to successfully detect misinformation.
In 2015, a problem-solving test in computerized environments revealed that Israel had the widest digital gap among the OECD countries. The Arab and Haredi populations have significantly lower digital literacy levels than the non-Haredi Jewish population (Central Bureau of Statistics 2016). The Haredi community places emphasis on basic literacy skills (i.e., reading, writing, and understanding texts) by focusing in the early school years on reading fluency and on the preliminary study of Jewish texts (Barth et al. 2020). Due to significant differences between the education of Haredi boys and girls, different gender-based levels of literacy are evident; for boys, the educational emphasis is on preserving the ideal of a “society of learners,” in which men devote their lives to the Torah study. Accordingly, the Haredi education system for boys is geared towards this objective by focusing on basic literacy skills (Friedman 1991; Spiegel 2011). As a result, Haredi women are often the main providers of the family and are expected to support their husbands’ primary vocation as “Torah students.” Therefore, educational institutions for girls recognize that basic literacy alone is no longer sufficient to prepare them for their roles. Consequently, girls’ education combines Jewish and secular studies to facilitate their integration into the job market (Cohen and Barth 2018; Reiman 2012).
Despite this emphasis on women’s digital literacy, which will enable their integration into the workforce, various studies have found a relatively low ability to solve problems in an electronic environment among ultra-Orthodox men and women, which hinders their integration in the job market (Weiss, 2016). Therefore, despite having ostensibly acquired more digital expertise than “Torah Students,” many Haredi women seeking employment have not gained the necessary digital literacy to navigate the online world effectively and sufficiently.
Measuring digital literacy with eye tracking
Prior studies indicate that the eyes tend to fixate on salient or informative areas and that cognitive processes both shape and are shaped by ocular behavior. Consequently, cognitive psychologists regard eye movements as observable markers of cognitive processing and attention and ocular behavior as an overt measure of the mental selection process (Rayner 2009; Steinfeld and Shaked 2021; Steinfeld 2016; Steinfeld et al. 2016).
The main advantage of eye tracking as an objective measure is that it is not subject to a social desirability bias. Furthermore, it is a measurable, objective, and replicable observation tool that can provide qualitative and quantitative data on users’ webpage-scanning patterns. Recently, eye tracking has been introduced as an accurate, in-depth, and unbiased measurement of digital skills. Steinfeld et al. (2025) employed eye tracking to examine how participants in a public digital literacy program for disadvantaged populations scanned webpages. While the participants showed improvements, they were largely limited to basic tasks. More advanced skills, such as efficient online searching and sophisticated use of digital platforms, saw only slight improvement. Moreover, the participants’ gaze remained relatively unfocused, indicating continued difficulties in locating information and performing tasks.
This study examines the way in which participants scan a webpage containing information, especially focusing on metadata areas, that is, areas that contain information related to the item (“data about data,” Mayernik 2021), which includes cues attesting to the item’s credibility. In its basic form, metadata is information pertaining to, but not part of, the relevant content. In the context of misinformation identification, previous research points to various cues in metadata areas of social media posts and digital content as reliable and important indicators of post credibility, among them account bio and political affiliation (Haupt et al. 2021), account age, number of user friends/followers, post/tweet length, number of emojis, number of URLs contained in the post/tweet, use of exclamation or question marks, number of retweets/shares/likes or mentions (Ajao et al. 2022; Krishnan and Chen, 2018), comments and replies to the tweet/post text (Sharma et al. 2019), and image metadata (image geolocation and image credibility) (Safieddine et al. 2016).
Several recent studies have employed eye tracking to investigate misinformation. Fake news items were associated with increased eye regressions (Bozkir et al. 2022) and fixation count (Lutz et al. 2020). Misinformation-correcting tweets were found to draw greater attention to tweet images if they were humorous (Kim et al. 2021). Metadata was also the focus of Kießling et al. (2022). They investigated attention to Fake News flags and found that flags for news items received more visual attention than flags for users or sources. Their study aligns with the current study in exploring user attention to metadata, although it did not integrate user digital literacy and was limited specifically to flags.
Novelty and Contribution of the Study
Although previous studies have examined the relationship between digital literacy and the successful identification of misinformation, user attention to metadata as informative areas that attest to message credibility has received scant scholarly attention. In this study, we present a novel approach and methodology for examining user scanning patterns on websites and social media pages to assess the reliability of information, thereby contributing to our understanding of how users evaluate and identify misinformation. This study focuses on the ultra-orthodox population of Israel. Research on this population is relatively scarce, particularly regarding the use of digital platforms. Although the Internet is restricted in ultra-Orthodox society, mainly allowed for work purposes and prohibited for social uses and entertainment, its use is nevertheless expanding in this society (Cohen, 2023). Accordingly, exposure to risks, including the exposure to and influence of misinformation, also potentially increases. In this study, we sought to examine the ability of ultra-Orthodox to identify false messages and link this ability to digital literacy practices. The research findings could help in the design of policy and training programs for the ultra-Orthodox to improve their digital skills and critical consumption of information online.
Method
This study examines the digital literacy gaps between Haredi and non-Haredi Jews in Israel, as reflected in their ability to identify misinformation and the practices they use to evaluate the authenticity of online messages. Building on the body of research linking digital literacy to individuals’ ability to detect misinformation (Bulger and Davison 2018; Cooke 2018; Kahne and Bowyer 2017; Levi 2018; Vraga and Tully 2021), this study posits that differences in digital literacy can help explain variations in how diverse groups evaluate and identify false information online. Despite growing recognition of metadata as reliable and important indicators of post credibility (Ajao et al. 2022; Haupt et al. 2021; Krishnan and Chen 2018; Safieddine et al. 2016; Sharma et al. 2019), relatively little is known about how differences in scanning or attending to metadata may mediate the link between digital literacy and successful misinformation detection. Eye tracking research has shown that visual attention patterns can serve as reliable, objective markers of people’s cognitive processes when evaluating online content (Rayner 2009). The present study extends this approach by focusing on a culturally specific context (Haredi vs. non-Haredi Jews in Israel) where digital literacy gaps have been documented (Central Bureau of Statistics 2016; Weiss 2016). It seeks to determine whether these gaps, along with the ways in which participants attend to metadata, mediate group differences in misinformation detection.
To this end, the following hypotheses are examined:
H1. Differences in digital literacy will be found between Haredi and non-Haredi participants, with the former demonstrating lower digital literacy than the latter This prediction stems from studies documenting persistent digital divides in Israel, particularly among the Haredi population due to cultural restrictions on technology and education systems that deprioritize digital skills (Central Bureau of Statistics 2016; Cohen2023; Weiss 2016).
H2. Differences in the ability to identify misinformation will be found between Haredi and non-Haredi participants, with Haredi participants being less successful in identifying misinformation. This hypothesis builds on research linking lower digital literacy to increased vulnerability to misinformation (Bulger and Davison 2018; Cooke 2018), and on evidence that individuals with less formal education or digital exposure are more likely to unintentionally share false content (Steinfeld 2022a).
H3. Digital literacy will positively correlate with success in misinformation identification This expectation aligns with extensive research highlighting digital literacy—especially critical evaluation skills—as a key predictor of users’ ability to assess credibility and avoid being misled online (Bulger and Davison 2018; Cooke 2018; Kahne and Bowyer 2017; Levi 2018; Vraga and Tully 2021).
H4. The correlation between digital literacy and successful misinformation identification will be mediated by webpage scan patterns. Non-Haredi participants, with higher digital literacy, will dwell longer on webpage areas that provide credibility cues (the metadata areas), and consequently, will be more successful in identifying misinformation than Haredi participants. This hypothesis builds on eye tracking literature showing that gaze patterns reflect cognitive processing and attention allocation (Rayner 2009; Steinfeld 2016; Steinfeld and Shaked 2021), research linking digital literacy with effective information scanning (Steinfeld et al. 2025), and studies identifying metadata as key indicators of information credibility (Ajao et al. 2022; Haupt et al. 2021; Krishnan and Chen 2018; Safieddine et al. 2016; Sharma et al. 2019).
Participants
A total of 83 subjects aged 18–63 (M = 34.43, SD = 13.71) participated in the study. Of these, 45% ( N  = 37) were Haredi and 54% ( N  = 45) were non-Haredi (46% secular, 5% traditional, and 4% religious).1 One participant did not state his level of religiosity, and was excluded from the group comparison.
The mean age of the Haredi group was 31.64 years (range = 21–62, SD = 9.04) and of the non-Haredi group, 36 years (range = 18–63, SD = 15.99).2 51% of the participants were women (49% in the Haredi group, and 51% in the non-Haredi group).
Haredi participants were recruited from three Haredi workplaces in central Israel: two innovation centers that provide coworking spaces for Haredi freelance and business owners and a newspaper editorial office. We focused on working participants who are daily Internet users to ensure that the two groups would be comparable in their daily use and acquaintance with computers and the Internet. Non-Haredi participants were recruited from several workplaces and included additional volunteers. Recruitment was facilitated by personal connections between the research team and research assistants and with the help of the Haredi Institute for Public Affairs.
Materials
The research was conducted in two stages. First, participants were requested to perform a series of tasks on a computer while their eye movements were monitored. Upon completion, they were asked to complete the questionnaire.
Eye tracking
Participants’ eye movements were recorded using the SMI RED-m eye tracker, a non-invasive device mounted on a panel attached to a computer screen. The device uses an infrared eye camera to track both eyes and operates at a 120 Hz sampling rate, which is sufficient for capturing all visual fixations.
Participants were asked to assess the credibility of 11 news items, of which five were authentic and six were false, at their own pace. 3 The texts were presented in their organic environment, namely, the original tweet, Facebook page, or website on which they were initially published. This way, beside the contents of the texts in question, other aspects were presented that could provide information related to their authenticity (metadata), such as authors’ identity, links to familiar websites, mentions, shares and retweets, verification by the platforms (the blue V sign), a “forwarded many times” warning, comments by other users etc. Participants were asked to rate the items on a scale from 1 (definitely false) to 10 (definitely true). A “Fake Test” score was calculated for each participant according to their rating of the items. A higher test score indicates better success in identification (the lowest possible score was 11 and the highest was 110). Test scores ranged from 45 to 107 (M = 81.98, Mdn = 84, SD = 12).
Questionnaire
The post-session questionnaire comprised 25 questions measuring participants’ digital literacy, social media use, prior experience with online misinformation, political orientation, activism, efficacy, and demographics. These items were included because prior research has linked such factors to misinformation detection (e.g., Bulger and Davison 2018; Cooke 2018; Guess et al. 2019; Kahne and Bowyer 2017; Levi 2018; Steinfeld 2022a; Vraga and Tully 2021).
Digital literacy and Internet use were assessed through several series of questions. Because self-assessment of competencies may be subject to bias, we combined questions measuring experience with capital-enhancing activities, confidence in performing tasks, and knowledge of technological concepts. This approach covers various approaches to measuring digital literacy and skills based on self-reports (Hargittai and Dobransky, 2017). - Capital-enhancing activities frequencies (combined and adapted from Hargittai and Dobransky 2017; Van Deursen et al. 2012; Lev-On et al. 2022): four indices were calculated regarding the frequency of carrying out various activities online: making decisions and consuming news, paying bills and searching for health information online, searching and social media, gaming and installations. Activity frequency was rated on a scale from 1 = never to 5 = daily. These capital-enhancing activities are closely tied to vulnerability to misinformation, as they often require individuals to critically evaluate the accuracy and credibility of online information in order to make informed decisions, particularly in domains such as news consumption, health information, and financial transactions. As such, they are highly relevant to research on misinformation detection and digital literacy.

- Knowledge of technological concepts (adapted from Hargittai and Hsieh 2012): the participants were asked to rate their familiarity with six Internet concepts on a scale from 1 = not familiar at all to 5 = very familiar.

- Confidence in performing tasks (adapted from Bunz, 2004): the participants were asked to rate the extent to which they felt confident in their ability to perform various tasks online on a scale of 1 = not at all confident to 5 = highly confident.

Perceived ability to identify misinformation (Steinfeld 2022a): the participants were asked to rate their ability to identify “fake news” and whether they estimated that they were better than others at doing so, on a scale from 1 = to a great degree, to 5 = not at all.

Findings
Digital literacy (H1)
No significant differences were found between the two groups (Haredi and non-Haredi) regarding the measures of activity frequency, except for installations and gaming. The Haredi participants reported a lower frequency of performing this activity (mean = 2.07, std = 0.68) compared with the non-Haredi participants (mean = 2.43, std = 0.95) (t (78.55)  = 2.03, p  < 0.05). Furthermore, no significant differences were found between the groups in terms of participants’ confidence in performing the tasks.
Despite both groups reporting similar patterns of Internet use, differences were found in their knowledge of technological concepts (technological knowledge). The Haredi group’s familiarity with concepts was significantly lower (mean = 2.75, std = 1.14) than that of the non-Haredi group (mean = 3.44, std = 0.99) (t (80)  = 1.96, p  < 0.01).
These findings partially support Hypothesis H1 only regarding the knowledge variable and the frequency of games and installations. Other digital literacy variables did not differ between the groups.
Misinformation (H2)
Assessing the ability to identify misinformation
No differences were found between the groups in the participants’ self-assessments of their ability to successfully identify misinformation, both in their own assessed ability and their assessment of success relative to others.
Sharing misinformation
The Haredi participants reported spreading false information unknowingly (mean = 1.86, std = 0.93) more often than the non-Haredi participants (mean = 1.45, std = 0.71) (t (76)  = 2.20, p  < 0.05).
Success in identifying misinformation
The Haredi participants had significantly poorer results on the misinformation detection test, scoring an average of 9.5 points lower (mean = 77, std = 12.85) than the non-Haredi participants (mean = 86.6, std = 9) (t (63.51)  = 3.80, p  < 0.001).
Due to the way the score was calculated, one can presume that the differences lie partly in the fact that the Haredi participants tended to rate the items less decisively, choosing scores closer to the mid-scale, possibly because they felt less confident in their assessment of the news items’ authenticity. To test this possibility, a confidence rating index was calculated so that each rating was scored according to the degree of certainty (i.e., responses at the extreme ends of the scale, 1 or 10, indicating complete confidence in the answer, received the highest score; responses near the middle, 5 or 6, received the lowest score).
A t-test for independent samples showed that the differences between Haredi and non-Haredi participants in the degree of confidence in the news items’ authenticity were not significant. This means that, on average, the Haredi group was not more or less confident in responses than the non-Haredi group.
These findings support Hypothesis H2: the ultra-Orthodox participants were less successful in identifying fake messages.
Page scanning
This study found significant differences between the two groups in several measures relating to how they scanned metadata areas. Table 2 summarizes these differences.
Analysis of the data obtained from the eye tracking device showed that the Haredi participants scanned the items’ metadata areas significantly less than the non-Haredi participants. The number of fixations and relative dwell time in the metadata areas were significantly lower for the Haredi group, and the relative fixation durations were marginally significantly lower in that group. In addition, the average time spent on the items’ webpages (social media pages, news website pages etc.) was lower among the Haredi participants than among the non-Haredi participants. Predicting success in identifying misinformation (H3, H4)
Pearson correlation tests revealed a significant correlation between technological knowledge (as a measure of digital literacy) and gazing at metadata. Specifically, there was a significant correlation between technological knowledge and the number of fixations (r = 0.27, p  < 0.05), fixation duration (r = 0.30, p  < 0.01), and dwell time in these areas (r = 0.29, p < 0.01).
A significant correlation was also found between fixation duration (r = 0.23, p  < 0.05) and dwell time (r = 0.23, p  < 0.05) in the metadata areas, and success in misinformation identification (fake test score). Finally, a significant correlation was found between technological knowledge (as a measure of digital literacy) and success in misinformation identification (r = 0.38, p  < 0.001), confirming Hypothesis H3 .
However, the mediation hypothesis H4 , which proposed that better success in identifying misinformation among non-Haredi participants is mediated by dwelling in the metadata areas, was not supported 4. In other words, Haredi participants’ technological knowledge was significantly lower than that of non-Haredi participants; they focused significantly less on metadata areas and were less successful in identifying misinformation. Technological knowledge and dwelling on metadata areas predict success in identifying misinformation, but do not mediate the relationship between belonging to the Haredi group and success in misinformation identification.
Finally, to examine the interaction between belonging to the Haredi group, familiarity with technology, dwell time in metadata areas, and misinformation identification, a regression test was performed. The model included the demographic variables, belonging to the Haredi group, previous experience with online misinformation, and variables that individually correlated significantly with successful misinformation identification: the number of social networks the participant uses, technological knowledge, perceived ability to detect misinformation, and gaze measures of metadata areas. We added interaction terms for the interaction between being Haredi, knowledge of technological terms, and metadata dwell time. Adding the interaction terms resulted in a decreased model fit and Adjusted R 2 (ΔR 2  = 0.008, F-change (3,40)  = 0.28, p  = 0.84). Therefore, we excluded interaction terms in the final model. We tested multiple models and selected the one that offered the best balance of fit and predictive power, as indicated by the highest adjusted R 2 and the model F-statistic.
The model explains 51% of the variance in the fake-test score (F (14,43)  = 5.68, p  < 0.001, R 2  = 0.52). Among all the variables entered, the significant predictors of the test score were technological knowledge (β = 0.0.45, p  < 0.001), number of social networks (β = 0.29, p  < 0.01), income (β = 0.25, p  < 0.05) and dwell time in metadata areas (β = 0.24, p  < 0.05). Ultra-Orthodoxy was not a significant predictor in the regression model. Table 3 summarized the regression results.
Summary and Discussion
The misinformation phenomenon has significant and extensive implications and may affect attitudes and beliefs pertaining to population groups, processes, individuals, and institutions (Benkler et al. 2017; Greenhill and Oppenheim 2017). The ability to identify misinformation is a critical aspect of digital literacy (Bulger and Davison 2018; Cooke 2018; Levi 2018). This study examines one dimension of digital literacy—namely, the ability to identify misinformation—by comparing the performance of Haredi and non-Haredi Jews in Israel.
The findings reveal that there are indeed differences between Haredi and non-Haredi groups regarding the detection of misinformation. Objective measurements (via eye-tracking) show that Haredi participants were less attentive to metadata areas containing information about the message examined, including cues on its authenticity and credibility, and were less successful in identifying misinformation, whereas both groups assessed their own abilities to detect false content at similarly high levels. This disparity between perceived competence and actual performance, particularly in the Haredi sample suggests an overconfidence effect, aligned with the Dunning-Kruger effect (Kruger and Dunning 1999), which suggests that people lacking expertise in a given domain tend to overestimate their competence. The higher frequency of self-reported unintentional misinformation sharing among Haredi participants further highlights this gap between subjective assessments and real-world behavior.
Nevertheless, statistical analysis indicates that once factors such as actual digital knowledge, social network usage, attention to metadata, and income are accounted for, belonging to the Haredi group is in itself no longer a significant predictor of misinformation-detection success. This finding implies that differences in digital literacy—rather than purely cultural identity—underlie the variance in participants’ ability to spot misinformation. Rather than attributing differences solely to cultural or religious identity, the focus shifts to developing and evaluating one’s practical digital skills—a set of competencies that can, in principle, be taught, measured, and improved. However, the broader implications of these results should be considered cautiously, as the study’s sample is culturally specific and may not reflect other populations or religious communities.
Theoretical and Practical Contributions
A key theoretical contribution of this study lies in the introduction of psychophysiological measurements—namely, eye-tracking—into digital-literacy or skill assessment. While previous research has employed various subjective (e.g., self-assessment of skills, experience with capital-enhancing activities and confidence in performing tasks) and objective methods (e.g., knowledge quizzes, performance tests, direct observations) to evaluate digital competence, psychophysiological approaches have seldom been proposed in this field. Eye-tracking data offers critical insights into the cognitive processes underlying misinformation detection by capturing how participants allocate visual attention. Specifically, dwell times and fixation patterns on metadata areas can be interpreted as external indicators of deeper cognitive engagement with this content. Longer dwell times and concentrated fixations suggest that readers are scrutinizing credibility cues, reflecting a more effortful analytical approach that aligns with higher-order cognitive skills, such as critical thinking and fact-checking. These patterns are consistent with previous findings linking increased eye regressions and fixations to fake news items (Bozkir et al. 2022; Lutz et al. 2020), underscoring how visual attention mirrors the mental effort expended to evaluate message credibility.
From a psychological standpoint, directing attention to metadata supports the formation of accurate credibility assessments because it prompts active evaluation of source reliability. Eye-tracking measures offer a “window” into participants’ cognitive strategies (Rayner 2009; Steinfeld 2016; Steinfeld and Shaked 2021) and reveal whether individuals systematically scan credibility markers or merely skim superficial cues. Such data provides valuable insights into deeper cognitive and behavioral processes that may not be evident through self-reports alone.
In addition, although previous studies have examined the relationship between digital literacy and misinformation identification, user attention to metadata as an essential indicator of message credibility has received scant scholarly attention. By presenting a novel approach and methodology to examine user-scanning patterns on websites and social media pages, this study advances our understanding of how users evaluate and identify misinformation in real time. Such a focus on metadata scrutiny—an often overlooked but highly informative component—broadens the scope of digital literacy research and highlights new avenues for exploring how specific online behaviors affect misinformation detection.
The choice to focus on the ultra-Orthodox population in Israel further underscores the study’s originality. Research on this group’s internet usage remains relatively scarce, especially regarding daily interactions with digital platforms. Although the Internet is formally restricted in many ultra-Orthodox communities—where it is primarily allowed for work purposes and prohibited for leisure or social uses—its adoption is rapidly expanding (Cohen 2023). This growth raises the potential for greater exposure to misinformation, thus elevating the importance of studying how the ultra-Orthodox evaluate and navigate online information. By investigating their ability to detect false messages and connecting it to digital literacy practices, this research offers culturally specific insights that can guide policy and tailored training programs aimed at improving both digital skills and critical consumption of information in this unique context.
From a practical standpoint, these findings suggest a clear need for interventions that address the disconnect between subjective confidence and objective competence. For instance, policy initiatives or educational programs could train users—particularly in the ultra-Orthodox community—to recognize, interpret, and rely on metadata cues as part of a structured approach to verifying content authenticity. More broadly, disseminating these skills and reinforcing critical consumption of online information can help mitigate vulnerabilities to misinformation within populations that have recently expanded their digital presence but may lack formal digital literacy education. Ultimately, by combining psychophysiological measurement tools with a culturally aware approach, this study offers a novel, replicable framework that can inform targeted strategies for reducing misinformation exposure across diverse communities.
Study findings in relation to existing literature
The study’s findings align with earlier work indicating that subjective confidence in digital abilities does not necessarily predict actual performance (Ben-Amram 2018; Gross and Latham 2009). More broadly, this dynamic mirrors the well-known Dunning-Kruger effect (Kruger and Dunning 1999). By focusing on a religious minority community—often overlooked in digital literacy research—the present study expands our understanding of how cultural factors may intersect with online behaviors.
Furthermore, although some research identifies lower levels of online engagement in Haredi communities (Weiss 2016), the present sample suggests that Haredim who actively work in computerized environments exhibit browsing frequencies comparable to non-Haredim. This finding underscores that increased Internet exposure does not necessarily translate to advanced digital literacy skills, thereby reinforcing the importance of targeted efforts to equip new or less experienced users with the tools to critically assess online information.
These findings also contribute to ongoing discussions about how fake news and misinformation thrives in environments where audiences lack robust digital literacy (Bozkir et al. 2022; Lutz et al. 2020). The eye-tracking data reveal a tangible link between visual attention to credibility markers (e.g., metadata) and success in detecting misinformation. Equipping individuals with strategies to identify such markers could therefore serve as an important safeguard against both accidental and intentional misinformation dissemination.
From an audience perspective, it is crucial to recognize that even groups adopting the Internet at an accelerated pace might underestimate their vulnerability to misleading content. By demonstrating how a community’s confidence can diverge from actual skill, this study points to a broader need for digital literacy programs that combine awareness-building with practical skill development in evaluating sources and verifying online claims.
Disparities in digital literacy and potential consequences
The findings suggest that Haredi participants—due to lower digital literacy skills—could be at higher risk of being influenced by misinformation. Such susceptibility has public and societal ramifications, including the potential for deepening social rifts, harming social cohesion (Schulman and Siman-Tov 2020), or encouraging skepticism toward legitimate information sources. In this regard, it is worth noting that there was a significant difference in primary source for news consumption between the study groups (χ2 = 17.15, p  < 0.01). 40% of the non-Haredi group reported television as their primary source for news consumption, followed by news websites (36%) and social networks (18%). In the non-Haredi group, television was a negligible source for news (5%) and instead, this group reported greater reliance on news websites (65%) and social networks (27%) for news consumption. This reliance on online sources for news among Haredim emphasizes the importance of enhancing digital literacy and misinformation detection skills in this population, as they greatly rely on these sources for news. Moreover, the inability to identify misinformation may also indicate a difficulty in critical reading skills and in detecting other problematic texts, such as phishing messages and various other cybercrimes, thus rendering this population more vulnerable to possible harm on the Internet.
At the same time, it is essential to note that the Haredi participants in this study represent a subset of the community with relatively high employment rates and computer usage. More conservative Haredi groups that maintain limited internet use might face additional or distinct challenges, an avenue future research could explore.
Implications for policy and intervention
The study’s findings highlight the importance of tailored interventions that address both subjective perception and objective performance. Initiatives could focus on training users to locate and examine metadata thoroughly, employing culturally suitable content and delivery methods. Especially in communities like the Haredi population, where participants may not fully recognize their own skill deficits, awareness campaigns emphasizing the risks of misinformation—and the value of vigilant online behavior—could prove particularly effective.
Moreover, policymakers interested in fostering digital inclusion might consider adopting psychophysiological assessment tools (such as eye-tracking) in program evaluations or pilot studies. Such tools can help reveal subtle but critical gaps in how information is processed, guiding the development of more impactful digital literacy curricula. In this study, by bridging technical results with underlying psychological processes, we could better understand why digital literacy interventions that encourage active scanning of metadata are essential for enhancing misinformation-detection skills, particularly among populations with limited technological familiarity.
Conclusion
A combination of subjective and objective indicators reinforces the understanding that the lower digital literacy of the Haredi participants forms the basis of their reduced ability to detect misinformation. Critically, this gap persists despite their increasing reliance on the Internet. By integrating psychophysiological measures with self-reported data, the study illuminates the cognitive and behavioral processes at play when users confront questionable information. Although the findings should be interpreted cautiously in view of the sample’s cultural specificity, they underscore the broader point that digital literacy training—specifically tailored to a community’s context—can play a pivotal role in mitigating misinformation and promoting greater societal cohesion. Ultimately, closing the gap between perceived and actual online competence is an important social goal, not only for the Haredi community but also for other populations grappling with the rapid spread of digital misinformation.
Research Limitations and Follow-Up Studies
Owing to this study’s focus on a culturally and religiously specific population, its broader generalizability is limited. Future research could investigate other marginalized or minority groups in varying global contexts to determine whether similar disparities between self-assessed and actual digital literacy appear. The Haredi group participating in the study was characterized by high digital literacy relative to the general Haredi population because the participants work in computerized environments, whereas members of other groups in Haredi society either do not work or work in traditional, non-technological environments. This means that the differences found in this study between the Haredi and non-Haredi groups will probably increase when examining more conservative Haredi populations with lower digital literacy levels. To examine this issue in depth, additional research should be conducted with Haredi groups that do not work (for example, Torah students) or have less exposure to computerized, online environments to identify the extent of their exposure to misinformation and their ability to detect it, and to examine options and suitable tools for outreach efforts among these groups. In addition, as with any experimental research in laboratory conditions, it is possible that the presence of the researchers in the lab influenced, to some extent, the behavior of the participants in the study and led to a heightened desire to demonstrate knowledge and a higher response speed. Finally, this study focuses on the ability to detect misinformation. Follow-up studies can employ the methods developed in this study to examine the ability to identify other texts, diverse and broader digital skills, and the extent of their influence on individual decision-making in the context of other risky online behaviors, in economic, security, and personal contexts.
