Introduction In the modern contemporary world, the digital environment has transformed the patterns of distribution, consumption, and opposition of health-related information: social media 1 , in particular, X as a source of real-time information, has become one of the platforms where millions of people follow breaking news, scientific discovery, and personal accounts 2 . Although this democratization of communication has made awareness very swift and easy to engage in, it has been coupled with a breeding ground of the inability to control the spread of misinformation, and in most instances 3,4 , unverified or even purposefully falsified information spreads further and faster than verified medical knowledge. This was made revoltingly obvious during the COVID-19 pandemic, when conspiracy theories, false cures, and vaccine hesitancy all thrived on social media platforms, undermining trust in government, causing panic, and in a few extreme instances having a literal, detrimental impact on health policy 5 . The dynamics of COVID-19 have also been seen to be not limited to the viral outbreak rather exhibiting a troubling trend in the overall trajectory of a global health crisis where misinformation in the form of stigmatized narratives to wrong information about transmission and infection prevention spready very quickly on digital platforms, as seen in the more recent outbreak of the monkeypox virus 6 . How to cope with misleading misinformation in a hyper-connected world in an ecosystem where mechanisms of amplification like retweet and hashtag increase the rate of viral spread of content being shared, what should we do when the line between credible updates and damaging misinformation becomes rather thin 7 , with the World Health Organization even terms this a parallel phenomenon to an infodemic. The risk of such an informational epidemic is not merely to mislead the community but also to distort health behavior, fuel stigma, and impede proper response to the crisis 8 , which is why it is not merely an academic fancy or a fancy of the crusader, but a truly global public health priority to develop effective computational engines against misinformation detection 9 .
Traditional methods of combating misinformation detection have undergone a massive transformation over the last decade, starting with rule-based systems and pre-existing ML algorithms that did not rely heavily on hand-made lexical, syntactic, or statistical features 10 . The conventional methodology proved to be of some insight, even though it failed in its adaptability to the dynamic, informal, and cacophonous communication of the social media discourse 11,12 . The appearance of deep learning (DL) frameworks was a significant shift, with transformer-based networks like BERT and RoBERTa reaping tremendous success in the task of modeling the contextual semantics and progressing along the axis of natural language processing, including sentiment analysis, stance detection, and fake news prediction 13,14 . The models excel at more subtle meaning extraction in a text because they take into consideration bi-directional context, and thus can surpass traditional classifiers that considered language to be a bag of random words 15 . Nevertheless, in spite of these advantages, transformers remain naturally weaker at modeling sequential or temporal dependencies over longer spans of the discourse, which is an important aspect in identifying misinformation that can spread slowly through a conversation, retweet, or narrative change 16 . Recurrent neural networks, and in specific Gated Recurrent Units (GRUs), on the other hand, are specifically built to remember temporalities and also to capture dependencies across the sequences, thus are super weapons in terms of capturing the flow and build-up of the narrative 17 . Nevertheless, these types of models can not usually be in a position to capture the deep contextual insights and semantic interactions as those naturally offered by transformers by diminishing the text to linear tendencies, lacking the comprehension of the fuller meaning 18 . As such, approaches that have been developed to detect misinformation prioritize one or the other determinant of semantic depth or sequential continuity, and not both, creating a vacancy in the design of misinformation detection systems 19 . This disparity is even more urgent in the realm of health-related falsehoods, where both situational subtlety, e.g., the ability to parse satire compared to deleteriously inaccurate assertions, and temporal precedence, e.g., the process of how stories unfold within threads or viral cascades, are also important.
Adding to this difficulty is the quality of the data available, which, though useful, is limited in volume and is noisy and variable in the definition of classes 20 . Usually, a typical annotation scheme on datasets involves the utilization of a binary classification (such as misinformation or other) in the case of monkeypox-related tweets, an example of a dataset to annotate. This generalization can be seen as an effort to make the task of doing classification less cumbersome; however, the so-called other category is a form of heterogeneity and will include things that are either neutral, extraneous, or indeterminate. This heterogeneity allows label noise, and models find it hard to distinguish reliably and form information between text that is truly informative and text that is non-informative 21 . As a result, there is a risk of defeating performance, specifically in situations when inaccurate trends in the distribution of the other class are overfit. Collectively, these limitations highlight the current research problem on the need to develop a semantically-rich and time-sensitive misinformation detection system that is resilient to both label noise and class imbalance, exemplifying real-world health communication data.
It is within this context that the motivation behind our research question is evident, where textual misinformation detection systems have not been able to completely balance both the requirement of deep contextual learning and a capacity to model dependencies following a sequence in an ongoing online discussion 19,22 . That failure is particularly stark in the area of high-stakes memes like public health, where the risks of getting it wrong are high-vaccination reluctance, stigmatization of groups, a lag on preventive actions, and damaging trust in scientific expertise. So, the key question we face will be how to design a misinformation detection system that not only performs well at detecting false narratives with a high level of accuracy but also reflects robustness to the ambiguities of annotation, as well as the noise and volatility of social media data.
This study aims to overcome this challenge by proposing a hybrid DL architecture that combines the complementary advantages of transformer-based and recurrent neural architectures. We laid out a system that integrates the rich representational capabilities of RoBERTa with the modeling of sequential dependencies of GRU to create a model that is optimally positioned to identify misinformation in a rich dataset. The practical and scientific motivations of our work are as follows: at a pragmatic level, the model can potentially enable policymakers, health communicators, and social media operators to mitigate the effect of misinformation in extreme health crises; the scientific contribution pertains to the frontier of natural language processing because it suggests a hybrid solution that could effectively mediate between two separate paradigms of modeling by setting an intermediate example to be followed. The testing of this model on a closely annotated dataset of monkeypox-associated tweets, both under a binary and a ternary labeling scheme, will enable us to formally evaluate the effectiveness and robustness of this model, as well as reflect on the ambiguity/overlapping nature of the classes.

Literature review The spread of misinformation and false news, especially with regard to health crises and political occurrences, has been of significant interest to the research community in recent years. The scientific community has studied various computational and theoretical methods to identify and analyze misinformation, including sentiment analysis and topic models, as well as more complex ML and DL methods. These works not only outline the trend in methodological advances in fake news detection but also discuss the problems of scalability, the applicability in different languages, and interpretability. This review provides a synthesis of major contributions in various areas with a focus on sentiment-based tracking of misinformation sources, hybrid classification schemes, and the application of AI on real-life deployment of real-world solutions.
Morita et al. 23 proposed U-MAS, a big data processing pipeline that combines LDA, sentiment analysis, and a misinformation classifier to track health-based misinformation in X. The system was good at handling large-scale data and offered intuitive dashboards with a correlation of a classifier of 0.82. Nevertheless, use of expert-specified queries, relatively low topic unity (0.54), and evaluation measures emphasize the requirement of more expounded validation, multilingual implementation, and desirable modelling processes.
Hajli et al. 24 discussed the empowering difference of social bots programmed by AI in X, where they propagate misinformation within the network, with actor-network theory (ANT) as their theoretical tool. They tested 30,000 tweets, using text mining, text preprocessing, and a bag-of-words text model, in order to illustrate the possibility of early discovery of the harmful bots. Although the study provides a new theory-driven insight, a simple bag-of-words representation does not provide linguistic richness, and applying the study to the study of English-only tweets excludes its further generalization.
Dedeepya et al. 25 suggested a hybrid detector of fake news on social media, a combination of SVM and KNN, with the inclusion of the extra classifiers (RF, LR, CART, NNET), and the focus on the feature set that represents the behavior of users, the content, and the dynamics of the social network. Cross-validation was robust, and detection performance was better than traditional approaches. Although this model is good at bringing linguistic and social context into play, the model is complex and thus cannot be scaled easily, and performance on multilingual or cross-platform datasets is not evaluated.
Hasan et al. 26 presented a Stochastic Gradient Descent-based Ridge Classifier (SGDR) with complex string processing to classify news with sentiment analysis. The model handled both structured and unstructured data with an accuracy of 98.12% and a higher standard as compared to the KNN, Decision Tree, and AdaBoost baselines. Although very accurate and resource-efficient in low-resource settings, the method has been applied exclusively to English language headlines, and results on multilingual or highly noisy data sets have not been evaluated.
Akhtar et al. 27 proposed an AI- and ML-enabled structure of fake news and disinformation (FNaD) detection in the context of reducing supply chain disruptions, with case studies on Indonesia, Malaysia, and Pakistan. The model yielded promising performance with regard to metrics including MRR (0.663 train, 0.647 test), P@5, and NDCG@5, making it have practical applicability in a managerial decision-making context. Weaknesses comprise a lack of geographic diversity, a possible bias present in the dataset, and the necessity to assess on a wider scale of sectors and languages.
Ahammad 28 applied sentiment analysis with topic modeling (TM) to assess the COVID-19 misinformation on 10,254 news headlines. LDA also surpassed NMF and LSA with coherence scores of − 0.66 and 0.573, respectively, and showed key topics such as vaccines, crime, quarantine, and politics. The study can miss fine-grained and multilingual misinformation due to limitations related to the use of English headlines and rule-based sentiment labels as a means of identifying misinformation.
Hashmi et al. 29 implemented a multimedia-aware framework of detecting fake news that uses FastText embeddings and integrates hybrid CNN-LSTM models and transformer-based models (BERT, XLNet, RoBERTa). Its methodology led to excellent accuracy and F1-scores (0.979–0.99) on WELFake, FakeNewsNet, and FakeNewsPrediction datasets. Interpretable AI (LIME) and LDA were used in decision interpretation. Computational complexity of the model, although not a significant issue in high performance, complicates application in the conditions of limited resources, but evaluation with evolving, multilingual, or noisy content that is media-rich is still limited.
Gupta et al. 30 examined the COVID-19 infodemic with a three-fronted methodology that involved LDA topic modeling, sentiment/emotion analysis, and network analytics (page rank, betweenness, eccentricity, closeness). The research showed that fake news elicited comparatively stronger emotions, such as anger and fear, than did real news, which offers some guidance in the design of credible fake news detection and recommendation engines. The weaknesses are that they are dependent on English-written press; the dataset contains biases, and its usability to other programs of social media programs or changing misinformation trends is limited.
Joshi et al. 31 suggested a misinformation detection framework that can be both described and applied to various domains, based on the use of Domain Adversarial Neural Networks (DANN) in conjunction with LIME to provide interpretability. The model outperformed F1-score, accuracy (+ 3%), and AUC (+ 9%) in a case of domain shifts, thus being robust across a variety of social media and COVID-19 misinformation, as presented case. At a disadvantage are the use of English-language datasets and the possibility of performance degradation in highly noisy or multilingual data, and complexity in large-scale deployment.
Polu 32 suggested a fake news detection system based on DL developed on the basis of transformer networks (BERT, RoBERTa, XLNet), graph-based contextual analysis, and explainable AI (XAI) to evaluate the interpretability. The highest accuracy was observed within the LIAR, FakeNewsNet, and BuzzFeedNews datasets on RoBERTa with 96.2%. The significance of features of semantics, style, and discourse was discussed, as well as measures of adversarial robustness. They are also limited by their computational intensity, the English-language nature of the datasets, and little testing on dynamic or multilingual misinformation.
Zamil and Charkari 33 offered a multimodal framework to detect fake news, where they combine text (Electra, XLNet) and image (EfficientNetB0 + ELA) features using LIME, so they can be explained easily. The model was tested on CASIA, MediaEval, and Weibo data and exhibited elevated reliability and explainability in fake news verification in the social networks. Among its limitations, high computational cost, possible dependence on the quality of datasets, and unsatisfactory testing on multilingual or changing misinformation situations can be distinguished.
Jahanbakhsh et al. 34 applied a more customized AI model that teaches the machine to understand the subjective evaluations of social media posts with the idea of guessing how the user might react to misinformation. The model is further updated, using user responses, in an iterative process, and displays that the AI predictions do have the ability to affect the judgment of the users, but that reasoning alleviates bias. They include the scalability to use with large numbers of users, the risk of overfitting based on individuals’ tastes, and a limited scope since the algorithms can only be tested on datasets inside of X.
Kozik et al. 35 suggested a hybrid model based on the meta-learning framework to provide fake news detection through the combination of multiple smaller NLP-based models rather than retraining a single, very large model across each domain. The method can improve the inter-domain generalization and reduce retraining. Drawbacks are that it may be complex to use models, being dependent on English datasets, and that it is likely to be difficult to scale to multilingual or very diverse content.
Bagozzi et al. 36 proposed supervised ML methods to detect misinformation propagated in X based on a large volume of state-based misinformation by Russian sources. With the incorporation of topical-tactics into the classification techniques, the authors have better detection as the optimal random forests and logistic regression yielded the superior results.
Setiawan et al. 37 concentrated on the emergence of fake news on social media networks such as Facebook and X. They suggested an ML optimization approach using NLP-based characteristics and utilized a hybrid SVM-based classification. The accuracy of their method was 91.23%, demonstrating good performance in textual FND. Nonetheless, the data are one-dimensional, involving only the text form, and there is no multimodal and cross-domain generalization possible.
Bhardwaj, Bharany, and Kim 38 investigated how to detect fake news and spam activity in social media through emotion-based sentiment analysis. In the proposed system, the identified content entities are rated to determine fake posts and bot accounts and displayed with an accuracy of 99.68%, which is more efficient than similar techniques. Although the study is limited to the text by lacking the measurement of multimodal data, the potential of automated sentiment-based methods of detecting large-scale misinformation is evident.
Sadiq, Aljrees, and Ullah 39 came up with a model based on CNN and FastText embeddings to identify human-versus-bot-produced tweets in social media. The research refers to the increasing problem of deepfakes and machine-generated content generated by such sites as X. They benchmarked their method against the conventional ML models, other DL architectures, as LSTM and CNN-LSTM, and found performance to be better than those. Experiment insights showed that the combination of CNN-FastText is precise in categorizing deepfake tweets, thus indicating potential efficacy in identifying deepfake tweets.
Hamed, Aziz, and Yaakub 40 suggested a model of fake news detection based on BiLSTM, which takes into account sentiment analysis of the content of the news along with emotion analysis of the comments of its followers. The model successfully notifies the publisher and crowd standpoints in the Fakeddit dataset, thereby increasing the accuracy of detection. This strategy gained an AUC of 96.77%, which exceeds the traditional approaches. It is, however, confined to one data set and fails to consider cross-platform or even multilingual situations.
Edinger et al. 41 employed a DL pipeline to study the proliferation of empirically unfounded claims about mpox on social media. S-BERT embeddings were used along with PCA and UMAP to reduce dimensionality, and then a k-means cluster analysis method was used to discover seven thematic clusters. Findings emphasized that deceptive or non-relevant messages travelled harder and far more than official messages. The methodology has the advantages of paying attention to the real-time-based monitoring, but is discriminative of both the English-speaking tweets and just one health event.
Alkhaldi et al. 42 constructed a deep-learning framework called SFODLD-SAC that uses sentiment analysis to study the emotions of people on X during the COVID-19 pandemic. The model has a cascaded RNN with TF-IDF feature extraction coupled with hyperparameter optimization performed with the Sunflower Optimization algorithm. The experiments have shown a highly accurate study on a Kaggle benchmark dataset, with results of 99.65%. Weaknesses are that they had to rely on tweets in English and the possible bias of the dataset based on social media user demographics.
Raza et al. 43 implemented FakeWatch, an election-based fake news detector based on ML traditional models and enhanced LMs. They demonstrated using a curated North American dataset that LMs have a slightly higher level of accuracy, yet classical models such as logistic regression and random forests retain their competitive edge because of their efficiency. The publicly searchable data set and predictive models they offer offer a scalable benchmark on which future misinformation studies can be developed.
Although much progress has been made, the literature reviewed shows that there are some important limitations. Most studies are limited to the English language datasets, making them not that generalized between multilingual and cross-cultural settings. A number of methods exclusively prioritize accuracy over applicability, interpretability, and stability to changing misinformation patterns. Besides, deployment in low-resource environments is hindered by computational intensity and biases of the dataset. On balance, although these studies provide a good ground for misinformation identification, in future studies, special attention will need to be paid to language and modality diversity and adaptivity approaches. Methodology This work proposes an interpretable text classification system with a multi-step and structured interpretation presented to harmonize contextual transformer-based text feature representation with sequential dependencies in a hybrid architecture. This is done by first carefully preprocessing the dataset through the preprocessing and conversion of raw forms of textual data into contextualized token embeddings in a pre-trained RoBERTa model to provide strong semantic expression. These embedding representations are subsequently fed to a GRU-based recurrent module so as to learn some temporal order and interaction among tokens within the sequence. In order to improve performance further, an end-to-end model is also designed that combines a transformer encoder with a recurrent unit, trained jointly with a single optimization objective. This allows the framework to dynamically optimise the semantic representations towards downstream classification objectives, giving both a high level of contextual awareness and fine-grained. Lastly, a detailed list of assessment criteria and visualization methods is also used in establishing the efficiency of the suggested framework competently. Dataset In this study, a curated social media dataset focusing on the Monkeypox outbreak discourse is utilized. Monkeypox misinformation: X dataset 44 was collected from X during the global Monkeypox spread in mid-2022, a critical period when both verified information and misleading content were rapidly circulating. **The dataset was originally collected using keyword-based queries related to Monkeypox, and duplicate or non-relevant entries were filtered during preprocessing.** It comprises a total of 5787 tweets with 19 attributes, each enriched with user-level metadata and manually assigned sentiment/relevance labels, thereby supporting both binary and multi-class classification tasks. All data used in this study are publicly available, and no personally identifiable information was utilized, ensuring compliance with ethical research standards. A summary of the features is presented in Table 2.
Each record in the dataset encompasses multiple dimensions of information. Tweet content includes the raw text, posting time, and source platform (e.g., X Web App, iPhone), which capture the linguistic and contextual characteristics of user discourse. User metadata provides important profile details such as verification status, URL presence, account description, account creation time, and reported location, offering insights into user credibility and demographics. To reflect audience interaction, engagement metrics record retweet count, reply count, like count, and quote count, while user statistics cover follower count, following count, total tweet activity, and listed count, thus highlighting network influence and activity levels. For supervised learning tasks, the dataset incorporates sentiment annotations. A binary class (0/1) separates negative from positive sentiment (or irrelevant vs. relevant discourse). The annotation process follows predefined labeling criteria provided with the dataset, although potential subjectivity and label noise are acknowledged as inherent limitations. Before class balancing, the dataset exhibited class imbalance, which was later addressed using SMOTE during the training phase. The integration of textual content, user profile features, and engagement dynamics makes this dataset particularly valuable for studying health-related misinformation, providing a robust foundation for developing and benchmarking advanced natural language processing and DL models. Dataset preprocessing The effectiveness and the ability to work on natural language processing (NLP) and DL models depend greatly on data quality, structure, and consistency of the input. This paper fits a detailed preprocessing pipeline to prepare the social media-founded dataset to reach credible monkeypox discourse classification. Preprocessing workflow is carried out in such a way that textual and metadata data are normalized, noiseless, and semantically oriented. In particular, steps involved are missing value imputation, temporal feature extraction based on timestamps, text normalization involving tokenization, stopword elimination, and lemmatization, categorical encoding of user-level features, numerical scaling, and up- and down-sampling of classes by means of controlled data augmentation. All these combined steps can be used to guarantee that the dataset resembles a realistic internet discussion, but it is also compatible with the powerful DL models.

Data augmentation and SMOTE-based class balancing To further address class imbalance and strengthen the robustness of the experimental evaluation, Synthetic Minority Over-sampling Technique (SMOTE) was incorporated in the revised study. SMOTE generates synthetic samples of the minority class by interpolating between existing minority instances in the feature space, thereby mitigating classifier bias toward the majority class. The method was implemented using the imbalanced-learn library in Python, with the sampling strategy configured to balance the minority class to match the majority class distribution. The thorough preprocessing approach used in the study made both the textual and the metadata data standard, semantically consistent, and devoid of inconsistency. Attribute completion was rectified in a very systematic manner, time clues replaced, and normalization in linguistics decreased the noise and retained the semantic quality. Moreover, it was based on categorical encoding, numerical scaling, and data augmentation that enabled balanced and representative datasets. Lastly, the information leakage was prevented by stratified data splitting. All these efforts provided a strong basis for DL training to achieve effective classification of the monkeypox discourse.
Proposed RoBERTa-GRU hybrid model The hybrid RoBERTa-GRU model proposes an interpretable DL model to achieve highly accurate text classification by integrating the capacity of the pre-trained transformer models in the contextual representation of words and phrases and the temporal dependency modelling capabilities of recurrent neural networks. It is built on the premises of contextualised semantic-learning, temporal sequence modelling, and task-specific representations refinement, making the model more expressive and robust when compared to conventional stand-alone transformer-based or recurrent architectures. In contrast to possibility, in conventional ways of thinking, where a frozen language model or sequential modelling may only be used, the proposed framework uses a new end-to-end design where end-to-end gated recurrent processing is used to refine the semantic valued information at the subword level. The architecture comprises a cascade of parameterised operations which slowly converts raw token level inputs to high level, task oriented representations that prove well-suited to model the multifaceted dependencies and linguistic frequencies of a textual dataset. The model is directly fed with raw text as the input at the first step of the pipeline where the studied encoding is the RoBERTa encoder that further translates the text into contextual embeddings. At this juncture, the textual representations contain full contents of semantic information yet they are not temporally ordered. A subordinate, sequential transformation by GRU unit component then captures the word-to-word temporal dependencies and long range interactions. This yields sophisticated sentence-level representations that can characterise the input in a categorical fashion and aligned with downstream classification goal. First by using RoBERTa to produce semantically rich token-level representations and, second, by further enriching them with a temporal aggregation implemented by GRU, the framework could obtain both coarse-grained semantic dependencies and fine-grained sequential dynamics. Model training parameters and hyperparameters for the hybrid RoBERTa–GRU model The design of the proposed hybrid RoBERTa-GRU model is trained with a well-thought-out combination of architectural and optimisation hyperparameters to achieve both high performance and generalisation capacities. The preprocessing step entailed tokenising the input sentences with RoBERTa byte–pair encoding-based tokeniser and truncating/padding the sentences to a fixed sequential length of 128 tokens which balances context coverage and computational cost. The RoBERTa backbone is already initialised using the pre-trained roberta-base weights, and is not set as a frozen network, but contextual representations can be trained on the downstream task.
The recurrent part of the architecture is applied as a one-layer GRU having a single 128-unit hidden dimension. This dimension was chosen to offer capacity enough to represent temporal dependencies, but not to introduce the complexity that has not been required. The last hidden state of the GRU is then fed into two-unit fully connected layer representing the number of classes in the binary classification problem. The hybrid model is trained with the help of the cross-entropy loss function and optimised through the AdamW optimiser with a starting learning rate of 2 10 -5 as recommended by the best practices of transformer fine-tuning. A batch size of 16 is exploited thus to prevent the GPU memory saturation that would arise when large numbers of long input sequences are being processed concurrently.
The model is trained within 30 epochs. During training, we update the parameters of both the GRU and the transformer backbone in an end-to-end manner, allowing the latter to be adapted task-specifically to the contextualised embeddings. In order to keep a check on convergence behaviour as well as prevent overfitting, accuracy and loss are tested out on a separate validation set after every epoch. The output performance curves prove the consistency of convergence of the hybrid model.
The overview of all training and architectural hyperparameters is given in Table 3. Together, these two components constitute the overall architecture, in which a pre-trained Transformer encoder is first used to derive contextualized token representations, which are subsequently refined through a recurrent GRU layer to enhance sequential reasoning and improve downstream diagnostic performance.

Experimental setup and hyperparameters configuration Google Colab Pro+ with an NVIDIA A1000 GPU (40 GB VRAM) and a capacity of 64 GB of system RAM was used to conduct all experiments, guaranteeing fast training and large-scale transformer-based models treatment. A programming environment supported by Python 3.10, PyTorch 2.2.1, and HuggingFace Transformers 4.40.0 libraries was used to incorporate RoBERTa and GRU into the model. Other preprocessing tools were offered by pandas, NumPy and scikit-learn.
Monkeypox-related tweet dataset was pre-processed in several steps: duplicates were removed, special characters were deleted, the URLs were normalized, and the RoBERTa tokenizer was used with a maximum length of 128 tokens. To promote unbiased testing, the dataset was partitioned into training and testing with ratios of 80:20, respectively, without dissimilarity in label distribution.
Training was done using a batch size of 32 and a maximum sequence length = 128 tokens. To avoid overfitting, early stopping (with patience = 5 epochs) was used. Model training was performed up to 30 epochs, and the best-performing model on checkpoints across the validation set was used. Accumulation gradient was applied to stabilize changes, and training mixed-precision (FP16) was activated to optimize the memory footprint and GPU computation.
To assess, the standard and advanced measures were applied to give an overall picture of performance. Results were reported on accuracy, F1-score, ROC AUC, Cohen Kappa, and in loss curves. Demographics and ROC/PR were essential to test robustness to class imbalance and noisy annotations. Reproducibility was supported with fixed random seeds in numpy, torch, and CUDA.

Table 4 provides a direct comparison of model performance before and after applying SMOTE-based class balancing, enabling a clear evaluation of the impact of class imbalance on misinformation detection. The results show how different categories of models respond to imbalanced data and synthetic resampling.
Logistic Regression, used as a simple baseline, exhibits the weakest performance under the original imbalanced setting. It achieves low accuracy and very poor precision, indicating a large number of false-positive predictions and a limited ability to distinguish misinformation from non-misinformation content. Although recall remains moderate, the very low F1-score reflects an unbalanced trade-off between precision and recall. This limitation is largely caused by the skewed class distribution, which biases the model toward the majority class and highlights the need for SMOTE-based resampling.
Ensemble-based methods, including Random Forest and XGBoost, perform noticeably better than Logistic Regression due to their non-linear decision boundaries and ensemble learning strategies. Random Forest attains relatively high accuracy and precision; however, its recall remains low under the imbalanced setting, indicating difficulty in detecting minority-class misinformation samples. XGBoost follows a similar trend, achieving modest improvements in recall and F1-score, but its performance also plateaus due to limited semantic abstraction and the absence of explicit temporal modeling. After applying SMOTE, both ensemble models show clear gains in recall and F1-score, confirming the effectiveness of synthetic oversampling for improving minority-class detection.
Deep learning models demonstrate a clear advantage over traditional and ensemble approaches. RoBERTa achieves very strong baseline performance, with near-perfect accuracy and recall, demonstrating the effectiveness of transformer-based contextual embeddings in capturing subtle linguistic cues. Its high ROC-AUC score further indicates strong discriminative capability across different decision thresholds. Nevertheless, RoBERTa does not explicitly capture sequential dependencies, which can be important in narrative-driven misinformation and motivates further improvements through hybrid modeling.
The GRU-based model focuses on sequential dependency modeling through gated recurrent mechanisms and achieves a balanced trade-off between precision and recall. Under SMOTE-based balancing, the GRU model shows improved stability in minority-class detection, particularly in terms of recall and F1-score. However, the lack of large-scale pre-trained semantic knowledge limits its ability to fully capture deeper contextual information compared to transformer-based models.
By integrating the complementary strengths of both architectures, the proposed Hybrid RoBERTa-GRU model delivers the strongest overall performance. It consistently achieves the highest values across accuracy, precision, recall, F1-score, and ROC-AUC, indicating robust and stable discriminative capability. This hybrid design effectively combines deep contextual understanding with sequential reasoning, enabling reliable handling of both semantic complexity and temporal dynamics in health-related misinformation.
When evaluated under SMOTE-based class balancing, all models exhibit improved class-wise stability, particularly with respect to minority-class sensitivity. While overall accuracy changes only marginally for deep learning models, balanced performance improves substantially, especially for traditional and ensemble classifiers. Most importantly, the Hybrid RoBERTa-GRU model maintains consistently superior performance under SMOTE-based evaluation, confirming that its effectiveness does not depend on majority-class dominance and demonstrating its robustness in imbalanced misinformation detection scenarios.

This outstanding ROC-AUC figure confirms the strength of the hybrid architecture since it holds a steady performance in the variable placement of the decision boundary and achieves a similar classification performance. The analysis carried out in Fig. 11(right) shows the same trend with the Precision-Recall score achieving consistent performance that hovers near 1.0 across all models. GRU model has a good reliability on precision-recall performance, whereas RoBERTa incurs robust performance with a profound contextual embedding at the deep level. The proposed Hybrid RoBERTa-GRU model performs the best in PR-score showing it can effectively address the possible imbalances in classes and still achieve high precision points at various recall levels. GRU has sequential processing capabilities, which, when combined with the advanced attention mechanics of RoBERTa, are allowed to stay ahead of both individual architectures in terms of performance. The analyses on these findings have confirmed together that the proposed Hybrid RoBERTa-GRU model is superior in terms of having better overall performance under complex classification tasks of all aspects of evaluation dimensions, which justifies its efficiency as an augmented model in case of sophisticated multitask classification.

RoBERTa error analysis: As shown in Fig. 13a, the RoBERTa baseline demonstrates strong classification performance with 1411 correctly classified samples in Class 0 (negative sentiment) and 3047 correctly classified samples in Class 1 (positive sentiment). However, the model exhibits notable misclassification patterns with 61 false positives in Class 0 and 48 false negatives in Class 1. The confusion matrix reveals that RoBERTa occasionally struggles with contextual ambiguity, resulting in a misclassification rate of approximately 4.1% for Class 0 (61 out of 1472 total samples) and 1.6% for Class 1 (48 out of 3095 total samples). These errors primarily occur in cases involving subtle semantic variations, ironic expressions, and complex contextual expressions where multiple sentiment indicators conflict. The model’s attention mechanism, while powerful for contextual understanding, occasionally over-emphasizes certain words, leading to misinterpretation of overall sentiment polarity.
GRU error analysis: The GRU model confusion matrix, presented in Fig. 13b, shows 1434 correct classifications for Class 0 and 3519 correct classifications for Class 1. However, the model demonstrates higher misclassification rates with 63 false positives in Class 0 and 28 false negatives in Class 1. The GRU architecture’s sequential processing limitations become evident through its difficulty in capturing long-range dependencies, resulting in misclassification rates of approximately 4.2% for Class 0 (63 out of 1497 total samples) and 0.8% for Class 1 (28 out of 3547 total samples). The model particularly struggles with negation patterns and contextual shifts within longer sequences, where early sentiment indicators may overshadow later contradictory information. Additionally, the GRU’s vanishing gradient problem affects its ability to maintain contextual information across extended text sequences, leading to incomplete sentiment understanding in complex narratives.
Hybrid RoBERTa-GRU error analysis: The proposed hybrid model’s confusion matrix, illustrated in Fig. 13c, demonstrates superior classification accuracy with 1937 correct classifications for Class 0 and 1947 correct classifications for Class 1. Remarkably, the hybrid architecture achieves minimal misclassification with only 22 false positives in Class 0 and 0 false negatives in Class 1. This exceptional performance translates to misclassification rates of merely 1.1% for Class 0 (22 out of 1959 total samples) and 0% for Class 1 (0 out of 1947 total samples), establishing the hybrid model’s superiority over both baseline approaches. The mathematical analysis reveals that the hybrid model achieves a TPR of 1.0 for Class 1 and 0.989 for Class 0, while maintaining an exceptionally low FPR of 0.011 for Class 0 and 0.0 for Class 1.
The quantitative comparison reveals that the hybrid model reduces total misclassifications by 79.8% compared to RoBERTa (22 vs. 109 total errors) and 86.8% compared to GRU (22 vs. 167 total errors). The hybrid model’s superior performance stems from its synergistic combination of RoBERTa’s contextual understanding and GRU’s sequential processing capabilities. By leveraging RoBERTa’s attention mechanisms for deep semantic comprehension and GRU’s recurrent structure for sequential pattern recognition, the hybrid architecture effectively addresses the individual limitations of both baseline models. The RoBERTa component captures complex contextual relationships and semantic nuances, while the GRU component maintains sequential coherence and handles temporal dependencies. This complementary approach enables the hybrid model to correctly classify challenging cases involving double negations, sentiment transitions, and mixed emotional expressions that typically confuse individual architectures. The dramatic reduction in false negatives and false positives across both sentiment classes validates the hybrid model’s robustness in handling complex sentiment classification scenarios, including cases involving sarcasm, mixed emotions, and contextual ambiguity that challenge individual architectures.

Zeng et al. 45 reached 87.5% accuracy on the PolitiFact dataset, using an ensemble of transformers of the three models BERT-large, DeBERTa-large, and RoBERTa-large. Although this illustrated the power of ensembling models, the training time of these large models limits their applicability. Also, Pendyala et al. 46 applied Mistral-7b to the LIRA dataset but obtained a rather low performance (59.10%), and this fact testifies to the complexity of applying large LLMs to misinformation-detection tasks.
Waheed et al. 47 used a combination of BERT and LSTM on various datasets (Fake Newsnet, LIAR, PHeme, and PolitiFact), achieving a high accuracy of 94.6%, in which the hybridization of contextual embeddings with sequence modeling demonstrated its usefulness. Mahmood et al. 48 have further advanced with a pure LSTM-based architecture, achieving 98.84% accuracy on X data, indicating that recurrent models are competitive in the short-text domain, social media.
Gifu et al. 49 used a ConvNet+LSTM model on George McIntire’s fake-real news corpus, reporting 52.77% accuracy, with the researchers noting the difficulty of capturing misinformation by sequential models alone when faced with complex datasets. According to Stewart et al. 50 , who used the BERT with the ChatGPT-generated dataset, their approach yielded 84.1% accuracy, and Bao et al. 51 applied multimodal analysis (text + image) using RoBERTa with nearly perfect 99.2% accuracy. Small BERT was employed by 52 on one social media dataset set with 97-99% accuracy, confirming once again the versatility of the transformer-based models in misinformation detection.
In this backdrop, the accuracy of our proposed model, RoBERTa+GRU, was superior with 99.44% on the X dataset than all the other comparison models. Particularly, it outperforms the ensemble of Zeng et al. by 11.94%, the hybrid method of Waheed et al. by 4.84%, the LSTM model of Mahmood et al. by 0.6%, Bao et al.’s multimodal RoBERTa by 0.24%, and Najee et al.’s small BERT by up to 2.44%. Such findings highlight the efficacy of a combination of contextual embeddings of RoBERTa and sequence representation by GRU, not only providing state-of-the-art accuracy, but also efficiency in misinformation detection with real-life situations.
Limitations Despite its strong performance, the proposed model has several limitations. First, the dataset is limited to X (formerly Twitter), which may restrict generalizability to other platforms. Second, the analysis is conducted on English-language data only, limiting multilingual applicability. Third, the use of SMOTE introduces synthetic samples that may not fully reflect real-world distributions. Finally, the model requires substantial computational resources, which may limit deployment in low-resource environments. Future work will focus on extending the model to multilingual and multimodal settings to enhance generalizability across diverse real-world scenarios.
Conclusion This study highlights how Artificial Intelligence is potentially revolutionizing how the online misinformation menace can be solved. The combination of the contextual power of RoBERTa and the sequential modeling properties of GRU makes the proposed hybrid model even more accurate and resilient against health misinformation detection, including close-to-perfect scores during the evaluation process through the Monkeypox X dataset. These results demonstrate the need to integrate complementary deep architectures to model both semantic richness and a temporal flow of social media discussion.
In addition to its technical remarks, this research focuses on the more general implications. Misinformation detection cannot simply be a computation problem, but also a social requirement, which impacts the population and societal health, policy-making, and reliance on digital environments. Though the proposed model has raised a desirable benchmark, it still has shortcomings, mainly the need to rely on English-only datasets, its high computational complexity, and no validation on other languages and modalities, and platforms. It is necessary to tackle these issues if one wants to deploy in the real world, especially in a limited or multilingual setting.
Future directions ought to work on broader distributions of datasets, the incorporation of multimodal information like image and video, and scalable, interpretable, and real-time systems. It will also be essential to cooperate with policymakers, healthcare establishments, and social media to implement these AI solutions in effective interventions. Finally, this work offers the basis to establish resilient, AI-enabled structures that could ensure information integrity, foster citizens’ trust, and address the far-reaching evils of digital disinformation.
