1. Introduction In recent years, the rapid advancement of technology has brought with it a multitude of advantages to our world, enhancing peoples’ lives and contributing to a higher comfort, satisfaction, and standard of living [1]. The significant impact of technological progress across various domains (e.g., education [2], communication [3], economy [4], transport [5], health [6], etc.) is indeed beneficial, contributing to both the efficiency of these processes and quality of life. However, it additionally generated and introduced new issues [7], including the one that will be addressed in the present paper—“disinformation”.
Along with the technological progress and the exponential growth in popularity of social media platforms, the issue of fake news dissemination has become more and more prevalent [8]. The fact that, at present, everyone has access to the Internet and almost every individual has at least one account on a social network (e.g., Twitter, Facebook, Instagram, etc.) has made it easier than ever before to share any type of content and information online [9]. In addition to the real and useful information spread across the Internet, inaccurate data generated by the lack of information [10] or even false content deliberately disseminated by ill-intentioned people [11] is also present.
To begin this discourse, it is compulsory to explain and establish a distinct differentiation between the terms associated with fake news, more specifically the two separate categories in which they can be divided: misinformation and disinformation.
Misinformation refers to wrong, erroneous content distributed as a consequence of using unreliable sources, or an individuals’ lack of information, education, misunderstandings, and misinterpretations [12]. Thus, it refers to the unintentional distribution of false information [13].
Furthermore, disinformation, the subject covered in this article, also refers to the spread of false information, but, in this case, it is intentionally shared [12]. The main desires of this phenomena are to manipulate, control, and influence people [14]. This type of information (e.g., deepfakes, propaganda, conspiracy theories) is carefully considered and thoroughly examined and formatted by malefic individuals, with the purpose of generating panic and confusion among people, obtaining certain political or economic advantages, initiating various conflicts and damages, and even controlling the world [15,16]. As a result, various issues related to disinformation have been analyzed over time in the scientific literature, starting from studies featuring branches of disinformation—such as propaganda-related topics [17,18], deepfake topics [19,20], conspiracy theories [21,22], or disinformation in general—in various contexts such as natural disasters [23], climate change [24,25], politics [26,27,28], and health issues [29,30,31]. Given how straightforward and easy it is to spread fake information on social media, this situation is extremely alarming [32].
With the advent of artificial intelligence, particularly through the advancement of machine learning and deep learning techniques, there has been a remarkable increase in the number of researchers leveraging these methodologies [33]. These techniques have been applied to a diverse array of domains, including but not limited to sentiment analysis [34,35], fake review detection [36], fake news detection [37], frailty detection in elderly populations [38], e-commerce [39], and the ride-hailing industry [40].
Among the most prominent machine learning algorithms employed in this field are support vector machines [41], random forests [42], and naive Bayes [43]. In the realm of deep learning, convolutional neural networks [44], recurrent neural networks [45], generative adversarial networks [46], and transformers [47] are particularly popular.
As a result of their high applicability and the advantages they offer, these techniques have become great tools in combating the spread of fake information [48]. They have demonstrated increased effectiveness in the automatic detection of false content and the identification of manipulation [49,50].
By reviewing the existing literature, one can easily notice many articles written in this domain, each with its own original approach. As also mentioned in the above sections, the main goal of this manuscript was to analyze the papers published in the scientific literature that address the crucial issue of disinformation and employ machine learning or deep learning techniques. From the beginning, a clear delimitation between the terms “misinformation”, “disinformation”, and “fake news” is established, with the focus being exclusively oriented towards disinformation, compared to other similar studies which are oriented towards misinformation detection [51] or fake news research [52]. As noted in other studies, many authors neglect or misinterpret these contrasts between the terms and discuss the phenomenon of “fake news” as a whole [53], without carefully taking into account these differences, as is the case in this study.
Furthermore, this paper offered a novel perspective, provided numerous graphs, tables, and figures generated from Biblioshiny, and divided the analysis into distinct sections (dataset overview, sources, authors, papers, and mixed analysis). An additional section, which is usually either very concise or not found at all in bibliometric investigations, is a review of the most cited papers [54,55]. For instance, this paper offered a comprehensive evaluation of the existing literature, providing a summary of 20 papers written in the studied area. Apart from these, word analyses, including bigrams, trigrams, a co-occurrence network for the terms, and thematic maps, were additionally provided and explained.
Continuing with the discourse, the present manuscript also includes a section dedicated to mixed analysis, which is not frequently found in such investigations.
Taking all of this into consideration, the purpose of this bibliometric investigation is to analyze the existing literature around the field of machine learning and deep learning applications in disinformation detection, to provide a broad vision of the current trends in the topics addressed, and to bring to the surface interesting details that can lay the foundation for subsequent studies, lead to the development of modern and effective solutions to combat this dangerous phenomenon, and also transform the online environment into a safer place.
The motivation behind this work was represented by the urgent need to understand the challenge of disinformation on social media platforms, evaluating the research progress, identifying the main gaps in this domain, and investigating the published articles, their content, and their impact on the scientific community. The information provided by the bibliometric analysis can contribute to a deeper comprehension of the field and facilitate the adoption of enhanced strategies to address this alarming event.
In the following pages, an in-depth investigation is conducted, with a focus on the existing literature, providing insights about the dataset overview, authors, sources, affiliations, collaborations, word analysis, thematic maps, and many other factors. This manuscript aims to obtain better knowledge of this issue and understand the gaps in the literature and the areas that require additional investigation. Furthermore, in order to combat this dangerous issue, both the authorities and researchers must collaborate and consolidate their efforts.
Thus, the research questions considered in this paper are as follows:
- Q1: Which are the most significant journals around the topic of machine learning and deep learning applications in disinformation detection, considering both the number of published papers and the impact based on H-index?

- Q2: Who are the most engaged scientists in this research field according to the number of published papers, and how can their impact and contribution to the scientific community be characterized?

- Q3: What observations can be drawn regarding the degree of collaboration in this area, between both academics and countries, and how do these impact the progress of the scientific field?

- Q4: Which are the most involved affiliations and countries based on the number of papers, and what observations can be made in terms of annual scientific production growth?

- Q5: What knowledge can be gained from the analysis of the top 10 most cited documents globally, and how are these claims supported and validated further by word analysis and thematic maps?

The first phase, namely dataset extraction, is of utmost importance since it represents the collection of data. The papers have been extracted exclusively from Clarivate Analytics’ Web of Science Core Collection database (also known as the WoS database) [59] and then filtered by the presence of relevant keywords and by applying various exclusion criteria: the language used, the type of the document, and the year of publication. 2.1. Dataset Extraction The first phase of the process is represented by the dataset extraction. In this article, the authors opted to use the Web of Science database [60], a popular and widely used database within the scientific community, due to the huge amount of works included, which cover many areas and fields. The choice of using a single database for extracting the papers may result in potential criticism, but it must be mentioned that this decision is grounded in some rationales, all of which are outlined below.
The first solid justification considered for this decision is represented by the database’s increased popularity within the scientific community due to the pronounced amount of works included from all over the world and the variety of fields and topics covered compared to other competitors (such as Scopus and IEEE), a fact also highlighted by some academics in their manuscripts, e.g., Bakir et al. [61] and Cobo et al. [62]. Furthermore, the intuitive and organized interface was also among the benefits.
Another decisive factor taken into account was that scientists can export data in their raw format from the Web of Science database and upload them directly into Biblioshiny, an R tool, used for generating figures, graphs, and tables.
The database’s prominent place is further strengthened by its position as the sole database for gathering data collection sets in many other bibliometric studies conducted by various authors on different topics (sentiment analysis during the COVID-19 pandemic [17], machine learning techniques applied in fake news [18], Twitter platform [63], and digital governance [64]).
Apart from these mentioned aspects, it must be highlighted that in the Web of Science database, the authors benefit from personalized access to sources. In this manner, according to some existing papers in the scientific literature by Liu [65] and Liu [66], it is stated that a dataset is considered truly relevant for a bibliometric analysis if, and only if, the authors benefit from full access to sources (all 10 indices: Science Citation Index Expanded, Social Sciences Citation Index, Arts & Humanities Citation Index, Emerging Sources Citation Index, Conference Proceedings Citation Index—Science, Conference Proceedings Citation Index—Social Sciences and Humanities, Book Citation Index—Science, Book Citation Index—Social Sciences and Humanities, Current Chemical Reactions, and Index Chemicus). As for the present research, the authors had complete access to all indices.
Moreover, another factor that further contributed to the choice of using a sole database for the collection of papers is represented by the challenges that would have been encountered in terms of individual analyses performed in the article.

The first exploratory step consists of obtaining only the works relevant for the proposed study, more specifically only the papers that approach machine learning or deep learning techniques in disinformation detection. Towards this goal, the authors searched for relevant keywords in either the title, abstract, or authors’ keywords. The first query returned a total of 3714 papers that contain, at least once, in one of the three sections, the word “disinformation”. The second query generated a huge number of 360,293 entries involving “machine learning”, while the third one uncovered 230,203 pertinent papers associated with “deep learning”.
Since the intention is to exclusively retrieve papers that address the disinformation issue through the lens of either machine learning or deep learning, the second exploratory step focused on filtering and grouping the results obtained in the previous step. The initial query included in this phase yielded 545,575 papers that contain “machine learning” or “deep learning” in the title, abstract, or authors’ keywords, while the next one intersected the papers obtained in the first query with the fourth one so only the papers that deal with both disinformation and machine learning or deep learning are selected. At the end of this step, the data collection set included 216 papers, suggesting a significantly reduced dataset compared to the high values previously obtained.
The third query incorporated a language restriction; namely, only English-written papers were considered eligible for this bibliometric investigation. This decision was also taken by other authors in various publications around different subjects [67,68,69] and was based on the fact that the majority of papers are written in this worldwide language of discourse, which is well known by the researchers and target audience considered for this manuscript. As can be observed, this hypothesis is further supported by the quantity of papers obtained after applying this rule: from the initial set of 216 papers, only 2 of them were non-English papers.
The next query focused on the document type and excluded all the works that were not marked as “article” in the Web of Science database, an important step discussed in the paper belonging to Donner [70]. Another aspect that must be clarified here is represented by the fact that all relevant research studies are indexed by Web of Science as an “article”, so conference proceedings are not excluded from this category [71]. This rule almost halved the dataset, reducing it to 131 articles.
The sixth exploratory step removed the papers published in the ongoing year, 2024, at the moment of writing the article, as they were considered incomplete in terms of scientific publications. Consequently, three papers were excluded.
The last step consisted of the manual review of the 128 papers that were obtained after applying the previous filters. Following this process, it emerged that only 77 articles address machine learning and deep learning applications in disinformation detection. The goal of this step was to accurately filter the data collection set and remove papers that do not address this topic and were erroneously included after filtering. For instance, an example of a mistake found through this manual selection is represented by the use of the word “disinformation” in the authors’ keywords, as an example of fake news, rather than being the article’s primary subject.
In light of the aforementioned aspects, the final dataset included 77 English-written articles, published between 2018 and 2023. All those papers were analyzed from multiple perspectives and facets in this bibliometric investigation.

As already mentioned above, the top country was established according to the total number of citations (TC), with the values ranging between 16 and 55. The total number of citations per year (TCY) indicator is situated between 3.33 and 16, while the normalized TC (NTC) falls between 0.99 and 10.67.
While the TC and TCY indicators are easy to understand, some further explanations are needed for the NTC indicator. In fact, the NTC indicator is determined by dividing the average number of citations received in a year by a publication by the average number of citations received by all the articles included in the dataset belonging to the same year as the article for which the NTC is determined [93,94]. Thus, the NTC indicator shows how many times an article has performed better in terms of citations than the other articles included in the dataset.
Situated in first position is the manuscript belonging to Rubin [95] (TC = 55, TCY = 9.17, NTC = 2.72), followed by the paper written by Sear et al. [86] (TC = 42, TCY = 8.40, NTC = 4.20), and then the one by Masood et al. [84] (TC = 32, TCY = 16, NTC = 10.67).
These high records suggest the relevance of the papers, their increased visibility within the scientific community, together with the academics’ rising interest in the topic addressed.

The manuscript that holds the first position in the hierarchy of the top 10 most cited documents globally is the one belonging to Rubin [95]. It includes many examples and explanations, and as suggested by the title, the paper addresses the challenging topic of disinformation and misinformation associated with “fake news”. Through this study, the author wants to present the issue of fake news spread, find possible solutions and strategies that can control the propagation of this phenomenon, and contribute to a safer online environment. The term “triangle” refers to the three parties the author identifies as being involved in this problem using an epidemiological model: virulent pathogens (fake data collected from multiple sources), susceptible hosts (uneducated, ignorant readers), and favorable environments (social media platforms that facilitate the spread of incorrect data). A conceptual model was integrated with automated detection techniques (machine learning and natural language processing). The results proved that those methods are very useful for fake news detection, but at the same time, individuals must educate themselves and make sure they obtain their information from reliable sources. This so-called “epidemic” of fake news, as specified in the title of the article, is a dangerous problem, and the author encouraged future researchers to extend this study further, improve the existing automated detection methods, and propose new strategies that are efficient for combating the spread of false information on social media.
The second article listed in Table 7 is written by Sear et al. [86] and addresses a topic in the health domain. The COVID-19 pandemic was a challenging worldwide incident that had a major impact on the population and attracted the attention of many academics. The present study examined the consequences of the COVID-19 pandemic, especially the debate on Facebook around vaccinations. At the same time, it intended to comprehend how individuals are influenced by these narratives. The authors collected the data from Facebook posts associated with anti-vaccination and pro-vaccination in the period of the COVID-19 pandemic (17 January 2020 to 28 February 2020), performed a manual classification of clusters (“anti-vax” and “pro-vax”), and examined them through machine learning analysis using latent Dirichlet allocation. The quality of the results was assessed through coherence score and human inspection. Increased involvement was observed within the anti-vax communities, and it was noticed that the emergence of the pandemic significantly influenced the narratives, generating the spread of fake news and conspiracy theories. As for future work, the academics suggested further investigation into the long-term effects and the usage of improved machine learning techniques, and highlighted the importance of developing effective strategies that can limit the spread of disinformation.

The study can be considered a solid basis for the development of the field, and the authors warn readers about the danger of disinformation and recommend the treatment of this subject with high priority.

Papadopoulou et al. [97] address a topic related to user-generated videos and their spread on online platforms. Smith et al. They analyze and compare the performance of classification and prove the initial hypothesis by employing relevant indicators (e.g., precision, recall). The results show high efficiency compared to traditional methods, and in terms of future work, it is suggested to continue the study of these aspects and also focus on more recent elections.
The last article found in the top 10 most cited documents globally is the one by Hajli et al. Furthermore, incorporating psycholinguistic insights into disinformation detection can significantly enhance our understanding of the linguistic nuances that characterize misleading information [110,111,112]. Considering the papers included in our dataset, we have observed that only one of them discusses this issue of psycholinguistic insights, namely the study conducted by Uyheng et al. [113]. In their paper, the authors illustrate how online trolling, which is a form of disinformation, can be associated with distinct linguistic features such as lower cognitive complexity and more abusive targeted language. The study reveals that troll messages often exhibit less complexity, which contrasts sharply with the more sophisticated and nuanced communication typically employed in legitimate discourse. Moreover, in their study, the authors demonstrate that psycholinguistic features can inform the development of effective detection models [113]. To this end, the authors propose a model, called TrollHunter, which showed an accuracy rate of 89%. Through the model, the authors reveal the importance of identifying abusive language, which resulted in an average 12.25% improvement in the model’s performance, highlighting the significance of context in detecting trolling behavior. Thus, the findings advocate for the integration of psycholinguistic markers into machine learning models for disinformation detection, highlighting the fact that models become more robust against the subtleties of disinformation tactics when the psycholinguistic markers are considered. Furthermore, the study shows that the language of disinformation can vary significantly, necessitating a nuanced understanding of linguistic cues.
Given the importance of including psycholinguistic insights in models in the case of disinformation, and the fact that, in the extracted dataset, it was only a single paper that addresses this issue (representing 1.30% of the papers in the dataset), the integration of psycholinguistic markers should be considered more in future studies in this field.

Even if the ongoing year is not complete from a scientific point of view, the authors followed the same steps specified in Section 2, extracted the papers from Web of Science database, filtered them by the same criteria, and manually selected only the relevant studies for this bibliometric investigation. As a result, a total of 10 papers have been obtained.
In the following text, we have sorted them according to the number of citations, even though this indicator does not have much relevance given the limited amount of time between the papers’ publishing and indexing dates and the moment in which they received citations.
The first paper according to the number of citations is the one written by Gambín et al. [114], which is focused on deepfakes. The authors analyze the content of deepfakes, how they are generated and spread across social media platforms, and the deep learning techniques employed, offering an overview of the current trends and risks to the entire population and public opinion. Moreover, the power of blockchain in combating disinformation and detecting deepfakes in online environments is also highlighted. The article discusses future trends, possible effective technologies, and challenges that can be further addressed by the scientific community.
The article by Al-Adwan et al. [115] is also oriented towards deepfake detection. Considering the increasing tendency of sharing fake video content on online platforms, for manipulation purposes, the researchers conducted a study in which they tested the performance of a model in detecting deepfake videos. They used a convolutional neural network (CNN) and recurrent neural network (RNN), together with a particle swarm optimization algorithm (PSO). Public datasets were used in the investigation, and the results obtained are satisfactory, compared to other methods generated recently, with their arguments supported by indicators such as F1 score, sensitivity, accuracy, and specificity.
Lakzaei et al. [116] bring to the scientific literature a manuscript in which they investigate the phenomenon of disinformation on social networks and discuss the existing forms of disinformation and the effective methods and techniques used for automatic detection of this content, focusing particularly on graph neural networks (GNNs). The paper includes a section dedicated to future research directions and possible areas that require more investigation.
The paper by Lebernegg et al. [117] focuses on disinformation, more specifically on analyzing the writing style of fake news. By using tree-based machine learning algorithms, it is observed that the text found in fake news is more informal and slightly emotional, with the constant use of certain words being noticed. The outcomes prove that this approach can facilitate the fight against disinformation and ease the early detection of content intended to induce confusion or panic or influence people.
Yang et al. [118] demonstrate in their paper how coordinated disinformation networks, with multiple actors involved, can substantially increase the impact of fake content on social media platforms due to the rapid spread of inaccurate information and public involvement. The authors highlight that in order to counteract disinformation, the entire network (all the actors involved) should be targeted, not only some accounts.
The manuscript belonging to Nawaz et al. [119] is also oriented towards deepfake analysis and delimitation between authentic and fake posts. The authors’ approach was to use deep learning techniques to detect different types of manipulation through video content—FaceSwap (FS) and Face-Reenactment (FR) deepfake detection (AUFF-Net). The model used showed satisfactory results on the FaceForensic++ dataset, proving its performance and effectiveness in the fight against manipulation of media content on social networks.
In the manuscript written by Horák et al. [120], newspaper articles are analyzed in order to identify the ones that are intended for propaganda. Gathered from Czech newspapers, the dataset contains 8646 articles, and it is further used for training and propaganda detection techniques. The style analysis contributes to identifying manipulative strategies, generating outstanding results.
Doshi and Schmidt [121] discuss, in their paper, the use of an external observer for examining the information shared across social networks and identifying the areas which may contain false information disseminated with the purpose of manipulation or deception. This is achieved through data analysis and machine learning techniques, proving that the observer can be considered a valuable weapon in the fight against disinformation.
The article written by Vashishtha et al. [122] brings to the readers’ attention a deep learning-based system, which has the primary goal of contributing to combating disinformation on social networks by distinguishing between real and fake content. The approach proposed in this paper is to use the recommended optical flow technique, which seems to obtain satisfactory results on the FaceForensics + + dataset for both DeepFake and FaceSwap.
Vysotska et al. [123] present a model based on machine learning and natural language processing for detecting political propaganda and disinformation. The model uses logistic regression and numerous methods (TF-IDF, “Bag of Words”, word2vec), operating at both document and sentence level. The outcomes indicate increased efficiency, with the accuracy being higher at document level compared to sentence level.

5. Limitations This section dedicated to limitations aims to objectively illustrate the manner in which the investigation was carried out and the methodology used together with the rationale behind this decision.
As for the present bibliometric analysis, 77 articles were extracted from the Web of Science database. An initial starting point of the discussion should be centered on this aspect. For the data collection set, the authors considered only one database, namely Web of Science, rather than using multiple options, such as IEEE or Scopus. Since there are individual analyses (e.g., most cited papers, etc.) conducted in this manuscript, the use of multiple databases would have generated numerous issues and challenges in identifying the top papers, with respect to the number of citations. The authors’ decision to use a single database was supported by multiple arguments, even though they are aware about the alternatives and do not consider them irrelevant or insignificant. The increased appreciation and recognition within the scientific community, its updated version with a huge variety of papers from all the domains, the comprehensive evaluation and analysis of other bibliometric papers with a similar approach and methodology, the simplicity of integration and compatibility with Biblioshiny (the R tool utilized to create the figures and graphs in the third section), and the interactive, intuitive, and easy-to-use interface were all among the key factors that contributed to this decision.
Moreover, since Biblioshiny is designed to operate with only one database, for this manuscript, it was an important aspect which was considered beneficial and advantageous and did not impact the analytic process. Hence, scientists are aware of the consequences generated by using a sole database and encourage individuals who are interested in expanding the research to consider this possibility and use a multi-database approach to obtain the largest possible dataset.
Another limitation that must be listed here is represented by the articles’ selection based on some predefined keywords (“machine learning”, “deep learning, and “disinformation”). Even if the scientists’ desire was to exclude the papers that do not address topics related to machine learning and deep learning applications in disinformation detection, there is a chance that certain mistakes were accidentally made. Some important papers might have been omitted due to certain factors such as distinct terminology.
The exclusive inclusion of English-written manuscripts is also recognized as a constraint. Even though English is considered a familiar language for most of the academic personas engaged in writing studies on different subjects, there may be a possibility that some essential non-English works were disregarded.
Furthermore, an additional limitation is associated with the document typeset. In order to obtain a uniform and consistent dataset, the authors’ only included the works marked as “articles” in the Web of Science database in the analysis. This constraint had a pronounced impact on the final dataset, and possibly on the results, considering that some important information from other works, such as books and conference papers, may have been lost.
The exclusion of the studies conducted in the current year (2024) can also represent a limitation, even if, from a theoretical point of view, this ongoing year should not be considered relevant at this point, as it is not concluded in terms of scientific publications.
Finally, though not necessarily a limitation, the manual sorting of the papers at the end aimed to analyze each work individually and understand its focus. However, human nature makes us prone to errors.
Apart from the mentioned limitations, it should also be highlighted that the present paper does not contain a clear distinction between different types of fakes, such as environmental, political, ethnic, etc. The authors recommend future researchers to take into consideration these aspects for upcoming studies and conduct a careful delimitation of these types. This will enhance the understanding of how each fake affects public opinion and will facilitate the development of more effective strategies associated which each category. Interested readers can start by reading the articles belonging to Roozenbeek et al. [124] and D’Errico et al. [125].
The study’s limitations were all highlighted and discussed in this section, with the aim of providing a comprehensive and impartial assessment for the interested readers. The topic of disinformation detection holds significant relevance in the current era, while the rapid evolution of machine learning and deep learning techniques offers a wide range of benefits. The authors encourage future researchers to carefully prepare the dataset, take into consideration all the limitations presented in this section, expand the topic further, embrace new strategies, and implement cutting-edge solutions. A high involvement in this domain is crucial, and at the same time, the studies conducted within the scientific community can contribute to the fight against this major challenge.
6. Conclusions Given the aforementioned information, the primary intention of this paper is to conduct an in-depth bibliometric analysis around the topic of machine learning and deep learning applications in disinformation detection. This challenging issue has attracted the interest of several researchers due to its utmost importance and the negative effects it has on society. The annual growth rate of 96.1% indicates academics’ intense involvement and dedication to this crucial field. The fact that the research in this area began to gain popularity from 2018 suggests that as the technology has advanced and social media platforms have grown, the phenomenon of disinformation has become more widespread.
For this bibliometric investigation, 77 English-written articles were selected from the Web of Science database and analyzed through various facets (dataset overview, sources, authors, papers, and mixed analysis). The process of data selection included multiple filters (keyword search, language, document type, and year of publication), followed then by a careful manual selection. By investigating the journals, the most influential and significant source in this research domain, considering both the number of published papers and impact, is IEEE Access . In terms of authors, two main prolific and active scientists were revealed, each with four papers. At the same time, George Washington University is the affiliation that marked the greatest involvement (nine papers). Furthering the narrative, the countries that registered an increased production in this domain are the USA (eighteen published papers) and China (eight works). In addition, the increased number of citations accumulated by both countries strengthen their position as influential leaders in this research area. Moreover, another unmasked aspect is the substantial degree of collaboration in this domain and the increased number of partnerships within countries and authors from all over the world. From the review of the top 10 most cited documents globally, it was observed that the papers can be divided into two distinct categories: review papers and original research articles. All of them were focused on the fake news issue, addressed various events (e.g., the COVID-19 pandemic, French presidential election), collected relevant datasets from multiple social media platforms (e.g., Twitter, Facebook), and used different techniques and methods (e.g., machine learning, deep learning, natural language processing). The word analysis uncovered hidden trends and current topics often addressed. Some examples are listed as follows: “fake news detection”, “social media”, “ethics”, “disinformation detection”, “machine learning”, “deep learning”, etc.
Therefore, a topic of high importance in the current digital era is covered in this manuscript. There is an urgent need to understand, detect, and control fake news together with the phenomena of misinformation and disinformation, especially as they are spreading rapidly on social media networks. The repercussions of disseminating misleading content or data meant intentionally to produce fear and confusion are certainly not something to be ignored, taking into consideration that they have a severe effect on individuals’ decision-making processes, mental health, and behavior. Key domains such as politics, economy, or even health, are affected by fake news distribution, with the intention of inciting hatred, influencing opinions, and promoting certain conspiracies or evil intentions.
To extend further the research in this domain, a good approach would be to perform bibliometric investigations on datasets collected from other existing databases, such as Scopus or IEEE, and compare the obtained results. This avenue can be helpful as there might be differences between various databases. For example, a first search of the Scopus database applying a similar succession of search steps—through the use of the search query TITLE-ABS-KEY (disinformation) AND (TITLE-ABS-KEY (machine_learning) OR TITLE-ABS-KEY (deep_learning)) AND PUBYEAR > 2016 AND PUBYEAR < 2024 AND (LIMIT-TO (DOCTYPE, “ar”)) AND (LIMIT-TO (LANGUAGE, “English”))—resulted in a dataset comprising 180 papers compared to the 128 papers in this study. On the other hand, a search performed on the IEEE database has revealed that the number of papers dedicated to disinformation, namely having “disinformation” in either the title, abstract, or author keywords, without considering the machine learning or deep learning component, for the time period before 2024 and for papers published in journals, revealed a reduced number of papers, namely 33. Thus, the differences in the dataset extraction based on various databases could be further addressed in a future study. Other possible future research directions are listed here, together with some papers conducted in the specified area that can further help in extending the work: misinformation construction that characterizes fake news, which may be automatically recognized alongside discrediting, polarization, or conspiracy [126], detection of opposing indices in order to discriminate between them using supporting psycholinguistic literature [127], and the expression of positive emotions and ethical language [128].
This paper brings a new contribution to the scientific literature and can be considered a solid foundation for future research, since it covered most of the aspects associated with the field of machine learning and deep learning applications in disinformation detection. The authors highlight that scientific involvement is critical for comprehending the current circumstances. The academics have the opportunity and knowledge to develop, enhance, and strengthen the effectiveness of deep learning and machine learning methods, as well as establish initiatives and strategies to address and combat this serious challenge.
