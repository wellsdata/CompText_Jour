Keywords:
epistemic beliefs, truth relativism, truth realism, receptivity to misinformation, bullshit receptivity, conspiracy theories, spreading misinformation
Despite the growing concern with 'post-truth' attitudes in today's society, the role of belief in truth relativism has been surprisingly neglected in the psychology of misinformation. In the current research, we replicated findings of positive correlations between belief in (subjectivist) truth relativism and bullshit receptivity and conspiracy ideation, respectively. Introducing a new paradigm for investigating the tendency to distribute misinformation, we also found novel evidence that belief in truth relativism was positively related to distributing science misinformation and negatively related to distributing accurate science information. Moreover, our results revealed that belief in truth realism was negatively correlated with distributing science misinformation. These effects held up over and above competing predictors. Both social conservatism and economic right-wing ideology were positively associated with conspiracist ideation and distribution of science misinformation but not bullshit receptivity. Our attempt to induce epistemic beliefs to examine their causal impact on misinformation susceptibility and distribution was, however, largely unsuccessful. Future research on the receptivity to and distribution of misinformation should acknowledge the role of epistemic beliefs in general and subjectivist truth relativism in particular.
1. Introduction
According to several scholars, the relativization of facts has been one of the greater threats against public debate in developed western nations of recent years. When facts, or truth, are portrayed as a matter of mere perspective, evidence can be neglected and public discourse filled with misleading information, impairing citizens' ability to make informed decisions (e.g. [1,2]).
The notion that truth is a matter of perspective is a form of relativism-that is, the philosophical stance of viewing phenomena such as beauty (aesthetic relativism), moral rightness (moral relativism) or truth (truth relativism) as contingent on perspective or context (e.g. subjective or cultural), and thus dependent on who or where judgement stems from. By contrast, realism holds that the nature and attributes of these phenomena are independent of human perspectives of them. To the truth realist, what is true or false is a matter of correspondence between propositions and objective states of affairs.
Curiously, despite the apparent upsurge of relativism (or 'post-truth' attitudes) at a societal level, the role of truth relativism at the level of the individual has until recently been neglected. Nevertheless, a handful of recent studies have indeed found links between belief in truth relativism and receptivity to misinformation. Aspernäs et al. [3] found that subjectivist truth relativism, i.e. the view that truth values depend on the feelings and mental lives of the individual (but not cultural truth relativism), was positively associated with receptivity to pseudo-profound bullshit and belief in conspiracy theories, as well as with fact resistance and dogmatism. Similarly, Garrett & Weeks [4] found that participants who viewed truth as determined by political power (rather than something objective) were more likely to believe in conspiracy theories and were less accurate in their beliefs about political and scientific issues (with associations in the opposite direction for need for evidence). Similar results were later found with fake news discernment and belief in conspiracy theories as outcomes [5,6].
These results provide promising evidence of a link between epistemic beliefs about truth and receptivity to misinformation but do not reveal whether these links represent causal relations, nor the direction of such causal relations. For example, it could be that belief in subjectivist truth relativism (henceforth belief in truth relativism) causes receptivity to misinformation because belief in truth relativism makes an individual find their emotional response towards information sufficient to deem it as true (and thus have a lower threshold for acceptance of statements) even in the absence of correspondence with reality. However, it could also be the opposite case that belief in conspiracy theories, and acceptance of nonsensical bullshit, reinforce beliefs that encourage personal intuitive evaluations of truth values. As for conspiracy theories, adopting a conspiracist view of having the ability to see things as they 'actually' are may create an overconfident or dogmatic stance, or perhaps conspiracist narratives encourage people to follow their own intuitions rather than listening to official authorities and credible sources. What is more, it is indeed possible that other factors, such as cognitive ability, affect levels of both belief in truth relativism and receptivity to misinformation.
In the current study, we aim to shed light on these issues by investigating whether the previously found association between belief in truth relativism and receptivity to misinformation represents a causal relation, as in whether belief in subjectivist truth relativism causes receptivity to misinformation. More specifically, we attempt to investigate whether belief in subjectivist truth relativism causes receptivity to pseudo-profound bullshit and conspiracy theories within a priming paradigm. Moreover, we also investigate whether belief in truth relativism causes the tendency to distribute misinformation with a novel measure designed specifically for this purpose. Learning more about whether these relations are indeed causal, and, if so, whether it is belief in subjectivist truth relativism that causes receptivity to misinformation and not the other way around, is helpful when planning interventions to mitigate misinformation susceptibility.
1.1. Inducing relativism
To our knowledge, the only two articles in which researchers have investigated the causal consequences of adopting a relativist mindset have covered moral relativism. In these studies, researchers have compared the effect of meta-ethical views on various behaviours by inducing relativism and its counterpart.
In two studies, Rai & Holyoak [7] induced meta-ethical views in their participants by exposing them to either moral relativist ('... We have to step back from our immediate gut reactions and realize that our own moral beliefs are simply a product of our cultural upbringing rather than any objective set of criteria...') or moral absolutist ('...Our moral beliefs are based in intrinsic facts about what is right and wrong in the world... ') arguments on female genital mutilation. They thereafter investigated the participants' tendency to cheat (study 1) or engage in marginal theft (study 2). Their studies showed that inducing moral relativism caused the participants to cheat and steal more compared with participants who were induced with moral absolutism.
These findings are in line with the results from Young & Durwin's [8] study on charitable giving in which a canvasser induced moral realism ('Do you agree that some things are just morally right or wrong, good or bad, wherever you happen to be from in the world?') or moral antirealism ('Do you agree that our morals and values are shaped by our culture and upbringing, so there are no absolute right answers to any moral questions?') to passersby (participants). The participants who were induced with moral realism were more likely to donate money to charity (study 1) and to donate more money to charity of their choice (study 2), as compared with participants who were primed with moral antirealism or who were simply approached by the canvasser but not induced by any meta-ethical views.
These studies make up interesting examples of how different philosophical standpoints or views can be induced to test how they affect behaviours. A belief induction can be a simple and straightforward way to investigate whether the exposure to narratives about truth (temporarily) affects people's beliefs about truth, and in turn their receptivity to misinformation. Although many results from priming studies have been found difficult to replicate (e.g. [9,10]), both Rai & Holyoak [7] and Young & Durwin [8] reported successfully priming relativist views within the moral domain.
In the current research, we therefore expanded on these studies by inducing relativist and realist views on truth. We did so by letting participants respond to rhetorical statements that were created with the intent of signalling relativist or realist views on truth. In order to keep the aim concealed, these statements did not explicitly or exclusively describe views on truth but rather included uncontroversial topics with details that alluded to relativist (e.g. 'Believing too strongly in 'the one and only objective truth' can be harmful') or realist (e.g. 'Believing too strongly that 'all truths are equally true' can be harmful') views. Moreover, we intentionally chose items that many people plausibly would agree with in order to increase the probability that participants would rate the statements favourably. Our rationale behind this procedure was that performing the act of giving positive responses to these statements would evoke the relativist or realist beliefs alluded to in the statements. Inspired by Young & Durwin [8], the idea behind this form of inducement was the assumption that laypeople plausibly subscribe to aspects of both relativism and realism and that attributes of a context affect which of these will dominate their current mindset. Moreover, we created the relativist and realist counterpart statements with wordings that were as similar as possible and differed only in terms of whether they alluded to relativist or realist standpoints. As our manipulation check intended to determine if the induction was successful also functioned as a measure of individual differences in belief in truth relativism, the design allowed us to investigate both the causal and correlational links between belief in subjectivist truth relativism and misinformation receptivity.
1.2. Receptivity to misinformation
Misinformation comes in different shapes and forms, and being receptive to one of them does not necessarily make you receptive to the other. Sometimes misinformation consists of information that is false, but it could very well be the case that most misinformation lacks false content and is rather misleading [11,12] in that it can lead to false convictions or other negative consequences, such as impeding the formation of true beliefs [13]. In the current research, we measure receptivity to two forms of misinformation that cover both false and misleading misinformation and that previously have been found to correlate with belief in truth relativism: pseudo-profound bullshit [3,14] and conspiracy theories [3-6].
1.2.1. Bullshit receptivity
Common for different forms of bullshit is their producers' disregard for truth. A bullshitter is neither aiming to accurately portray reality nor to intentionally lie about it. Rather, s/he speaks to evoke a certain feeling of awe or amazement in its recipient by embellishing the message with pretentious but empty wording [15]. Although bullshit can include false utterances, it is typically different from blatantly false misinformation and rather deceives in its misleading and vague content, as is the case in the pseudo-profound bullshit included in the current research (e.g. 'Wholeness quiets infinite phenomena').
Several studies have found positive associations between receptivity to pseudo-profound bullshit and epistemic inclinations, such as greater intuitive cognitive style and supernatural belief [16], as well as apophenia, i.e. a greater observation of non-existing patterns [14,17,18]. Moreover, a recent comparison of many different epistemological predictors of receptivity to bullshit (e.g. analytical, intuitive apophenic, dogmatic and dichotomous thinking) found belief in subjectivist truth relativism to be one of the strongest predictors of general bullshit receptivity [14]. This finding is an interesting corroboration of the results from the study by Aspernäs et al. [3] as it establishes the unique contribution of belief in truth relativism over and above many other predictors, across several different kinds of bullshit, e.g. corporate bullshit, post-modern bullshit and leftand right-wing bullshit. These associations could be due to the subjectivist judging veracity based on what feels right as opposed to searching for evidence that something is accurate. As for truth realism, a person with realist views could be less impressed with such content as the unclear message is difficult to match with an external reality.
1.2.2. Belief in conspiracy theories
Although conspiracy theories can indeed identify actual conspiracies, as in the case of the Watergate scandal in the 1970s, the conspiracy theories included in psychology research typically make claims that contest official evidence-based accounts, which in turn are accused of being mere cover ups [19]. For instance, we included the following statement in the current research: 'Experiments involving new drugs or technologies are routinely carried out on the public without their knowledge or consent'. One can indeed find circumstantial evidence that supports this claim, but the evidence supporting the 'official accounts' that most drug companies follow regulations on the need for permission before testing their products is stronger. In other words, conspiracy theories include not only claims about specific situations, such as drug experiments, but also encourage narratives that official accounts and the common sources of information such as scientific studies cannot be trusted, indirectly misleading people to mistrust important societal institutions.
The element of secrecy (Lewandowsky et al. 2013 [20]) combined with explanations that are often vague and not falsifiable [21] make them difficult to empirically debunk. Hence, reinforcing the belief that truth is relative to personal experience may increase the likelihood that participants will believe in these theories that are not amenable to refutation through factual evidence. Moreover, conspiracy theories are often imbued with emotional appeal [22,23 ], which may also make them more attractive to individuals who believe that subjective feelings determine what is true. Belief in truth realism, on the other hand, may be associated with rejecting conspiracy theories due to the difficulty of identifying clear empirical observations that prove their veracity; or vice versa, that conspiracy ideation fosters an aversion to the realist notion that the contents of statements need to match external reality in order to be true.
1.3. Tendency to distribute misinformation
Determining who spreads misinformation is as important as determining who is susceptible to it. The spread of falsehoods is by no means unique to the 21st century but has indeed been facilitated by the internet, which enables a rapid spread of misinformation (e.g. [24]) threatening freedom of speech and freedom of press globally [25]. Critical to this development has also been the shift towards the internet as the primary source of news and information [26], and the launch of social media networks and digital communication which enable not only socializing but the spreading of misinformation and rumours.
But why do people share misinformation? According to the naïve account, people share (mis)information simply because they believe it to be true and want to enlighten others, perhaps because of a heightened vulnerability to believing in the information due to alignment with one's political opinions (e.g. [27,28]). According to the competing polarization account, however, misinformation is spread for reasons other than believing in it. For example, the results from a large-scale global experiment conducted in 16 countries indicated that people tended to report willingness to 'share news that they would be able to identify as false if asked' ([29; p. 1509]). Similarly, Osmundsen et al. [30] found that people who hate their political opponents are the most likely to share selectively chosen content to insult a political opponent.
In this study, we shed light on factors that hitherto have been almost completely neglected in research on misinformation spreading tendencies, namely that of epistemic beliefs. To our knowledge, Chua and Banerjee's study [31] is the only previous attempt to examine similar theories. Their results showed that participants who view the internet as a credible source of information about health (e.g. 'When I encounter difficult health-related problems, I feel safe if I find information about them on the Internet') were more likely to respond 'yes' than 'no' to whether they wanted to share online health rumours in their social media networks than individuals who were more sceptical of the internet (e.g. 'I often doubt if the Internet really is a good source for health-related information'). They selected eight rumours from a Chinese rumour-verification website where domain specific experts mark rumours as true or false. Prior to taking the questionnaire, participants reported whether they had seen the rumours, to which all participants said no. While the results do indicate a link between epistemic beliefs and sharing misinformation, their measure captures only specific views. In the current study, we took a more general approach and sought to measure epistemic beliefs about the nature of truth. We did so by using measures that have been validated in previous research and that have been shown to correlate with susceptibility to several forms of misinformation [3], but whose role in the tendency to spread misinformation has not been investigated.
1.4. Overview of research
We investigated whether epistemic beliefs (self-reported truth relativism and truth realism) predicted receptivity to misinformation and distribution of misinformation in social media, and whether these associations held up when controlling for demographic and ideological individual differences as well as level of social media activity. We also investigated whether induced truth relativism and truth realism affected the tendency to fall for and distribute misinformation. We induced epistemic beliefs by using a novel procedure that let participants report their agreement with rhetorical statements that covertly alluded to relativist or realist beliefs about the nature of truth.
We measured receptivity to misinformation in terms of the tendency to perceive nonsensical sentences as profound (i.e. receptivity to bullshit) and belief in conspiracy theories. Our measure of bullshit receptivity consisted of a shorter version (32 [3]) of the original scale [16]. Half of this measure consisted of aphorisms, and the other half consisted of pseudo-profound bullshit sentences. In our measure of belief in conspiracy theories, we included a broad range of conspiracy theories from several previously used scales (e.g. [17,33]). In so doing we intended to capture a more general conspiracy mentality (e.g. [34]) rather than belief in narrower specific conspiracy theories.
We developed a novel paradigm that measures people's willingness to distribute misinformation online as a function of its congruence with their prior opinions. The participants were repeatedly reminded to imagine having a large-scale social media account and chose how they wanted to portray and share scientific results on this fictitious social media account with a supposedly large audience. Asking participants to estimate their willingness to share misinformation (rather than observing actual individuals' sharing behaviour) is by far the most common procedure, aside from a few exceptions, such as Osmundsen et al. [30] who examined authentic profiles and behaviours on the X platform (previously Twitter). Self-reported willingness to share misinformation online has previously been found to correlate with actual sharing behaviour on X [35] and should thus be able to function as a proxy for behaviour. Importantly, however, our procedure has several benefits as compared with previous research using self-report measures of willingness to share misinformation; we took measures to enhance a 'real life' experience, as opposed to merely exposing participants to a news item that is fake and then asking them to report the degree to which they are willing to share it online (e.g. [29]). For example, we repeatedly instructed them to imagine that this was their own social media account, and we used prewritten statements about what to share about the scientific results to help envision their post. The scientific results covered well-known controversial topics to which we assumed that the participants intuitively would know their attitude (i.e. gender differences, organized crime and unemployment). For each topic, the participants were presented with search results that were either very supportive of their own hypothesis, supportive of the other hypothesis or unclear. Using the words 'your own hypothesis' and 'the other hypothesis' allowed us to control what information the participants were exposed to, which would not be possible in a real life setting on actual social media platforms. They were thereafter asked to choose from the set of prewritten statements that summarized the search results. We assumed that participants would be tempted to choose to present the scientific literature in a way that was more favourable to their own hypothesis, as people tend to be more positive towards (scientific) information that is in line with their prior beliefs (e.g. 36, 37; 38; 39; 40 [41]); and that this tendency would be stronger in participants who had been induced with relativism. The emphasis on personal experience in relativism may make the participant's feelings towards the issue more salient and important in determining how to present the findings. Conversely, participants induced with realism may be more reluctant to downplay the weight of the scientific evidence when this speaks to their disadvantage.
Our time-stamped preregistration (https://aspredicted.org/y88k-kprb.pdf) included the study design, planned sample size, inclusion and exclusion criteria, planned primary analyses, and two (H3 and H4)1 of four overarching hypotheses.
Correlational hypotheses:
H1. Self-reported belief in truth relativism will predict (i) higher bullshit receptivity, (ii) stronger belief in conspiracy theories, and (iii) a stronger tendency to distribute misinformation.
H2. Self-reported belief in truth realism will predict (i) lower bullshit receptivity, (ii) lower belief in conspiracy theories, and (iii) a stronger tendency to distribute accurate information.
Experimental hypotheses:
H3. Participants with induced truth relativism will: (i) be more receptive to bullshit, (ii) express greater belief in conspiracy theories, and (iii) be more likely to distribute misinformation, compared with those in the control group.
H4. Participants with induced truth realism will: (i) be less receptive to bullshit, (ii) express less belief in conspiracy theories, and (iii) be less likely to distribute misinformation, compared withthose in the control group.
2. Method
2.1. Participants
We recruited participants with the help of PFM Research during the first half of 2024. PFM is an independent data collection company with access to a large panel of Swedish participants that are roughly representative of the Swedish population in terms of age, gender, education level and region of living in Sweden. We requested 1000 participants with complete responses and received data from 1261 subjects who gave their consent to participate. After we had excluded participants in accordance with our preregistration, our final sample consisted of 1008 participants (499 women, 505 men, 4 with unspecified gender; Mage = 48.50 years; SD = 15.92).
2.2. Procedure and materials
We conducted an online experiment programmed in Qualtrics. All materials, including data, codebook, stimulus material and the preregistration, can be found on our OSF-page: https://osf.io/x4ivq/overview?view_only=4865f700ef6e43799aa3c906d7bb7410.
Our participants were randomly assigned to one of three conditions: (i) Control; (ii) Relativism; (iii) Realism. Participants who gave their informed consent were randomly assigned to one of the three conditions and completed an online survey consisting of four blocks. Figure 1 illustrates the four blocks and the study section in each block. Below we describe each section of the survey in the order they were presented to participants.
2.2.1. Instructions and informed consent
An English translation of the first page including instructions and informed consent can be found in the electronic supplementary material. This page included information about the attention check in the survey that participants would have to pass to complete the study.
2.2.2. Block 1: inducing relativist or realist epistemic beliefs
After an initial pilot study2 provided tentative support that the rhetorical statement manipulation influenced subsequently reported belief in truth relativism, we proceeded and conducted the slightly improved belief induction experiment described below.
2.2.2.1. Condition assignment
Participants were first randomly assigned to one of the three conditions, each including at least 300 participants, one control condition and two belief inducement conditions (one relativism and one realism).
2.2.2.2. Rhetorical statements (belief inducement manipulation)
In this section, all participants read and reported their agreement with seven statements presented in a fixed order (see table 1); the statements in the relativism condition expressed positive views on relativism, and the statements in the realism condition expressed positive views on realism. We told participants that we were interested in their attitudes towards various matters but, unbeknown to them, the seven statements were framed to generally encourage a high level of agreement. The statements in Block 1 thus represented a belief inducement manipulation (not a measure) and resembled the approach used by Young & Durwin [8]. Participants in the relativism condition responded to statements inducing truth relativist beliefs, and participants in the realism condition responded to statements inducing truth realist beliefs. Our rationale behind this was that by performing the act of reporting a positive attitude towards several statements, participants would activate beliefs that were congruent with the content of these statements. In the control condition, we created statements that many would likely agree with while not activating any beliefs of relevance to this study. We did this to ensure that all conditions were similar in terms of reinforcing a tendency to report a high degree of agreement.
To further boost the level of agreement with the statements, participants rated their level of agreement on a Likert-scale that was intentionally skewed. More specifically, any response above 1 indicated at least some level of agreement with the statement: 1 = 'Do not at all agree', 2 = 'Agree a little bit', 3 = 'Partially agree', 4 = 'Agree fairly much', 5 = 'Mostly agree', 6 = 'Agree completely'. Note that the rhetorical statements constituted our experimental manipulation only. Although mean agreements to all of these statements are reported in table 1, we never used these responses in the subsequent analyses.
2.2.3. Block 2: measuring receptivity to misinformation
In the second block, we measured receptivity to misinformation. This block also included two additional pages with rhetorical statements, and a measure of belief in truth relativism and truth realism respectively (which also functioned as a manipulation check of the belief inducement in Block 1). We operationalized receptivity to misinformation in two ways, i.e. (i) receptivity to pseudo-profound bullshit, and (ii) belief in conspiracy theories.
2.2.3.1. Bullshit receptivity
In this section, participants responded to how profound they found eight sentences (see table 2). We used a Swedish short-version of the Swedish bullshit and profoundness receptivity scale (32 [3]), which consists of four pseudo-profound bullshit sentences (e.g. 'The future elucidates irrational facts for the seeking person') and four aphorisms (e.g. 'Your teacher can open the door, but you have to step in'). Previous assessments of this scale showed excellent structural validity, i.e. bullshitand profoundness-receptivity were homogenous factors that were distinct from each other (42). All items were presented in a randomized order. The participants rated the degree to which they found the items profound on a scale from 1 ('Not at all profound') to 5 ('Very profound'). We assessed the distinctness of bullshit receptivity from receptivity to profound sentences through confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) in accordance with our preregistration on the current data. We performed CFA with items as indicators and maximum likelihood estimation in terms of the Comparative Fit Index (CFI), the Root Mean Square Error of Approximation (RMSEA) and the Root Mean Squared Residual (SRMR). Common rules of thumb suggest that obtaining CFI > 0.95, RMSEA < 0.06 and SRMR < 0.08 is desirable under many circumstances (43). The CFA showed that a two-factor model, χ2(19) = 33,93, p = 0.019, CFI = 0.992, RMSEA = 0.028 (90% confidence interval [CI] = [0.011, 0.043]), SRMR = 0.0276, was clearly superior to a one-factor model, Δχ2(1) = 448,28, p < 0.001, χ2(20) = 482,21, p < 0.001, CFI = 0.753, RMSEA = 0.151 (90% CI = [0.140, 0.163]), and SRMR = 0.1143. These results, along with reliabilities for the bullshit sentences (α = 0.8; ωh = 0.76; ωt = 81) and profoundness sentences (α = 0.69; ωh = 0.68; ωt = 0.69), respectively indicated that the bullshit and profoundness receptivity scales were homogenous and distinct from each other.
2.2.3.2. Belief in conspiracy theories
The participants responded to seven items intended to measure belief in conspiracy theories (see table 3). The statements were presented in a randomized order. The participants rated the statements on a scale from 1 ('Do not at all believe in') to 5 ('Believe completely') (M = 2.77, SD = 0.92). We sought to include measures of general belief in conspiracy theories, as opposed to belief in more specific conspiracy theories. We chose items from the Conspiracy Mentality Questionnaire (44), from the factors malevolent global, personal wellbeing and control of information from the Generic Conspiracist Belief Scale (45), from Krouwel et al. [33] and from the other conspiracy theories in van Prooijen et al. [17]. Moreover, we sought to select items by choosing theories that overall would be equally as attractive to rightand left-wing participants to avoid ideological tilt [46]. Assessments of reliability in accordance with the preregistration indicated that a single general factor indeed saturated the variation in the items (α = 0.88; ωh = 0.80; ωt = 0.92).
2.2.3.3. Additional rhetorical statements
After the misinformation sections, the participants were asked to report their level of agreement with additional rhetorical statements (see electronic supplementary material, table S4). These rhetorical statements were similar to those used in Block 1 and were added to 'boost' the relativist and realist beliefs induced in that block. The additional rhetorical statements encouraged participants in the relativism-condition to agree with truth relativist statements, participants in the realism-condition to agree with truth realist statements, and participants in the control condition to agree with statements that were unrelated to epistemic beliefs. The participants responded on the same skewed Likert-scale as used in Block 1.
2.2.3.4. Self-reported epistemic beliefs
After the last social media scenario, all participants responded to six items measuring epistemic beliefs inspired by a validated measure of belief in truth relativism [3] on a new separate page (see table 4). Importantly, these six items had two separate purposes: to serve as (i) a measure of individual differences in epistemic beliefs and (ii) a manipulation check to determine if the experimental manipulation had the intended effect. Three of these items assessed belief in truth relativism and the other three items assessed belief in truth realism. Participants rated their level of agreement with these statements on a normal (not skewed) Likert-scale ranging from 1 = 'Do not at all agree' to 5 = 'Agree completely'.
Our preregistered plan was to deem the manipulations successful if participants in the relativism condition expressed stronger relativist views on the nature of truth compared with participants in the control condition, and if participants in the realism condition expressed weaker relativist views on the nature of truth compared with participants in the control condition. Again, an initial pilot study had provided tentative support that the rhetorical statement manipulation influenced subsequently reported belief in truth relativism.
We originally intended to compare the three conditions in terms of belief in truth relativism by using an aggregated variable consisting of the six items measuring belief in truth relativism and truth realism (reversed belief in truth relativism items) respectively into a general belief in truth relativism scale (M = 2.77, SD = 0.63). However, as the reliability of this scale was poor (α = 0.57; ωh = 0.26; ωt = 0.7) we conducted a CFA to compare the fit of a two-factor model (separating the non-reversed from reversed items) to that of a one-factor model (all six items in the same model). The CFA showed that a two-factor model χ2(8) = 16,96, p = 0.031, CFI = 0.991, RMSEA = 0.033 (90% CI = [0.010, 0.056]) and SRMR = 0.0287, was superior to a one-factor model, Δχ2(1) = 162,09, p < 0.001, χ2(9) = 179,05, p < 0.001, CFI = 0.825, RMSEA = 0.137 (90% CI = [0.120, 0.155]) and SRMR = 0.0925. Thus, our tests of the manipulation and of possible experimental effects on other measures consisted of the belief in truth relativism items divided into two scales: belief in truth relativism and belief in truth realism.
2.2.4. Block 3: measuring tendency to distribute misinformation
Next, we asked participants to imagine that they had a social media account with thousands of followers. We then informed them that their task was to state how they would express themselves on social media in three hypothetical scenarios.
All participants first read the gender scenario:
You have an account in social media with thousands of followers and a high reach. What you write in your posts will be read by many people.
Imagine that you have a clear hypothesis about whether gender differences are explained primarily by biological factors or cultural factors, and that your personal experience gives clear support for your hypothesis.
Imagine also that you search for scientific studies that have investigated this topic and find all scientific studies from the last 10 years. All studies that you find are of roughly the same size and quality. When you compile the studies, you find...
Participants had previously been randomly assigned to one of three 'order-conditions'. Depending on their order-condition, the following part of the gender scenario consisted of one of the following versions.
Version (a): own hypothesis has more support
60 studies that support your hypothesis,
10 studies that support the other hypothesis, and
Version (b): ambiguous support
30 studies that support your hypothesis,
29 studies that support the other hypothesis, and
27 studies that do not support any of the hypotheses.
Version (c): other hypothesis has more support
10 studies that support your hypothesis,
60 studies that support the other hypothesis, and
16 studies that do not support any of the hypotheses.
All participants were then asked the following: 'How would you choose to describe what explains gender differences on your social media account?', to which they were able to choose one of the following alternatives.
'Scientific studies overall show...'
(1) '... an overwhelming support for my hypothesis as the primary explanation of gender differences.'
(2) '... a strong support for my hypothesis as the primary explanation of gender differences, but a few studies support the other hypothesis as the primary explanation of gender differences.'
(3) '... a somewhat stronger support for my hypothesis as the primary explanation of gender differences and a slightly weaker support for the other hypothesis as the primary explanation ofgender differences.'
(4) '... that it is difficult to draw any conclusions about what explains gender differences.'
(5) '... a strong support for the other hypothesis as the primary explanation of gender differences, but a few studies show that my hypothesis as the primary explanation of gender differences.'
(6) '... an overwhelming support for the other hypothesis as the primary explanation of gender differences.'
After the gender scenario, participants read the crime scenario and later the unemployment scenario (see electronic supplementary material, Table VI to see all versions of all scenarios). In all scenarios, the participants chose which description they would use to summarize the investigated scientific studies as described above.
Importantly, all participants read one scenario in Version A (own hypothesis had more support), one scenario in Version B (ambiguous support) and one scenario in Version C (other hypothesis had more support). Specifically, as a function of the randomly assigned order-condition, they read the following scenario-version combinations (see electronic supplementary material):
Order-condition I: Gender-Version A; Crime-Version B, Unemployment-Version C
Order-condition II: Gender-Version B; Crime-Version C, Unemployment-Version A
Order-condition III: Gender-Version C; Crime-Version A, Unemployment-Version B
For each participant, we calculated an accuracy score and a misinformation distribution score by using a coding scheme based on which version of the scenario the participant had read and which description they chose (see tables 5 and 6).3 The accuracy score illustrates the number of times (0-3) a participant chose an accurate description (green squares in table 5). The misinformation distribution score illustrates the number of times (0-3) a participant chose a description of their own hypothesis as having more scientific support than was warranted (yellow squares in table 5). Hence, the higher the total misinformation distribution score, the greater the tendency to distribute misinformation.
Some participants chose a description that portrayed 'the other hypothesis' as having more scientific support than was warranted (grey squares in tables 5 and 6). These descriptions were recorded as neither accurate nor misinformation distributing as it is difficult to interpret whether they were a result of excessive carefulness, inattentiveness or incompetence. To illustrate, a participant who, over the three scenarios, chose one 'excessively careful reporting' (grey square), one 'accurate reporting' (green square) and one 'distribution of misinformation' (yellow square) received an accuracy score of +1 and a misinformation distribution score of +1.
2.2.5. Block 4: assessing individual differences
In this block, we assessed various individual differences among the participants.
2.2.5.1. Perceived explanation of the scenario-topics
In this section, we asked the participants about how they personally attribute or explain gender differences, organized crime and unemployment respectively (i.e. the topics in the three scenarios in Block 1). We did this to investigate associations between the participants' prior beliefs in the respective topics and their behaviour in the information distribution task. For each topic, participants reported their level of agreement with the two 'explanations' mentioned in the scenarios earlier (see electronic supplementary material). The statements with the explanations were presented in a randomized order. Participants responded on a non-skewed Likert-scale from 1 ('Do not at all agree') to 5 ('Agree completely').
2.2.5.2. Demographics
Participants thereafter stated their gender (1 = Male; 2 = Female; 3 = Other/prefer not to say), current age (open ended) and place of residence (1 = 'Small village on the countryside', n = 172; 2 = 'Smaller town', n = 225; 3 = 'Medium sized city', n = 269; and 4 = 'Larger city', n = 342).
They also reported their education level: 1 = 'Not finished elementary school' (n = 1), 2 = 'Finished elementary school' (n = 40), 3 = 'Finished high school' (n = 363), 4 = 'Started studies at the university' (n = 115) and 5 = 'University degree' (n = 489). Participants who reported having at least started university studies also reported whether they mainly studied subjects in the humanities ('For example languages, history, philosophy, arts, literature, sociology'; n = 196) or natural sciences ('For example biology, technology, mathematics, physics, chemistry, medicine'; n = 275) or 'Other/Do not know' (n = 133).
Finally, participants reported whether they had any social media account, and, if so, how active they were on social media in terms of time spent on social media and sharing of information on a scale from 1 = 'Not at all active' to 5 = 'Very active' (M = 2.68, SD = 1.16).
2.2.5.3. Political orientation
In the last section, participants reported their political self-placement on two items (social issues and economic issues; see table 7).
The social issues item read: 'People sometimes speak about how socially liberal or conservative they are. A socially liberal person value individual freedoms and rights the highest. A socially conservative person value norms and traditions the highest. Using this definition, would you describe yourself as socially liberal or conservative?'
Participants responded on a Likert-scale from 1 = 'Very liberal' to 7 = 'Very conservative' (M = 3.40, SD = 1.52).
The economic issues item read: 'People sometimes speak about how leftist or rightist they are on economic issues. A leftist person emphasizes financial equality. A rightist person emphasizes the right of each individual to benefit from financial successes. Using this definition, would you describe yourself as leftist or rightist on economic issues?' Participants responded on a Likert-scale from 1 = 'Very leftist' to 7 = 'Very rightist' (M = 4.07, SD = 1.74).
Finally, they also reported their most-liked political party ('If there was an election today, what party would you vote for?'), and their least-liked political party ('Which of the following parties do you like the least?'), by selecting one of the eight parties in the Swedish parliament (see electronic supplementary material).
2.2.5.4. Hypothesis guessing
The participants were able to, in their own words, report what they believed was the purpose of the study.
2.2.5.5. Debriefing
In the very last section of the survey, participants in the relativism and realism conditions read a debriefing statement disclosing the purpose of the relativist and realist statements respectively depending on which condition they were in.
2.2.6. A ttention check
We included one attention check to be able to exclude participants who may have been responding without paying attention. The attention check item was mixed into the bullshit items in Block 2 ('This is an attention test-show that you are attentive by on this statement selecting 'Somewhat thought-provoking'). We excluded 109 participants who failed to pass the attention check in line with the preregistration.
2.2.6.1. Inclusion criteria
In line with our preregistration, we included all participants who provided informed consent, passed the attention check and finished the study.
3. Results
3.1. Correlational results related to hypotheses H1 and H2
Congruent with earlier findings [3], the correlations displayed in table 7 indicate that belief in truth relativism was moderately positively related to bullshit receptivity (95% BcA CI of r [0.284, 0.408], p < 0.001) and to belief in conspiracy theories (r [0.311, 0.431], p < 0.001). These associations held up after controlling for belief in truth realism, political orientation, demographics and level of social media activity (as well as profoundness receptivity in the regression model predicting bullshit receptivity; see table 8).
Belief in truth relativism was also positively related to the misinformation distribution score (i.e. the number of times a participant chose to share and present information as having greater scientific evidence than was warranted; 95% BcA CI [0.165, 0.284], p < 0.001) and negatively related to the accuracy score (i.e. accurately presenting information regardless of whether this information confirmed or disconfirmed prior beliefs; 95% BcA CI [-0.303, -0.182], p < 0.001). These associations remained significant after controlling for the same factors as in the regression models predicting receptivity to misinformation (see table 8).
Interestingly, belief in truth realism was not significantly correlated with bullshit receptivity or belief in conspiracy theories (see table 7), but weakly negatively associated with the misinformation distribution score (95% BcA CI [-0.160, -0.037], p = 0.002) and weakly positively with the accuracy score (95% BcA CI [0.036, 0.161], p = 0.002). The two latter held up after controlling for other factors in regression analyses (see table 8). Overall, this suggests that belief in truth relativism is a better predictor of receptivity to misinformation than a lack of belief in truth realism, and that belief in both truth relativism and truth realism play a role in the tendency to distribute misleading and accurate information, respectively.
3.2. Exploratory correlational results
Several significant bivariate correlations not directly related to our hypotheses were found (see table 7). Some of the more relevant of these are presented below. Number in brackets illustrate 95% BcA confidence intervals of the correlation coefficients. Unless reported otherwise, all reported p's are <0.001.
The misinformation distribution score was weakly positively associated with both bullshit receptivity [0.106, 0.227] and belief in conspiracy theories [0.201, 0.315], while the accuracy score was weakly negatively associated with bullshit receptivity [-0.270, -0.153] and belief in conspiracy theories [-0.345, -0.230]. This suggests that the people who are more susceptible to misinformation are also more likely to share it.
As for political orientation, social conservatism (versus liberalism) and economic rightist (versus leftist) ideology respectively were both weakly positively correlated with belief in conspiracy theories ([0.073, 0.200] and [0.101, 0.221]) and the misinformation distribution score ([0.079, 0.208] and [0.084, 0.206]), and weakly negatively with the accuracy score ([-0.207, -0.087] and [-0.184, -0.066]). These associations were robust in the regression analyses with competing predictors reported in table 8. Both social conservatism (versus liberalism) and economic rightist (versus leftist) ideology respectively were positively but not significantly related to bullshit receptivity.
Social media activity level was weakly positively correlated with belief in truth relativism [0.075, 0.200], bullshit receptivity [0.050, 0.181], belief in conspiracy theories [0.103, 0.229], and the misinformation distribution score ([0.033, 0.161] p = 0.002), and weakly negatively with the accuracy score ([-0.164, -0.040], p = 0.001) and social conservatism (versus liberalism; [-0.135, -0.011], p = 0.020). The associations with belief in conspiracy theories, the misinformation distribution score, and the accuracy score held up after controlling for other variables in the regression analyses reported in table 8.
3.3. Experimental results
3.3.1. Inducing relativist and realist epistemic beliefs
In line with our predictions, the one-way ANOVA with belief in truth relativism as the dependent variable was significant F(2, 1005) = 19.88, p < 0.001, and planned contrasts revealed that participants in the realism inducement condition had a significantly lower mean level of belief in truth relativism (M = 2.53, SD = 0.99) than participants in the control group (M = 2.95, SD = 0.97; t(1005) = 5.65, p < 0.001, d = 0.43 [0.28, 0.58], and participants in the relativism inducement condition (M = 2.93, SD = 0.98).
However, in contrast to our expectations, there was no significant difference in mean level of belief in truth relativism between the participants in the relativism inducement condition and the participants in the control group (t[1005] = 0.27, p = 0.787, d = 0.02 [-0.13, 0.17]).
Also contrary to our predictions, a one-way ANOVA with belief in truth realism (the three reversed items) as the dependent variable was non-significant (F[2, 1005] = 0.711, p = 0.491), and the planned contrasts revealed that there were no significant differences between the participants in the control group (M = 3.93, SD = 0.66) and the participants in the realism inducement condition (M = 3.98, SD = 0.73; t[1005] = -0.854, p = 0.393, d = 0.07[-0.08, 0.22]) and participants in the relativism inducement condition (M = 3.92, SD = 0.68; (t[1005] = 0.318, p = 0.751, d = -0.02 [-0.17, 0.14]), respectively.
Although the manipulation checks suggest that the belief inducement was mostly unsuccessful, we went on to test if the experimental manipulation affected misinformation receptivity and distribution. The rationale for this is that it is possible that the experimental manipulation was strong enough to influence our misinformation measures (which followed right after the manipulation), but not our manipulation check (which was assessed later).
3.3.2. Induced epistemic beliefs and receptivity to misinformation
By contrast to our predictions in H3a and H4a, the results from one-way ANOVA did not show any experimental effects from the belief inducements on our measure of bullshit receptivity (F[2, 1005] = 0.11, p = 0.90). In other words, participants in the control group did significantly differ from participants and the relativism inducement condition; (H3a: t[1005] = -0.235, p = 0.82, d = 0.02 [-0.13, 0.17]), nor from participants in the realism inducement condition; (H4a: t[1005] = 2.46, p = 0.81, d = -0.01 [-0.16, 0.14]) respectively (see table 9). In line with our preregistration, we also performed an ANCOVA to examine H3a and H4a while controlling for the profoundness ratings. Similar to the above, the results from the ANCOVA did not indicate any experimental effects (F[2, 2004] = 0.13, p = 0.88, η2 = 0.00) on bullshit receptivity when controlling for profoundness receptivity.
The results from the one-way ANOVA investigating H3b and H4b were also contrary to our predictions, showing no significant experimental effects of the belief inducements on belief in conspiracy theories (F[2, 1005] = 2.29, p = 0.10). More specifically, the participants in the control group did not significantly differ from the participants in the relativism inducement condition (H3b: t[1005] = 0.02, p = 0.98, d = 0 [-0.15, 0.15]), nor from the participants in the realism inducement condition (H4b: t[1005] = 1.88, p = 0.06, d = -0.14 [-0.29, 0.01]) in terms of belief in conspiracy theories.
3.3.3. Induced epistemic beliefs and the distribution of misinformation
The results from the one-way ANOVA investigating H3c and H4c did not show significant experimental effects on the misinformation distribution score (F[2, 1005] = 0.23, p = 0.797) nor on the accuracy score (F[2, 1005] = 0.87, p = 0.418). See table 9 for information on means and standard deviations.
4. Discussion
Misinformation continues to have negative effects on society [47], and clarifying who believes in and distributes it-and why they do so-informs decisions on how to combat it. In the current research, we shed light on the relation between epistemic beliefs about the nature of truth and the tendency to fall for and distribute misinformation which has been surprisingly neglected considering how impactful the narrative about the current 'post-truth' era has been on a societal level. In this study, we were able to replicate previous findings on the role of belief in truth relativism and to present new evidence of a link between belief in truth relativism and the willingness to spread misinformation. We investigated the latter in a novel paradigm with promising design features. Our efforts to induce beliefs and investigate whether the links to belief in truth relativism represented causal associations were, however, mostly unsuccessful.
4.1. Experimental results
Inspired by Young & Durwin [8], we attempted to induce beliefs in truth relativism and truth realism respectively by exposing participants to rhetorical questions that alluded to these respective beliefs. The belief inducement was partially successful in terms of lower subsequent belief in truth relativism in the realism condition (i.e. the group of participants who were exposed to rhetorical questions that alluded to realist views) than in the control group and in the relativism condition. We did, however, not find any other significant differences in belief in truth relativism between the conditions, nor did we find any differences in belief in truth realism between any of the conditions.
Naturally, as the belief inducement did not create significant differences in epistemic beliefs across all groups of participants, we found no experimental effects from the belief inducement on our measures of receptivity to misinformation, i.e. bullshit receptivity and belief in conspiracy theories, nor on the misinformation distribution score (the number of times a participant chose to share information as having greater scientific evidence than was warranted on their social media account) and its logical counterpart-but not complete opposite-the accuracy score (the number of times a participant chose to accurately present information regardless of whether this information confirmed or disconfirmed prior beliefs). There could be several reasons as to why the results from the belief induction were in contrast to previous research on inductions of belief in moral relativism and its subsequent effect on behaviour [7,8].
First, it could be that the belief induction simply did not induce beliefs. If so, this could be due to the general difficulty to prime people, as previously mentioned by, for example, Harris et al. [9] and Yong [10]. It could also be that people in general are more used to reflecting on moral issues than on epistemic matters such as the nature of truth. If so, it may be easier to induce moral beliefs than beliefs about the nature of truth, which would explain why the attempts by Rai & Holyoak [7] and Young & Durwin [8] were more successful.
On the other hand, the participants in the realism condition did report significantly lower mean levels of belief in truth relativism, indicating that the belief induction had some form of effect. It could be that both inductions did indeed induce one type of epistemic beliefs but that the manipulation check failed to capture these specific beliefs. For instance, the items that were intended to induce belief in truth relativism may have captured forms of relativism other than subjectivism, which is the form of relativism that our manipulation check measured and that previous research has indicated is associated with receptivity to misinformation [3,14]. Our attempt to choose topics that many people agree with to evoke the relativist or realist beliefs that we assumed that the participants to some degree already subscribed to may have been at the expense of targeting subjectivism. Furthermore, the fact that the significant difference consisted of participants in the realism condition reporting lower levels of relativism rather than higher levels of realism indicates that the items inducing belief in truth realism may have induced belief in anti-relativism more than it induced belief in truth realism. Despite us attempting to create a balanced set of items with as similar wording as possible, there was indeed one pair of items where the realism version expressed anti-relativist views (i.e. 'It is wise to acquire a correct understanding from legitimate sources rather than trusting your gut') and the relativism version did not express analogous anti-realist views (i.e. 'It is wise to create your own understanding of reality rather than trusting so called experts'). Furthermore, if the inductions really did have effects, those effects may simply have been too brief to affect downstream consequences, as indicated by a literature review of priming of the belief in free will [48].
All things considered, we hope that this experiment and its outcome along with transparent access to all our research material will be informative to researchers with similar objectives. We also hope that the null results will contribute to the literature on priming in providing a more accurate and truthful picture of the difficulty to successfully induce people into holding specific beliefs. In keeping with the sentiment of this study, we moreover stress the need for transparent and sound publishing practices that include null results in order to combat publication bias and the canonization of false facts (e.g. [49,50]).
4.2. Correlational results
Nevertheless, we were able to replicate the findings that belief in truth relativism is related to bullshit receptivity [3,14] and to belief in conspiracy theories [3]. Importantly, these associations held up after controlling for belief in truth realism, political orientation, demographics, level of social media activity (and profoundness receptivity in the regression model predicting bullshit receptivity). Moreover, our results indicated that belief in truth relativism predicted the willingness to spread misinformation over and above political orientation, demographics and level of social media activity.
Interestingly, belief in truth realism was negatively associated with the misinformation distribution score and positively with the accuracy score. This could be because belief in truth realism, i.e. the belief that what is true or false is a matter of correspondence between propositions and objective states of affairs, cultivates a willingness to focus more on evidence and the results from examinations of reality, than on your gut response. Naturally, it could also be the opposite that a tendency to prioritize evidence over personal conviction encourages epistemic beliefs that reflect such behaviour. Yet another possibility is that our measure covered factors other than belief in truth realism that are relevant when judging how to describe scientific research in your social media network. The items in our measure of belief in truth realism slightly resembled Garrett & Weeks' [4] measure of need for evidence as well as the actively open-minded thinking scale [51], particularly the factor of fact resistance (e.g. 'Beliefs should always be revised in response to new information or evidence'; reversed item). Need for evidence was negatively associated with belief in conspiracy theories and positively with accurate beliefs about political and scientific issues [4]. As for actively open-minded thinking, it has been positively associated with less bias [52], and fact resistance has previously correlated positively with bullshit receptivity and belief in conspiracy theories [3]. Perhaps our measure covered the priority of evidence over personal opinion common to both need for evidence and actively open-minded thinking and that this is what explains the significant relations to belief in truth realism in the current study. Nevertheless, individual differences in both belief in truth realism and the willingness to prioritize evidence over personal conviction may play a particularly important role when it comes to spreading misinformation that consists of results from empirical research studies where judging evidence is central. This would explain why we found that belief in truth realism was associated with misinformation related to science but not with pseudo-profound bullshit or belief in conspiracy theories. More specifically, truth realism may be more relevant for how people judge scientific facts than how they judge meaningfulness, whereas subjectivism may entail a susceptibility to both falsities and nonsense designed to evoke feelings. In future studies, researchers may want to examine how belief in truth realism relates to different forms of misinformation as well as to thinking dispositions, such as actively open-mindedness and reflexive open-mindedness [53] to shed light on important antidotes to misinformation susceptibility. It would also be valuable to learn more about actual behavioural patterns of approaching (scientific) evidence and the consequences of belief in truth realism.
Overall, our results support the hypothesis tested in this study, namely that epistemic beliefs play a role in the distribution of misinformation. What is more, the misinformation distribution score was positively correlated with both bullshit receptivity and belief in conspiracy theories. These results indirectly lend credence to the naïve account, which states that people who are more likely to share misinformation are also more likely to believe in misinformation (e.g. [28]). In future research, it would be particularly worthwhile to investigate the potential contributions of a broader set of epistemic beliefs, including both other forms of relativism (i.e. wherein truth is seen as relative to the worldview of a political group) and beliefs concerning the sources, structure or limitations of our knowledge (e.g. trust in experts or dichotomous thinking [14]). This might yield additional insight into, for example, how political polarization and selectiveness about information sources are driving the spread of misinformation in today's digital world.
The paradigm for investigating the tendency to distribute misinformation that is introduced in the current study has promising design features as compared to both authentic social media settings and hypothetical such with self-report measures. First, our procedure is relatively 'context free' as it holds constant what information participants are exposed to by asking them to imagine a situation where scientific data either supports or contradicts their prior beliefs, and to share how they would summarize the scientific data. Second, this paradigm constitutes an improved form of self-report measures of the willingness to distribute misinformation as it enhances the experience of an actual social media setting. What is more, it involves (hypothetical) decision making as well as a performance measure of the ability to evaluate the information in the scenarios. In future studies, researchers may want to use a similar paradigm in real social media settings to examine whether the results hold outside of fictive survey scenarios.
It is also noteworthy that the positive associations between social media activity level and belief in conspiracy theories and the misinformation distribution score, and the negative association with the accuracy score, all of which held up after controlling for competing predictors, corroborate previous findings of correlations between social media use and receptivity to misinformation (e.g. [54,55]). This further speaks to the relevance of a measure with a social media setting.
As for political orientation, social conservatism (versus liberalism) and economic rightist (versus leftist) ideological self-placement were both positively correlated with belief in conspiracy theories, but not with bullshit receptivity. Although we did not include predictions about political orientation, these findings are in line with previous research showing that conservatism is associated with greater conspiracy ideation (e.g. [56]) and in contrast to findings that conservatism is related to greater bullshit receptivity (e.g. [14]). The fact that we found no significant association between social conservatism (versus liberalism) and bullshit receptivity may be because we used a single-item self-placement measure of ideological position rather than measuring actual conservative preferences. Moreover, both social conservatism (versus liberalism) and economic rightist (versus leftist) ideology were positively related to the misinformation distribution score, and negatively to the accuracy score, also after controlling for demographics. This corroborates previous findings that conservatism is linked to greater sharing of misinformation [28] and informs debates about whether ideological asymmetries in misinformation susceptibility are limited to social ideology or whether they are similar in the economic ideology domain. The fact that both measures of political ideology predicted three out of four misinformation related variables (belief in conspiracy theories, the misinformation distribution score and its logical counterpart-but not opposite-the accuracy score) but not bullshit receptivity may be because those three measures cover political matters that are both social and economic. Nonetheless, the relation between belief in truth relativism and receptivity to-and distribution of- misinformation consistently remained after controlling for both measures of political ideology.
5. Conclusion
In the current research, we replicate previous findings of positive correlations between belief in truth relativism and susceptibility to misinformation, and present new evidence of a positive correlation between belief in truth relativism and distributing misinformation-over and above competing predictors. Our results also reveal that belief in truth realism is negatively associated with the distribution of misinformation. Our attempt to induce epistemic beliefs to examine whether their association with misinformation represents causal relations were mostly unsuccessful. We also introduce a promising novel paradigm for investigating the tendency to distribute scientific misinformation. In future studies on the receptivity to and distribution of misinformation, researchers should consider the role of epistemic beliefs in general and subjectivist truth relativism in particular.
