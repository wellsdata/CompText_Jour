1 Introduction Human papillomavirus (HPV) is a common infection, affecting about four out of five people at some point in their lives. While most HPV infections clear without causing clinically significant disease, low-risk HPV types such as 6 and 11 can cause genital warts and high-risk HPV types such as HPV 16, 18, 31, 33, 35, 39, 45, 51, 52, 56, 58, 59 and 68 are associated with certain cancers including cervical, oropharyngeal, anal, vulva, vaginal and penial cancers [ 1 ]. Socioeconomic status can be an important factor in cancer inequities, including cervical cancer. The Cancer Inequalities Report (2014–2018), published by the National Cancer Registry Ireland (NCRI) in February 2023 shows females in the most deprived quintile had a significantly higher age standardised incidence rate of cervical cancer (84 % higher) compared with those in the least deprived quintile [ 2 ].
The combination of HPV immunisation and regular cervical screening offers the best protection against HPV related cervical cancer [ 3 ]. Since the introduction of the HPV vaccine, many countries, including Ireland, have seen major declines in HPV infection and associated high-grade cervical neoplasia [ 4–7 ]. In 2020, the World Health Assembly adopted a global strategy for cervical cancer elimination. The focus of this strategy is on vaccination, screening and treatment. The World Health Organization (WHO) roadmap to cervical cancer aims to reduce cervical cancer to 8 ]. In Ireland, Migone et al. demonstrated that HPV vaccine uptake was lower in disadvantaged schools (79.4 % vs 85.0 %; difference 5.58 %, 95 % CI 2.69–8.21) compared to other schools during the 2013–2014 [ 9 ]. Ensuring equitable HPV vaccine uptake in disadvantaged populations is crucial to achieving the WHO goal.
In 2015–2016, Ireland experienced a period of negative publicity and misinformation surrounding the HPV vaccine and its safety leading to a sharp decline in vaccine uptake. Similar declines occurred in Denmark, France, Romania, Colombia and Japan [ 10–13 ]. In Ireland, the misinformation about HPV vaccine was amplified by lobby groups, and the airing of a documentary on national television “Cervical cancer vaccine- is it safe?”. Despite no evidence linking the vaccine to conditions such as postural orthostatic tachycardia syndrome (POTS) and related syndromes, uptake dropped [ 14 ]. The WHO and National Immunisation Advisory Committee (NIAC) in Ireland, continued to recommend HPV vaccination. In August 2017, the HPV Vaccination Alliance launched a multi-stakeholder campaign to address the decline in HPV vaccine uptake. The campaign used emotionally-based communication, featuring real-life stories like that of Laura Brennan, a cervical cancer advocate, combined with factual information about the vaccine [ 15 ]. This approach, along with efforts to counteract misinformation on social media, led to an increase in vaccine uptake in subsequent years [ 16 ]. Vaccine hesitancy is complex and influenced by demographic and socio-economic factors. Previous research showed lower HPV vaccine uptake in disadvantaged Irish schools, but no study has assessed the impact of the misinformation on these disadvantaged Irish schools compared to the non-disadvantaged schools. This study aims to determine if the HPV vaccine misinformation disproportionately affected Delivering Equality in Schools (DEIS) schools (i.e. socioeconomically disadvantaged schools) compared to non-DEIS schools. The analysis covers academic years 2013–2014 through to 2018–2019, focusing on three periods: 1) Prior to the misinformation (2013–2014/2014–2015), 2) During the misinformation (2015–2016/2016–2017) and 3) Recovery (2017–2018/2018–2019).
2 Methods This is a register-based cohort study using routinely collected data on HPV vaccine uptake in girls only, combined with data on the DEIS status of schools from the Department of Education (DOE), during academic years 2013–2014 to 2018–2019.
2.1 Setting In Ireland, a school-based immunisation programme for girls in first year of secondary school (12–13 years) started in 2010 with a three dose quadrivalent HPV vaccine schedule, changing to two doses in academic year 2014–2015. Local school immunisation teams deliver the vaccines. Uptake is recorded in the National School Immunisation System (SIS), overseen by the National Immunisation Office (NIO). SIS tracks school details, total number of girls enrolled in 1st year in each year (denominator), total number of vaccinated girls per year of school (numerator). The system records personal identifiers (name, age, personal public service (PPS) number), name of parent/guardian where age appropriate, record of consent or decline and data on children who were not vaccinated by virtue of non-attendance. It does not include individual demographic data like socio-economic group, ethnicity or parental education level. This information is used by the NIO and Health Protection Surveillance Centre (HPSC) to compile vaccine coverage statistics and guide vaccine programmes and communication strategies.
The DEIS programme, introduced in 2005 by the Department of Education (DOE) targets schools with high concentrations of students at risk of educational disadvantage. At secondary school level, DEIS inclusion is based on the percentage of students with a HP Deprivation Index of −10 or below indicating disadvantaged, very disadvantaged and extremely disadvantaged [ 17 ]. The target cohort for DEIS are those that are 1 Standard Deviation or more below the mean HP score [ 17 ]. Students identifying as traveller or Roma, homeless or residing in International Protection Accommodation were categorised as disadvantaged as part of the scheme. The total number of schools designated as DEIS fluctuates over time due to school openings, amalgamations, closures and changes to DEIS criteria. During the study period 2013–2019, about 30 % of secondary schools in Ireland were designated as DEIS schools. The DOE Post Primary School reports provide characteristics of the schools.
2.2 Defined study periods
2.2.1 Prior to the misinformation During academic years 2013–2014 and 2014–2015 HPV vaccine uptake was high, national figures at individual level, showed uptake peaking at 87.8 % in 2014–2015 [ 18 ], comparable to other school vaccines like TdaP (tetanus, diphtheria, pertussis) and Meningococcal C vaccine.
2.2.2 Period of misinformation In 2015–2016 and 2016–2017, negative publicity and misinformation about HPV vaccine safety lead to a sharp decline in uptake. This resulted in national uptake rates, at individual level, dropping to a low of 56.3 % in 2015–2016 [ 18 ].
2.2.3 Recovery period During 2017–2018 and 2018–2019, a multi-stakeholder HSE response with evidence-based messaging improved attitudes towards HPV vaccination, raising national HPV vaccine uptake, at individual level, to 73.7 % in 2018–2019 [ 18 ].
2.3 Study population This study population included HPV vaccine-eligible girls (∼12 years old) in 1st year of secondary school in Ireland, who were offered the vaccine from academic years 2013 -2014 through to 2018–2019. It covered girls-only and mixed-gender schools (female students only). Schools for students with disabilities were excluded as their immunisation is age-based. Boys-only schools were excluded since boys were not vaccinated during the study period. Data from 2019 -2020 onwards was excluded due to the impact of COVID-19 on the vaccine programme.
Aggregate data on vaccine uptake by school was collected from the SIS. DEIS status of all schools were identified from the 2018–2019 Department of Education Post Primary report [ 19 ] and combined with the data extracted from SIS. First, an analysis was performed which included only schools available across the three study periods to ensure minimal fluctuations in the number of DEIS schools for each academic year did not impact on the findings. This analysis confirmed that there was no change to the study findings when all schools were included. In the interest of presenting the complete real-time national landscape, we have presented the data as HPV uptake across all eligible schools for each timeframe.
2.4 Data analysis The primary outcome variable “complete vaccine uptake percentage” was calculated as the percentage of eligible girls who completed the vaccine schedule for each school. For academic year 2013–2014, a completed schedule consisted of 3 doses of HPV vaccine. From 2014–2015 onwards, a completed schedule consisted of 2 doses of vaccine. Vaccine uptake percentage was calculated for DEIS and non-DEIS schools. The general linear model univariate analysis function was used to analyse the HPV vaccine uptake by DEIS status with the complete vaccine uptake percentage (dependent variable), DEIS status (fixed factors). This was performed for each respective academic year. The level of statistical significance was set at 0.05.
3 Results
3.1 Study population There were between 30,000 and 32,000 girls eligible for vaccination in each relevant academic year. Table 2 shows the breakdown of the number of DEIS schools and non-DEIS schools and total number of eligible students per year (total number of female students enrolled for the relevant academic year).
3.2 HPV vaccine uptake status HPV vaccine uptake was examined among three categories of student i) Those who had complete HPV vaccine uptake; ii) Those who had incomplete HPV vaccine uptake; iii) Those who were eligible but did not participate in the vaccine programme. Fig. 1 shows that the complete HPV vaccination rate was highest in the early years of the study, reaching 84.8 % (95 % CI 84.7–84.9) in 2014–2015. However, uptake declined to a low of 55.2 % (95 % CI 54.9–55.5) in 2016–2017. There was an increase in uptake in 2017–2018 and again in 2018–2019, reaching 72.9 % (95 % CI 72.7–73.1). During this period, the overall uptake of other school immunisation programs (MenC and Tdap) remained stable, consistently above 80 % year-on-year from 2013 to 2019 (data not shown). All further analyses were performed on complete vaccination data only.
3.3 HPV vaccine uptake across DEIS and non-DEIS Schools Fig. 2 shows percentage complete HPV vaccine uptake in DEIS and non-DEIS schools between academic years 2013–2014 and 2018–2019. There is a lower HPV vaccine uptake in DEIS schools (green line) compared to the non-DEIS schools (blue line) and this difference can be seen throughout the years (2013–2019).
While mean HPV vaccination was initially high in the early years of the immunisation programme for both DEIS and non-DEIS schools a widening gap can be seen in subsequent academic years. During the period of misinformation, vaccine uptake dropped significantly to 57.6 % (95 % CI 56.3–59.0) in non-DEIS and 49.6 % (95 % CI 47.1–52.1) in DEIS schools. The gap widened further during the recovery period from 76.5 % (95 % CI 75.2–77.8) in non-DEIS schools to 64.5 % (95 % CI 62.0–67.1) in DEIS schools.
The general linear model was used to analyse the mean difference in percentages of vaccine uptake the between the DEIS and non-DEIS schools from academic year 2013–2014 to 2018–2019 ( Table 3 ). A Univariate analysis was performed individually for each academic year. There is a statistically significant difference between uptake in DEIS and non-DEIS schools throughout the study period. The mean difference increases from the 2016–2017 academic year onwards with the mean difference in vaccine uptake percentage increasing from 4.4 % in 2013–2014 to 12.0 % in 2018–2019.
3.4 HPV Vaccine uptake level of  ≥85 % During the period 2013–2018, the HSE set a HPV vaccine uptake target of 85 %. Before the misinformation period, 65 % of schools met this target. However, during the misinformation period, the percentage of schools achieving the target plummeted to 2.4 %. In the recovery period, the proportion of schools meeting the target increased to 21.6 %.
Fig. 3 illustrates the percentage of schools that achieved ≥85 % HPV vaccine uptake. Overall, a higher percentage of the non-DEIS schools met the HSE target compared to DEIS schools. The disparity was greatest in 2014–2015, with 71.0 % of non-DEIS schools versus 51.2 % of DEIS schools reaching the target ( p p  = 0.90), during which the overall percentage of schools meeting the ≥85 % was also low ( n  = 14).
4 Discussion
4.1 Principle findings In this study population, HPV vaccination was highest in the early years of the national immunisation schedule, reaching 84.8 % in 2013–2014. However, during the misinformation period uptake dropped to 55.2 % in 2016–2017. Although there was a partial recovery in 2018–2019, HPV vaccine uptake did not return to initial levels, reaching 78.4 % in 2022–2023, still below the 90 % target set by the WHO as part of the elimination of cervical cancer initiative.
Migone et al. previously showed lower HPV vaccine uptake in DEIS schools compared to non-DEIS schools in a single academic year only (2013–2014) [ 9 ]. This study confirms and expands on those findings, showing that the disparity persisted over six academic years (2013–2019), widened during the misinformation period, peaking in the recovery period (2017–2019). This suggests that disadvantaged schools were disproportionately affected by the misinformation. The multi-stakeholder campaign may not have been as effective in these socioeconomically deprived schools, indicating a need for targeted interventions. This is of particular importance as women living in the lowest deprivation quintiles have a higher incidence of cervical cancer, underscoring the need for focused HPV vaccination promotion [ 2 ].
The reasons for reduced HPV vaccine uptake and the limited response to the multi-stakeholder campaign are complex and need further investigation. School-based vaccination programmes are effective but face challenges in DEIS schools due to higher levels of absenteeism (including expulsion and suspension) compared to non-DEIS schools. As a result, students may not be present on the day of vaccination [ 20 ]. Previous studies have highlighted the importance of healthcare professionals, with many parents reporting that they would discuss concerns around vaccination with their GP as a trusted source of information [ 21 , 22 ]. In Ireland, access to GPs is more difficult in deprived areas, which may hinder accurate information dissemination. Further to this, a report published by the Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD) found that disadvantaged groups also tend to have lower trust in the government, affecting the impact of government-led campaigns in areas of deprivation compared to areas that are more affluent. Women and people with less education and lower incomes tend to trust the government less and may get their information elsewhere [ 23 ]. Decision on vaccine uptake of school immunisations is largely made by parents, mothers in particular [ 22 ]. Overall parents of students in DEIS schools have lower levels of education compared to parents of children in non-DEIS schools and come from socio-economically disadvantaged homes [ 24 ]. It has been suggested that those with lower education status may be more susceptible to misinformation. This can lead to a more pronounced response to misinformation and make it more challenging to guide them towards correct information [ 25 ]. Local advocates in Ireland like Laura Brennan, advocated for HPV vaccination before her untimely death in 2019 from cervical cancer, have been influential in promoting vaccination.
Qualitative studies on parent's attitudes to HPV vaccination have identified influences and barriers in decision making around vaccination. Internationally, systematic reviews show that parents have concerns about HPV vaccine safety, timing of the vaccine and about sex-related issues [ 26 , 27 ]. In Ireland, a study found that parents were concerned about side effects of the HPV vaccine and the timing (age) of vaccination [ 22 ]. The authors reported that radio talk shows were a prominent source of information. Effective collaboration between health services and media is crucial for accurate information dissemination. Risk perception, cancer risk, and side effects were major decision drivers [ 22 ]. The study which includes 39 % of participants with children in DEIS schools revealed parents felt their daughters were too young for the vaccine and lacked HPV knowledge. [ 22 ].
HPV Vaccination Catch-Up Programmes have run at various times since the decreased vaccine uptake and offer the opportunity to further avail of vaccination for those who may have missed the HPV vaccine previously. Although not included in this analysis, these clinics could address the issue of missed vaccinations during and after the misinformation period if they are tailored to underserved communities to ensure equitable vaccine uptake. While national media campaigns promoted the catch-up programmes, they did not specifically target lower socioeconomic groups or DEIS schools nor tailored for disadvantaged communities. However, some regions made additional efforts to reach these groups, recognising the lower uptake in DEIS schools and the higher eligibility for catch-up. A study in Vancouver found that regions offering repeat HPV vaccine doses in later academic years had higher uptake, compared to regions that did not reoffer at a later time [ 28 ]. This could help with school absenteeism seen in more disadvantages areas. A Swedish study showed that nurse-provided face-to-face education in schools increased HPV vaccination rates. Such initiatives are not currently in place in Ireland but could be beneficial for DEIS schools in Ireland [ 29 ].
4.2 Strengths and limitations This is the first study to show the impact of the misinformation period on HPV vaccine uptake in the Irish School Immunisation Programme. Analysing data from 3528 schools records, encompassing 187,675 students over six academic years. It provides crucial evidence on inequities worsened by misinformation, this is important to inform further directions for the HPV vaccination programme.
The study focused on 12 to 13 year old girls vaccinated in the schools immunisation programme, excluding boys and students attending special schools. While findings may be relevant to these groups, the data cannot be extrapolated beyond the studied population. The study used aggregate data, preventing analysis of individual level characteristics like home address for area-level deprivation analysis. Address data during the study period was not of sufficient quality to match addresses to small area deprivation scores. Due to the introduction of Ireland's postcode system (Eircode) in July 2015 and its inconsistent collection, DEIS status of schools was used as a proxy for socioeconomic status of students. Retrospective entry of Eircodes was not feasible in the timeline of this study. The study measures vaccination trends over time, but it cannot imply causation and potential bias or unmeasured confounding factors cannot be ruled out.
In 2014–2015 the HPV vaccination programme changed from a three-dose to a two dose schedule with almost no impact on complete vaccine uptake in both DEIS and non-DEIS schools. However, this was prior to the misinformation period and the impact of this schedule change, if any, during the misinformation period in DEIS compared to non-DEIS schools cannot be determined by this study.
5 Conclusions This study revealed a significant difference in HPV vaccine uptake between DEIS and non-DEIS schools, with a more pronounced decline during the misinformation period in DEIS schools. The gap in uptake between these schools widened over time, highlighting the disproportionate impact of misinformation on disadvantaged schools. Given the strong link between disadvantage and cervical cancer incidence, these findings underscore the need for targeted interventions in disadvantaged schools.
The study also emphasizes the importance of monitoring vaccine uptake and shows the positive impact of the HSE's information campaign on increasing HPV vaccine uptake. The campaign's success, involving factual communication and personal stories, suggests that combating misinformation is crucial. The findings contribute to evidence on the effects of misinformation on vaccine uptake in deprived populations and highlight the need for proactive measures to prevent misinformation and address vaccine inequalities.

Specific Intervention Details
Provide Information and Time • Information leaflets were provided to parents early. • Vaccination sessions in schools were delayed to give parents extra time. • An additional fact sheet about the HPV vaccine and cervical cancer was provided.
Ensuring Access To Trusted Sources Of Facts • Website www.hpv.ie was updated for better user experience and received WHO accreditation. • Testimonials from parents and from young people who had had the HPV vaccine were included. Videos from healthcare professionals highlighting the importance of the HPV vaccine were included. • Compared the possible side effects of getting Cervical Cancer with the possible side effects of getting the HPV Vaccine.
Catch-Up Programme • Provided an additional opportunity to join the vaccination programme.
Build Relationships with Cancer Groups • Collaboration with the Irish Cancer Society, National Cancer Control Programme, and Cerviva (a multidisciplinary research consortium promoting quality HPV research). Developed consistent messaging about cervical cancer morbidity and mortality.
Engage with Other Groups to Promote Vaccination • HPV Alliance launched by the Irish Cancer Society in 2017, to bring together organisations to present facts about the HPV vaccine. • Engaged with the Department of Education and school bodies, providing information about HPV to teachers and principals. • The Health Minister and HSE board members launched the HPV campaign each year.
Provide Training to Health Professionals • General Practitioner (GP) e-learning modules, Irish College of General Practitioners (ICGP) meetings, Royal College of Physicians Ireland (RCPI) faculty meetings, Pharmacists, factsheets, articles in journals, talks during training days, and national conferences.
Social Media Listening and Data Studio Analysis • Communication teams monitored discussions on the topic and analysed visitor data, including page visits and search terms. • Social Media Campaign used patient advocacy stories and videos, with consistent messaging and slogans like #ProtectOurFuture.
Social Media Platforms • Implemented processes to help prevent the sharing of misinformation.
