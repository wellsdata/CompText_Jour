1 Introduction
1.1 Vaccine misinformation
The pervasive spread of vaccine misinformation has accelerated in the years leading up to and since the SARS-CoV-2 pandemic [ 1 , 2 ]. Misinformation is false, inaccurate, or misleading information that is spread by uninformed or incompletely informed agents [ 3–5 ]. Cognitive scientists define misinformation belief as the failure to identify logical fallacies (e.g., ad hominem, personal attacks, cherry-picking) [ 6 , 7 ], while social scientists describe misinformation belief as stemming from sociocultural factors, including one's world view [ 8–10 ] and mistrust in institutions and government [ 11–15 ]. Many vaccine-related conspiracy theories provoke and reinforce distrust of scientific, medical and government institutions.
Several studies have identified misinformation as one of the causes of decreasing vaccination rates and accompanying spikes in vaccine preventable diseases in parts of Australia and other countries [ 16–18 ]. A large-scale randomised control trial involving 8001 participants conducted during the COVID-19 pandemic (September 2020) measured the impact of exposure to misinformation on intention to receive a COVID-19 vaccine. The trial estimated that misinformation could reduce intentions to “definitely” receive a COVID-19 vaccine for approximately 6 % of the UK population (54 % to 48 %) and the US (43 % to 37 %), with misinformation that was perceived by the public to sound scientific being most effective in changing intentions [ 19 ].
1.2 Inoculation against misinformation
Stemming from cognitive science, ‘psychological inoculation’ proposes that teaching individuals to recognise misleading rhetorical techniques could establish resilience to future exposure to misinformation [ 20–22 ]. Gamified inoculation interventions use game-like elements (e.g., rewards, quizzes) to engage players and reinforce content. These interventions have demonstrated significant reductions in misinformation credibility assessment and discernment, and intentions to share the misinformation, however little effect on true health information discernment and sharing intentions [ 23 ].
Cranky Uncle – Vaccine is an online vaccine misinformation inoculation game [ 24 ] where the player progresses through ten levels, each giving an explanation and example of a technique commonly used to spread vaccine misinformation. Players complete interactive quizzes before progressing a level. The misinformation disseminator is ‘Cranky Uncle’, a character who becomes humorously angrier as the player progresses. A health care professional refutes Cranky Uncle and educates the player, and four other characters provide diversity and additional information in the quizzes. The game's characters and script have been adapted for Kenya, Uganda, Rwanda, and Ghana through co-design methods [ 25 , 26 ]. This adaptation process allows each culture to shape the game to be culturally relevant for them. The game has been applied to school settings [ 24 ], is a recommended gamified inoculation intervention by UNICEF [ 17 ], and its effectiveness in teaching misinformation discernment and resistance has been demonstrated in a prospective trial [ 27 ].
1.3 Misinformation susceptibility and vaccine hesitancy among the Arabic-speaking community in Australia
Arabic is the third most commonly spoken language in Australia, and the seventh most common in the state of Victoria [ 28 ]. Victoria is home to 25 % of Australia's Arabic speaking population (approx. 90,000 people), most of whom emigrated in the last 15 years [ 29 ].
The influence of sociocultural factors on vaccine misinformation susceptibility and hesitancy was evident in the disproportionate impact of the SARS-CoV-2 virus on the Arabic-speaking communities. By June 2022, people born in the Middle East had the highest age-standardized death rate from COVID-19 at 46.9 deaths per 100,000 people, compared with those born in Australia, at 7.6 deaths per 100,000 [ 30 ]. The context and mechanisms of misinformation beliefs and sharing in Arabic-speaking communities in Australia have not been described in existing studies on COVID-19 vaccine misinformation [e.g., [31,32] ].
1.4 Aims
In 2023, the Victorian state government supported a project to adapt and evaluate the Cranky Uncle – Vaccine game for an Arabic-speaking Australian audience. In this paper, we describe the community consultation process that informed the cultural adaptation of the game.
This study aimed to explore the content, context and mechanisms by which vaccine misinformation is shared and countered in Arabic-speaking Australian communities to inform the adaptation of a psychological inoculation intervention.
2 Method
2.1 Study design
We conducted a qualitative study involving community focus groups to explore vaccine misinformation beliefs and experiences, followed by workshops where misinformation beliefs were discussed in the context of adapting the intervention. We applied a transcendental (descriptive) phenomenological approach to data collection and analysis [ 33–35 ], reflecting methodological principles of openness to participants' lifeworld, questioning researcher pre-understanding, and adopting a reflective attitude [ 36 ]. This method was chosen because it permits an exploratory approach to understanding the subjectivity of phenomena as they are lived and perceived by individuals.
2.2 Participants and recruitment
The same method of recruitment was used for the focus groups and workshops. Participants were eligible to take part if they were aged 16 years or older, lived in Victoria, Australia, and spoke Arabic. To ensure new and diverse voices and views were considered, those who had participated in the focus groups were ineligible to participate in a workshop. Young people were targeted in ensure their representation, in anticipation that they may be a target audience for the game's dissemination.
Recruitment plans and materials for both project phases were developed and implemented in collaboration with project's Arabic Community Advisory Group. Focus group discussion participants were recruited from November through December 2023, and workshop participants in February 2024. Flyers were shared through social media and instant messaging apps, as well as printed to be shared offline. The advisory group disseminated the flyers through their formal and informal networks in the Arabic-speaking communities in Victoria, Australia. For the workshops, flyers were also disseminated to online groups with large local Arabic-speaking memberships. The flyers had a link and QR code to a REDCap form that provided a participant information sheet, confirmed eligibility and registered individuals' preferred contact details and availability. Interested individuals were contacted by SV (in English) and KN (in Arabic) to answer questions, register their informed consent and interpreter preferences, and schedule their participation.
2.3 Data collection
2.3.1 Focus groups
Semi-structured online focus group discussions were held over Zoom from December 2023 to January 2024. Group sizes varied from 2 to 6 participants, plus an Arabic-English translator was available if requested by a participant. Focus groups were facilitated by two English-speaking researchers with qualitative research expertise (SV, female, PhD Psychology and JK, female, PhD Public Health). Participants were first asked to share myths or misinformation they had heard circulated in their community, focusing initially on COVID-19 and then on routine vaccines. Then they were asked questions like “Who were the main people who spread these myths?”, “Who did they most strongly influence?”, and “How did you see misinformation affecting people's views or behaviours?” The sessions were approximately 60 min long and were audio recorded using the integrated Zoom recording function. Recordings were professionally transcribed, and participants were sent a $20 digital gift card as honorarium after their participation.
2.3.2 Group workshops
Two face-to-face group workshops were held on separate days in March 2024 to introduce and discuss the concept of the online game Cranky Uncle - Vaccine and adapt the characters for an Arabic-speaking audience in Australia. The workshops used the methods established through previous adaptations [ 25 , 26 ]. During activities to adapt the script and characters, participants were prompted with open-ended questions to describe vaccine myths they had heard, and to reflect on how misinformation tended to spread in their community and among whom. Detailed feedback on the game characters, structure and script will be published with those of a randomised control trial of the game's effectiveness in teaching vaccine misinformation discernment. The in-person workshop environment allowed participants to play the game and discuss the script in small, facilitated groups.
Professional Arabic-English translators were present at both workshops to interpret between the English-speaking researchers and participants. The translators also facilitated small-group discussions during structured workshop activities. Each workshop took 3.5 h, including breaks. Participants were given a $200 electronic gift card as honorarium after their participation. Audio recordings were taken with the consent of all participants. These recordings were not transcribed, but detailed notes of observations were taken by the researchers and translators, and were discussed in a post-workshop debrief.
2.3.3 Data analysis
Data from the focus group and workshop discussions relating to the context and mechanisms of misinformation belief and sharing were combined and analysed together. Themes were defined through an inductive, iterative process of examination and re-examination of data, and identification of repeated and salient themes, while capturing reflections throughout the process [ 36 ]. Using a deductive method to address the research questions directly, these themes were then grouped under the broader constructs of interest – the content, social contexts and mechanisms by which misinformation was spread. Discussions between researchers and facilitators helped to define interactions between the parts (data) and the whole (phenomenon) [ 35 , 37 ], and to strengthen validity and neutrality of analyses [ 38 ].
2.3.4 Reflexivity statement
A priority of the research team was to undertake the study with cultural sensitivity and community guidance at all stages. Most members of the research team do not share a cultural background with the Arabic-speaking community, thereby limiting our cultural capability. These researchers relied on guidance from an Arabic-speaking research team member (KN, male, Diploma Community Services, Community Liaison Worker) and an advisory group composed of leaders from the Arabic-speaking community in Victoria, Australia. This group met approximately every 6 weeks throughout the project and communicated between meetings as required. When collecting data, researchers who do not speak Arabic applied the principles of transcendental phenomenology of suspending preconceptions and openness to the participants' words and constructions [ 34 ].
Another critical concern for this project was the need for sensitivity in all participant interactions around the COVID-19 pandemic. This community experienced among the highest rates of infection and deaths in Australia and had been stigmatised by the broader community. This may have caused the researchers to probe more gently to avoid highly sensitive discussions that were not directly related to the topic of interest.
2.4 Ethical approval
This study was approved by the [Removed for anonymity] Human Research Ethics Committee (HREC 100909).
3 Results
3.1 Participants
Eighty-one individuals participated in one of four focus groups ( n  = 16 participants) or two workshops ( n  = 65). Women and younger adults aged 18 to 45 years were over-represented (75 % and 65 % of participants, respectively), with most participants in the 36–45 age group. A substantial proportion of participants preferred to speak in Arabic, although all were able to communicate with non-Arabic speaking researchers in English. Religious identity was not explicitly asked, however discussions revealed that the focus groups comprised female Christians, while the workshops included Christian and Muslim attendees of both genders. 3.2 Content of common vaccine misinformation
All participants had heard more than one piece of misinformation about COVID-19 vaccines and reported regular and widespread dissemination of misinformation across the community throughout the pandemic (see Table 2 for a list of vaccine misinformation, grouped by themes presented in Pickles et al. [ 12 ]).
“We ' ve heard that some issues about [the COVID-19] vaccine and our people, they develop mental health issues.” (P4.1).
“They say that they will insert a chip in your body, and they will be tracking you. They will get your confidential information. They will attack your security.” (P4.2).
“…rumours [that the COVID-19] vaccine is going to reduce immunity for humanity. They can ' t protect themselves … even from the simple diseases.” (P1.1).
Participants did not report hearing misinformation about other vaccines, though it was unclear whether this reflected an actual lack of circulating misinformation or if COVID-19 vaccine misinformation overshadowed that of other vaccines in participants' memories. There was general low awareness of vaccines other than the routine childhood vaccines. Participants in the workshops were eager for more information about different types of vaccines to be added to the game to improve awareness and understanding.

3.3 Social context
Information context: Seeking the Goldilocks Zone of vaccine information.
The community has access to many sources of formal and informal information, but participants indicated that where there is a dearth of clear, consistent, culturally appropriate information, susceptibility to misinformation increases. In other words, misinformation thrived whenever there was too much or too little available information (anything outside the “Goldilocks Zone”).
During the COVID-19 pandemic in particular, many found it difficult to make sense of all the different types of information being presented. For example, death and disease rates presented without sufficient context about how vaccines work led to mistrust and uncertainty:
“They used to post on Nine News that today this is the deaths that we had, and this is the amount of people that were sick with COVID vaccines for the day, and I think people … just thought that the COVID vaccine is not working.” (P2.2).
Participants also spoke about the destabilising impacts of inconsistent messaging from trusted sources of health information:
“I ' ve asked my husband to take the whooping cough [vaccine] but he didn ' t want to take it. He goes ‘No, I ' ve asked my GP, and my GP said it ' s fine’ and I said to him ‘Well my GP said you have to take it’ and we were like really confused about it.” (P3.1).
While appropriate and accurate translation of information is important, participants explained that even written materials in Arabic were unlikely to be accessible to those with poor reading or health literacy and may be considered less trustworthy than information delivered by a respected member of their own community. Official written information about the pandemic and vaccines, whether delivered in Arabic or English language, was likely to be less accessible and acceptable to this segment of the community and potentially met with suspicion.
“Sometimes the Arabic that it ' s been translated to is very formal, so they won ' t understand, … especially if they don ' t have a lot of health literacy background, so I think it would be more beneficial of someone, like expert conveying the information to them rather than reading.” (P2.5).
“…that ' s why they [rely on] social media; because there ' s a lot of images, and there ' s a lot of experiences, like documented on video. That ' s why they believe that more.” (P2.3).
Participants suggested that public health communication would be delivered effectively when face-to-face by a trusted community member, who could deliver complex information succinctly, in dialect, and with appropriate cultural competence.
3.4 Personal context: seeking hope and normality
Providing examples from the COVID-19 pandemic, participants described how misinformation can take hold when people are looking for reassurance and relief from fear and anxiety. Misinformation that suggested that the virus was not a high-risk pathogen, or that there were low-cost and accessible methods of prevention and treatment, served to ease anxiety and distress. Many people also sought and trusted information that reinforced their cultural background and traditions:
“When the COVID start there is many people, they say, ‘Okay, nothing ' s happened here, and all good.’ …Overseas in Iraq, or in my country, there is many people, they say ‘That ' s all lying, the corona, it ' s not a real thing’.” (P2.5).
“Our community more like old people, like they … relied on herb medication, not vaccines or tablets.” (P2.4).
However, observing illness and deaths in the community as the virus spread made some people more resistant to this kind of misinformation, particularly as they saw positive effects of vaccination.
“Many people honestly they rejected the idea [of taking the COVID-19 vaccine] at the beginning but then once they have seen that they getting sick and it ' s death behind there.” (P3.3).
“We ' ve heard many … cases that pass away of the disease … they are our relatives [and] from our social networks. We ' ve got other people who get the vaccine, they get to recover; they have very light symptoms.” (P4.2).
3.5 Mechanisms of misinformation dissemination in the community
3.5.1 Disseminators
All participants knew a person who had shared vaccine misinformation. However, there were no shared characteristics defining those people, suggesting that people of any age, gender and background could be sharing vaccine misinformation. The research team was concerned that the diversity of agents sharing misinformation may reduce the relatability of the Cranky Uncle character of the game as the disseminator and require his gender, age or other characteristics to be adapted. When explicitly explored in the workshops, there was no resistance to the character, and in fact it resonated with participants. A minority of younger participants (20–30 years of age) suggested that the character should not be too elderly because it would be culturally inappropriate to refute an elder. Participants agreed that it was appropriate for the Cranky Uncle character to look approximately 50–60 years of age. It was also important that he was not too ignorant, so that he remained a respected elder and was a realistic representation of the disseminators they knew.
3.5.2 Dissemination media
Misinformation was most common and impactful when spread through word-of-mouth because friends and family were trusted, communicated in a culturally relevant manner and were easily understood. Social media was described as a common source of misinformation for people of all ages, though most participants said they personally did not view it as a credible source. One reason people suggested that others might seek information on social media was that videos tended to be more accessible to individuals with less health literacy.
“…somebody that might not have the language, like English language or a way to communicate, might be more influenced by social media, and also would always fight that social media always gives us good tips about life in general, and vaccines.” (P2.1).
Young people reported seeing videos on Instagram and TikTok that they thought were probably untrue, but they weren't completely confident. For example, one participant tested whether the vaccine contained magnets by placing a dollar coin on her arm to see it would ‘stick’. Her laugher while telling the story was an attempt to minimise the concern she had clearly felt after seeing the video, evidenced by her concerned expression.
“They used to say, ‘It ' s magnetic. There ' s something dangerous they ' ve putting your body.’ [Laughs] That was a little bit funny as well. And I tried it, but it didn ' t work, so it was just tricking people [Laughs] . ” (P2.2).
Some participants said they specifically avoided vaccine-related information from social media because they were motivated to be vaccinated by the illness and deaths in their community and did not want to be confused or conflicted.
Importantly, the reach of misinformation on social media was not restricted to individuals with direct access to those platforms. Misinformation initially seen on social media also spread through word of mouth to people who did not personally have access to it. This means that even if people were intentionally avoiding social media, they could still be an audience of the misinformation through their familial and social networks. Through the interaction between digital and word-of-mouth pathways, misinformation spread widely, regardless of age, accessibility to platforms or community groups.
“So, it might be a good way to raise people ' s awareness about the vaccine through multiple platforms and documentary … and provide some time for people to digest … issues about the vaccine as well.” (P4.2).
3.5.3 Misinformation audiences
COVID-19 vaccine misinformation had a broad audience of all ages, cultural and religious groups within the Arabic-speaking community. However, there was a general belief that individuals with higher levels of education and those who had grown up in Australia were less likely to believe or share misinformation because of their English and health literacy.
“Because some people they ' re not educated. They will believe in these things if they hear it from other people. …especially older people. They don ' t know what ' s going around. They would believe in these things.” (P4.4).
Individuals with less reading fluency, in English or Arabic, were seen as less trusting of official information and more susceptible to misinformation on social media because of its use of images, video and personal experiences communicated in lay language. This is often older people who emigrated later in life and whose education was affected by war or discrimination.
“When it ' s English they don ' t really understand what people are saying; that ' s why they go off [to] social media, because there ' s a lot of images, and there ' s a lot of experiences, like documented on video. That ' s why they believe that more.” (P2.1).
“But I think when someone ' s speaking in Arabic [in an educational message] they would understand more than the written language, because … a small amount of people don ' t know how to read Arabic as well, they only speak Arabic.” (P2.1).
3.5.4 Myth disruptors: trusted community members
University-educated people (especially trusted if educated in Australia) and health professionals were community members who were trusted to provide accurate and relevant vaccine information. While in traditional Arab culture, older people lead and guide younger people, participants explained that many parents relied on their adolescent and young adult children to communicate vaccine information, advice, regulations and policy throughout the pandemic.
“We might know some, like more stuff than them, because we speak better English, and we can communicate properly.” (P2.1).
Although young people had been exposed to misinformation and may have been inclined to believe it, they were also tasked with the responsibility of keeping the family updated on the quickly changing health and regulatory landscape. Some young people explained that they tended to use online search engines to find medical or government websites and considered Australian sites to be very relevant and credible.
“My aunt and my mum and the older generation, like 60-70, they were waiting for us to translate the new [official pandemic] information. So, we would have to jot down the new regulations or the new restrictions when there was a [media] release it was, like, a follow up from one of the cousins who can read and speak English.” (P3.1).
“I search things on the internet, and it has to be from a credible resource. It has to be like AU, no American website, Canadian, whatever. And then if it ' s a .au it has to be something like a health organisation, scholars, something that is a study or by a, like, Monash [University or Health] .” (P3.1).
Older participants also explained that they attend health information sessions that are regularly delivered by local Arabic-speaking medical professionals and community health workers. They provide talks on a range of health issues, and during the pandemic, these sessions focused on providing up-to-date information on the virus and its vaccines.
“I always attend the sessions in our like maybe community gathering or religious places or whatever, … and they always educate us that we need to follow the advices from the government. So, then I educate my mind and then I go ahead with it.” (P3.4).
These sessions are open to all community members, however, some “stay at home, they don't understand what's going around and they will be locking their mind against it” (P3.4).
3.5.5 Implications for the game's adaptation and dissemination
In the focus groups, participants were asked about the potential audience for a gamified inoculation app, and in the workshops, participants provided feedback on the Cranky Uncle - Vaccine game's characters, content and potential audience.
There was general support and enthusiasm for a psychological inoculation game on vaccines. Two key suggested audiences were secondary school children who could play the game as part of their health education curriculum, and older people who attend Arabic-speaking community groups, where health information sessions are regularly held. This approach would support preventative efforts and target susceptible groups. In the workshops, these age groups showed good engagement with the game and its principles, and adolescents navigated the game with ease while some older people needed a little assistance when starting to play. After having played Cranky Uncle Vaccine , workshop participants suggested that it was important for players to receive additional information about vaccines and the diseases they prevent, and that if it is played in school or community education settings, that broader health education could complement the misinformation discernment skill development offered by the game. While the game referred to vaccine misinformation in general, participants also suggested that information and analogies in the game should directly refer to different vaccines to educate player and help them recognise the generalisability of the game to vaccines beyond COVID-19.
“I ' d say like middle aged people to older people, I feel like they ' d find this very beneficial as well to be educated from like to know what to believe or not and like what sounds like a conspiracy theory and what does not and doesn ' t sound as realistic as it should be.” (P3.2).
4 Discussion
This paper discusses the findings from a series of Arabic-speaking community consultations to support the adaptation of the Cranky Uncle – Vaccine digital misinformation inoculation game. We explored the content, context and mechanisms of vaccine misinformation beliefs and dissemination among Arabic-speakers. The study found that Arabic-speaking communities in Australia were more susceptible to misinformation when seeking accessible information and a sense of normality amid personal risk. This was reflected in the themes of the most salient misinformation, which centred around vaccine side effects and explanations for the COVID-19 pandemic's origins. Misinformation spread through online and offline channels, reaching across different demographic groups. However, formal education may have moderated belief in misinformation. Trusted community leaders and young adults played a crucial role in disrupting misinformation by accessing and communicating official information in a culturally appropriate manner. Participants suggested that Cranky Uncle – Vaccine would have good utility as an interactive health education tool that is delivered in face-to-face structured educational setting with secondary school children and older people. As has been suggested in previous adaptations [ 25 , 26 ], the game would also benefit from integrating dynamic graphics and video to improve accessibility.
Two contexts of misinformation beliefs and sharing emerged– the information and personal contexts. The information context was one in which a lack of clear, consistent, and culturally appropriate information (the Goldilocks zone of information that is ‘just right’) left people confused and distrustful. It is important for any intervention against misinformation to address the issue of missing information evolving into misperceptions [ 39 ]. The personal context was of fear and uncertainty of the vaccine's side-effects, and secondarily of mistrust in authorities. Although conspiratorial misinformation was most reported and top-of-mind, participants were most concerned about potential side-effects they had heard of through misinformation. This observation is congruent with an Australian survey that found that 40 % of participants believed misinformation about COVID-19 vaccine side effects, while only 14 % believed conspiratorial misinformation [ 12 ]. This may be because the consequences of side effects are direct and personal, and poor health literacy may increase uncertainty around medical issues like side effects.
In all discussions, participants reported that trusted community members are essential to countering misinformation and disseminating health information. These individuals included health professionals and university-educated people, and they were more strongly trusted if educated in Australia. They model behaviour and communicate health information using culturally appropriate language and channels, both informally and formally. Previous research has shown that unmet vaccine information needs of new migrant populations will often be filled by misinformation and reinforce distrust in vaccines and health authorities [ 11 , 39–41 ]. Therefore, culturally appropriate, community-led and consistent health communications may not only promote healthy behaviours but also diminish the influence of misinformation and enhance trust in health authorities. Building a broader foundation of trust was a recommendation given by Australians born in the World Health Organization's Eastern Mediterranean Region in a study of barriers to accessing official information about COVID-19 vaccines [ 41 ]. Australian studies [ 42 , 43 ] have demonstrated that practitioners with high cultural competence can tailor information more effectively to a community's values, norms and needs, such as using simplified health information for low health literacy, contextualising cultural health practices, providing information for non-Medicare recipients, and explaining Australian and overseas immunisation approaches and vaccines. While trusted community members are often ideal health communicators, cultural competence education for cross-cultural communicators can significantly improve accessibility and trustworthiness of the communication [ 42 ].
The mechanisms of misinformation belief and sharing among Arabic-speakers in this study were similar to those reported in other countries during the pandemic [ 6 , 44 , 45 ]. Vaccine misinformation achieved deep penetration into the Arabic-speaking community during the COVID-19 pandemic by being easy share online and offline, through social media and established and trusted networks of friends and family. Also, its content was communicated online via videos and pictures that were accessible regardless of language, health literacy, reading comprehension abilities, or social media competence. The content was emotive and reinforced existing fears and beliefs about health, medicine, vaccines, and institutions. This supported the generation of a substantial list of COVID-19 vaccine misinformation in the study. Although participants' familial and social networks were global, most believed that the misinformation they saw and heard was locally sourced, which gave it greater credibility and relevance.
The findings lend support for the utility of the Arabic version of the Cranky Uncle – Vaccine game disseminated and promoted by trusted community members. The established process of adaptation of this game [ 26 ], which includes discussions of the context and mechanisms of misinformation sharing, is community-centred and results in characters that reflect the community, and a plain language script with information that addresses the community's vaccine concerns. As recommended by participants, the characters represent a broad cross-section of the community because the audience for misinformation is broad. The myth disruptor is a health worker, who meets the expectations that this individual should be well-educated and connected to the Arabic-speaking and mainstream Australian communities. Importantly, the game is unlikely to have a large organic uptake from individuals because the community operates within a collective framework that prioritises group activities. It would be most readily accepted by the community if it is disseminated in community health and education settings by a trusted community member as an interactive element of broader health discussions. There is also support for it to be part of adolescents' health and critical thinking education. These suggestions echo the recommendations for improved pandemic communications offered by Australians born in the World Health Organization's Eastern Mediterranean Region in a study of barriers to accessing official information about COVID-19 vaccines [ 41 ]. In that study, participants suggested improved quality of information from official sources, using diverse modes of dissemination, two-way communication with communities and communicating uncertainty.
4.1 Limitations
Despite several attempts to ensure representation of the diversity within the Arabic-speaking community, sampling bias is likely to have affected the applicability of the findings. Only women and Christians participated in the focus group discussions, despite several efforts to recruit men and people of other faiths. Feedback from the Advisory Group members who recruited for the focus groups and workshops suggested that women were more likely than men to take interest in and responsibility for a family's vaccinations. Participants of the workshops were much more representative of the community's diversity, possibly because recruitment used posts to large Arabic-speaking social and interest groups on social media, the interactive nature of the workshops, the larger honorarium, and the good-will that was established through the focus group discussions.
Discussions were led by vaccine uptake researchers from Murdoch Children's Research Institute. This knowledge may have shaped what was disclosed or withheld by participants due to social acceptability bias. For example, most participants emphasised that they had received all recommended COVID-19 vaccines and were less forthcoming with accounts of vaccine hesitancy. Discussions might have also been shaped by the language used by the researchers when referring to misinformation examples given by participants as “myths”.
Finally, the focus groups comprised only Christian women, and this likely narrowed the experiences and viewpoints heard. While vaccines are the purview of women in many households, men may have expressed different viewpoints, e.g., greater distrust of institutions. Additionally, there may be distinct cultural and religious views and experiences of Muslims that were not heard in the focus group discussions, e.g., religious objection to certain vaccine ingredients. The research team took advantage of the diversity in the workshops to explore key concerns, such as the character roles and representation of men and Muslims in the game. However, there were limited time to explore the content and mechanisms of vaccine misinformation in depth with these groups. These are gaps that could be explored in future research.
5 Conclusion
Vaccine misinformation was shared widely among participants and their social networks during the COVID-19 pandemic, irrespective of demographics such as age, gender and health literacy. Its dissemination was a response to the unfamiliarity of the virus and vaccines and the ambivalence and fear that it elicited. The myths provided a sense of knowing, hope and agency when they were scarce. Reliance on misinformation waned as myths became evidently untrue, and health information was disseminated by trusted community leaders and young adults who were able to effectively communicate information from mainstream Australian sources.
These insights will inform the vaccine information and myths referenced in the Cranky Uncle Vaccine game and help us identify the game's target audience, its role in the community's discourse on misinformation and health, and a dissemination strategy for the game after the project's completion. The next steps for the game are implementation research studies and randomised control trials to rigorously investigate its real-world effectiveness.
