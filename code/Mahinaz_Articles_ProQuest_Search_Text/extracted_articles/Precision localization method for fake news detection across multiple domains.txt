Introduction
The proliferation of fake news poses a severe threat to social trust, political stability, and public health. With the widespread use of the internet and social media, the speed and reach of information dissemination have expanded unprecedentedly. While social media has facilitated information sharing, it has also led to the rapid spread of unreliable or low-quality news, resulting in widespread negative impacts [1]. Fake news not only undermines public trust in the media, but can also incite social panic, mislead public opinion, and even influence major decisions. Therefore, effectively detecting and curbing fake news has become an urgent and important issue. By curbing the spread of false information, it helps ensure the authenticity and accuracy of public opinion, preventing the public from being misled and avoiding social panic and instability. At the same time, it promotes the enhancement of media responsibility, increases information transparency, and fosters the standardized development of the news industry. Additionally, it can reduce political manipulation and social division, promoting overall social harmony and stability.
A classic example of fake news is the conspiracy theory regarding the “cure” for COVID-19, where misinformation suggested that injecting disinfectants could kill the virus. This type of false information exploits the gullibility of individuals who lack scientific literacy or are predisposed to believe biased information, thus achieving the manipulator’s intent and resulting in harm to innocent people. However, manually fact-checking such news is time-consuming and challenging. Consequently, the field of fake news detection has emerged, integrating multidisciplinary technologies such as natural language processing, machine learning, data mining, and psychology, with the goal of developing automated tools and algorithms to identify and filter out false information.
To address this issue, researchers have proposed various methods. Early studies primarily focused on content-based detection methods, analyzing linguistic features, sentiment tendencies, and word frequency statistics of news texts to assess their authenticity. However, as the production techniques of fake news have become increasingly sophisticated, content analysis alone has proven insufficient to counteract various disguises and misleading information. Consequently, researchers have begun to explore multidimensional detection methods, including social network analysis, user behavior analysis, and cross-platform data integration. Most studies [2, 3] focus on single-domain fake news detection, such as in politics or health. However, within a specific domain, the quantity of fake news might be extremely limited. Therefore, creating deep learning methods based on such insufficient single-domain data often results in unsatisfactory performance. In practical scenarios, real-world news platforms publish diverse news across various domains daily [4]. Thus, leveraging data from multiple domains to address data sparsity and improve performance across all domains are promising. Nevertheless, data distribution varies by domain, known as domain shift [5]. For example, news in the fields of technology and healthcare tend to be highly specialized, often involving complex content. These reports usually explain the principles, applications, and potential impacts of new technologies, with a tone that is more neutral. In contrast, social and entertainment news is typically written in a lighter, more casual style, with emotions that are more polarized. Addressing the significant challenge of domain shift makes multi-domain detection particularly difficult. Current research continues to make significant strides in resolving this problem [6].
By integrating multi-domain data, data scarcity is no longer a limitation. Alongside advancements in deep learning technologies, new approaches have emerged for fake news detection. The construction of complex neural network models allows for the extraction of more nuanced features from extensive datasets, thereby enhancing detection accuracy and robustness. Additionally, interdisciplinary collaboration has driven significant breakthroughs in this field. By incorporating psychological and sociological theories, researchers have acknowledged that online information often consists of a blend of true and false content designed to deceive and manipulate readers’ emotions.
Although these studies consider various factors, such as sentiment and semantic style, the amalgamation of data and features also leads to domain shift. The concept of multi-domain (multi-task) learning involves jointly learning a set of domains (tasks), which has proven effective in many applications [7, 8]. These studies focus on capturing relationships between different tasks with various representations. Each task is reinforced through interconnections, including differences in task-related correlations. However, due to the severe issue of domain shift, existing multi-domain (multi-task) frameworks are not suitable for fake news detection [9]. Therefore, significant efforts continue to address the multi-domain data detection problem in fake news.
Related work
In terms of methodology, many existing studies on fake news detection employ an intuitive approach: detecting fake news based on content analysis. Research by Capuano et al. [10] indicates that manual fact-checking methods are impractical when faced with a vast amount of fake news information. Some researchers focus on analyzing dissemination patterns, as studies have shown that fake news spreads differently from real news [11]. Consequently, numerous studies based on dissemination methods have emerged. Information dissemination on the internet is highly temporal, characterized by sudden updates, rapid spread, and quick disappearance [12]. Additionally, some studies use knowledge-based methods to detect fake news. Mayank et al. [13] proposed a framework based on knowledge graphs (KG), which includes a news encoder, an entity encoder, and a final classification layer for detecting fake news articles. Initially, techniques based on natural language processing are used to encode news headlines. Then, named entity recognition is employed to identify and extract named entities from the text. Finally, the embeddings from both parts are combined to detect fake news.
In terms of algorithmic techniques, most existing studies employ natural language processing (NLP) and deep learning algorithms, such as LSTM and ensemble learning models, to identify fake news across various languages and domains [14]. Guo et al. have been exploring ways to automate fact-checking, using NLP and machine learning techniques to automatically predict the veracity of reports [15]. As research has progressed, Hu et al. [16] designed a novel graph neural model that compares news with external knowledge bases. They constructed a directed heterogeneous document graph that includes topics and entities for each type of news. Different entity representations are then compared, and the comparison features are input into a fake news classifier. The use of event adversarial neural networks (EANN) has provided a new perspective on multimodal fake news detection strategies [17]. This method combines the advantages of TextCNN [18] and image analysis, significantly improving the identification of fake news. This further demonstrates the effectiveness of integrating multiple technologies for fake news detection. We chose to use CNN for local, in-depth feature extraction, while using a linear network to capture features over a longer sequence range.
In terms of using large models, Xu et al. [19] compares the performance of traditional offline models and the latest large language models (LLMs) in fake news detection. Traditional offline models, trained on static data, struggle to adapt to the rapid changes in news dissemination, whereas LLMs, with their real-time processing and dynamic learning capabilities, can identify fake news more effectively. Qin et al. [20] proposed a method for fake news detection that utilizes LLMs. It integrates internal knowledge and external tools through a stepwise structured workflow, enabling efficient, training-free news authenticity verification with good interpretability and adaptability. However, its reliance on domain-specific knowledge and external resources adds complexity, and it has certain limitations when handling multimodal data and social network relationships. Teo et al. [21] explores the effectiveness of combining LLMs with traditional machine learning methods for fake news detection. The study finds that integrating LLMs with machine learning models significantly improves overall performance. However, this approach has limitations, including high computational resource requirements and dependence on data diversity and representativeness, which may pose challenges when handling domain-specific or emerging content. In summary, using large language models for fake news detection shows clear advantages when dynamic information, such as internet connectivity, is available. However, in offline mode and with limited resources, certain limitations remain.
This work focuses on addressing the issue of multi-domain fake news detection [22]. There has already been some progress in this area. Qin et al. proposed and evaluated a mixture of sequential experts (MoSE) model [23], which combines LSTM and multi-gate mixture of experts (MMoE) model [24] to handle user activity data. This approach aims to improve the modeling of sequential user activity flows in multi-task learning, enhancing prediction accuracy and model utility. Silva et al. [4] introduced the EDDFN model, which employs multiple domain-specific subnetworks and domain-shared subnetworks to model common knowledge across all domains. However, research indicates that modeling common sense knowledge across numerous domains simultaneously is challenging [25]. Consequently, EDDFN is less likely to be suitable for real-world news platforms that have a large and continuously growing set of domains. Unlike EDDFN, which uses a hard-sharing mechanism, M3FEND [26] and MDFEND [27] employ a soft-sharing mechanism to aggregate specific shared knowledge from different domains. However, they still face certain issues regarding accuracy and threshold settings, what is commonly used are hyperparameters for configuration, whereas we use dynamically adaptive weights obtained through the neural network. This work builds upon these models, the main focus of the work is on addressing the issue of domain shift.
Method
Problem definition
Let S be a piece of news on internet, with the text consisting of T tokens (words). We extract sentiment features from the news segment, denoted as E = { e p , e n } , which represents both positive and negative sentiments. Each piece of news has a ground truth label y ^ ∈ { 0 , 1 } , where 1 indicates the news is fake, and 0 indicates it is real. Additionally, each piece of news is manually labeled as belonging to a specific domain, with the domain label d = { d 1 , d 2 … … d N } , where N represents the number of domains.
Given a news segment S and its domain label d , the goal of multi-domain fake news detection is to determine whether the news is fake or real (Fig. 1). [See PDF for image]
Fig. 1 Model architecture


Word embeddings
First, consistent with the conventional use of BERT, we tokenize the text of the news and add a [CLS] token at the beginning to represent the classification mark and a separator [SEP] at the end of the news. We then use RoBERTa-wwm [28] (a Chinese pre-trained model based on an improved version of BERT [29]) to perform word embeddings on these tokens, resulting in ω = { w 1 , w 2 … … w n } , where w ∈ R d denotes the embedding dimension.
After obtaining the word embeddings, to address the domain shift problem caused by differences in data among news from different domains, we input these embeddings into three distinct modules: the domain feature extraction, the domain auxiliary positioning network (APN), and the gate network. The primary goal is to accurately locate news from multiple domains and improve the accuracy of multi-domain data processing.
Field positioning
To achieve high-precision multi-domain fake news detection, it is crucial to address the domain shift problem. Therefore, we have designed the model with a focus on the following three aspects:
- Domain feature extraction


In the aforementioned research, it was mentioned that TextCNN was used in EANN. In our study, we found that TextCNN is more suitable for quickly processing and classifying short texts, such as brief microblogs, tweets, or simple headlines. This is because its shallow structure is not appropriate for longer texts like news articles. Therefore, we chose DPCNN (deep pyramid convolutional neural networks) [30], which is better suited for long texts, for domain knowledge extraction, and made improvements based on its fundamental principles to better suit the multi-domain tasks of this paper.
First, we use an initial convolution to preliminarily extract features related to fake news. This includes two key differences from traditional convolution: first, we use length-invariant convolution, meaning the length remains unchanged after convolution; second, we employ a pre-activation method, which can be expressed by the following formula: 1
H 0 = Conv ( relu ( x ) , W )
In traditional convolution, activation occurs after the convolution operation. Pre-activation, characterized by its linearity, can streamline the training of deep networks, akin to the function of constant error carousels in LSTM. Experimental findings consistently demonstrate that pre-activation yields superior performance compared to post-activation methods.
After obtaining preliminary knowledge extraction through the initial convolution, we proceed to use multiple parallel convolutions for precise extraction of knowledge from different domains. The overall structure is illustrated in Fig. 2. Each convolution includes a max pooling layer and two convolutional layers. To make deep network training feasible, we employ additive shortcut connections similar to those in ResNet [31], achieving identity mapping without any need for dimensional matching. This significantly alleviates the problem of gradient vanishing. 2
H l , 1 n = MaxPool ( H 0 ) 3
H l , 2 n = Conv ( relu ( H l , 1 n , W l , 2 ) ) 4
H l n = Conv ( relu ( H l , 2 n , W l ) ) 5
H l , res n = H l - 1 n + H l n where n represents the number of parallel precise domain extractions, which will be validated in the final experiments. [See PDF for image]
Fig. 2 Domain feature extraction architecture


Each subsequent convolution is specifically designed to enhance the precise extraction of features within a particular domain, based on the final domain labels during the training process. After each stack of subsequent convolutions (two layers of length-invariant convolution), max pooling is applied. This compresses the sequence length to half of its original size, allowing the network to perceive text segments that are twice as long, thereby achieving a deeper level of feature fusion compared to TextCNN. In this study, to meet the timeliness requirements of news detection, we limited the stacking to two convolutional layers. We can refer to the explanation of CNN hidden layer visualization. At the shallow layers, the network captures the edges of shapes, and at deeper layers, these edges combine to form 2D shapes and 3D structures. Similarly, in long-sequence texts, repeated convolutions and pooling operations are applied to extract deeper features within local ranges. The only limitation is that the perceivable sequence length is finite, which is why we employ the next section’s auxiliary localization network.
- Auxiliary Positioning Network (APN)


Due to the architecture of convolutional neural networks (CNNs) used in domain feature extraction, which typically employ local receptive fields and pooling operations to extract features, this method is highly effective for image processing. In images, local features can be combined to form higher-level global features. However, in text processing, important information may span longer ranges within sentences where CNNs perform inadequately in capturing these long-range dependencies. Although we addressed some of these issues by using DPCNN to stack convolutional layers, we refrained from deeper stacking due to timeliness concerns. Moreover, considering the nature of convolution and pooling operations, convolutions are inherently translation invariant, meaning they do not heavily consider positional changes (e.g., an object moving in an image can still be recognized). However, in text processing, this characteristic may hinder the precise capture of relationships and dependencies between words. The order and position of words are often crucial in text, potentially leading to the loss of context information, especially in tasks requiring precise sequential information such as sentiment analysis and semantic analysis.
To address these challenges, we integrate a feedforward neural network (APN) alongside CNN to enhance auxiliary positioning. APN incorporates domain-specific adjustments by learning domain tags, enabling dynamic parameter adjustments based on domain information to effectively mitigate domain shift. 6
y APN = FFN ( W , ω )
In the APN, the core idea is to perform the classification task on the word embeddings ω . By using a SoftMax function, we obtain the domain weights α . Unlike the domain feature extraction, the APN does not yield a single result but rather assigns different weights based on the news domain. This implies that multiple domains can have significant probability values, which aids in refining the results obtained from domain feature extraction. It is crucial to distinguish that the output here pertains strictly to classification results, whereas domain feature extraction outputs encompass comprehensive content information derived from word embeddings.
- Sentiment Analyst


In our analysis of the dataset, we found that fake news often attracts readers’ attention through exaggerated, inflammatory, or extreme emotional language. Sentiment analysis can help identify these abnormal emotional tendencies, providing clues for detecting fake news. For instance, fake news may use strong negative emotional vocabulary such as anger, fear, or hatred, whereas real news usually employs more neutral and objective language.
Furthermore, fake news tends to manipulate readers’ reactions through extreme emotions, inciting panic or anger to generate more comments and shares, thereby achieving rapid dissemination. Sentiment analysis can quantify and assess the emotional extremity of news content, helping to identify reports that may have exaggerated or manipulative tendencies. Additionally, fake news often contains contradictory emotional expressions, such as inconsistent emotional tones within the same article. Sentiment analysis can effectively detect these contradictions, revealing internal inconsistencies in the article, which can serve as a basis for identifying fake news.
Therefore, we employ the sentiment analysis model fine-tuned by Zhang et al. [32] based on the Chinese-RoBERTa-wwm-ext-base. This model has been optimized using sentiment analysis datasets from eight Chinese domains, totaling 227,347 samples. The final sentiment analysis result is denoted as V emo .
Given the powerful performance of BERT, many researchers have developed optimized models for specific domains [33, 34]. To more accurately capture semantic information across different domains and supplement the original semantics with residuals, we perform targeted feature extraction for specific domains. However, since we did not find all domain-specific models, we conducted new feature extraction specifically for the scientific and medical health domains, while other domains still use RoBERTa-wwm. The new features obtained are denoted as ω ¯ = { w ¯ 1 , w ¯ 2 … … w ¯ n } .
The entire gated structure is also a feedforward neural network, which can achieve adaptive recognition through the integration of attention mechanisms and linear projection transformations. The inputs and outputs of the gated structure can be denoted as follows: 7
g = softmax(Gate ( V emo ⊕ β e ⊕ ω ¯ , W ) )
Finally, g represents the weight vector of the importance of different domain feature extraction. By integrating the domain feature extraction and the auxiliary positioning network discussed earlier, the final features of the news can be represented as follows: 8
V news = ∑ n = 1 e μ n α n g n
Prediction
After the multi-domain positioning, the final classification features are obtained. These features are then passed through a multilayer perceptron (MLP) to perform the classification task, resulting in the detection outcome of whether the news is fake or real. 9
y ^ = softmax ( MLP ( V news ) )
The loss function used is the binary cross-entropy loss, which is the most commonly used loss function for binary classification tasks. 10
L = - 1 N ∑ i = 1 N ( y i log y ^ i + ( 1 - y i ) log ( 1 - y ^ i ) )
N is the number of samples. y i is the ground truth label for the i -th sample, where y i = 1 if the news is fake and y i = 0 if the news is real. y ^ i is the predicted probability that the i- th sample is fake.
Fast model
Given the rapid dissemination of information on social media and news websites, fake news can quickly spread in a short time. To effectively curb the spread of fake news, detection algorithms must possess real-time processing capabilities. Fast algorithms can immediately identify and flag fake news upon its release, thereby reducing its spread and impact. For social media platforms and news aggregation sites, if detection algorithms are slow, it may result in delayed loading or untimely updates of information, affecting users’ trust and experience with the platform. On the same hardware conditions, faster algorithms can process more data or perform more detection tasks, thus improving the overall performance and economic efficiency of the system. This is especially important for small- to medium-sized platforms with limited resources, making speed a critical indicator for practical application.
Therefore, based on the algorithm from the previous section, we have specifically optimized the algorithm for speed. In feature extraction, we used a distilled version of the RoBERTa-wwm model called minibrt-h288, and removed the Sentiment_analyst and Domain_BERT modules. We also introduced a dual-model neural network acceleration based on gradient optimization [35]. In this approach, the neural network is used for prediction, backpropagation is employed to calculate sensitivity information, and an additional reverse model is constructed to directly compute sensitivity, enhancing prediction accuracy. By employing these optimizations, we achieve faster processing speeds, enabling real-time detection of fake news while maintaining high accuracy. This approach ensures that our system can handle the demands of real-world applications, providing timely and reliable detection of fake news across multiple domains. Experiment
Dataset
The dataset we use is the Weibo21 dataset created by Nan et al. [27] for multi-domain fake news detection. It includes fake news and corresponding real news fragments from December 2014 to March 2021. These news fragments have been verified by NewsVerify, a platform focused on discovering and verifying suspicious news fragments on Weibo.
For each piece of news, the dataset includes: - The most direct news content text.

- Different modalities, including images.

- Temporal information such as timestamps.

- Social reactions to the news, including comments.


The dataset consists of 4488 fake news items and 4640 real news items. It spans nine domains: science, military, education, disasters, politics, health, finance, entertainment, and society. Experimental environment and basic parameters
All our experiments were conducted on a system with the following specifications: CPU: Intel i9-14900 K; GPU: ROG 4080S; Memory: 64 GB RAM. All comparative model methods used the same parameters: Word Embedding Dimension: 768 (for BERT-based models); Maximum Sequence Length: 170; Optimizer: AdamW; Learning Rate: 4e-5; Parameter Optimization Framework: Optuna; Batch Size: 64.
To ensure the validity and reliability of our experiments, each experiment was repeated more than 10 times. In both single-domain and multi-domain tasks, we applied n -fold cross-validation to our dataset, randomly selecting onefold as the test set and the remaining folds as the training set. The distinction is that in the case of the single-domain task, we only select data from a single domain to be used as the training set. The average results from these repetitions were used for analysis. Our algorithms used in experiments have been made publicly available on GitHub.
Baseline
In the experiment, there are three types of baselines: (1) Single-domain baselines: TextCNN [18], BiGRU [36], and RoBERTa [28]; (2) Mixed-domain baselines: TextCNN [18], BiGRU [36], RoBERTa [28], StyleLSTM [37], and DualEmo [38]; (3) Multi-domain baselines: EANN [17], MMOE [24], MOSE [23], EDDFN [4], MDFEND [27], and M3FEND [26].
The single-domain baseline refers to experiments conducted separately on the data of each domain, so the accuracy for all data is the average of the previous nine single-domain results.
BiGRU : A widely used text encoding baseline model for fake news detection. We implemented a single-layer BiGRU with a hidden size of 300.
TextCNN : A popular text encoder, implemented with 5 kernels, each with 64 channels, and strides of 1, 2, 3, 5, and 10.
RoBERTa: The word embedding method in this paper directly uses an MLP for classification and prediction after obtaining the word embeddings of the text.
The distinction between mixed-domain and multi-domain baselines lies in the use of only one expert in the extraction networks for experiments across all domains, while other processes remain consistent.
BiGRU, TextCNN, and RoBERTa : Implemented in the same way as in the single-domain methods.
StyleLSTM : Utilizes a BiLSTM to extract news content representation, which, along with style features, is fed into an MLP for prediction.
DualEmo : Uses BiGRU to extract news representation and leverages emotion features to determine if the news is real or fake.
EANN : Consists of a feature extractor, event discriminator, and fake news detector, aiming to learn domain-invariant representations.
MMoE : A multi-domain model using a mixture of experts (MoE) architecture, where different domains share specific expert heads.
MoSE : A multi-domain model based on LSTM, designed to replace the experts in MMoE.
EDDFN : A multi-domain model that integrates both domain-specific and shared knowledge, using MLP modules for feature extraction and final classification.
MDFEND : A multi-domain fake news detection model that utilizes a domain gate to select appropriate experts in the MoE for processing.
M3FEND : A multi-domain fake news detection model that incorporates semantics, sentiment, and style. It innovatively adds a domain memory bank to enrich domain information.

From the aforementioned experiments, we can observe that our proposed method achieved the best results on the vast majority of the data, and even for the remaining datasets, it ranked within the top three in terms of performance. The scientific domain data showed poorer performance due to the relative scarcity of fake news, leading the domain feature extraction to lean toward other domains. Overall, the results from the mixed-domain approach were superior to those from the single-domain approach, and the multi-domain results outperformed the mixed-domain ones. These data demonstrate that in the realm of news detection, as in other fields, richer data and features contribute to enhanced accuracy.
The results from the multi-domain and mixed-domain approaches indicate that using a single expert system for hard sharing makes it inherently difficult to accurately pinpoint domains. Even with systems like MDFEND and M3FEND that employ multiple systems for soft sharing, domain confusion issues still arise. Therefore, the critical task remains addressing domain shift and confusion. Our model results clearly show that, given the same data, achieving more precise domain localization is key to improving accuracy and other performance metrics.
We conducted an in-depth analysis of the results and the dataset and found that in the fields of science and politics, the sentiment information in the news tends to be more neutral, making it difficult to capture significant emotional cues. As a result, the performance in these areas is relatively average. In contrast, financial and entertainment news in China often exhibits a strong negative tone. While social news also tends to have more emotional variance, it is generally less extreme than the other two categories, as it is less frequently sourced from official news outlets. Therefore, social news tends to be relatively more balanced in its reporting. From this, we can observe that when sentiment fluctuations are either very low or very high, they can impact the results. This may indicate that the model has moderated polarized sentiments in order to achieve more accurate performance and affects the accuracy of field positioning.

Conclusion
In this study, we addressed the problem of fake news detection across multiple domains. Building on existing research, we identified and summarized its limitations revealing that aside from the influence of data, the accuracy of feature extraction and domain localization are critical factors for detection accuracy in multi-domain scenarios.
In terms of feature extraction, most notably, we demonstrated the issues inherent in traditional methods such as TextCNN for extracting textual information and introduced improvements. Our approach primarily involves using multilayer CNNs for deep mining through pooling, which extracts localized information up to the key information in short sentences. A fully connected network is then used to extract information from long sequences, and an attention mechanism unifies and integrates local and long-sequence information. For domain localization, we effectively distinguished between domains by leveraging sentiment information. Our method was evaluated on multiple datasets, and the experimental results proved that our proposed approach has significant advantages.
To achieve real-time detection, we modified certain modules of the model, significantly enhancing inference speed with only a minor performance trade-off. We have already applied this model on our university forum, achieving certain results in public opinion control. In future research, we will focus on more comprehensive feature extraction to further improve detection accuracy.
