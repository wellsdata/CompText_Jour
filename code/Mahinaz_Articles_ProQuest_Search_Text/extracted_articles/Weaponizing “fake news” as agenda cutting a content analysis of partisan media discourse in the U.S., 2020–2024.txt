1 Introduction
Since 2016, the term “fake news” has undergone a notable transformation. In a dictionary sense, it refers to false or fabricated news reporting, but in contemporary political discourse it has increasingly become a politically charged label used to challenge the credibility of journalists, media organizations, political opponents, and public institutions (see also Oxford English Dictionary, 2026;Egelhofer and Lecheler, 2019;Tandoc et al., 2018;Waisbord, 2018a,2018b). In contemporary media discourse, “fake news” no longer functions solely as a neutral descriptor of false information; it also operates as a rhetorical weapon through which actors seek to delegitimize competing narratives, weaken trust in rival sources, and shape how audiences interpret political information (Benkler et al., 2018;Egelhofer and Lecheler, 2019;Waisbord, 2018a,2018b).
This transformation is particularly relevant in the polarized U.S. media environment, where partisan media systems, social media platforms, and countermedia outlets contribute to the fragmentation of public discourse by encouraging audiences to consume information that confirms existing political identities (Benkler et al., 2018;Guess et al., 2020a,2020b;Stroud, 2011). Accusations of “fake news” serve as symbolic cues that direct audiences toward distrust of opposing outlets while reinforcing loyalty to ideologically aligned sources (Hameleers and van der Meer, 2020;Pennycook and Rand, 2021). The period from 2020 to 2024, encompassing the COVID-19 pandemic, the 2020 and 2024 presidential elections (Matthews, 2024), the 2022 midterm elections, and ongoing debates over platform moderation, misinformation, and media legitimacy, provides a critical context for examining these dynamics (Newman et al., 2025;Pew Research Center, 2026).
Although prior scholarship has extensively studied misinformation, disinformation, and public trust, comparatively little systematic attention has been paid to how news outlets themselves strategically employ the label “fake news” in their discourse (Egelhofer and Lecheler, 2019;Tandoc et al., 2018). Existing evidence suggests that the term may function differently across ideological contexts, yet systematic comparative research is limited. Understanding these differences is important because the political effect of the term depends not only on the truth of specific claims but also on how the label is used to frame actors, suppress rival narratives, and shape public judgments of credibility (Benkler et al., 2018;Fletcher and Nielsen, 2019;Waisbord, 2018a,2018b;Egelhofer and Lecheler, 2019).
This study conceptualizes weaponized “fake news” rhetoric as a form of agenda manipulation, encompassing agenda cutting, agenda building, and agenda steering (Amazeen and Vargo, 2021;Balles et al., 2026;Vargo and Amazeen, 2021). At the level of interpretation, framing theory explains why the label is rhetorically powerful: by emphasizing selected aspects of reality, “fake news” encourages audiences to read a target as unreliable, deceptive, or politically motivated rather than simply factually incorrect (Entman, 1993;Egelhofer and Lecheler, 2019). At the audience level, agenda-melding theory helps explain why such labels matter in high-choice media environments, where individuals combine cues from multiple sources to build personally and politically meaningful agendas; in this context, “fake news” accusations can steer audiences toward identity-consistent sources and away from rival ones (Shaw et al., 1999a,1999b,1999c;Vera-Revilla et al., 2025). Countermedia scholarship further clarifies how antagonistic outlets, particularly those positioned against mainstream journalism, may use “fake news” rhetoric to contest institutional authority and strengthen alternative epistemic communities (Heft et al., 2020;Loosen, 2018;Rone, 2022).
Guided by these theoretical perspectives and prior research on fake news rhetoric, partisan media, and countermedia dynamics (see Sections 2.1–2.5), this study addresses two research questions: how do left-leaning, centrist, and right-leaning outlets differ in the frequency and functional use of “fake news,” including accusatory, defensive, analytical, and meta-commentary uses?; and who or what is most often labeled as “fake news” across ideological categories, and to what extent does verifiable evidence support such claims? Building on prior work suggesting that right-leaning and countermedia outlets are more likely to use delegitimizing rhetoric against mainstream journalism, while left-leaning and centrist outlets more often frame fake news in analytical, corrective, or procedural terms, the study hypothesizes that right-leaning outlets will use the term more frequently and accusatorily, left-leaning outlets will employ it more analytically, conservative outlets will target mainstream media, liberal outlets will target right-wing sources, and centrist outlets will provide comparatively stronger evidentiary support. To examine these questions, the study conducts a comparative content analysis of nine prominent U.S. news outlets representing left-leaning, centrist, and right-leaning categories during 2020–2024, analyzing usage type, target, tone, evidence provision, and political context to provide an operational framework for understanding how “fake news” functions as agenda cutting, agenda building, and agenda steering in polarized media discourse (Benkler et al., 2018;Egelhofer and Lecheler, 2019;Newman et al., 2025).
2 Literature review
2.1 “Fake news” as a strategic rhetorical tool
The term “fake news” initially referred to deliberately fabricated information presented as legitimate news. Since the 2016 U.S. presidential election, the meaning of “fake news” has expanded beyond false or misleading content (Tandoc et al., 2018;Egelhofer and Lecheler, 2019). In contemporary political communication, “fake news” functions as a rhetorical label used by media actors, politicians, and audiences to challenge the credibility of opposing sources. Rather than being solely descriptive, the term has become a contested political signifier, whose meaning depends on the user, the target, and the political context (Bennett and Livingston, 2020;Lazer et al., 2018).
Accusations of “fake news” operate as tools of delegitimization. Labeling a journalist, outlet, political actor, or public claim as “fake news” not only calls into question factual accuracy but can also undermine the authority of the source itself (Bernecker et al., 2021;Scardigno and Mininni, 2020;Waisbord, 2018a,2018b). The term may close down debate rather than encourage verification, working hegemonically to frame opponents’ claims as untrustworthy before evidence is considered (Zompetti, 2019). Audience reception is shaped by political identity: accusations targeting out-group sources are more likely to be accepted, whereas accusations involving ideologically aligned actors are often rejected (Pennycook and Rand, 2021;Hameleers and Van Der Meer, 2020). This dynamic highlights the power of “fake news” rhetoric in polarized environments, linking misinformation to rhetorical influence, institutional trust, and political identity (Benkler et al., 2018;Lazer et al., 2018).
2.2 Framing mechanisms in “fake news” interpretation
Framing theory explains how “fake news” shapes public interpretation. Framing involves selecting certain aspects of reality to define problems, diagnose causes, make moral judgments, and suggest remedies (Entman, 1993;Deaville and Lemire, 2021). Describing a story, institution, or actor as “fake news” frames the target as biased, deceptive, or politically motivated (Sample et al., 2020;Ramirez-Salina et al., 2025). Embedding the term in broader political messaging renders selective interpretations cognitively accessible, transforming the label into a mechanism of strategic persuasion (Deaville and Lemire, 2021;Ramirez-Salina et al., 2025).
Ideological differences in framing appear in the literature, although the exact pattern varies by context and outlet type. Left-leaning outlets have been shown in some studies to frame “fake news” as a threat to democratic institutions, electoral integrity, or public health, particularly when the term is discussed in relation to misinformation or systemic risk (Zhou and Shen, 2024; see alsoEgelhofer and Lecheler, 2019;Bennett and Livingston, 2020). Right-leaning outlets, especially in studies of partisan or countermedia environments, have been found to use the label to emphasize mainstream media bias and to reinforce distrust of established journalism, although such patterns may differ across national and outlet-specific settings (Benkler et al., 2018;Heft et al., 2020;Rone, 2022). Centrist or institutional outlets more often adopt procedural, verification-oriented, or platform-governance frames, but this, too, appears to depend on the media system and the issue context under study (Sample et al., 2020;Fletcher and Nielsen, 2019;Sarmento, 2025). Taken together, these framing differences may shape how audiences evaluate information legitimacy and may contribute to broader mistrust, though the magnitude of this effect likely varies across political and media environments (Zapata et al., 2025;Hameleers and van der Meer, 2020;Zhou and Shen, 2024).
Functional uses of the term include accusatory frames, defensive frames, analytical usage, and meta-commentary (Boudana and Segev, 2026;Ramirez-Salina et al., 2025;Scherling, 2026;Sarmento, 2025). These distinctions allow the study to move beyond frequency counts to examine how “fake news” performs rhetorical and communicative work in partisan media discourse (Sample et al., 2020).
2.3 Agenda manipulation: cutting, building, steering, and melding
Agenda manipulation extends analysis beyond framing to consider how media influence issue visibility, legitimacy, and salience (Vargo and Minooie, 2025). Agenda building highlights the promotion of preferred narratives (Vargo et al., 2018), whereas agenda cutting strategically reduces the visibility or legitimacy of competing narratives (Amazeen and Vargo, 2021). Weaponized “fake news” rhetoric exemplifies agenda-cutting by discrediting rival claims without engaging with evidence (Amazeen and Vargo, 2021;Vargo and Minooie, 2025).
Agenda cutting is linked to agenda steering, which is particularly useful for analyzing the present study because the label “fake news” does more than dismiss a claim: it can direct audiences toward preferred interpretations and ideologically aligned sources in a fragmented media environment (Vargo et al., 2018). Accusations of “fake news” therefore serve as cognitive shortcuts, signaling which outlets, actors, or stories to trust or reject, especially in a polarized context where audiences rely on partisan cues to navigate competing information streams (Nazar, 2024;Rone, 2022). Agenda-melding theory is likewise well suited to this study because it explains how audiences combine information from multiple sources to form personalized agendas, making them especially vulnerable to repeated “fake news” cues that reinforce ideological silos and sharpen partisan divisions (Shaw et al., 1999a,1999b,1999c;Vera-Revilla et al., 2025). In this sense, the “fake news” label is effective not only because it discredits opponents but also because it helps organize the ways audiences sort, prioritize, and integrate media messages within the broader agenda structure of high-choice media systems.
This theoretical alignment makes agenda-setting, agenda-steering, and agenda-melding perspectives uniquely suited for analyzing the contemporary “fake news” discourse. Rather than operating merely as a tool for fact-checking or descriptive media criticism, the strategic deployment of the “fake news” label functions as a powerful cognitive shortcut for audiences navigating a high-choice media environment. By weaponizing the phrase to attack political opponents or rival media outlets, elite actors and partisan outlets engage in a form of agenda-setting that actively diminishes the visibility and perceived legitimacy of competing narratives. Concurrently, this rhetoric performs agenda steering by providing strong identity-consistent cues that guide audiences away from out-group information channels and deeply entrench them within alternative, ideologically isolated epistemic communities, a process that directly drives the mechanics of modern agenda-melding. It is from this theoretical synthesis of attention redirection and source delegitimation that the core predictions of this study are derived.
2.4 Countermedia dynamics and asymmetric polarization
Countermedia outlets position themselves against mainstream journalism, presenting themselves as necessary alternatives (Frischlich et al., 2023;Hopp et al., 2020;Loosen, 2018). Many prominent U.S. countermedia outlets are right-leaning, where distrust of mainstream media is central to political identity and audience engagement (Blassnig et al., 2019;Rone, 2022). The term “fake news” in these outlets poses a systemic challenge to journalistic authority and normative legitimacy (Heft et al., 2020;Loosen, 2018).
Countermedia outlets position themselves against mainstream journalism, presenting themselves as necessary alternatives (Frischlich et al., 2023;Hopp et al., 2020;Loosen, 2018). Prior research suggests that many prominent U.S. countermedia outlets are right-leaning and that distrust of mainstream media is often central to their political identity and audience engagement (Blassnig et al., 2019;Rone, 2022). These insights are directly relevant to the present study because they suggest that “fake news” rhetoric may function differently depending on whether an outlet is reporting on misinformation, defending its own legitimacy, or actively delegitimizing mainstream journalism. In right-leaning countermedia, the term is especially likely to operate as a challenge to journalistic authority and normative legitimacy, whereas left-leaning and centrist outlets may use it more often to discuss misinformation, electoral integrity, public health, or platform governance (Heft et al., 2020;Loosen, 2018;Scherling, 2026;Fletcher and Nielsen, 2019). Prior work on asymmetric polarization further informs the present study by suggesting that ideological sectors do not engage with “fake news” in the same way: right-leaning outlets are more likely to use accusatory rhetoric, left-leaning outlets tend to frame structural or institutional threats, and centrist outlets emphasize procedural verification (Blassnig et al., 2019;Scherling, 2026). Taken together, these findings justify the study’s comparative design and help explain why the analysis expects distinct patterns in agenda-setting, agenda-building, agenda-cutting, and audience distrust across ideological categories (Benkler et al., 2018;Kaiser et al., 2025;Hopp et al., 2025).
2.5 Research gap and contribution
Despite extensive research on misinformation, disinformation, and media trust, important gaps remain in the literature. Prior work has often concentrated on false content, audience exposure, or single-outlet or single-sector contexts, rather than on how media organizations themselves strategically use the label “fake news” across ideological environments (for example, seeEgelhofer and Lecheler, 2019;Tandoc et al., 2018;Bennett and Livingston, 2020). This study addresses that gap by conducting a comparative analysis of nine U.S. outlets across left-leaning, centrist, and right-leaning categories over the 2020–2024 period, allowing us to examine how rhetorical use varies across ideological settings rather than only within a single sector or event cycle. By integrating framing theory, agenda-melding, agenda-cutting logic, and countermedia scholarship, the study conceptualizes “fake news” as a mechanism for agenda-cutting, agenda-building, and agenda-steering. It operationalizes this through coding categories such as usage type, target, tone, evidence provision, and political context. In doing so, the study provides a systematic empirical basis for understanding the politics of credibility in contemporary U.S. media systems.
2.6 Research questions and hypotheses
RQ1 : How do left-leaning, centrist, and right-leaning outlets differ in the frequency and function of “fake news” use?
RQ2 : Who or what is most often labeled as “fake news,” and how often does factual evidence across ideological camps support such claims?
H1 : Right-leaning outlets will use “fake news” more frequently and more accusatorily than centrist or left-leaning outlets. In contrast, left-leaning outlets will use it more often in analytical or meta-commentary ways.
H2 : Conservative outlets will more often label mainstream media as “fake news,” liberal outlets will more often target right-wing sources, and centrist outlets will be more likely to provide explicit factual evidence when invoking the term.
3 Materials and methods
3.1 Study design
This study employed quantitative content analysis complemented by qualitative thematic illustration to examine how U.S. news outlets used the term “fake news” from January 1, 2020, to December 31, 2024. Content analysis enables systematic, replicable, theory-guided examination of media texts (Krippendorff, 2018;Neuendorf, 2017). The study conceptualized “fake news” as a mechanism of agenda manipulation, encompassing agenda cutting, agenda building, and agenda steering, consistent with prior research on its function as a politically charged label used to delegitimize opponents and shape credibility judgments (Tandoc et al., 2018;Egelhofer and Lecheler, 2019). The design was informed by framing theory and agenda-melding theory (Entman, 1993;Shaw et al., 1999a,1999b,1999c).
3.2 Sampling frame and outlet selection
The sampling frame included news reports, analyses, opinion columns, editorials, and relevant broadcast or online transcripts from nine major U.S. news outlets, grouped into left-leaning, centrist, and right-leaning categories. This structure facilitated comparisons of how “fake news” rhetoric varied across media environments shaped by partisan credibility cues and distrust of opposing sources (Benkler et al., 2018). The outlet grouping was based on a combination of external media-bias assessments and established scholarly precedent, with the classification used here serving as an analytical rather than absolute ideological designation. Specifically, CNN, MSNBC, and The New York Times were treated as left-leaning; Associated Press, Politico, and USA Today Online were treated as centrist/institutional outlets; and Fox News, The Daily Caller, and Conservative Daily News were treated as right-leaning. Because some rating systems place certain outlets near the boundary between center and left-of-center, we interpret these categories comparatively and transparently rather than as fixed labels. The unit of analysis was the individual article or transcript, coded once according to the dominant use of “fake news” in the text (Krippendorff, 2018;Neuendorf, 2017).Table 1presents the final coded sample by outlet and ideological category.

3.3 Article identification and inclusion criteria Articles were identified through systematic keyword searches in Nexis Uni, an academic research database that provides access to news articles, broadcast transcripts, and related legal and business sources. Searches were conducted separately for each of the nine selected U.S. news outlets and focused on the rhetorical use of the term rather than on broader categories such as misinformation or disinformation (Wardle and Derakhshan, 2017;Egelhofer and Lecheler, 2019). Retrieved items were screened and coded according to the study’s coding scheme, including usage type, target, tone, evidence provision, and political context. Items were excluded if they were duplicates, automated updates, letters to the editor, republications without original framing, or incidental mentions lacking sufficient context for coding. Only items published within the study period, appearing in one of the nine selected outlets, and containing the exact phrase “fake news” in the headline or body text were retained for analysis (Krippendorff, 2018). 3.4 Sampling procedure
A stratified sampling strategy ensured comparability across outlets and ideological categories. Eligible items were collected separately for each outlet, with manageable samples retained when volumes were large and all retrievable items included when fewer were available (Neuendorf, 2017). The final dataset consisted of 1,972 coded items: left-leaning ( n = 614), centrist ( n = 690), and right-leaning ( n = 668). Duplicates were removed based on headline, date, author, outlet, URL, or database identifier.
3.5 Coding categories and measures
A structured codebook captured five variables: usage type, target of labeling, tone, evidence provision, and political context. The coding scheme was developed through a hybrid approach, drawing first on prior typologies of fake news rhetoric, framing, delegitimation, and information disorder, and then refined during pilot coding to ensure that the categories captured the range of uses observed in the dataset (Krippendorff, 2018;Neuendorf, 2017). More specifically, the categories were informed by prior work on the rhetorical and delegitimizing functions of “fake news” (Tandoc et al., 2018;Egelhofer and Lecheler, 2019;Zompetti, 2019), by framing and information-disorder scholarship (Entman, 1993;Wardle and Derakhshan, 2017;Lazer et al., 2018;Allcott and Gentzkow, 2017), and were adjusted during coding to ensure that the codebook remained exhaustive and practically applicable to the texts analyzed. Each item received one code per variable based on the dominant function of “fake news” in the text. Usage types were accusatory, defensive, analytical, or meta-commentary. Target categories included mainstream/center-left media, right-leaning media, political actors or parties, platforms/technology companies, foreign actors/state media, specific stories or claims, or no identifiable target. Tone was coded as alarmist/hostile, neutral/analytical, dismissive/satirical, or mixed. Evidence provision measured reliance on strong evidence, formal fact-checks, expert or official sources, non-expert/partisan sources, no evidence, or undetermined. Political context included electoral politics, public health, immigration, economic policy, race and policing, platform governance, media trust, crime/legal issues, and other general criticism.
To ensure strict transparency and allow for systematic replication, variables were operationalized using explicit criteria and textual anchors developed during the pilot phase. “Usage type” was classified based on the text’s primary communicative intent. “Accusatory” use was coded when the label functioned as a direct delegitimizing attack aimed at undermining a specific target’s credibility (e.g., Fox News stating, “The mainstream media continues to push fake news to protect their preferred political candidates”). “Analytical” use was coded when the term was embedded in a descriptive, conceptual, or systemic discussion of information ecosystems (e.g., an AP report noting, “Researchers are investigating how algorithmic amplification accelerates the spread of fake news during election cycles”). Tone of coverage was explicitly differentiated between “alarmist/hostile” and “neutral/analytical.” Texts were coded as “alarmist/hostile” when they relied on emotionally charged language, hyperbole, or threat-oriented framing to instill urgency or outrage (e.g., using terms such as “assault on democracy,” “dangerous lies,” or “corrupt deception”). Texts were coded as “neutral/analytical” when they maintained a detached, matter-of-fact expository style, relying on objective reporting verbs and legal or academic framing. Evidence provision measured the explicit presence of verifiable grounding. Strong evidence required the inclusion of explicit text anchors, such as direct references to official public records, named institutional experts, or established non-partisan fact-checking organizations (e.g., “According to verified data from PolitiFact.”). Items were coded as “no explicit evidence” when an assertion of falseness was made as a bald narrative claim without any accompanying external source verification or systemic contextual support.”
3.6 Coder training and reliability
A single coder applied the codebook after a pilot phase to refine categories and reduce ambiguity, coding ambiguous items as “cannot determine” when appropriate (Krippendorff, 2018;Neuendorf, 2017). Consistency was assessed by recoding 10% of the sample ( n = 197) after 4 weeks without reference to the original coding. Cohen’s kappa values exceeded 0.80, indicating high reliability (Cohen, 1960). To ensure high reliability of quantitative data and address the limitations of single-coder analysis, an inter-coder reliability test was conducted. A second independent communication researcher was trained on the structured codebook using an independent pilot dataset. Following the training phase, this second coder independently evaluated a randomly selected 10% sub-sample of the primary dataset ( n = 197). Inter-coder agreement was calculated using Cohen’s kappa, yielding values ranging from 0.81 to 0.87 across the five primary variables (usage type, target, tone, evidence provision, and political context). These metrics exceed the standard threshold of 0.80, demonstrating strong inter-coder reliability and confirming the replicability of the coding instrument.
3.7 Data analysis
Analysis proceeded in two stages. First, descriptive statistics summarized the distribution of usage type, target, tone, evidence provision, and political context across outlets and ideological categories (Neuendorf, 2017). Second, chi-square tests of independence examined associations between ideology and the categorical variables, with Cramér’s V reported as an effect size to assess the strength of the relationships and standardized residuals used to identify which cells contributed most to statistically significant patterns. Where expected cell counts were low, categories were combined with appropriate caution or interpreted with caution. Statistical significance was set at α = 0.05.
3.8 Qualitative thematic illustration
High-salience patterns were contextualized using qualitative thematic illustration to demonstrate how accusatory, defensive, analytical, and meta-commentary uses appeared in practice, providing interpretive support for quantitative findings (Krippendorff, 2018).
3.9 Ethical considerations
The study analyzed publicly available news content, without human participants, private data, interviews, surveys, or experiments. Formal ethics approval was therefore not required. Illustrative excerpts adhered to copyright standards and were appropriately attributed.
3.10 Data availability
The author retained the coded dataset, codebook, and analysis materials. Shared datasets should include metadata, outlet name, publication date, URL or database identifier, and coding variables rather than full text. Availability is subject to copyright and institutional requirements.
4 Results
4.1 Sample overview
The sample was relatively balanced across ideological categories, although the centrist group contained the most coded items. Because some outlets contained more single-mention or low-context cases, several items were coded as “cannot determine.” Accordingly, the interpretation of rhetorical function focuses primarily on clearly codable cases. The initial stratified sample yielded 1,972 items retrieved via keyword searches (left-leaning: n = 614; centrist: n = 690; right-leaning: n = 668). However, during the structured coding process, a subset of items ( n = 676) was identified as containing single-mention, incidental, or low-context references to the keyword, lacking sufficient text to determine a dominant rhetorical function. To preserve data integrity and prevent skewing of inferential statistics, these uncodable items were systematically excluded from subsequent cross-tabulations. This resulted in a final analytical sample of 1,296 clearly codable cases for the chi-square tests of independence across usage type, target identity, and tone.
4.2 Usage type of “fake news”
The results show clear ideological differences in how the phrase “fake news” was used. Among clearly codable items, left-leaning outlets used the term mainly in an analytical way. Of the 604 codable left-leaning items, 414 articles or transcripts (68.5%) used “fake news” to discuss misinformation, disinformation, media systems, political communication, or democratic risk, while 12.4% used the term in a directly accusatory way.
This pattern is illustrated by cases in which left-leaning outlets framed “fake news” as a broader democratic or informational problem, for example, in discussions of misinformation ecosystems, election integrity, and public trust. In contrast, accusatory cases were coded when the label was directed at a specific political actor, outlet, or claim as a delegitimizing accusation rather than a descriptive or analytical reference.
For example, an item discussing the spread of misinformation during an election cycle was coded as analytical because “fake news” was used to describe a broader information problem rather than to attack a specific opponent. By contrast, an item criticizing a misleading claim from a political actor was coded as accusatory because the phrase was used to challenge the credibility of a specific target. Similarly, a centrist item explaining how social media platforms amplify false claims was coded as analytical because the emphasis was on verification, platform governance, and information systems, whereas a right-leaning segment referring to mainstream media as “fake news” was coded as accusatory because the label functioned as a direct delegitimizing attack on an institutional source.
Centrist outlets also used the term mostly analytically, although the pattern was weaker than in left-leaning outlets. Among 334 codable centrist items, 195 (58.4%) were analytical and 99 (29.6%) were accusatory. Analytical cases were typically characterized by explanatory or contextual language, such as references to misinformation systems, platform governance, fact-checking, election integrity, or public trust, where “fake news” was used to describe a broader information problem rather than to attack a specific target. By contrast, accusatory cases were coded when the label was used to challenge the credibility of a named actor, outlet, or claim, often signaled by delegitimizing verbs and adjectives or by direct attribution of deception. This suggests that centrist outlets often used the term to explain or contextualize misinformation, while also reporting cases in which actors accused others of producing or spreading “fake news”.
Right-leaning outlets showed the strongest accusatory pattern. Among 358 codable right-leaning items, 188 items (52.5%) used “fake news” accusatorily. This was substantially higher than in the left-leaning and centrist categories. Accusatory cases were commonly marked by direct attacks on mainstream media, journalists, or opposing political actors, with the phrase functioning as a delegitimizing label rather than as a descriptive reference. Fox News was especially influential in this pattern, with 187 of 250 codable Fox items (74.8%) coded as accusatory. 4.3 Target of the “fake news” label
The target analysis also revealed substantial ideological differences. In right-leaning outlets, the most common target was mainstream or center-left media. Among codable right-leaning items, 208 items (58.1%) targeted mainstream or center-left outlets. This pattern was driven primarily by Fox News, where attacks on mainstream journalism were the dominant form of the label. For example, items were coded as targeting mainstream or center-left media when “fake news” was used to dismiss established news organizations or to present them as politically biased or untrustworthy.
Left-leaning outlets showed a more distributed target pattern. Their most common target was also mainstream or center-left media, but this accounted for only 24.4% of left-leaning items. In these cases, the label often appeared in discussion of attacks on mainstream outlets rather than as a direct attack by the outlet itself. For example, items were coded in this category when they discussed accusations that mainstream journalism was misleading, biased, or manipulative. Left-leaning outlets also frequently targeted political actors, platforms, specific stories or claims, and foreign actors.
Centrist outlets showed a different pattern again. Among codable centrist items, the most common target was platforms or technology companies, which appeared in 123 items (36.8%). These cases typically situated “fake news” in relation to social media, platform moderation, online information systems, and technology governance (Lu et al., 2026). For example, items were coded in this category when the label was linked to platform responsibility for the circulation of false claims.

4.4 Tone of coverage
Tone also varied across ideological groups. Right-leaning outlets showed the strongest pattern of hostility. Among codable right-leaning items, 295 items (82.4%) were coded as alarmist or hostile. These cases typically used emotionally charged language, strong evaluative wording, or threat-oriented framing to present “fake news” as an immediate danger, political threat, or institutional failure. Centrist outlets also had a high proportion of alarmist or hostile content, with 259 items (77.5%). However, in the centrist category, this often reflected reporting on threats, attacks, foreign interference, or misinformation harms rather than direct partisan attack. Left-leaning outlets were more mixed. An alarmist or hostile tone still appeared in 50.3% of left-leaning items, but left-leaning outlets also had a much larger share of dismissive or satirical tones, especially when transcripts discussed “fake news” rhetoric critically or mockingly.
The relationship between ideology and tone was significant, χ 2 ( 6 , N = 1 , 306 ) = 218.91 , p < 0.001 , Cramér’s V = 0.289. This suggests that ideological identity was associated not only with how the term was used, but also with the emotional or evaluative style surrounding it.
4.5 Evidence provision and fact-checking language
Evidence provision showed a more complex pattern. Left-leaning outlets had the highest share of strong-evidence items, with 387 items (63.0%) coded as strong evidence. Right-leaning outlets had 233 items (34.9%) coded as strong evidence, while centrist outlets had 157 items (22.8%). Strong evidence was coded when an item explicitly relied on verifiable sources such as official records, named experts, data, or formal fact-checks to support the claim being made. By contrast, items were coded as having no explicit evidence when the text made an accusation or assertion without providing a clear supporting source. Centrist and right-leaning categories also included many items coded as having no explicit evidence, partly because several rows contained “cannot determine” or low-context cases.
Fact-checking language appeared frequently across all ideological groups. It appeared in 96.3% of left-leaning items, 95.5% of centrist items, and 93.0% of right-leaning items. Here, fact-checking language refers to explicit terms and constructions such as “fact-check,” “debunk,” “verified,” “confirmed,” or “false claim,” as well as references to verification processes or corrective reporting. Although the difference was statistically significant, the effect size was very small, suggesting that verification language was common across the dataset and not strongly divided along ideological lines (Lee et al., 2025).
4.6 Political context
The phrase “fake news” appeared most often in relation to election and voting politics. This was true across all three ideological groups, but the pattern was strongest for right-leaning outlets. Among codable right-leaning items, 328 items (91.6%) were connected to election or voting contexts. Left-leaning outlets also showed a strong election focus, with 494 items (80.6%), while centrist outlets had 221 items (66.6%). Items were coded as election/voting context when “fake news” was explicitly linked to campaigns, candidates, ballot counting, election integrity, voter fraud, turnout, or post-election disputes.
Other issue areas appeared much less frequently. Centrist outlets had a somewhat broader distribution, including media/journalism trust, COVID/public health, immigration, economic issues, and platform/technology contexts. Left-leaning outlets also included some coverage of crime, policing, technology, journalism trust, and public health. Right-leaning outlets were more concentrated around election and voting narratives. Items were coded into these other categories when “fake news” was primarily connected to the named issue area rather than to electoral politics.
The association between ideology and political context was statistically significant, χ 2 ( 16 , N = 1 , 303 ) = 139.56 , p < 0.001 , Cramér’s V = 0.231. This indicates that the issue context of “fake news” rhetoric differed across ideological outlet groups. Election and voting politics dominated across all three groups, but the intensity of this focus varied by ideology.
Overall, the results show that the phrase “fake news” was not used consistently across ideological media categories. Left-leaning outlets most often used the term analytically, usually to discuss misinformation, democratic risks, political communication, or broader media distrust. Centrist outlets also leaned toward analytical use, but they more often linked the term to platforms, technology, and information governance. Right-leaning outlets, especially Fox News, used the term more often as an accusatory label.
These findings support the study’s expectation that right-leaning outlets would use “fake news” more accusatorily than left-leaning or centrist outlets. They also suggest a broader theoretical pattern: the label operates as a framing device that assigns blame, as an agenda-cutting device that weakens rival claims, and as an agenda-steering device that directs audiences toward ideologically aligned sources. In left-leaning and centrist outlets, by contrast, the term more often functions as an analytical or corrective frame, supporting agenda building around misinformation, verification, and institutional trust. In this sense, “fake news” functions not only as a description of misinformation but also as a rhetorical tool for boundary-making and credibility contestation in polarized U.S. media discourse.
5 Discussion
5.1 Overview of the findings
The findings show that “fake news” is not used as a neutral or uniform term across the U.S. media system. Instead, its meaning and function vary by outlet ideology and by communicative purpose. Across CNN, MSNBC, The New York Times, AP, Politico, USA Today Online, Fox News, The Daily Caller, and Conservative Daily News, the phrase appears as a flexible rhetorical device: at times analytical, at times defensive, and at times openly accusatory (Nielsen and Graves, 2017). This pattern addresses RQ1, which asks how left-leaning, centrist, and right-leaning outlets differ in the frequency and function of “fake news” use.
The additional contribution of the present study lies in showing that these differences are not merely semantic, but patterned and ideological: outlet position shapes whether “fake news” is used primarily to explain misinformation, defend institutional credibility, or delegitimize rival sources. In this sense, the study extends prior work by demonstrating how the same term operates differently across media environments, and how those differences map onto distinct rhetorical roles in partisan discourse. Rather than treating “fake news” as a single contested label, the analysis shows that its function is conditioned by outlet ideology, target selection, and communicative context. This helps explain not only what the term means, but what it does in polarized media systems.
5.2 Ideological differences in use
The clearest pattern concerns the contrast between analytical and accusatory uses. Left-leaning outlets used “fake news” mostly in an analytical way. In the left-leaning category, 68.5% of codable cases were analytical, indicating that the term was usually embedded in discussion of misinformation, democratic risk, election integrity, public trust, or platform governance. This pattern supports RQ1 and aligns with H1, which predicted that left-leaning outlets would use the term more often in analytical or meta-commentary ways.
Centrist outlets also used the term primarily analytically, though less strongly than left-leaning outlets. In this group, 58.4% of codable cases were analytical and 29.6% were accusatory. This suggests that centrist outlets often framed “fake news” as a public information problem while also reporting direct accusations made by political actors and media institutions. That pattern is consistent with the broader role of centrist journalism as verification-oriented and procedural, especially in coverage of misinformation and accountability.
Right-leaning outlets showed the strongest accusatory pattern. In that category, 52.5% of codable cases used “fake news” accusatorily, which supports H1. The result also aligns with countermedia scholarship showing that right-leaning media ecosystems often position themselves in opposition to mainstream journalism and institutional authority (Benkler et al., 2018;Haller et al., 2019). In this context, “fake news” functions less as a neutral descriptor and more as a symbolic marker of distrust.
5.3 Framing and delegitimation
The results support the relevance of framing theory. Framing works by selecting certain aspects of reality and making them salient so that audiences define problems, assign blame, and evaluate solutions in particular ways (Entman, 1993). In this study, “fake news” appears to function as a framing device because it is frequently attached to specific targets and used to organize interpretation around credibility, bias, and legitimacy rather than simply factual accuracy.
This framing function was clearest in right-leaning outlets, where mainstream or center-left media were the most common target. Among codable right-leaning items, 58.1% targeted mainstream or center-left outlets. This pattern supports RQ2 and H2, because it shows that conservative outlets most often labeled mainstream media as “fake news.” It also suggests that the accusation can extend beyond a specific claim toward the credibility of the institution itself.
The data therefore suggest that “fake news” can operate as a boundary-making device in some cases, particularly when it is directed at mainstream media institutions. In those instances, the label may help distinguish trusted in-group sources from distrusted out-group sources and contribute to broader credibility contests in polarized media environments. This interpretation is consistent with prior work on the delegitimizing and hegemonic uses of fake news rhetoric, although the present study speaks more directly to patterned rhetorical usage than to audience-level effects (Waisbord, 2018a,2018b;Zompetti, 2019).
This systematic targeting pattern highlights how right-leaning media architectures utilize weaponized rhetoric not merely to critique individual stories, but to execute a broader epistemic challenge against institutional journalism. This finding directly mirrors the dynamics identified by countermedia scholars (e.g.,Heft et al., 2020;Rone, 2022), who argue that contemporary alternative news ecosystems sustain audience engagement by positioning themselves as defensive bulwarks against a fundamentally compromised “mainstream” consensus. By embedding the “fake news” label into daily elite rhetoric, these outlets turn political disagreement into a fundamental crisis of source legitimacy.
5.4 Agenda cutting and steering
The findings also support the study’s argument that “fake news” rhetoric functions as an instrument of agenda manipulation. In accusatory form, the phrase can operate as agenda-cutting by reducing the visibility and legitimacy of rival claims, outlets, or political narratives (Amazeen and Vargo, 2021;Vargo and Minooie, 2025). In the present data, this appears most clearly when outlets use the label to delegitimize a source rather than to discuss the content of a specific claim in analytical terms.
The results also suggest different rhetorical orientations across ideological groups. Right-leaning outlets more often connect “fake news” to mainstream or center-left media, while left-leaning outlets more often use the term analytically to discuss misinformation, election integrity, or platform governance. Centrist outlets more often connect the phrase to platforms, verification, and information systems. Taken together, these patterns suggest that “fake news” helps organize how media outlets position issues, sources, and credibility claims within broader agenda structures. They are also consistent with agenda-melding theory in a limited, theoretical sense, insofar as repeated exposure to such cues in high-choice media environments may contribute to audience-side agenda construction, though the present study does not directly measure audience effects (Maniou and Bantimaroudis, 2026;Shaw et al., 1999b;Vera-Revilla et al., 2025).
5.5 Targets of the label
The target findings deepen the interpretation of RQ2 and H2. Right-leaning outlets most often targeted mainstream or center-left media, centrist outlets most often focused on platforms and technology companies, and left-leaning outlets showed a more distributed pattern across media organizations, political actors, platforms, claims, and foreign actors. This distribution suggests that each ideological group located the source of “fake news” in different centers of power and influence.
For right-leaning outlets, the problem was most often framed as mainstream media credibility. For centrist outlets, the problem was more often framed as the circulation of information through digital platforms and online systems. For left-leaning outlets, the problem appeared more broadly connected to democratic institutions, misinformation ecosystems, and political communication. These differences matter because the target of the label helps define the frame through which the issue is interpreted: if “fake news” is attached mainly to mainstream journalism, the frame encourages distrust of traditional media; if it is attached to platforms, the frame points toward regulation, moderation, or media literacy; and if it is attached to political actors or specific claims, the frame emphasizes verification and accountability. In this sense, the data show not direct solutions, but different framed responses implied by the way the label is deployed.
This is consistent withWardle and Derakhshan’s (2017)framework of information disorder, which emphasizes that misinformation is not only about content but also about actors, circulation, and context. The present study adds that media outlets themselves actively participate in assigning blame and defining the source of disorder.
The centrist preoccupation with digital platforms and technology companies further underscores this structural divergence. While partisan sectors locate the threat of ‘fake news’ within rival human or political actors, centrist journalism exhibits a clear pattern of platform skepticism, viewing the crisis as a procedural and algorithmic malfunction of the attention economy. This aligns closely with the work ofFletcher and Nielsen (2019)on generalized media skepticism, suggesting that institutional journalism treats digital infrastructure as the primary vector of informational disorder, thereby framing solutions in terms of technocratic governance, policy regulation, and structural content moderation rather than ideological warfare.
5.6 Tone and emotional intensity
The tone results show that “fake news” rhetoric is often emotionally charged. Right-leaning outlets had the highest proportion of alarmist or hostile tone, followed by centrist outlets, while left-leaning outlets were more mixed and included a substantial share of dismissive or satirical tone. This pattern reinforces the idea that “fake news” is not merely descriptive language but a confrontational rhetorical tool.
The high level of hostile tone in right-leaning outlets supports H1, because it suggests that accusatory uses are often accompanied by strong evaluative language. The centrist pattern may appear surprising, but it likely reflects the seriousness of the issues being reported, especially election interference, platform harm, and public misinformation. The left-leaning pattern is notable because dismissive or satirical tone appeared more often there than in the other categories, suggesting that these outlets sometimes treated “fake news” rhetoric as something to critique or mock rather than simply report.
This matters because repeated elite discourse about “fake news” can shape public trust and increase cynicism toward media institutions (Van Duyn and Collier, 2019). The present study suggests that outlet-level repetition of the label may contribute to that broader effect, especially when the term is used with a hostile tone.
5.7 Evidence provision and fact-checking
The evidence findings partly complicate the original expectation in H2. The hypothesis predicted that centrist outlets would be more likely to provide explicit factual evidence when invoking the term. However, the results show that left-leaning outlets had the highest share of strong evidence, while centrist and right-leaning outlets had larger shares of cases with no explicit evidence. This means H2 is only partially supported on the evidence dimension.
This result should be interpreted cautiously, however, because a substantial number of centrist and right-leaning cases were coded as “cannot determine” or had low contextual clarity. The presence of fact-checking language across all ideological categories also shows that verification discourse is widely normalized across the media system, even if it is not used in the same way. In some cases, fact-checking terms were tied to concrete sourcing practices, such as references to official data, expert testimony, or formal debunking. In other cases, the language functioned more as a rhetorical marker of credibility than as evidence that directly supported the claim being made.
For that reason, future research should examine not only whether fact-checking language appears, but also how evidence is deployed, whose evidence is privileged, and whether the evidence genuinely supports the accusation.
5.8 Political context
The political context findings show that elections and voting were the dominant setting for “fake news” discourse across all ideological categories. This pattern is not surprising given that the study period included the 2020 presidential election, the 2022 midterms, and the 2024 presidential election. Still, the strength of the election pattern is important. Among codable right-leaning items, 91.6% were connected to election or voting contexts, while left-leaning and centrist outlets were also heavily election-focused, though their remaining cases were spread more evenly across other issue areas such as media trust, public health, platform governance, crime, immigration, and other topics.
This finding strengthens RQ2 because it shows that the label was most often activated in electoral settings, where credibility and legitimacy are especially consequential. During election periods, “fake news” can be used not only to challenge media reports but also to question vote counts, candidates, campaigns, and democratic institutions. That makes the phrase politically powerful because it shapes how audiences interpret democratic conflict and information credibility.
The election-centered pattern also extends prior scholarship showing that fake news became especially prominent after the 2016 U.S. election (Allcott and Gentzkow, 2017;Lazer et al., 2018). The present study shows that by 2020–2024, the phrase remained deeply embedded in election discourse, but with expanded rhetorical functions that include accusation, counter-accusation, and institutional distrust.
5.9 Theoretical contribution
This study makes three main theoretical contributions. First, it supports the argument that “fake news” should be studied as both content and rhetoric, because the label does rhetorical work by framing actors, assigning blame, and shaping credibility judgments. Second, it extends framing theory by showing that the same label can perform different interpretive functions across ideological media environments. Third, it contributes to agenda manipulation theory by showing that “fake news” can be used not only to discredit rivals but also to reinforce preferred narratives and credibility boundaries in polarized media systems.
5.10 Practical implications
The findings have practical implications for journalism, media literacy, and democratic communication. For journalists, the study suggests that using the term “fake news” without clear definition can unintentionally reinforce confusion. Because the phrase is politically loaded, journalists should use more precise terms such as misinformation, disinformation, manipulated content, false claim, or unverified allegation when appropriate.
For media literacy educators, the findings show that audiences need to understand not only how false information spreads but also how accusations of falseness are used strategically. Media literacy should therefore ask who is using the label, who is being targeted, what evidence is offered, and whether the label is being used to correct misinformation or dismiss an opponent.
For democratic communication, the results suggest a possible risk: repeated use of “fake news” as an accusatory label may contribute to distrust in shared institutions and make public debate harder. However, this inference remains indirect, because the present study analyzes media texts rather than audience reception. Future research using surveys, experiments, or interviews would be needed to test whether repeated exposure to such rhetoric actually produces the trust effects implied here.
5.11 Limitations and future research
This study has several limitations. First, the sample includes only articles and transcripts containing the exact phrase “fake news,” so it does not capture related terms such as misinformation, disinformation, hoax, propaganda, or false claim unless “fake news” also appeared. Future research should compare these terms directly and may also benefit from a corpus-based approach to examine how the phrase is used across broader language environments rather than only as an exact keyword.
Second, some outlet files contained a substantial number of “cannot determine” cases, especially in the centrist and right-leaning categories. This may reflect limited context, transcript structure, or coding ambiguity. Future studies should use full-text sources where possible and apply intercoder reliability checks.
Third, the ideological categories are useful for comparison but may mask important differences within each group. For example, Fox News strongly shaped the right-leaning pattern, while The Daily Caller and Conservative Daily News showed different distributions. Future research should examine outlet-level variation more closely.
Finally, this study focuses on media texts rather than audience responses. It shows how outlets use “fake news” rhetoric, but not how audiences interpret it. Future research could combine content analysis with surveys, experiments, interviews, or reception studies to examine how repeated exposure affects trust, polarization, and attitudes toward corrections.
6 Conclusion
Overall, the study shows that “fake news” functions as a contested political label within the U.S. media system. The findings answer RQ1 and RQ2 by showing that outlet ideology shapes both how the term is used and what kinds of targets it is attached to.
The study provides partial to strong support for the hypotheses. H1 is supported because right-leaning outlets used “fake news” more accusatorily, while left-leaning outlets used it more analytically. H2 is supported in the pattern of targets, since conservative outlets more often labeled mainstream media as fake news and liberal outlets more often targeted right-wing sources, although the evidence-support expectation was only partially supported.
Taken together, the findings show that the politics of “fake news” is not only about whether information is true or false, but also about how credibility is framed, who is positioned as trustworthy, and how media organizations compete to shape public judgment in a polarized information environment.
