Headnote Abstract
As sustainable development pressures escalate, stakeholders increasingly prioritise sustained corporate innovation resilience. However, these pressures also incentivise firms to adopt symbolic greenwashing tactics to mislead stakeholders, while the existing literature has paid limited attention to how such deceptive practices erode firms' innovation capabilities. This study examines the impact of corporate greenwashing on innovation resilience using a panel dataset of 5,183 Chinese listed firms from 2015 to 2024. We find a significant negative relationship between corporate greenwashing and innovation resilience, suggesting that symbolic environmental management does not generate the technological depth required to withstand external shocks. Mechanism analysis indicates that this adverse effect operates through increased negative media coverage and reduced research and development expenditure, which jointly undermine corporate reputation and heighten structural rigidity. Further evidence shows that external institutional investors mitigate this negative relationship by strengthening monitoring and information verification, whereas government subsidies exacerbate it by creating soft budget constraints and fostering organisational inertia. The findings extend the literature by showing that innovation resilience depends not only on substantive technological investment and capability building, but also on limiting symbolic environmental communication that misallocates resources and weakens external support.
Keywords: Greenwashing, innovation resilience, institutional investors, government subsidies.

Introduction
With frequent market disruptions, stringent environmental regulations, rising social expectations, and intense market competition, corporate stakeholders are increasingly focused on long-term survival capabilities rather than short-term financial returns (Peng & Jia, 2024; Wu et al., 2024; Guo et al., 2025). Therefore, the resource-based view and dynamic capability theories are gaining broader recognition, encouraging firms to pursue sustainable technological advantages (Oeij et al., 2017; Boh et al., 2020; Dinu et al., 2023; Mihai et al., 2024; Luo et al., 2025). Previous studies suggested that genuine corporate social and environmental strategies may enhance the firm's long-term financial performance (Pava & Krausz, 1996). Nevertheless, within the context of global climate change and sustainable development, increasing amounts of institutional pressure are being directed toward environmental compliance (Qian et al., 2025; Zhu et al., 2025). Corporate environmental, social, and governance (ESG) legitimacy is becoming a growing focus for investors, regulators, and corporate management (Torelli et al., 2020; Li et al., 2023; Xu et al., 2025). Another indicator within sustainable development is innovation resilience, which is gaining prominence as a critical measure of corporate capability while incorporating uncertainty factors. Innovation resilience assesses a firm's capacity to sustain its innovative activities and recover efficiently from external shocks, considering both technological output and environmental adaptability (Oeij et al., 2017; Luo et al., 2025). From a theoretical perspective, signalling theory posited that deceptive corporate communications and symbolic greenwashing strategies can distort market signals, thereby damaging corporate reputation, misallocating resources, and eroding competitive advantages in the evolving global economy (Fedorova & Stepanov, 2024; Luo et al., 2025; Xu et al., 2025). As the relationship between environmental legitimacy and technological capability becomes a growing concern for market participants, the role of greenwashing and innovation resilience in shaping firms' strategies becomes increasingly pertinent.
As the world's second-largest economy and largest greenhouse gas emitter, China has made a firm commitment to sustainable development, pledging to achieve carbon neutrality by 2060. This commitment has made environmental compliance and green technological strategies critical topics in firm governance (Zhang, 2022; Fang & Liu, 2025). Specifically, Li et al. (2023) and Xu et al. (2025) examined Chinese listed firms and found a significant relationship between corporate environmental behaviour and short-term market valuation, with this relationship being influenced by stakeholder perceptions. Similarly, Oeij et al. (2017) analysed samples from manufacturing industries and concluded that genuine environmental performance stimulates green innovation by alleviating financing constraints and attracting stakeholder support. Furthermore, scholars who studied other economies have also provided evidence that deceptive environmental communication carries severe consequences. For instance, researchers analysed European markets, finding a positive relationship between corporate greenwashing and immediate reputational penalties (Torelli et al., 2020; Ferrón-Vílchez et al., 2021 ; Xu et al., 2025). On the other hand, Qian et al. (2025) and Zhu et al. (2025) examined global firms, demonstrating that symbolic environmental claims lead to higher perceived market risk, greater stakeholder scepticism, and lower investor demand.
Nonetheless, Zhao et al. (2023) and Lu et al. (2025) pointed out that greenwashing is often driven by managerial myopia and represents an illusory tactic rather than a substantive strategy. This deceptive practice may lead to severe resource misallocation within the organisation, making it difficult for firms to build robust technological foundations, which can result in improper capital allocation (Peng & Kong, 2024; Guo et al., 2025). Additionally, management may exploit greenwashing for speculative purposes to cater to regulatory priorities and secure policy dividends (Sun & Zhang, 2019; Peng & Kong, 2024). Previous studies also found that greenwashing leads to short-term market value fluctuations and reputational damage (Li et al., 2023; Fedorova & Stepanov, 2024; Xu et al., 2025). Although corporate greenwashing is generally considered harmful to firms' operations and potential investors, there is limited research on whether this deceptive behaviour fundamentally threatens the firm's long-term innovation resilience. Especially, some transmission mechanisms proposed by recent literature argued that greenwashing alters stakeholder trust and internal resource allocation (Fedorova & Stepanov, 2024; Lu et al., 2025; Xu et al., 2025). However, it is unclear how greenwashing affects innovation resilience through the dual pathways of negative news proliferation and research and development expenditure suppression. Furthermore, Zhang (2022) and Fang and Liu (2025) mentioned that public policies and external governance shape corporate environmental behaviour. However, whether external funds can mitigate the adverse effects of greenwashing and whether government subsidies inadvertently exacerbate these vulnerabilities remain unexplored in the existing literature.
This paper aims to investigate whether corporate greenwashing negatively impacts innovation resilience, as well as to explore the potential transmission mechanisms and boundary conditions. This study expands the discussions of Wu et al. (2024) and Lu et al. (2025) on corporate environmental tactics and technological capabilities by demonstrating the relationship between greenwashing and innovation resilience. In this way, it addresses gaps in the existing literature. Our findings indicate that greenwashing negatively affects firms' innovation resilience. Furthermore, we identify negative news and research and development expenditure suppression as potential transmission mechanisms, which provide a new perspective on the structural consequences of deceptive environmental claims. Moreover, this study demonstrates an asymmetric moderating effect, showing that external funds can mitigate these negative effects, whereas government subsidies strengthen the detrimental impact. These findings offer practical implications for firm operators, investors, and policymakers, which suggest strategies to enhance corporate governance and optimise subsidy designs in sustainable investments. In general, this study provides empirical evidence using data from the largest emerging market, emphasising the importance of substantive technological accumulation over symbolic environmental management.
1. Literature review and hypotheses development
According to the resource-based view theory, corporate resilience and long-term competitive advantages are derived from idiosyncratic capabilities and substantial technological accumulation (Peng & Jia, 2024; Wu et al., 2024). Oeij et al. (2017) and Xu et al. (2025) suggested that innovation resilience and sustainable competitive advantages depend on adaptive recovery, technological continuity, and stakeholder trust. In contrast, greenwashing represents a decoupling strategy wherein management allocates organisational resources towards superficial impression management rather than substantive capability enhancement (Torelli et al., 2020; Ferrón-Vílchez et al., 2021 ; Lu et al., 2025). When confronted with exogenous market shocks, firms relying on deceptive environmental claims lack the fundamental technological infrastructure and operational agility required to adapt effectively (Peng & Kong, 2024; Guo et al., 2025). Consequently, the artificial advantages constructed through greenwashing fail to protect the firm during periods of instability, leading to a significant reduction in innovation resilience (Wu et al., 2024; Lu et al., 2025). Therefore, this study proposes Hypothesis 1.
Hypothesis 1: Corporate greenwashing has a negative effect on innovation resilience.
Signalling theory suggests that, in an environment of information asymmetry, credible corporate environmental and innovation-related information can function as a positive signal that helps stakeholders evaluate firms' technological prospects and long-term development potential (Fedorova & Stepanov, 2024; Xu et al., 2025). However, deceptive corporate communications produce the opposite effect once stakeholders perceive a gap between symbolic environmental claims and substantive actions, since such an inconsistency carries a high risk of exposure and subsequently triggers intense media scrutiny (Torelli et al., 2020; Li et al., 2023; Xu et al., 2025). Once greenwashing practices are identified by stakeholders, they generate a rapid increase in negative news coverage that severely damages corporate reputation (Ferrón-Vílchez et al., 2021 ; Li et al., 2023; Fedorova and Stepanov, 2024). Innovation resilience relies heavily on collaborative networks and the continuous inflow of external resources during crisis periods (Oeij et al., 2017; Boh et al., 2020). The proliferation of negative news undermines stakeholder confidence, thus severing these critical support networks and leaving the firm isolated when external disruptions occur (Torelli et al., 2020; Fedorova and Stepanov, 2024; Xu et al., 2025). Therefore, the resultant legitimacy crisis operates as a primary external mechanism through which greenwashing diminishes innovation resilience. Thus, we propose Hypothesis 2.
Hypothesis 2: An increase in negative news mediates the negative relationship between corporate greenwashing and innovation resilience.
From the perspective of resource allocation and managerial myopia, the difference between genuine green strategies and greenwashing lies in how firms allocate scarce organisational resources. Oeij et al. (2017) and Peng and Jia (2024) suggested that sustained innovation depends on continuous technological investment, adaptive capability, and substantive resource commitment. In contrast, greenwashing is a short-term opportunistic behaviour (Zhao et al., 2023; Lu et al., 2025). Maintaining a deceptive environmental posture necessitates substantial financial commitments to marketing and public relations, which directly crowd out investments in substantive research and development activities (Peng & Kong, 2024; Guo et al., 2025). This chronic suppression of research and development expenditure prevents the firm from building the robust technological reservoir necessary to absorb and respond to market or technological disruptions (Lu et al., 2025). Without continuous technological upgrading, firms become structurally rigid and highly vulnerable to external shocks, thereby losing their innovation resilience (Peng & Jia, 2024; Wu et al., 2024; Guo et al., 2025). Therefore, Hypothesis 3 is proposed.
Hypothesis 3: The suppression of research and development expenditure mediates the negative relationship between corporate greenwashing and innovation resilience.
External institutional investors constitute an important governance mechanism for corporate oversight and information verification (Menkhoff, 2002; Qian et al., 2025; Zhu et al., 2025). Compared to dispersed stakeholders, who find it difficult to distinguish symbolic environmental claims from substantive practices under information asymmetry, institutional investors are better positioned to process complex corporate information and identify discrepancies between reported environmental claims and actual corporate practices (Boh et al., 2020; Zhao et al., 2023). By exerting pressure on management through active monitoring, external funds compel firms to maintain a baseline of substantive innovation and reduce their reliance on symbolic actions (Qian et al., 2025; Zhu et al., 2025). This enhanced governance restricts the extent to which resources are diverted towards deceptive practices, thereby buffering the firm's technological capabilities and mitigating the detrimental impact on innovation resilience (Zhao et al., 2023; Zhu et al., 2025). Thus, we propose Hypothesis 4.
Hypothesis 4: External institutional investors weaken the negative effect of corporate greenwashing on innovation resilience.
While government subsidies are designed to stimulate innovation, their impact depends on whether firms use these resources to support innovation or to meet environmental policy expectations (Zhang, 2022). In the context of greenwashing, subsidies often produce unintended adverse consequences (Zhang, 2022; Fang & Liu, 2025). Firms frequently engage in deceptive environmental reporting specifically to cater to regulatory priorities and extract policy-driven financial rewards (Sun & Zhang, 2019; Peng & Kong, 2024). High levels of government subsidies create a soft budget constraint that fosters organisational inertia and discourages managers from undertaking the high risks associated with genuine technological innovation (Zhang, 2022; Lu et al., 2025). This policy-induced rent-seeking behaviour causes subsidised firms to become overly reliant on external financial support rather than internal capability development, thereby significantly amplifying their fragility when confronted with market volatility (Sun & Zhang, 2019; Fang & Liu, 2025). Thus, we propose Hypothesis 5.
Hypothesis 5: Government subsidies strengthen the negative effect of corporate greenwashing on innovation resilience.

Drawing on information asymmetry theory and recent empirical methodologies, this study conceptualises corporate greenwashing as the positive divergence between environmental information disclosure levels and substantive environmental performance (Ma et al., 2025; Zhu et al., 2025). Corporate management has strong incentives to selectively disclose favourable environmental information to acquire policy benefits and market reputation, which generates a significant discrepancy between external claims and actual green practices (Sun & Zhang, 2019; Peng & Kong, 2024; Lu et al., 2025). To accurately quantify this divergence, this study selects the corporate environmental disclosure score provided by the Chinese Research Data Services Platform (CNRDS) to represent external environmental claims, while employing the Shanghai Huazheng Stock (HZ) ESG rating score to reflect the actual underlying environmental governance performance (Ma et al., 2025; Qian et al., 2025). 3. Empirical results and discussion
Table no. 2 reports a significant negative relationship between corporate greenwashing and innovation resilience at the 1% level in Model 1. The result remains robust after including control variables in Model 2. To further evaluate robustness, Models 3 to 8 present additional checks. Specifically, in Models 3 and 4 of Table no. 3, the dependent variable is replaced with alternative measures of innovation resilience. Following the methodologies of Peng and Jia (2024) and Wu et al. (2024), two alternative proxies are constructed based on city-level patent grants and corporate patent applications. The negative relationship persists. Furthermore, we remeasure the independent variable. In Model 5, the ESG score difference between CNRDS and Bloomberg is adopted as an alternative indicator. In Models 6 to 8, corporate greenwashing is reconstructed using pairwise differences in environmental pillar scores among CNRDS, HZ, and Bloomberg, following Ali et al. (2026). Across all alternative specifications in Models 3 to 8, the results consistently demonstrate that corporate greenwashing continues to exhibit a significantly negative effect on innovation resilience, with coefficients remaining significant at least at the 10% level.
To address potential endogeneity in the error terms, this study employs the instrumental variable two-stage least squares regression for estimation, as reported in Table no. 4. Following He et al. (2025a, 2025b), two instrumental variables are constructed in Models 9 to 12. Specifically, the first instrumental variable (IV1) is defined as the annual city-level average of corporate greenwashing, excluding the focal firm. This measure captures regional peer effects while mitigating mechanical correlation with the firm-specific error term. Additionally, the second instrument (IV2) is constructed as a Bartik-style variable to further alleviate endogeneity concerns. Following Bartik (2009) and He et al. (2025a, 2025b), IV2 is obtained by interacting the one-year lagged value of the firm's greenwashing with the first difference of the annual sample mean. This design links firm-level exposure to aggregate trends while remaining plausibly exogenous to contemporaneous firm-specific shocks. The validity of these instrumental variables was subsequently confirmed by several statistical tests. The Kleibergen-Paap rk LM statistics for Models 9 and 11 consistently reject the null hypothesis of under-identification, indicating that the instruments are relevant. Furthermore, the Kleibergen-Paap rk Wald F, Anderson-Rubin Wald, and Stock-Wright LM S statistics are all significant at the 1% level, rejecting the weak instrumental variable hypothesis. The p-values of the Hansen J statistic exceed 0.1, which suggests that instrumental variables satisfy the exogeneity condition. In the second-stage estimations, the coefficients on corporate greenwashing remain significantly negative at the 1% level. To mitigate selection bias caused by non-randomness, this study employs propensity score matching approaches for the regression analysis based on whether the firm engages in greenwashing. In Models 13 to 16, we apply one-to-one nearest neighbour matching, caliper matching, radius matching, and kernel matching, respectively. The regression coefficients of corporate greenwashing are all significant at the 5% level. These checks confirm the robustness of the regression results for the baseline models, which provide evidence that greenwashing negatively affects innovation resilience. Thus, Hypothesis 1 is not rejected.
Contrary to the findings of Peng and Jia (2024) and Wu et al. (2024), who found a positive relationship between authentic environmental strategies and innovation resilience, this relationship changes when corporate greenwashing behaviours are introduced. Some scholars suggest that significant greenwashing practices indicate that management allocates organisational resources towards superficial impression management rather than substantive capability enhancement (Torelli et al., 2020; Ferrón-Vílchez et al., 2021 ; Lu et al., 2025), a strategic flaw that the firm itself may not easily correct. Zhao and Wang (2025) argued that greenwashing behaviours can lead to a severe misallocation of resources. Fang and Liu (2025) mentioned that these deceptive practices often represent internal and environmental uncertainty. Since innovation resilience reflects the firm's ability to achieve growth through technological accumulation and system recovery (Peng & Jia, 2024; Wu et al., 2024; Guo et al., 2025), the firm is perceived as having unreliable sustainability credentials, and innovation resilience tends to decline. Additionally, management has difficulty identifying the most effective technological development strategies, resulting in insufficient attention to substantive research and development (Zhao & Wang, 2025; Ali et al., 2026).
On the other hand, Li et al. (2023), Li and Xiao (2025), and Xu et al. (2025) considered that information asymmetry arises when different market participants have unequal access to relevant information, which leads to suboptimal decision-making. Significant greenwashing creates uncertainty regarding a firm's true sustainability performance (Lu et al., 2025), making it difficult for investors, creditors, and other stakeholders to assess its environmental commitments accurately (Torelli et al., 2020; Xu et al., 2025; Zhu et al., 2025). As a result, firms with higher greenwashing tendencies are viewed as riskier investments (Menkhoff, 2002; Boh et al., 2020), thereby facing challenges in obtaining financing for research and development. Moreover, Zhao et al. (2023), Qian et al. (2025), and Zhu et al. (2025) noted that firms engaging in greenwashing find it difficult to attract green investment, as institutional investors perceive them to lack transparency in their environmental disclosures.
According to the signalling theory, Torelli et al. (2020), Li et al. (2023), Fedorova and Stepanov (2024), and Xu et al. (2025) argued that deceptive corporate communications inherently carry a high risk of exposure, thereby triggering intense media scrutiny and increasing negative news coverage. To explore the potential transmission pathway of corporate greenwashing on innovation resilience, we use NNO and NNP as mediating variables. The regression results are reported in Table no. 5, and after conducting the Sobel test and bootstrapping the indirect effects 5,000 times (see Table no. A2), we conclude that corporate greenwashing negatively affects innovation resilience by increasing negative news coverage. Hence, Hypothesis 2 is not rejected. These results are consistent with findings by Torelli et al. (2020), Li et al. (2023), and Xu et al. (2025), who noted that the proliferation of negative news severely undermines stakeholder confidence. Specifically, Torelli et al. (2020) and Xu et al. (2025) suggested that greenwashing practices generate a rapid increase in negative news coverage that directly damages corporate reputation. Furthermore, Boh et al. (2020) argued that innovation resilience relies heavily on collaborative networks and external resources, while Fedorova and Stepanov (2024) believed that negative news severs these critical support networks when external disruptions occur.
From the perspective of resource allocation, Lu et al. (2025) and Zhao and Wang (2025) argued that greenwashing operates as a short-term opportunistic behaviour that necessitates substantial financial commitments to superficial impression management, thereby crowding out investments in substantive research and development activities. To explore this secondary transmission pathway, we utilise RDa and RDb as the mediating variables. The regression results are similarly reported in Models 21 to 24, and the 5,000-time bootstrapping estimations along with the Sobel test confirm the significance of this indirect effect. We conclude that corporate greenwashing negatively affects innovation resilience by suppressing research and development expenditure. Hence, Hypothesis 3 is not rejected. These findings align with the research of Peng and Kong (2024), Guo et al. (2025), and Zhao and Wang (2025), who emphasised that the chronic suppression of research and development prevents firms from building a robust technological reservoir. Specifically, Lu et al. (2025) suggested that firms that reduce investment in innovation do not absorb and respond to market disruptions effectively. Furthermore, Wu et al. (2024) and Guo et al. (2025) noted that without continuous technological upgrading, firms become structurally rigid, while Peng and Jia (2024) concluded that this structural rigidity leaves firms highly vulnerable to external shocks, ultimately leading to a complete loss of innovation resilience.
Building on external governance mechanisms, agency theory offers a critical perspective on how the market disciplines deceptive environmental claims. According to Menkhoff (2002), Qian et al. (2025), and Zhu et al. (2025), external institutional investors constitute an important governance mechanism for corporate oversight and information verification. These investors possess sophisticated analytical capabilities that allow them to identify discrepancies between reported environmental claims and actual corporate practices (Boh et al., 2020; Zhao et al., 2023). By exerting pressure on management through active monitoring, external funds compel firms to maintain a baseline of substantive innovation and reduce their reliance on purely symbolic actions. Thus, in Models 25 to 26 of Table no. 6, we examine the moderating roles of FUND and FHOLD. The interaction terms (GW×FUND and GW×FHOLD) are significantly positive at the 1% level, suggesting that institutional ownership mitigates the negative effect of corporate greenwashing on innovation resilience. Specifically, the adverse impact of greenwashing becomes weaker for firms with higher institutional investor ownership. Therefore, Hypothesis 4 is not rejected. Our findings support Qian et al. (2025) and Zhu et al. (2025), who suggested that external institutional investors encourage substantive innovation and reduce firms' reliance on symbolic actions. This governance effect also limits resource diversion toward deceptive practices, thereby protecting technological capabilities and mitigating the adverse impact on innovation resilience (Zhao et al., 2023; Zhu et al., 2025).
Conversely, from the perspective of regulatory catering and resource allocation, high levels of government support can inadvertently exacerbate the vulnerabilities associated with superficial environmental strategies. Zhang (2022) and Fang and Liu (2025) noted that while government subsidies are traditionally designed to stimulate innovation, they often produce unintended adverse consequences in the context of greenwashing. Firms frequently engage in deceptive environmental reporting specifically to cater to regulatory priorities and extract policy-driven financial rewards (Sun & Zhang, 2019; Peng & Kong, 2024). High subsidies may also create soft budget constraints, fostering organisational inertia, and discouraging managers from undertaking the risks of genuine technological innovation. Thus, in Models 27 to 28 of Table no. 6, we examine the moderating effects of SUB/TA and SUB/OR. The interaction terms (GW×SUB/TA and GW×SUB/OR) are significantly negative at the 5% level and share the same sign as the main effect of corporate greenwashing. This finding indicates that government subsidies reinforce the adverse impact of greenwashing on innovation resilience. Accordingly, Hypothesis 5 is not rejected. These results are consistent with Zhang (2022) and Lu et al. (2025), who concluded that government subsidies create a soft budget constraint, fostering organisational inertia. Additionally, Sun and Zhang (2019) and Fang and Liu (2025) further emphasised that policy-induced rent-seeking can make subsidised firms overly dependent on external financial support rather than internal capability development, thereby amplifying their fragility under market volatility.
Conclusions
To examine the relationship between corporate greenwashing and innovation resilience, we construct an innovation resilience measure under a counterfactual framework based on the deviation between actual and expected patent grants. Corporate greenwashing is proxied by discrepancies among rating agencies, reflecting the divergence between external ESG assessments and firms' actual performance. We employ a panel dataset of 5,183 Chinese listed firms spanning 2015 to 2024, which is evaluated through fixed effects regression models. The empirical results show that
(1) Corporate greenwashing negatively affects firms' innovation resilience. Symbolic environmental management fails to provide the substantive technological accumulation required to withstand external shocks. This negative effect remains robust after variable substitution and the application of alternative estimation methods.
(2) The increase in negative news mediates the adverse impact of corporate greenwashing on innovation resilience. Deceptive environmental communication triggers intensified media scrutiny and raises negative news exposure, which damages corporate reputation and disrupts critical external support networks, leaving firms more vulnerable during periods of environmental volatility.
(3) The suppression of research and development expenditure acts as another vital mediating channel. Driven by managerial myopia, diverting financial resources towards superficial impression management crowds out substantive technological investments. This renders firms structurally rigid and highly vulnerable to market disruptions.
(4) An external institutional investor mitigates the negative effect. The presence of institutional investors strengthens monitoring and information verification, exerting disciplinary pressure that promotes substantive innovation.
(5) Conversely, government subsidies exacerbate the harmful effects of greenwashing. High subsidy intensity creates soft budget constraints, fostering organisational inertia and policy-induced rent-seeking behaviour, and ultimately increasing the fragility of subsidised firms.
Our findings provide empirical evidence that corporate greenwashing significantly undermines long-term innovation resilience through two channels: the proliferation of negative news and the suppression of research and development expenditure. Furthermore, the effectiveness of external governance mechanisms varies, with institutional investors offering protective oversight while government subsidies inadvertently amplify technological vulnerabilities. Although this relationship is evidenced in the Chinese context, these mechanisms offer broader implications for other emerging economies characterised by developing sustainability disclosure regimes, uneven external monitoring, and active state involvement in corporate environmental practices. Nonetheless, several limitations remain. First, the analysis is restricted to listed firms due to data availability, and the absence of nonlisted firms may limit the generalisability of the findings. Second, innovation resilience is measured using patent-based indicators, which do not differentiate between variations in patent quality or economic value. Moreover, although the findings reveal significant relationships, their boundary conditions require further investigation, particularly with respect to industry heterogeneity, ownership differences, and variations in regulatory intensity. Future research could address these limitations by incorporating broader firm samples and more refined innovation metrics. It may also explore how heterogeneous regulatory environments shape the structural consequences of greenwashing in different institutional settings.
