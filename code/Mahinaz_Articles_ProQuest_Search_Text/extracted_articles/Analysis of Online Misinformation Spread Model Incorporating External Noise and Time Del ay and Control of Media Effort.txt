Introduction
The popularity of digital media makes the propagation of online misinformation easier and more widespread. The spread of misinformation depends on how social network users respond to a particular event or piece of news and the role of the responsible media, websites and government input in combating that particular misinformation. Some researchers observed that many people get more attentive to psychologically appealing online fake news than the real one [1, 2–3]. Online misinformation covers various topics from cultural to personal life of public figures, political satire, any controversial topic which are confusing and may be distressful for someone or some group of people. For example, online misinformation regarding Ebola epidemic confused the health workers in US [4]. The Concept of information epidemic is analogous to the term epidemic in disease transmission. It describes the situation where user spreads an information without verifying its authentication through his/her social network so widely that the propagation turns into viral. Misinformation includes all kinds of fake news, hoaxes, rumors. According to some researchers, in case of US presidential election 2016, misinformation influenced the mass perception [5]. All above mentioned information motivate to study the dynamics and control of spread of misinformation through social media. To understand the characteristics of information epidemic thoroughly and to control or inhibit the spread of it a lot of research articles have been published on the dynamics of rumor propagation through online social networking sites (SNS) [6, 7–8]. Considering the differences between the nature of disease transmission and information epidemic, Daley and Kendall (DK) [9] formulated the classical model on rumor spread in 1964 considering three types of population ignorant, spreader and stiflers. Thomson and Maki (MT) [10] modified the Daley-Kendall model and formulated MT model by considering the hypothesis that the spreaders transform to stiflers by contacting another spreader or stifler. Control of rumor spread by awareness movement through conventional media is studied by Huo [11]. Affasinou [12] discussed the influence of education on rumor spread and created another Spreader-Educated Ignorant-Non educated Ignorant-Recovered (SEIR) model. [13] used multiplex network model to study information diffusion. [14, 15] investigated delayed rumor propagation model. [16] investigated the effect of forgetting mechanism, which signifies the transformation of spreaders to stifler class by losing their interest of the particular event. In [17] discussed bipolar social reinforcement including positive and negative social impacts in rumor spreading process. The effect of topological structure of the rumor spread model with heterogeneous complex network has been studied in [18]. In [19] introduced hesitation mechanism in rumor propagation which means after receiving the rumor, some of the ignorant individuals hesitate to spread and join hesitating group instead of spreader group. In [20] classified the crowd in the social network according to their behavior towards the rumor. Some of researchers discussed the stochastic effect on rumor propagation [21, 22, 23, 24–25]. Effect of noise is also discussed in case of tumor dynamics [26, 27].
In reality, not all the spreaders in the classical rumor-spreading model [9] behave in the same way. Some create misinformation by distorting sensitive facts and deliberately spreading it among large groups of users to influence mass perception [5]. The others spread it out of panic or confusion. The former is called an active spreader, and the latter is called a believer. The “active spreaders” group includes bots on social networking sites [28], malicious websites, social media influencers, and celebrities. Naturally, the active spreaders can spread misinformation at a significantly faster rate than the rest. Segregation of spreaders based on their attitude is a relatively new approach. To the best of our knowledge, this approach is explored sparingly. We, therefore, propose a realistic delayed model incorporating two types of spreaders and dividing the total users into four sub-categories, ignorants, believers, active spreaders, and stiflers. Here we consider delay because online information often lacks credibility, as people may not trust it instantaneously but accept it when it becomes popular in their social circle or after finding some supportive information [29]. To explain this mathematically, we have assumed that the transformation from ignorant class to believer or spreader class experiences a time delay τ . Then we derive the condition for the persistence of active spreaders and believers (spreading threshold) and study the local stability. In general, the spread of online misinformation gets influenced by various random external factors, like the level of media association, the social significance of the event, the sudden advancement of any epidemic situation, etc. So, the existence of noise in the spread of misinformation is a stark reality. To explain these situations more accurately, we induce white noise in the growth equation of ignorant, active spreader and believer population and analyze the asymptotic behavior of the perturbed system. Like the spread of contagious diseases, an optimization technique is needed to inhibit the spread of misinformation. In this case, responsible media, including government efforts, play a vital role in making the mass population aware of misinformation by publishing accurate data or broadcasting logical explanations. The role of awareness programs through mass media to control or curb rumor, fake news, disinformation, and misinformation is discussed in some recent works [11, 30, 31]. Although some of the preceding articles investigated the dynamics of the delayed rumor spread model [15], the effect of delay was not previously considered in the case of the optimization technique. Here we have investigated optimization techniques in a delayed scenario using Pontryagin’s Maximum Principle [32]. Moreover, we compare the results of the delayed optimal control model with the corresponding non-delayed system and observe the possible outcomes.
The organization of article is as follows: The mathematical formulation of the model and the positive invariant set of the system have been discussed in “Mathematical Formulation of the Model”. In “Analysis for the Non-delayed System”, stability analysis of equilibria has been done by calculating the spreading threshold. Also the effect of external noise is investigated in this section. Conditions of Hopf bifurcation with respect to time delay and streaming rate of misinformation have been investigated and proved in “Hopf Bifurcation Analysis”. In “Optimal Control of Media Effort”, a delayed optimal control problem is formulated and solved. In “Numerical Results” some significant numerical plots are presented and discussed to enhance our modeling and analyses. The paper ends with some concluding remarks in “Concluding Remarks”.
Mathematical Formulation of the Model
Nowadays, spread of misinformation is not only a propagation process of information or gossip but it also take the shape of fake news with harmful effects that can even endanger the security and stability of society [33, 34]. According to [9], the total number of social media users are divided into three categories ignorant, spreaders and stiflers. Moreover, in practical scenario, some users have great influence on others and consequently when they spread a piece of misinformation, that reaches a large number of people [33]. We include this kind of users in active spreader group. When an individual gets informed about any misinformation, they can respond to it in three ways: (i) may believe the misinformation out of their insecurities, (ii) not only believe but promote that misinformation to a large number of people out of some vested interest, or (iii) may simply ignore these online materials. Based on these behaviors of users towards a misinformation, the total population N ( t ) is classified into four disjoint groups as follows: x ( t ), the ignorant population who are still uninformed of the misinformation, y ( t ), the believer population who have encountered misinformation and believed it, y A ( t ) denotes active spreader class who create and deliberately spread the misinformation for different reasons and can influence a large number of people to spread it further, z ( t ) is the stifler class who do not spread the misinformation losing their interest. Here are some assumptions listed below: - There is a constant immigration flow Λ in the ignorant crowd per unit time. The persons who are new to the social media, are still uninformed about the misinformation. So, newcomers enter to the ignorant class.

- Each group except active-spreaders has same deactivation rate δ . As active spreaders are present social media and others only to create and promote misinformation purposefully, they do not deactivate their accounts but sometimes behave like neutrals after losing their interest and join stifler class with rate ϵ 2 .

- Active spreaders transmit misinformation in ignorant crowd in a rate α . The influence of believers to spread it further compared to active spreaders is negligible. So, we have considered that ignorant users know about misinformation only from active spreaders.

- As we have explained earlier that theonline information often lacks credibility [29], after having informed about the misinformation, ignorant users need some time τ to get convinced with active spreaders and the propagation process goes through a time delay. After accepting the misinformation as credible one, ignorant join believer class with proportion θ ∈ ( 0 , 1 ) and active spreader class with ( 1 - θ ) .

- People with good educational background and logical mindset, ignore misinformation and join in stifler group directly from ignorant group in ϵ 1 rate.

- With time some of believers converts into active-spreaders/stiflers with rate η , say progression rate. With time some people get more interested with the increasing popularity of misinformation and they join the active spreader class with proportion ϕ ∈ ( 0 , 1 ) and others forget the particular misinformation and join the stifler class with proportion ( 1 - ϕ ) .

Effect of Environmental Fluctuation
A number of factors affect the behavior of social media users during the rapid spread of misinformation. In general, various random external factors, like the level of media association, the social significance of the event, the sudden advancement of any epidemic situation, etc influence the propagation of misinformation. The arrival of new public events, that are connected to that misinformation, may speed up the spread of misinformation among large number users, while unrelated incidents will divert public’s attention and prevent spreading process [25]. Also, the users are often influenced by ‘cognitive bias’, which is the brain’s mechanism for simplifying and rationalizing the information. Studies have found that cognitive bias is often responsible for excessive behavior on social media [36]. According to [37], there exist uncertainty in the users’ decision making in social networks. While such uncertain factors are evident, it is difficult to quantify them in a deterministic model. The use of stochasticity is important to address the above mentioned factors in user’s behavior. Therefore, considering random disturbances by including white noise the growth of the ignorant, believer and active spreader class, we revise the system (1) with τ = 0 by inducing white noise in the growth terms of the population as follows. Optimal Control of Media Effort
When a harmful piece of misinformation goes viral (reaches a large number of users) on social media and elsewhere, various responsible media, including government input, start to act to refute the misinformation. Multiple websites play a significant role in debunking misinformation [38]. Responsible media and websites help to raise awareness against misinformation by stimulating common sense and developing scientific literacy so that people can distinguish between fake and genuine information. Publication of authentic information and promotion of legal action against creating and sharing malicious information by trustworthy and popular news agencies increase awareness against misinformation and discourage users from sharing it. The role of media in optimization techniques has previously been discussed in [30, 31, 39, 40]. Furthermore, media is the most efficient means of reaching all users. In this section, we sketch a delayed optimal control model where we can apply an optimal control policy over a finite time period. The ignorant population is encouraged by responsible media to check the authenticity of any online misinformation, to distinguish fake information from factual information, and to join the stifler class by ignoring misinformation at a rate of e 1 . Furthermore, trustworthy media announcements of actual news encourage believers to join the stifler class at a rate of e 2 . It is evident that e 1 is greater than e 2 . Here we assume that awareness campaigns are ineffective for people who deliberately spread misinformation out of some vested interest. Concluding Remarks
In this article, we have extended the conventional SEIR model to study the spreading dynamics of online misinformation. Here we have also proposed a stochastic model to analyze the effect of external fluctuation using white noise, which is very common in information propagation through a social network. Next, we discussed the impact of time delay, which represents the amount of time it takes for an ignorant user to convert into a believer or active spreader. Finally, we investigated an optimization technique for controlling an information epidemic using responsible media, including government input. Before introducing the delay, we calculate the spreading threshold, R 0 , which determines the conditions of online misinformation persistence and when it eventually disappears. It results in the stability switch from SFE to SEE as it crosses 1. When there is no delay, we have found conditions for the local stability of the spreader-existing-equilibrium. Subsequently, we have induced external noise into the system and proved the existence of a unique global solution of the stochastically perturbed system. We see that the SFE and SEE are no longer equilibria. Moreover, when R 0 is low enough, and noise intensities are not very high, the solution trajectories of the perturbed system oscillate very closely around the spreader-free-equilibrium but not stable in nature. Similarly, in the case of endemic equilibrium, i.e. R 0 > 1 , when noise intensities are limited, solution trajectories of stochastic system oscillate around SEE. If we set the noise intensity values too high, the believer and active spreader class go to extinction even with R 0 > 1 . This implies that white noise leads misinformation to go to extinction. We have further noticed that the system’s stability is lost beyond both the streaming rate and delay threshold values. When the time required to persuade an audience of misinformation exceeds τ 0 , the number of believers and active spreaders fluctuates, causing social unrest and confusion. Again, the higher value of the streaming rate of online misinformation, α , destabilizes the system.
An optimal control model using trustworthy media is set up and investigated in a delayed scenario to control or inhibit the misinformation propagation process. In the case of an information epidemic, the application of control of media efforts incorporating delay is a comparatively new and practical approach to curbing any viral online misinformation from social networks. Being trustworthy, responsible media, including government input, can easily reach all social network users and provide authoritative information to respond to public anxieties faster and reduce panic in an emergency. Although the media cannot influence active spreaders because they deliberately spread distorted news for personal or group gain, the control effectively reduces the believer population, as illustrated in Fig. 11b. Also, the average cost associated with the control is less than the cost of the system before the application of control (see Figs. 12,  15). When α increases, the duration of applying control needs to be extended. Although the associated expenditure increases a bit, see Fig. 15b; control reduces the believer population significantly, see Fig. 16. But the optimization technique ignoring the time-delay overestimates the cost (see Fig. 17b). Here, we have discussed the advantages of the optimization technique in a delayed scenario and found that it offers better results with lesser cost in practical cases (see Fig. 17).
It is clear from above discussion, awareness program using responsible media and government input is efficient for eradicating or inhibiting the spread of online misinformation from social networking sites and others. Incidentally, some responsible websites use this strategy to eradicate rumor, misinformation, disinformation from social networking sites and others [43]. Lastly, if everyone appears to work together to carry out misinformation debunking activities that help the public understand the truth, the spread of misinformation can be quickly stopped.
