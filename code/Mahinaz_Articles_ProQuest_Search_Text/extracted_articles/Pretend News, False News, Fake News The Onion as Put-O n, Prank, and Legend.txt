Headnote The Onion parodies the rhetorical strategies of local, national, and tabloid newspapers. As its mediation shifted from newsprint to website and ultimately to social media, the cues suggesting its interpretation as inherently parodic grew potentially diffuse, especially as the act of sharing comprises both accidental and creative decontextualization. This paper contributes to a discussion of "fake news" by tracing the consequences of shifting digital contexts, the ambiguity of original intent, and the rhetoric of parody as put-on.

in i could talk old-story good, Daniel J. Crowley discussed form and style in Bahamian tale-telling and described the multifunctional call-and-response formula "Bunday," which a teller uses to announce the intention to engage in tale, to check in with the audience over the course of talking, to indicate a caesura, and to close with flourish (1966:20-2). His ethnographic work helped to decenter narrative-and with it the study of folklore-from focusing on the performer and the text to the performer and audience coming together. Genres do not announce themselves; rather, they are announced and framed using the cues appropriate to that genre in that community, and that announcement is heard and interpreted (or, I'll stress now, not heard and/ or misinterpreted) by the listener.
Further evidence of the framing of genre comes negatively through the shaggy dog story. For instance, Eric Partridge points out that such a story is "a non-sequitur, not of faulty logic, but of attitude and response" (1953:83), and Jan Harold Brunvand suggests a sub-category of "hoax stories," defined as "jokes [that] defraud the listener into believing that he is hearing a true narrative and then suddenly turn out to be all nonsense" (1963:43). The defrauding of the listener or the non sequitur of attitude stems from deliberately withholding the genre-appropriate cues that orient one's anticipated movement through the text.
We can also turn to the practical joke, which Moira Marsh defines comprehensively as "a scripted, unilateral play performance involving two opposed parties-trickster and target-with the goal of incorporating the target into play without his or her knowledge, permission, or both" (2015:12). That the performance was even play (indeed that it was even performance) might never be revealed to or occur to the target, as in the case of the "Put-on":
What distinguishes this category of fabrication is that nothing is required of the target beyond a word or phrase that shows he or she believes the fiction. . . . The effectiveness of this type of fabrication does not require revelation, although in some contexts, this differential state of knowledge may be revealed quickly, before the targets make any adjustments to their external world, let alone other people's. (Marsh 2015:21-2)
Perhaps the best way of entering into the discussion of fake news and the Onion is to locate it in the emic genres of "bullshit" or "lies" (Tangherlini 2000). The expectations are for a fiction employing the motifs and characters of the quotidian and immediate world whose actions are motivated by a set of values that is recognizably operative in the world but not our own (or at least we would like to think so), all the while employing the form of the newspaper article, for centuries our default public genre for describing objective reality both through its explicit adverting to sources and data and through the aridity of its disinterested prose style.
For example, one frequent theme is inverting what constitutes something being story-worthy (Stahl 1989) and moving it from private to public discourse. The activities of an "area man" (the Onions term of art for the most generic protagonist) or someone with a similarly anodyne title are raised to the status of story: samples from one recent week include "Adrenaline Supply Intended for Lifting Car Off Loved One Called Upon to Carry 4 Grocery Bags at Once" (Onion 2017a)1 and "Woman Digs Excitedly into Ingrown Hair around Bikini Line Like Grave Robber Pillaging Spoils of the Dead" (2017d): their motivations are invariably human but fall more in the pitiable camp than they do the contemptible. As editor-in-chief Will Tracy expressed on an NPR Morning Edition broadcast in 2013, before "fake news" had its current connotation:
"I think that's one of the things that separates us from maybe other fake news outlets is most of what we do, actually, is focusing on the everyday minutiae, more so than what's happening in Washington," [Tracy] says.
Stories like the one with the headline "Area Man Knows All The Shortcut Keys" is funny "because you know somebody like that," Tracy says, "and it's put in that sort of news voice which elevates it to a certain level of importance that it doesn't actually merit." (quoted in NPR 2013)
Similarly, the found landscape of celebrity, public figures, and corporate entities provide fictive extrapolations of how they are perceived or, just as often, how they are perceived to be perceived in the public consciousness (see Onion 2005). They "work" insofar as (1) their performance of objective reportage demonstrates a fluency with the form; (2) the dissonance between the presented text and our understanding of the world is resolved through the recognition of an appropriate incongruity; and (3) a reflective process akin to the evaluation of legend, that is, how the story, which we know to be untrue, is not so much plausible as it is plausibly plausible, that we are not fooled by it but we can imagine those who would be.
The Onion began at the University of Wisconsin-Madison, the brainchild of students Christopher Johnson and Tim Keck, as a dressed-up advertising flyer. As Josh Modell, editor of its spin-off publication, the AV Club, puts it: "It was two guys who started out in 1988 to sell pizza coupons" (quoted in Sinkovich and Brindisi 2016:75). The following year, they sold it to their editor-in chief and their director of ad sales, who expanded circulation among campuses throughout the Midwest. By the mid1990s, it was generating annual profits of over a million dollars.2
Let us pause here and consider the printed satirical newspaper not as a genre, but as a context. The Onion was a tabloid newspaper with issues rarely exceeding 36 pages. It was a tangible thing that required encounter in the real world and was actively sought out as a source of satire. If a story were to catch a reader unaware that he was entering a playframe, it required looking past the publication's absurd title and focusing solely on the headline and the text. But that story was connected-in all senses, including the most literal, by being co-located on a contiguous tangible object-to other stories with a similar satirical intent.3
This brings us back to Marsh's "put-ons," which she describes with a concrete example. A stuffed jackalope in her office occasioned a successful put-on, when someone accepted her fanciful explanation of its origins. Immediately following, she had the option to "reveal outright that the jackalope was a leg-pull [which] could be construed as rude, even face-threatening," or she could "build on the initial lie with a bigger one . . . that creates, one hopes, a readily transparent fiction." By choosing the bigger lie, the fantastical nature of the jackalope is indirectly communicated "without the listeners' having to acknowledge that they'd fallen for the deception" (Marsh 2015:22). Marsh's strategy of playframe revelation is one of accumulation; the Onions is one of juxtaposition, but the effect is much the same. The non sequitur of attitude and response is reconciled by apprehending the playframe, that the formerly incongruous is in fact recognizably appropriate.
In 1996, the Onion launched its digital presence at www.theonion.com, stemming in part from the virality of a December 1995 story, "Clinton Deploys Vowels to Bosnia" (Onion 1995), which someone had transcribed and which was widely circulated by email. In the process, it lost its association with the Onion until it was read on NPR's Car Talk without attribution (Sinkovich and Brindisi 2016:76). As with most print publications, there had been hesitancy to move to a digital platform, but "going online . . . would mean that when a story got filched and began to crop up across the country in newspapers, Web sites, and TV programs, the Onion could prove that the copycats had stolen someone else's punch lines. Credit could go where credit was due" (Schillinger 1999). The issue of provenance was thus about affirming intellectual property rights and not about genre-framing.
The Onion soon found itself as one of the most-read humor sites on the internet. Links to individual stories were shared, almost exclusively by email. This was still, however, Web 1.0, where web navigation was directory- and not algorithm-based, and one needed to visit the site to read the content. The page for each story is surrounded by links to other stories, related headlines, and so forth, intimating at least the co-location of other spurious stories.
In one early example of the Onion misinterpreted as real news, Fred Phelps of the Westboro Baptist Church, the group best known for its "God Hates F·gs" campaign and its protests at military funerals, circulated a link to "'98 Homosexual-Recruitment Drive Nearing Goal" (Onion 1998b) as proof of a homosexual agenda. When his actions were brought to their attention, editors adjusted the link so it would land at the site's home page, and the story would be seen within the context of other stories and interpreted as the satire it was meant to be (Schillinger 1999).
"Meant to be" is the critical point, though, is it not? Satire operates much like fantasy, but rather than linking "realistic and supernatural events in an unstable way that forces the reader to 'hesitate' between two worldviews" (Ellis 2001:60 [quoting Todorov]), it links the realistic to a parallel world more expressly motivated by "stereotypes, fossilized beliefs, superstitious errors, crank theories, pedantic dogmatisms, oppressive fashions, and all the other things that impede the free movement of society" (Frye [1944] 2006:45). Often, sufficient texture and context is provided to key the audience into entering the performance knowing that no claims are made for the ontological reality of the events unfolding in the text. It may, of course, not be found to be humorous (if such was the intent) or insightful, or it may be interpreted to come from a position that itself takes as preferable "the things that impede the free movement of society," but rarely will it be taken for "truth."4
But the Onion is also satirizing genres of contemporary narrative-the newspaper story, along with editorials, infographics, vox populi, letters to the editor, and so forth- by employing the conventions of those genres. It is parody in both the technical and the more common meanings of the word: first, as in the words of Peter Narváez, that "it is built upon a pre-existing aesthetic structure and that in this building process the content or meaning of the initial structure is substantively but not substantially altered" (1977:33); and, second, as a means of making light of that pre-existing aesthetic structure. As Tim Keck, one of the Onions co-founders, expressed it at the 2013 Seattle Interactive Conference: "If you don't know the structure, you don't get the joke" (quoted in Soper 2013).
Not all parody is necessarily humorous, but the Onions parody tends to be, even when they have had to tread a line between satire and grief in such moments as their first issue following the attacks on the World Trade Center and the Pentagon, or in their habit of rerunning the exact same story under the headline "'No Way to Prevent This,' Says Only Nation Where This Regularly Happens" with only the slightest of details changed following mass shootings.5
Stories have been shared from the Onion since it was a print-only concern, and the move to digital, early in Web 1.0, made it even easier. The act of "mistaking for true," however, was largely only communicated to the Onion itself: a 1999 article in WIRED recounted regular calls and emails to their editorial offices asking for followup information. For example, shortly after the McCaughey septuplets were born in 1998, a story headlined "Chinese Woman Gives Birth to Septuplets: Has One Week to Choose" reported that in accordance with China's one-child policy, the surplus "would be flung from a mountaintop" (1998a). This resulted in numerous emails "informing the staff that heartsick churchwomen were holding prayer vigils" (Schillinger 1999).
"Poe's law" is an internet adage, first coined by Nathan Poe while commenting on www.christianforums.com, which says that "without a winking smiley or other blatant display of humor, it is uttrerly [sic] impossible to parody a Creationist in such a way that someone won't mistake [it] for the genuine article" (Poe 2005). It has been extended to cover any area of belief, most significantly for our purposes, politics. In the contemporary Onion story, arrived at by shared link and not from intentional site visit, what signifies the winky-face? The URL? Only for those familiar. The logo or the name? Perhaps, but that might prove insufficient. And if Poe's law holds, most may have discovered the readily transparent fiction, but not all.
Returning to the story of the Chinese septuplets, belief was occasioned first by the put-on of not apprehending the playframe of parody and subsequently because, however in extremis the story might be, it was somehow reconcilable with a worldview that maintains an understanding of China's one-child policy that can be described at best as "shaky" and more accurately as "virulently racist."
With social media and the advent of Web 2.0, however, not only were stories easier to share, but "sharing" moved from directing someone toward a site to repositioning it on one's own. Simon Bronner compared the personal Facebook page to the "bricoleur's overlay technique in scrapbooking and album keeping" (2009:56). This self-expressive ensemble comprises created and discovered content: on one hand, statuses, personal photos, videos, and other personal artifacts, and on the other hand, content shared either from elsewhere on the social platform or from the larger web. Pages become, as Danille Elise Christensen says about the scrapbook, creations that, when "approached ethnographically, [can be] experienced as exhibits that makers use to position themselves actively within symbolic discourses and regimes of value, as well as in relation to live audiences, both familiar and unknown" (2016:44).
On the Facebook page or on the Twitter feed, the shared story (or at least a preview thereof) not only sits alongside other contributions to the performance of self, but also it no longer sits alongside the other stories from its originating context. It carries with it some of the markers of its source, but those markers must both be noticed by and mean something to the reader. However, as Lynne S. McNeill discusses in this issue of JAF (McNeill 2018), in the social media universe what does have meaning is the relationship between the reader and the person doing the sharing. Using Robert Georges' (1969) insights into the social identity-pairings brought to the moment of performance that condition the new identity-pairing of storyteller and story listener, the performance of posting is conditioned by what the reader thinks of the sharer.
So when, in February 2012, Congressman John Fleming of Louisiana's 4th District linked to an Onion story declaring "Planned Parenthood Opens $8 Billion Abortionplex" (2011) and prefaced it with a statement that made explicit that he thought it to be true inasmuch as he was citing it as evidence, to wit, "More on Planned Parenthood, abortion by the wholesale" (quoted in Horowitz 2012), he did so bearing the imprimatur of a then-two-term member of the House of Representatives, as member of both the Armed Services and Natural Resources Committees, as sponsor of the Amtrak Secure Transportation of Firearms Act, as a member of the Naval Armed Services, as a medical doctor practicing both family medicine and drug and alcohol treatment, as a deacon and director of Sunday school at his church, and as owner of 36 Subway sandwich shops across Louisiana. Credibility is intertwined with our social commitments and our performed self.
Position statements like Fleming's accompanying comment are not always brief and tacit. As recorded by the Literally Unbelievable blog, people will go to great lengths to articulate precisely why an article confirms their worldview. In one case, an anonymized sharer commented on "Woman a Leading Authority about What Shouldn't Be in Poor People's Grocery Carts" (Onion 2014b) with a rundown of further ways the poor ought to adjust their diet (Hongo 2016b). In another, a commenter chastised President Obama for not thanking veterans (Hongo 2016a) in response to "Obama Praises Own Strength, Resilience in Face of Hardship in State of the Union" (Onion 2016). And finally, while sharing the news of "42 Million Dead in Bloodiest Black Friday Weekend on Record" (Onion 2012), comments ranged from a reaction against capitalism to fears that the Marxists would use this as an excuse to curtail it (Hongo 2015).
The issue of the Onions own intent is ambiguous: Are they engaged in put-ons and the deliberate desire to be believed, or are they "mere" satire that aims at lampooning a genre? According to a member of the editorial staff, interviewed by Slate:
"You'd be amazed," says Onion managing editor Robert Siegel, a boyish but exhaustedlooking 27-year-old. "People see things in print, especially when there's no byline, and they don't say, 'Gee, this article sounds pretty crazy.' They regard the printed word as having dropped from the heavens."
This, of course, is just what Siegel wants. "We love to be misunderstood," he smiles. (Schillinger 1999)
But in 2017, in a talk at Marquette University on media ethics, the Onions original editor-in-chief, Scott Dikkers, made a different distinction. "When asked if 'The Onion' is fake news, Dikkers replied, 'It's satire. It's totally different from fake news, and it bothers me when those fake news organizations are basically out there printing lies or propaganda [but] label themselves satire.'" He continued, "If 'The Onion' ever wrote a story that fooled people, that was an accident, and that was because they didn't know better" (quoted in Hutchison 2017). The current managing editor, Marnie Shure, takes a different tack. She distinguishes three branches of fake news: a label given by the powerful (and notably the current president) to unfavorable news coverage; the deliberate printing of "false stories in an attempt to mislead audiences"; and satire, which provides "an added layer of perspective on things when the news cycle can barely keep up with itself. I think satire is a very useful and necessary way to distil that news cycle" (quoted in Purcell 2017).
It would be nice to imagine that we are all clever enough to spot the false story and that we can stand in smug judgment of those who cannot. Were that invariably the case, the common internet refrain "Not the Onion" would never have needed to emerge. Since 2008, there has been an active subreddit called Not the Onion6 and a portal linking to news stories with Onion-worthy headlines that are not creations of humor sites, www.theon1on.com. As Tom Mould writes in this issue, "it is insulting, inappropriate, and incorrect to suggest people are ignorant or irrational if they believe something that our research suggests is not true" (Mould 2018:416), to which I would humbly add, it is also hubris.
The ambiguity of belief is nothing new to legend scholars, and I will leave most of the theorizing to my more learned colleagues. But with the Onion, we have narratives built upon the pre-existent aesthetic structure of the news story, a form of public expression connoting fact. Its origins announce how it ought to be apprehended, but when unmoored from those origins through its transmission on social media, it has fewer of its intended cues while taking on additional ones, namely, its implicit and sometimes explicit endorsement through the sharing actions of the individuals within the chain of transmission and the social identity pairings comprising that chain. It loses its Bunday, it loses its winky-face, and it gains the authority of trust.
We have an analogous movement from creativity to the purported real, as we have seen in the Slender Man phenomenon, so well-articulated in the contributions to a recent special issue of Contemporary Legend (Blank and McNeill 2015). As Lynne S. McNeill reminds us, the internet is self-archiving (quoted in Brogan 2017), so we can always find the source (which, we remind ourselves, was the original impetus for the Onion going digital in the first place). When the folk process starts with a fixed, created, identifiable, and extant text, we hold in reserve the option to, as it were, "mansplain."
But with fake news, in any of its manifestations, when ought we to explain, as friends, as folklorists, as citizens? How do we negotiate Marsh's warning of how revelation could be construed as rude or face-threatening? When do we violate the folklorist's Prime Directive and choose to intervene rather than see how it plays out? (see Kitta 2012, 2018). From our positions of privilege (by virtue of being in the academy, if from no other), when do we speak truth to power, and when do we use our power to speak to those with less?
