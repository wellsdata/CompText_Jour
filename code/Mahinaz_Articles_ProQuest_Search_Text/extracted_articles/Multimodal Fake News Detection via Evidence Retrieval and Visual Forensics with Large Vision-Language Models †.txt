1. Introduction The rapid proliferation of fake news on online platforms has emerged as a pressing societal challenge, undermining public trust, distorting facts, and influencing public opinion [1]. In response to this growing threat, the research community has explored a variety of mitigation strategies, among which automated fake news detection (FND) has gained significant attention. FND aims to automatically distinguish false or misleading content from truthful information, thereby enabling scalable and timely interventions. Prior studies have demonstrated the effectiveness of FND systems in identifying deceptive news articles across diverse modalities and domains [2,3,4].
In recent years, there has been a substantial increase in research dedicated to the monitoring and mitigation of fake news. Existing detection strategies can generally be divided into two primary paradigms: content-based approaches and propagation-based approaches. Content-based methods focus on identifying deceptive information by examining intrinsic characteristics of the news itself, such as linguistic patterns, sentiment cues, and the stylistic attributes of the author [5]. In contrast, propagation-based methods exploit the contextual and social signals related to how information spreads, including dissemination patterns across social media platforms, user interactions, and engagement trajectories [6]. These approaches often integrate auxiliary metadata—such as the credibility of the source account, the structure of the user’s social network, and the temporal and spatial context of the reported events—to assess the authenticity of the content [7]. We study content model-based detection method for fake news, and we do not go into the fact dissemination-based method.
In the initial stages, researchers predominantly employed machine learning algorithms [8] or deep learning algorithms [9] to assess news veracity through the manual design of semantic and propagation feature combinations. The latest research trends indicate that large language models have reshaped traditional NLP tasks, While recent efforts have begun to explore the use of LLMs for combating misinformation [10,11], these approaches remain constrained by the inherent limitations of LLMs in processing non-textual content. Consequently, the emergence of LVLM [12] presents a promising advancement for extending this line of research into the multimodal domain. Several factors motivate the adoption of LVLMs for multimodal misinformation detection.
First, large-scale vision-language pretraining enables LVLMs to jointly model textual and visual information, making them well-suited to capture named entities, visual semantics, and cross-modal associations in multimodal news content [13].
Second, LVLMs have demonstrated strong reasoning capabilities through their remarkable performance on a variety of tasks, including visual question answering, common-sense reasoning, and symbolic inference [14]. This indicates that even in zero-shot settings, LVLMs possess the potential to generate robust multimodal reasoning [15] by leveraging their integrated understanding of visual and textual inputs. Our findings show that LVLMs can generally deliver satisfactory performance due to their ability to process and reason over complex multimodal content. However, despite these advances, current models still encounter difficulties when external contextual understanding is required for accurate misinformation detection. Traditional methods for augmenting LLMs with external knowledge—such as Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) [16]—typically rely on generating queries directly from factual text. While such mechanisms have shown promise in fact-checking and verification-related tasks, they often fall short in multimodal contexts where subtle cross-modal inconsistencies and external evidence play a critical role. In this setting, the integration of retrieved evidence into multimodal reasoning becomes particularly important. This challenge is also closely related to claim verification and stance-based reasoning, where retrieved evidence may support, contradict, or be unrelated to the target claim [17]. Therefore, how to effectively leverage LVLMs to analyze the latent relationships between news text and accompanying images, while incorporating external evidence to infer news authenticity, remains an urgent and open research problem. To address this problem, we propose the MERF (Multimodal Evidence Retrieval and Forensics) Framework. Unlike traditional methods that typically convert all modalities into textual representations for unified analysis, MERF performs parallel retrieval of both textual and visual information to gather comprehensive evidence, thereby enhancing the reasoning quality of the LVLM, as shown in Figure 1.
Furthermore, our framework integrates multi-perspective textual analysis for initial judgment, enabling the model to capture more details. More importantly, to enhance visual forensics, we design a tampering-aware prompting strategy aimed at capturing potential inconsistencies within the image. This strategy guides the LVLM to identify suspicious regions and infer possible manipulations, along with corresponding confidence scores. This article is a revised and expanded version of a conference paper entitled “Multimodal Fake News Detection via Evidence Retrieval with Vision-Language Model”, accepted at FCS 2025, which was held in Guiyang, China, on 24–26 October 2025; the conference paper is to appear in the proceedings [18]. In summary, the major contributions of this paper are as follows: We propose MERF, a unified framework that leverages LVLMs for multi-angle textual analysis and preliminary authenticity assessment, and dynamically triggers external evidence retrieval when additional verification is needed;
We develop a web-based evidence retrieval module that gathers both textual and visual evidence, and further incorporate a tampering-aware visual forensics strategy to identify potential manipulations in news images;
We conduct extensive experiments on two public multimodal benchmarks (Weibo and Twitter), demonstrating consistent improvements over representative baselines, and provide ablation and cross-model evaluations to validate the effectiveness and generalizability of each component.

2. Related Work 2.1. Multimodal Fake News Detection The rapid growth of multimedia content has brought multimodal misinformation detection to the forefront of research, as such misinformation poses a significant threat to the integrity of information dissemination [19]. CAFE [20] is an ambiguity-aware multimodal fake news detection framework that adaptively aggregates unimodal and cross-modal features based on the estimated level of semantic inconsistency between text and image modalities. QMFND [21] utilizes a quantum convolutional neural network (QCNN) to fuse textual and visual features for fake news detection, aiming to improve classification performance through the expressibility and entangling capabilities of quantum circuits. FNR [22] utilizes a similarity-aware and transformer-based architecture to detect multimodal fake news, leveraging BERT and ViT for feature extraction and incorporating contrastive loss to model the relationship between text and image modalities for more accurate classification. However, these methods are typically built upon deep learning models, which inherently suffer from limited interpretability. Therefore, recently researchers have increasingly focused on leveraging LVLMs to tackle multimodal misinformation. For example, ref. [23] illustrates LVLM’s effectiveness in the task. IMFND explores the effectiveness of LVLMs in multimodal fake news detection and enhances their performance in few-shot settings by integrating predictions and probability cues from smaller models [24]. These studies reflect LVLM as a promising solution to multimodal misinformation detection.
2.2. Knowledge-Augmented Language Models Utilizing external knowledge to augment language models (LMs) has emerged as a promising solution in knowledge-intensive tasks [25]. Ram [26] proposed the RALM method, which enhances model performance by conditioning LLM on external knowledge sources during the generation process. Asai et al. [27] introduces self-reflection on retrieved passages to enhance LM’s quality and factuality. Nevertheless, the potential of LVLMs augmented with forgery-specific knowledge in multimodal fake news detection remains unexplored.
In addition, our work is also related to claim verification and stance-based reasoning. In such settings, the usefulness of external evidence depends not only on whether it can be retrieved, but also on its relation to the target claim, which may be supportive, contradictory, or unrelated. Although MERF is not formulated as an explicit stance classification model, its evidence-grounded reasoning process is closely connected to this line of research. By jointly considering textual evidence, image-context evidence, and visual forensic cues, MERF extends claim-evidence reasoning into a multimodal misinformation detection setting.
2.3. Vision-Based Fake News Detection With the increasing prevalence of multimodal content on social media, research on visual information in news has gained significant attention. Early studies on fake news detection primarily relied on statistical features extracted from images, often overlooking their semantic content. Subsequently, methods such as YOLO [28] and others have been widely adopted for visual feature extraction in deep learning-based approaches. However, conventional feature extraction techniques are typically limited to low-level pixel information and lack the ability to capture semantic alterations. Modern image manipulation techniques can subtly modify an image’s semantic meaning, making tampering difficult to detect through pixel-level analysis alone. To address this limitation, Jin et al. [29] proposed the M-DRUM framework, which utilizes ImageBind as a feature encoder to extract both visual and textual features from news images and headlines, aiming to identify potential signs of manipulation. An LVLM is then employed to perform a comprehensive evaluation. Liu et al. [30] proposed FKA-Owl, a knowledge-augmented framework that integrates forgery-specific cues—semantic inconsistency and visual artifact traces—into an LVLM to enhance their manipulation reasoning capabilities in multimodal fake news detection.
3. Proposed Method In this section, we present a framework named MERF (Multimodal Evidence Retrieval and Forensics), as illustrated in Figure 2. We begin by describing the initial stage inference process within the framework. Next, we elaborate on how to efficiently and effectively conduct forgery analysis. We then introduce our method for retrieving multimodal evidence from the internet for both textual and visual inputs. Finally, we demonstrate how MERF integrates external knowledge to refine the final prediction. Detailed prompt designs for each module are provided in Appendix A.
3.1. Initial Stage Inference In the initial stage, to efficiently leverage the reasoning capabilities of the LVLM, the proposed framework begins with a prompt-driven inference step. At this point, both the textual content and the associated image of a news instance are directly provided to the LVLM as input. Rather than relying solely on surface-level understanding, the model is guided by a set of multi-perspective prompts designed to elicit judgments from diverse cognitive standpoints. Specifically, we construct prompts to activate the LVLM’s implicit knowledge along two primary dimensions: (1) . Experience and common sense—the model evaluates the plausibility of the described event or narrative based on general world knowledge and prior expectations;
(2) . Linguistic and stylistic cues—the model assesses whether the tone, emotional intensity, or writing style conforms to credible journalistic standards.

These multi-perspective cues enable the LVLM to perform an initial credibility assessment without relying on external knowledge or retrieval-based augmentation. This process can be formalized as (1) P ( y | T , I ) = LVLM θ ( T , I ) ,
where T and I denote the news text and image, respectively, y ∈ { 0 , 1 } is the veracity label (0 for fake, 1 for real), and LVLM θ represents the large vision-language model parameterized by θ .
The output of this stage consists of a preliminary binary veracity prediction together with an associated confidence score. The LVLM then re-evaluates this result to determine whether additional evidence is necessary. If the available information is deemed sufficiently comprehensive, the model adopts the initial prediction as the final decision. Otherwise, the system proceeds to retrieve and analyze external evidence. If the retrieved evidence remains insufficient, inconsistent, or inconclusive, the instance is marked as internally uncertain, and the initial-stage prediction is retained for subsequent benchmark evaluation.
3.2. Manipulation Detection Components Two dedicated modules are developed to identify potential image manipulations and provide robust forensic analysis of visual content: Inconsistency Detection: This module analyzes intra-image coherence from multiple low- and mid-level visual cues, including lighting consistency, geometric perspective alignment, boundary smoothness, and resolution continuity, in order to identify suspicious regions indicative of manipulation such as splicing or replacement. The module outputs structured forensic evidence in JSON format, including the anomaly type, spatial location, and confidence score of each detected cue.
Formally, we define the set of anomalies detected within an image I as (2) A ( I ) = { a 1 , a 2 , … , a m } = f forensic ( I ) ,
where f forensic represents the forensic analysis function that extracts manipulation cues, such as lighting inconsistency or boundary irregularities.
To summarize the overall visual consistency of the image, we conceptually describe a visual consistency score as (3) S vis ( I ) = 1 − 1 m ∑ j = 1 m ∥ g ( a j ) − g ^ ( a j ) ∥ ,

where g ( a j ) denotes the observed characterization of the j -th anomaly cue, while g ^ ( a j ) denotes its reference characterization under visually consistent conditions. A lower score indicates lower visual consistency and a higher likelihood of manipulation. In the actual MERF pipeline, however, these anomaly cues are mainly retained as structured forensic evidence for downstream LVLM reasoning, rather than being used as an independently optimized scalar predictor.
Scene Graph Analysis: This module generates a semantic scene graph that encodes object-level information (e.g., categories, attributes, positions) and their inter-object relationships. In addition to representing the image’s semantic structure, it incorporates visual quality metrics, such as blur detection, color tone inconsistencies, and unnatural object placements. By comparing these properties against expected visual patterns and real-world knowledge, the module identifies discrepancies suggestive of semantic manipulation (e.g., object insertion or removal). This holistic structural representation enables fine-grained reasoning about the plausibility and integrity of the depicted scene.

Together, these components are particularly well-suited for high-stakes forensic scenarios, such as verifying the authenticity of politically sensitive imagery, detecting tampered content in crisis event coverage, or supporting multimodal fact-checking pipelines. The proposed consistency score S vis ( I ) also provides a quantitative measure to evaluate the likelihood of tampering, which can be seamlessly integrated into downstream reasoning processes.
3.3. Multimodal Retrieval 3.3.1. Content Retrieval To enrich the decision-making process with external information, we perform content retrieval by querying the web using the entire input news text, preserving its contextual richness and fine-grained details. Inspired by Li et al. [31], the Internet is treated as an open-domain external knowledge base. Unlike keyword-based search that may omit critical nuances, our approach ensures maximal coverage of semantically relevant information.
Formally, the textual retrieval process can be expressed as (4) R ( N ) = { d i ∣ Sim ( d i , N ) ≥ τ } ,
where N is the input news instance, d i represents candidate documents, and Sim ( d i , N ) denotes semantic relevance between the retrieved document and the input news. In practice, this relevance is assessed through an LLM-assisted filtering step rather than a fixed manually specified numeric threshold.
In practice, we employ a customized web crawler that interfaces with the Google Search API to automatically retrieve the top-10 webpages corresponding to the input news instance. The query is directly formed from the original news text, avoiding lossy preprocessing that could negatively affect recall. The retrieved URLs are denoted by R ( N ) = { URL 1 , URL 2 , … , URL n } , representing the candidate-related content. To ensure the quality and relevance of the collected documents, we apply a post-retrieval filtering step, in which each document is evaluated according to its semantic relevance to the original input. Low-relevance pages are filtered out through an LLM-assisted relevance assessment step, rather than a fixed manually specified numeric threshold, so as to reduce retrieval noise. Each retained document is further processed using a lightweight LLM-based pipeline to extract and structure key information, including the title, publication time, and main content body. This structured representation facilitates downstream evidence selection and consistency analysis. Only the most relevant retained evidence is serialized into the final prompt, so as to preserve informative context while controlling prompt length and reducing retrieval noise. An example of the retrieved and structured evidence is shown in Figure 3.
3.3.2. Image Context Retrieval Traditional retrieval methods often convert multimodal content into textual representations to support downstream analysis, focusing primarily on language-based contextual understanding. However, as illustrated in Figure 4, such approaches may overlook critical visual inconsistencies associated with misinformation. For instance, an image purportedly depicting a Canadian wildfire may, in reality, originate from a past wildfire in California. This type of image reuse or misattribution can significantly undermine credibility if the image’s true origin is not verified. To address this issue, we incorporate an image context retrieval module that enhances misinformation detection by uncovering the historical background of the input image I . We employ Google Reverse Image Search to retrieve up to 10 URLs containing identical or visually similar images.For each returned result, we determine the content type and apply appropriate processing: video content is briefly summarized, while text-based webpages are structured to extract key information such as title, time, and main content. The processed results are aggregated into an image evidence set E v , which captures diverse prior usages of the image. This enables more accurate assessment of visual–textual consistency and strengthens the system’s ability to detect image-related misinformation.
The retained image-context evidence is then normalized into a structured representation and incorporated into the final prompt-based reasoning stage together with textual retrieval evidence and forensic cues.
3.4. Refined Prediction In the final stage, the MERF framework integrates all intermediate results and delivers them to the LVLM through a unified, instruction-guided prompt designed to elicit holistic multimodal reasoning. The input to this stage includes the original news text and accompanying image, as well as outputs from the visual manipulation detection modules (e.g., inconsistency indicators and scene-graph evidence), structured textual and visual evidence retrieved from the web, and named entities or key concepts extracted during earlier processing.These diverse sources of information are composed into a single prompt that guides the LVLM to assess semantic consistency, detect cross-modal discrepancies, and incorporate external factual knowledge. The prompt formulation is designed to encourage contextual reasoning rather than shallow classification, allowing the model to weigh conflicting evidence, reconcile contradictions, and derive a comprehensive understanding of the news instance.
Based on this integrated prompt, the LVLM generates a final veracity judgment together with a detailed and human-interpretable rationale that outlines the main supporting evidence, conflicting evidence, and overall reasoning basis behind the decision. This structured explanation improves the transparency, interpretability, and credibility of the model output, which is particularly valuable in high-stakes scenarios. To conservatively handle ambiguous cases, the prompt design also allows the model to express uncertainty when the available evidence is insufficient, inconsistent, or inconclusive. In such cases, MERF records the instance as internally uncertain and falls back to the initial-stage prediction for benchmark evaluation, thereby maintaining robustness and avoiding overconfident conclusions.
Although MERF allows the model to express uncertainty during inference, all reported results follow the standard binary classification setting (real vs. fake) for consistency with existing benchmarks (Weibo and Twitter).
4. Experiments In this section, a series of experiments will be adopted to evaluate the performance of the MERF framework, and several practical examples will be analysed to demonstrate its performance in practical applications.
4.1. DATA To assess the fake news detection capabilities of LVLMs, we select two publicly available datasets that cover a diverse range of misinformation scenarios and include content in both English and Chinese. Table 1 shows the statistics of the dataset.
Weibo [32] is a Chinese multimodal fake news dataset collected from social media platforms, comprising both textual and visual content. Real news samples are sourced from the official Xinhua News Agency, while fake news is obtained from Weibopiyao—Weibo’s official rumor-debunking platform that aggregates misinformation through user reports and authoritative verifications. Following the preprocessing procedures adopted in prior work [32], we obtain a curated dataset of 7853 Chinese news articles.
Twitter [33] is a multimodal dataset consisting of social media posts collected from the Twitter platform, which include textual content, associated images or videos, and auxiliary social context. For our task, we retain only the text–image pairs as testing samples. It is worth noting that the Twitter test split is relatively imbalanced, containing 1217 fake instances and 2564 real instances, whereas the Weibo dataset is comparatively more balanced; therefore, accuracy alone is not always sufficiently representative for performance evaluation.
4.2. Experiment Settings 4.2.1. Large Vision-Language Models In this study, we utilize Qwen-VL-Max [34], the most advanced model in Alibaba Cloud’s Qwen vision-language model series, accessed via its OpenAI-compatible API. Building upon the Qwen-VL-Plus architecture, Qwen-VL-Max incorporates enhanced visual reasoning and instruction-following capabilities, achieving state-of-the-art performance across a variety of complex multimodal benchmarks. The model supports ultra-high-resolution image inputs (up to approximately 12 million pixels) and employs a Vision Transformer backbone with windowed attention, enabling efficient and adaptive processing of images with varying resolutions while preserving fine-grained visual details. These capabilities make Qwen-VL-Max particularly well-suited for tasks requiring precise image–text alignment, visual grounding via bounding boxes, and cross-modal semantic consistency verification, all of which are integral to the objectives of our research.
4.2.2. Reasoning Method We adopt two fundamental prompting strategies to activate the inherent reasoning capabilities of LVLMs in the context of misinformation detection.
Direct Prompting: In this approach, the LVLM is treated as an independent misinformation detector. The model receives the raw image–text pair as input, without any prior preprocessing or external evidence augmentation. It is then directly prompted to generate a veracity prediction, followed by an explanatory rationale, relying solely on its internal multimodal knowledge and representations.
Chain-of-Thought Prompting [14]: Building upon the Direct strategy, we further incorporate the Chain-of-Thought (CoT) mechanism, which has been shown to significantly enhance the reasoning capabilities of large language models. Specifically, we append the phrase “Let’s think step by step” to the prompt, encouraging the LVLM to explicitly articulate its reasoning process before producing a final decision. This method improves interpretability and facilitates more coherent multi-step inference.
4.2.3. Implementation Pipeline From an implementation perspective, each news instance is processed through the following pipeline: initial multimodal judgment, optional external evidence retrieval, visual forensic analysis, evidence structuring, and final prompt-based reasoning. The final prompt integrates the original news text and image, textual retrieval evidence, image-context evidence, and forensic cues into a unified structured input for the LVLM. All reported results follow the standard binary classification setting (real vs. fake).
4.3. Baselines We compare MERF with representative multimodal fake-news detection baselines, including traditional supervised models and prompt-based LVLM variants, in order to evaluate its practical effectiveness across different methodological paradigms. Since MERF is implemented as a retrieval-augmented LVLM framework rather than a task-specific supervised classifier, comparisons with prior supervised baselines should be interpreted as cross-paradigm evaluations rather than strictly controlled apples-to-apples comparisons.
The baseline results used in this study come from two sources. For MVAE, MFIR, COOLANT, SpotFake, and MCNN, we report the results published in the original papers, since these methods already provide results on both the Weibo and Twitter benchmark datasets. For MoPeD and MIMoE-FND, the original papers only report results on the Weibo dataset. Therefore, we obtain their results through the official implementations under our experimental setting, and further evaluate them on Twitter for comparison. We make this distinction explicit to improve the transparency of the comparison protocol.
MVAE [35]: introduces a multimodal variational autoencoder that learns shared latent representations from text and images. It combines an encoder, decoder, and fake news classifier to capture cross-modal correlations, enabling effective fake news detection based solely on post content, without relying on social context or propagation features.
SpotFake [4]: extracts text features using pre-trained XLNet and visual features using VGG19, which are then combined to provide multimodal features that are input to the classifier.
MoPeD [36]: introduces a modality perception learning framework that adaptively discovers determinative features from both unimodal and multimodal signals. It employs dual encoders (CLIP and modality-specific CNNs), a multilevel cross-modality fusion module, and a heterogeneity-aware aggregation mechanism to highlight critical factors for fake news detection.
MCNN [37]: detects fake news by learning coherent semantics between text features, visual features, and visual tampering features.
COOLANT [38]: proposes a cross-modal contrastive learning framework that aligns image and text representations via a dual-encoder and contrastive loss. It incorporates cross-modal fusion and attention guided by modality ambiguity to adaptively aggregate features, enabling accurate and interpretable fake news detection.
MFIR [39]: discovers multimodal inconsistent semantics through multimodal fusion and inconsistent reasoning for interpretable fake news detection.
MIMoE-FND [40]: proposes a hierarchical mixture-of-experts framework that models text-image interactions via an interaction gating mechanism. It dynamically routes samples to specialized fusion experts based on unimodal agreement and semantic alignment, achieving strong performance on multimodal fake news benchmarks.
4.4. Result 4.4.1. Performance Comparison Table 2 presents the performance of our proposed MERF framework against several representative baselines across the Weibo and Twitter datasets. Standard evaluation metrics—including accuracy, precision, recall, and F1-score—are reported for both rumor and non-rumor classes. It is worth noting that the Twitter test split is imbalanced (1217 fake vs. 2564 real). Therefore, Table 2 reports not only overall accuracy but also class-wise precision, recall, and F1 scores for both rumor and non-rumor categories. These category-level metrics provide a more reliable evaluation under class imbalance and help reveal whether improvements come from balanced performance across both classes rather than from bias toward the majority class alone. On the Weibo dataset, MERF achieves the highest overall accuracy of 0.934, outperforming all competing methods. It also delivers strong precision and recall across both classes, reflecting a balanced and reliable detection capability. Although MIMoR-FND shows slightly higher recall for rumors (0.942 vs. 0.936) and higher precision for non-rumors (0.913 vs. 0.892), its overall accuracy is lower than MERF. This indicates that while MIMoR-FND tends to make more aggressive class-specific predictions, it may lack the global reasoning and verification capabilities needed to handle ambiguous or borderline cases, which are effectively managed by MERF’s evidence-grounded approach. On the Twitter dataset, MERF attains the top accuracy of 0.887, matching the performance of MoPeD but outperforming it in F1-scores for both rumor and non-rumor categories. In particular, MERF achieves the highest F1-score for rumor detection (0.906), a crucial metric given the prevalence of subtle misinformation on social platforms. While methods like COOLANT and MIMoR-FND perform competitively, their performance is less consistent across both classes. MERF’s robustness in this noisy and low-context environment further underscores the effectiveness of its external evidence integration and visual forensic modules. In summary, although certain baselines show strengths in isolated metrics, MERF consistently delivers superior and well-balanced performance across all evaluation criteria, demonstrating its strong generalizability and reliability in real-world multimodal misinformation detection tasks.

4.4.3. Ablation Study In this section, we evaluate the contribution of both individual modules and input modalities within the MERF framework through a series of ablation experiments. Specifically, we include modality-level comparisons to examine the roles of textual input, visual input, and their multimodal combination, and we further design module-level ablation variants to analyze the contributions of external knowledge retrieval and visual forensics in enhancing fake news detection. All experiments are conducted under the same settings as in the main evaluation to ensure fair comparison. The results are summarized in Table 3.
Text-only: This variant uses only the raw news text as input, without incorporating the accompanying image, external evidence, or forensic cues.
Image-only: This variant uses only the accompanying image as input, without incorporating the news text, external evidence, or forensic cues.
TI-only: This variant uses only the raw news text and accompanying image as input, without incorporating any external evidence or forensic cues. It therefore serves as the basic text + image multimodal setting for assessing the inherent multimodal reasoning ability of the LVLM.
W/o MDC: We remove the manipulation detection components, including both inconsistency detection and scene graph analysis, while retaining all other modules.
W/o CR: This setting disables the content retrieval module, thus excluding external textual evidence from the decision-making process.
W/o ICR: This variant removes the image context retrieval component, preventing the model from accessing historical visual usage patterns.
In addition to accuracy, Table 3 also reports Macro-F1 to provide a more balanced evaluation under class imbalance, especially for the Twitter dataset. The modality-level comparison shows that Image-only yields the lowest performance, with accuracy/Macro-F1 dropping to 0.742/0.731 on Weibo and 0.698/0.681 on Twitter, indicating that visual information alone is insufficient for reliable fake news detection. Text-only performs slightly better, reaching 0.763/0.768 on Weibo and 0.713/0.725 on Twitter, while TI-only further improves the results to 0.796/0.804 on Weibo and 0.748/0.728 on Twitter. This suggests that textual and visual modalities provide complementary information, and that multimodal input is more effective than either single modality alone.
Removing the Manipulation Detection Components (W/o MDC) leads to a moderate performance drop, from 0.935/0.934 to 0.917/0.906 on Weibo and from 0.891/0.888 to 0.876/0.881 on Twitter, showing that image-level forensic reasoning contributes to overall robustness. The ablation of the Content Retrieval module (W/o CR) causes a substantial decrease, especially on Twitter, where the accuracy/Macro-F1 drops from 0.891/0.888 to 0.813/0.803. This indicates that many short or ambiguous posts benefit significantly from supplementary external textual evidence. Similarly, removing the Image Context Retrieval module (W/o ICR) also degrades performance, from 0.935/0.934 to 0.894/0.885 on Weibo and from 0.891/0.888 to 0.852/0.842 on Twitter, reflecting the importance of tracing visual provenance and prior usage patterns.
Overall, the results show that both modality-level fusion and module-level design are important for MERF. While combining text and images already improves results compared to single-modality settings, the full framework further benefits from external evidence retrieval and visual forensics, leading to the most robust performance across both datasets.
4.4.4. Inference Cost and Latency Analysis In addition to classification performance, we further analyze the practical inference overhead of MERF. Under our experimental setting, the average end-to-end prediction time for a single news instance is approximately 40 s, and the average token consumption is around 80,000 tokens per instance. This overhead is higher than that of conventional supervised classifiers or direct prompting variants, mainly because MERF involves multiple evidence-intensive stages, including external textual retrieval, image-context retrieval, visual forensic analysis, and long-context LVLM reasoning.
Although this cost makes MERF less suitable for strict real-time deployment, it remains acceptable in evidence-sensitive verification scenarios where interpretability, external grounding, and robustness are prioritized over latency.
4.4.5. Model Backbone Analysis To assess the adaptability of MERF across different LVLMs, we replace the default backbone with several representative models while keeping all other components unchanged. Table 4 shows the accuracy on Weibo (Chinese) and Twitter (English) datasets. Among the models, GPT-4.5 achieves the best overall accuracy (0.933 on Weibo, 0.886 on Twitter), reflecting strong cross-modal and multilingual capabilities. QwenVL-Plus and DeepSeek-VL2 also deliver competitive results, particularly in Chinese and English respectively, demonstrating MERF’s robustness across diverse pretraining schemes. In contrast, Qwen2.5-VL-32B shows noticeably lower performance (0.897 on Weibo, 0.793 on Twitter). Unlike the other models accessed via cloud-based APIs, it is deployed locally with quantization for resource efficiency. This setup may introduce inference constraints and limit the model’s ability to fully leverage its capacity in complex multimodal reasoning. The results highlight that deployment conditions and alignment quality can significantly affect downstream performance. Overall, MERF exhibits strong compatibility with a variety of LVLMs, maintaining high performance even under constrained or offline settings, which supports its practical flexibility across real-world scenarios.
4.5. Case Study To demonstrate the interpretability and effectiveness of MERF, we present a representative case involving a real news report about a liquefied gas truck explosion in Tianjin. The input text describes an incident occurring at 14:10 on 5 January 2016, in the Jinnan Development Zone, with five consecutive explosions and no reported casualties. The news cites CCTV as the source. Due to the potentially ambiguous nature of the event, MERF determines that external evidence is necessary. As shown in Figure 6, in the textual retrieval stage, multiple sources (Evidence 1–7) confirm the reported time, location, and event details. Even though one piece (Evidence 2) is dated later, its content aligns with the original report and likely reflects a retrospective article. These results suggest strong factual consistency. The image associated with the news is verified through manipulation detection and scene graph analysis. Visual coherence is high (0.95), with no signs of tampering or anomalies in lighting, perspective, or resolution. The image is further confirmed to be authentic through scene-level analysis, which identifies a severely burned vehicle consistent with an explosion. In image context retrieval, MERF identifies highly relevant visual matches. Image Evidence 3 corresponds directly to the described event, while Image Evidence 4 refers to a similar incident in the same district with slight timing differences. Other retrieved images are irrelevant or uninformative. Based on the alignment of textual and visual information, the credibility of the sources, and the absence of contradictions or manipulations, MERF concludes that the news is real. This case illustrates MERF’s ability to provide structured, explainable decisions grounded in multimodal evidence.
Beyond the successful case shown above, MERF still encounters difficulties in several challenging scenarios. First, if the retrieved evidence is weakly related, noisy, or partially conflicting, the final reasoning process may still be affected by imperfect external grounding. Second, some misinformation cases involve subtle visual manipulation or highly ambiguous text-image relations, making them difficult to detect even with the help of forensic cues. Third, when both the textual claim and the visual content are partially true but jointly misleading, the framework may still produce incorrect judgments due to the inherent complexity of cross-modal misinformation. These observations suggest that the main strength of MERF lies in evidence-grounded multimodal reasoning and contextual verification, while its remaining limitations are mainly associated with retrieval quality, subtle manipulation patterns, and highly ambiguous multimodal claims.
5. Conclusions In this work, we proposed MERF, a unified framework for multimodal fake news detection that enhances the reasoning capabilities of LVLMs through explicit evidence retrieval and visual forensics. By integrating multi-perspective prompt design, web-based content retrieval, reverse image search, and image manipulation detection, MERF addresses the challenge of factual consistency that LVLMs alone struggle to ensure. Experiments on Weibo and Twitter demonstrate that MERF outperforms existing baselines in accuracy, robustness, and interpretability, with ablation studies confirming the contribution of each component and cross-model testing validating its generalizability across different LVLM backbones.
Nevertheless, limitations remain. Reliance on web-based retrieval introduces latency, variability, and potential noise, and current focus on text and static images leaves out audio and video modalities increasingly common in misinformation. Moreover, the method’s effectiveness is still partly constrained by backbone model alignment and inference capacity.
In summary, MERF represents a promising step toward evidence-driven and explainable multimodal fake news detection. Future work will explore more efficient retrieval strategies, fine-grained evidence attribution, additional modalities, and lightweight deployment for real-time monitoring in high-stakes environments.
