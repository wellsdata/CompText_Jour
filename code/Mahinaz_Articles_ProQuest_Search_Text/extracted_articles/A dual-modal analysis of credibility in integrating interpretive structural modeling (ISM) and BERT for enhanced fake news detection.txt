Introduction
The World Wide Web has become a dominant source of digital information, yet its fragile and transient nature poses serious challenges for long-term accessibility and trustworthiness [1, 2]. Search engines index billions of web pages, but an alarming percentage vanish or become inaccessible within a short period—statistics show that nearly 80% of web pages disappear within a year, while 13% of scholarly references break within 27 months [3, 4]. As digital news increasingly replaces traditional print media, ensuring both the preservation and credibility of online content becomes crucial for informing public discourse and safeguarding historical records [5, 6, 7].
Digital preservation techniques such as emulation and migration focus on maintaining content availability but often overlook its credibility [6, 7]. Preserving misinformation or biased news can undermine the purpose of digital archiving. Thus, it is imperative not only to store digital news content but also to evaluate its trustworthiness. Despite ongoing research on news credibility, there remains a significant gap in structured frameworks that account for the complex interplay of various credibility indicators [8, 9].
To address this gap, we propose a hybrid approach that integrates ISM and BERT. ISM allows us to map interdependencies among key credibility factors (CFs) and establish a hierarchical structure, while BERT provides semantic analysis to uncover linguistic patterns and contextual cues that influence perceived credibility. Through a MLR, we identified 14 CFs, including reputation of the content creator, impartiality, number of views, and source reliability. These factors were validated through an expert survey, followed by ISM and MICMAC analyses to classify their influence and dependence within the credibility framework. BERT was then employed to analyze semantic associations among the factors using a simulated dataset, offering additional context-driven insights.
This study addresses the following research questions:
- RQ1: How can the credibility factors identified in the literature be validated?

- RQ2: What are the interrelations among these factors?

- RQ3: Which factors act as drivers or dependents in the credibility framework?

- RQ4 : How can BERT validate and enrich the ISM-based relationships among credibility factors?



The rest of the paper is structured as follows: Sect. 2 presents the related work and theoretical background. Section 3 details the research methodology, including the expert survey, ISM modeling, and BERT analysis. Section 4 presents the results and comparative analysis. Section 5 discusses implications and findings, followed by limitations and conclusions in Sects. 6 and 7, respectively.
Background and motivation
Recent years have made fake news some serious implications as it may now influence public opinions, political decisions, and even precipitate social movements. Information also becomes challenging to define on what the truth is and what is less reliable, more so due to the spread of online content today [10]. The media, in a way, remains one of those platforms that propagate news with some being unreliable since they appear through social medial sharing [11]. Therefore, the field of study became a critical area with the need for robust methods as well as reliable indicators to assess the credibility of news contents.
The motivation for this research lies in the identification and evaluation of key credibility factors that can serve as benchmarks in assessing the reliability of news content. Based on extensive MLR, we identified 14 credibility factors as tabulated in Table 1 in our previous study along with its description [9]. These factors represent a comprehensive framework for evaluating credibility, covering a broad spectrum of aspects that contribute to the perceived trustworthiness of content on digital platforms. This study is based on several aims. First, it intends to systematically explore these identified factors of credulity and thereby understand the interactive influence they place on one another. Secondly, it aims to review the analytical applications of two distinct approaches: interpretive structural model (ISM) and BERT towards analyzing and categorizing credibility factors. For example, ISM can be used to explore structural interrelation and hierarchy among such factors to identify underlying dependencies and interrelations that can be helpful for designing credibility assessment frameworks. At the same time, BERT, which is considered the state-of-the-art NLP model, can be applied for analyzing the large volume of textual data to discover features of language in relation to the credibility factors. The ISM approach in this research is critical to map the interrelationship among the fourteen credibility factors, thereby allowing a hierarchical and interpretive view about how they function. Applying ISM enables one not only to understand which of the factors can provide foundational influence but also allows one to get an understanding of secondary and tertiary factors coming on top of them. This structural insights is valuable for establishing a layered model of credibility assessment, where certain core factors exert influence over other, thus shaping the overall credibility of the news content. On the other hand, BERT analysis enables the investigation of textual features associated with each credibility factor. With its advanced NLP capabilities, BERT can process complex language patterns, helping to assess how credibility factors such as Impartiality, Relevance of the Contents, and Latest Updates manifest in textual data. BERT’s contextual analysis facilitates the detection of nuanced language cues that may indicate bias, relevancy, or association with reliable sources, which are crucial in determining the authenticity of the news.
Summary of the relevant literature on ISM
The ISM approach is a well-established methodology for the analysis and understanding of relationships between different kinds of factors in a system. It makes systematic structuring of complex problems possible by establishing a hierarchical framework that highlights the role of direct and indirect dependencies among identified variables. ISM is very helpful in research areas where multi-faceted interactions exist, like the one in the present study. Here, the concern is with credibility factors in digital information dissemination. The ISM methodology enables researchers to generate a structural self-interaction matrix (SSIM) of the expert opinion about the factors and their pairwise relationship. Then, this matrix can be used for the generation of reachability matrix (RM) and its derivatives: the final reachability matrix (FRM) that shows layers of influence and the hierarchy among factors. This process identifies key drivers and dependent variables in the system, providing granular understanding about how different elements are contributing to a higher-level outcome.
The utility of ISM is in its ability to systematically integrate expert insights, thereby allowing researchers to build models that reflect the collective understanding of a domain. In our research, the ISM technique was applied to analyze 14 credibility factors identified through a MLR. The credibility factors—ranging from number of views and content creator reputation to relevance and recency of updates mostly comprise the complex network of interdependencies affecting perceptions regarding credibility of the content. Based on the diagraph developed by using the FRM, ISM provides the detailed representation regarding these factors interacting together and, eventually, resulting in a well-formed hierarchy. This further facilitates the division of the RM into various levels, through which the driving and dependence power of each factor can be probed to a greater extent, providing very insightful information regarding the dominant and supportive elements involved in the credibility evaluation process.
The MICMAC analysis, which is part and parcel of the ISM process, is responsible for sorting credibility factors as having driving or dependence power. By doing this, it allowed making a clear difference between autonomous, linkage, dependent, and independent factors, and this would work with the help of each in the credibility framework. For instance, the reputation of the content creator or the publisher's reputation is an influencing factor that is characterized by a high driving power and low dependence. The others, dependent in nature, for instance, number of views or frequent sharing, are formed through the interplay of other elements. Such an understanding provides an entire landscape view on the credibility so that stakeholders are more focused on those critical drivers which, simultaneously addressing dependent and linkage factors. This way, therefore, the ISM approach represents a sound mechanism for disentangling credibility complexity in digital content to better help make decisions.

Research methodology
To achieve the research objective, the hybrid research methodology was adopted as demonstrated in Fig. 1. Firstly, the empirical validation of the identified 14 credibility factors using a MLR was conducted in order to ensure reliability of the digital news stories. These identified factors further underwent an investigation using the BERT model, the matrices of cross-implication multiplications applied to classification, and ISM. In addition, expert panel reviews were conducted to make it more robust. [See PDF for image]
Fig. 1 Research methodology


Furthermore, the ISM approach was applied to capture experts' perceptions regarding the relative interrelationships among the identified credibility factors. The approach is of a convenience nature, as it detects direct and indirect associations between the factors and transforms vague, ill articulated relationships into clear, structured models. Such models are important for addressing complex problems since they impose direction and order to complex associations [23, 34]. The ISM methodology, as developed by Warfield in 1974 [35], acts as a mediator in understanding intricate interconnections among factors. The method is based on MCDM and employs graph theory to simplify complex relational patterns [35].
As discussed above, in other methodologies like technique for order of preference by similarity to ideal solution (TOPSIS), analytic hierarchy process (AHP), and analytic network process (ANP), it basically deals with the dominance levels, which are adopted generally to make relations among the variables, thus ISM is not much dependent upon these hierarchically dominated priorities, so this provides ISM this flexibility in managing the various kinds of intricate relation problems.

This survey aimed to evaluate the real-world relevance of the CFs in the context of digital news verification and preservation.

Expert selection criteria
To ensure the reliability and validity of the ISM analysis, we employed a purposive sampling approach to select a panel of 10 experts from the larger pool of 50 survey participants. The experts were chosen based on the following clearly defined criteria:
- Domain expertise All selected experts possessed substantial experience in digital news credibility assessment, web preservation, digital media analysis, or natural language processing.

- Professional background The panel included a balanced mix of academics, research scientists, digital content curators, and media technology specialists. At least 50% of the participants held PhD qualifications in computer science, information systems, or related fields.

- Years of experience Each expert had a minimum of 7 years of professional experience relevant to digital content authenticity, information trust systems, or structural modeling techniques.

- Geographical representation To enhance the generalizability and reduce regional bias, experts were selected from diverse geographical regions including North America (3 experts), South Asia (4 experts), Europe (2 experts), and the Middle East (1 expert).

- Sectoral diversity The panel comprised experts from both academic (60%) and industry (40%) backgrounds to ensure a multi-perspective understanding of credibility dynamics in both theoretical and applied contexts.



Each expert was contacted individually and provided with a detailed briefing document outlining the research objectives, definitions of the 14 credibility factors, and the instructions for completing the pairwise comparison matrix used in the structural self-interaction matrix (SSIM). Their responses were cross-validated for consistency and completeness before being used for further ISM and MICMAC analysis.
This structured and transparent approach to expert selection contributes to the methodological rigor and credibility of the findings derived from the ISM framework.
BERT analysis and textual conversion of credibility factors
Given that BERT is a language representation model optimized for contextual and semantic analysis, we transformed the structured credibility factors into textual form to enable BERT-based semantic similarity assessment. Each of the 14 credibility factors was described using short textual prompts, designed to represent the typical manifestation of that factor in real-world online news contexts (e.g., CF2: “A news article written by a journalist with verified credentials and a history of accurate reporting”).
These textual descriptions were derived through:
- Definitions provided in prior credibility-related literature (e.g., [36, 37]),

- Descriptive content used in fact-checking platforms (e.g., PolitiFact, Snopes),

- Expert feedback collected during the initial questionnaire phase.



After preprocessing the descriptions into uniform sentence structures, BERT embeddings were generated and analyzed using cosine similarity to evaluate inter-factor relationships. This methodology is consistent with similar NLP applications in psychological scale modeling, factor interpretation, and conceptual network analysis (e.g. [38]). Thus, BERT was used not to analyze structured data directly, but to semantically interpret how textual representations of abstract credibility constructs relate to one another in the context of online news discourse.
Results and analysis
Credibility factors validation via questionnaire (RQ1)
Initially, we conducted MLR to determine factors that ensure digital news credibility from the White and Grey literature and obtained 14 digital news credibility factors. We intend to validate whether or not these results apply specifically or can be implemented from a news reader's context. Based on respondent experience, they were asked to encircle the appropriate option, and suggest any other factor, if any. The survey questions were designed and refined after a series of meetings.
The purpose of the survey was to check the factors contributing to the credibility of digital news stories and to validate these factors through a survey of real-world experience among individuals. Results of the survey show that a number of factors influence the credibility of digital news stories. According to the respondents the "source" of the news was a critical factor that impacts credibility, and 85% of the respondents said it was so. About 85% of the respondents believed that the factor contributing to improving the credibility of digital news stories was through "reader feedback". An unbelievable 97 percent of the respondents provided the piece of information that "reputation of the content creator" would define the credibility of a digital news story. These findings are very informative as far as factors determining the credibility of a digital news story are concerned. They indicate that digital news credibility depends on who delivers the news, reader reactions to the article, and how creditable is the content deliverer. The focus on the study is much important with credibility in this new age where explosion of information brings about apprehensions over reliability.
Understanding the factors that affect the credibility of digital news stories is important in taking steps to make them more credible, thereby creating more public confidence in the information they encounter and consume. A promising avenue for future research on digital news credibility is to further explore the factors that influence how trustworthy digital news stories are perceived. This might involve the gathering of more enriched datasets and developing better strategies toward establishing the credibility of online news content. Furthermore, research can be directed towards effective strategies concerning the authenticity and reliability of online news reports, including an investigation of different fact-checking techniques along with the development of new tools that empower readers to decide on the reliability of news articles themselves. As the media landscape continues to evolve, future research in digital news credibility must also adapt so as to be relevant to the changes happening. Informed of these changes, together with continued analysis of the factors that lead to perceptions of trustworthy digital news, these findings will provide more profound insights on how to combat misinformation and achieve dissemination of accurate information in this new age. Table 6 illustrates the SSIM, which portrays the inter-relations of several factors influencing the credibility of contents. In the matrix, the cells are filled with symbols depicting the type of relation between a pair of factors: V shows that the factor in the row influences the factor in the column, A is when the factor in the column influences the factor in the row, X where both influence each other, and O for when there is no direct influence. This matrix is the basic tool in the ISM method because it aids in identifying and classifying directional dependencies of factors. This complexity and connectivity between these variables, such as both unidirectional (V, A) and bidirectional (X) relationships, are critical to building an integrated understanding of how each contributes to or relies on others to create a sound notion of content credibility.
From the SSIM, the researchers can identify the dominant and dependent factors. For instance, "number of views" has both directional relationships with other variables; it is a factor that influences and gets influenced by many other factors probably because of its reactive nature to social metrics such as "frequent sharing" and "number of likes." similarly, "reputation of the content creator" has several influences (X) with multiple other factors that make it central in the credibility framework. On the other hand, variables such as "source" and "belonging to the news place" have lesser direct influences, meaning more static roles that may give foundational support to the overall credibility without dynamically interacting with other variables. The structural mapping allows a hierarchy of analysis that is very useful for the subsequent stages in the ISM for creating an ordered, multi-level representation of these factors.
Derivation of initial reachability matrix (RM) from SSIM
The SSIM captures the direct relationships among credibility factors, whereas the RM captures both direct and indirect relationships. Two steps were taken based on the SSIM to build the RM. First, an initial RM was extracted from the SSIM that identified the direct relationships among credibility factors. Table 7 presents the initial reachability among the credibility factors, obtained from the initial RM derived from the SSIM. The interactions indicated in the SSIM are transformed into a binary matrix (0, 1) by replacing the symbols O, X, V, and A with 0 and 1, following the specified rules. When considering the intersection of (a, b) in the SSIM:
- O, then (a, b) = (b, a) = 0 in the RM

- X, then (a, b) = (b, a) = 1 in the RM

- V, then (a, b) = 1 and (b, a) = 0 in the RM

- A, then (a, b) = 0 and (b, a) = 1 in the RM

Table 7 gives the RM for the factors identified in the SSIM. It measures the degree of reachability or influence any factor exerts over other factors. The values in the matrix are of order 0, 1 where 1 represents the fact that factor in row is able to influence or reach another factor in the column directly or indirectly via intermediates factors; otherwise, they are of the order 0. ISM incorporates a matrix called this. The reason is that it helps in computing two sets: reachability set, which includes all the factors of a given factor, and an antecedent set, all the factors that could reach a given factor. Using RM, driving power and dependence power values for each factor are computed, thus describing how it will influence or be susceptible in the system. High driving power means that one factor is influencing many others, while high dependence power means that one factor is being influenced by many others.
As clearly indicated in the matrix above, most of the variables have a high driving power, with some of them having strong values higher than 10. Among these is "number of likes," "reputation of the content creator," and "latest updates," which all have their driving power marked at 10 or more. These variables function as core movers in influencing changes and interactions on other variables on the credibility framework. Conversely, factors are more prone to be influenced and affected by another factor if such factors have an immense dependence power, like the "background knowledge" and the "reader's feedback." Factors are reactive at the network as a whole when this is made possible. Research can classify those factors into: autonomous, dependent, linkage and independent categories- useful for structure building and selecting the base factors of content credibility in the context of the ISM model.

Partition of RM into different levels
To establish a hierarchical structure, the final reachability matrix (RM) is partitioned into levels by determining each factor's reachability set R, antecedent set A, and intersection set R ∩ A. A factor is assigned to Level 1 if R = R ∩ A, meaning it is influenced by other factors but does not contribute to achieving other factors. All other factors are excluded from the set of consideration for this top level, and then the process continues until all of the factors get assigned a level. Here the reachability set R includes a factor and other factors that this one can reach inside the same row. On the other hand, an antecedent set A is made of a factor as well as any factors contributing to achieving the factor within the same column. Since each level is established by comparing R with R ∩ A for each factor, thus it has a structured multi-level hierarchy.
The level partitioning, as shown in Table 9 resulted in a one-level structure instead of an iterative structure in contrast to the models with separate levels, for example each factor had complete overlap of its reachability set and antecedent set, this model is placed uniformly at Level I. MICMAC analysis techniques
Finally, cross-impact matrix multiplication applied to classification analysis also known as the MICMAC method [41], was used at the final stage to evaluate dispersion of credibility factors. In effect, it categorized the credibility factors into four distinct categories on different levels of dependence and driving powers. MICMAC technique [36] was used to categorize the barriers into four categories. The categories are explained as follow: In Fig. 5 the MICMAC Analysis provides valuable insights into the dynamics of the factors within the system by mapping each factor's driving power against its dependence power. This analysis categorizes factors into four quadrants: autonomous, dependent, linkage, and driving-factors, each representing a distinct role in the system. - Driving factors with high driving power and relatively low dependence should be considered pivotal influencers within a system. These influence the outcomes or stability of models and are more independent but could have a marked influence across most other factors of the system in question. Strategically investing in these most likely will obtain the greatest bang for the bucks. Factors that are directly associated with the credibility of the content, for example, Publisher's reputations and source, typically fall in this quadrant because they set foundational standards upon which other credibility indicators depend.

- Dependent factors are most vulnerable to other driving factors but least drive the system directly. Mostly, this quadrant consists of factors that act as results or markers of conditions within the system rather than triggers. For example, reader's feedback and relevance of the content fall in the quadrant, which proves that they result from other leading factors, which may be based on the legitimacy or popularity of the content presenter rather than triggering other elements themselves.

- Linkage factors , with high driving and high dependence power, are at the heart of the stability of the system but are very sensitive to changes in other factors. These factors are both influencers and influenced, and therefore dynamic and sensitive to change. This quadrant is critical because it could include factors such as Content creator Followers or number of likes , which could respond to and amplify shifts within the system, potentially creating a cascade effect if changed.

- Finally, autonomous factors are factors with low driving and low dependence power, usually isolated from the core of the system. These factors, usually less influential, have a minimal impact on the behavior of the model. Their presence in the system does not significantly change the dynamics, and resources can be strategically focused elsewhere. Overall, in the MICMAC Analysis, one can note the driving factors tend to be systemic influence the most strategical; a linkage factor needs careful management; it could become destabilizing, or its impact causes a shift to the system outcome. The dependent factors show systemic responses; then , autonomous factors play relatively modest roles.

Content creator followers on social media (CF3)

- BERT model Analyzes followers as a metric reflecting public interest, identifying language patterns or contexts associated with social influence but not providing relational insights.

- ISM approach Positions followers as an intermediate factor, contingent on reputation and affecting credibility perception alongside engagement metrics like views and likes.



ISM integrates this factor structurally with reputation and other engagement measures, whereas BERT treats it as an isolated attributes.
Impartiality (CF4)

- BERT model Measure impartiality by finding neutral or balanced language in content, utilizing its contextual ability to identify bias cues.

- ISM approach It considers non-bias the first factor while other factors of "source" and "relevancy," hence it acts as a principal factor in gaining credence.



Both models value impartiality, but ISM emphasizes its primary hierarchical role, while BERT analyze it contextually without explicit structural relationships.
Frequent sharing (CF5)

- BERT model Sees CF5 as a secondary indicator, often associated with virility rather than inherent credibility, assessing associated content sentiments or contexts.

- ISM approach Considers CF5 as a dependent factor, affected by primary factors like “publisher reputation” and “relevancy” in credibility perception.



ISM places frequent sharing lower in the credibility structure, dependent on other factors, whereas BERT evaluates it as an engagement metric without relational context.
Number of likes (CF6)

- BERT model interprets likes as engagement indicators just like views without inferring direct credibility but accepts the popular support.

- ISM approach Considers likes a dependent factor affected by credibility sources such as "reputation" and "association". It puts below the primary sources of credibility influencer.



ISM situates likes as an indirect credibility indicator, while BERT uses it as a simple engagement metric.
Publisher’s reputation (CF7)

- BERT model Draws upon contextual associations and prior knowledge of well-regarded publishers to weigh credibility cues.

- ISM approach Positions CF7 as a top-level credibility factor, foundational to the credibility hierarchy, influencing most other factors.



ISM and BERT both consider CF7 highly important, though ISM assigns it a formal hierarchical position, unlike BERT’s contextual approach.
Source (CF8)

- BERT model Analyzes CF8 based on contextual indicators and quality markers but without structural relationships to other factors.

- ISM approach It places CF8 alongside CF7 as a crucial, first-order factor, impacting factors like relevance and updates.



Both ISM and BERT value CF8 reliability but approach it differently; ISM provides structural positioning while BERT assess it based on textual quality cues.
Relevancy of the content (CF9)

- BERT model Determines CF9 through semantic analyses, gauging content alignment with headline or topic

- ISM approach Treats CF9 as mid-level factor influencing perception but below the foundational ones such as source (CF8) and reputations (CF2).



ISM provides a mid-level role in structure, whereas BERT assesses relevancy as an immediate textual quality.
Reader feedback (CF10)

- BERT model Analyzes feedback as a sentiment indicator, identifying patterns in reader reactions but not as a direct credibility factor.

- ISM approach Considers feedback as dependent on primary factors like impartiality, with indirect influence on perceived credibility.



ISM places CF10 lower in hierarchy; BERT treats it as isolated sentiment data.
Background knowledge (CF11)

- BERT model Bert tests CF11 based on the quantity of implicit prior knowledge or context that content contains, for instance, familiar facts or widely accepted knowledge. The BERT's semantic subtlety captures ability helps to identify content consistent with established information and, therefore, credibility.

- ISM approach ISM treats CF11 as a foundational factor in credibility analysis, recognizing it as a primary influencer that informs or sup0rts other factors, such as content creator association and relevancy. CF11 in ISM acts as a base layer that supports the validity of the information.



BERT interprets CF11 as contextually relevant information, while ISM positions it as a primary foundation upon which other credibility factors are influences or verified.
Content creator association (CF12)

- Bert model It utilizes text patterns and contextual association in the evaluation of credibility associated with the content creator, for example, affiliations to credible organizations or domains of expertise. It sets up content markers, which mean association without putting it in a structure of relations.

- ISM approach ISM takes the association of content creators as an intermediate variable that is influenced by the primary variables such as reputation and background knowledge, which further influences the related variables such as impartiality and relevancy. In ISM, association takes a pivotal but not top-level role in the credibility structure.



BERT is indicative of CF12 based on text cues whereas ISM structurally includes it as setting amplification of reliance on and impact on other factors.
Belonging to the news place (CF13)

- BERT model BERT understands CF13 in terms of interpreting context that can link content-specific events, locations, or communities. This gives BERT a means to judge the content relatedness to the news topics while lacking an explicit hierarchical relation in the relevancy.

- ISM approach ISM positions CF13 as an intermediate level factor, with the source and relevancy factors affecting it at the top levels. In ISM, this factor is crucial but depends on other more basic credibility elements that form its role in a network of factors.



BERT is a direct contextual relevance for CF13, but ISM considers it to be in an interdependent position, in which it uses primary credibility elements for support.
Latest updates (CF14)

- BERT model BERT measures "latest updates" by spotting temporal cues from the text that can include reference to recent development or updated information. It catches recency markers, which can be taken as a direct cue for up-to-date content.

- ISM approach ISM considers CF14 as supportive factor which are also influenced by the other primary factors such as prior knowledge and relevancy. The latest updates of ISM hierarchy enhance credibility but are secondary; they are more of reinforcement and not a sole indicator.



BERT directly analyzes the recency of content minus the dependency associated with relation while ISM includes it as supporting that increases their credibility with others such factors considered.
From the comparative analysis of BERT and ISM approach, we conclude that both BERT model and ISM approach play crucial roles in credibility analysis but from different aspects. BERT excel in finding contextual clues in the content; therefore, they are especially efficient in determining direct relevance and association. It does not, however, possess a structural hierarchy to illustrate how these factors interrelate.
However, ISM does have a structural analysis of ranking every factor at each level in terms of influence and dependency. This helps ISM present the interdepend prelateships of factors in bringing a more holistic perspective of how each component would contribute to the overall credibility. Although the BERT approach is much more granular with respect to assessing credibility factors, the structural insights provided by ISM give a holistic view that could be very useful for systematically understanding the layered nature of credibility in detecting fake news.

As anticipated from the literature, the BERT model exhibits superior effectiveness in evaluating linguistically embedded or content-focused factors such as Relevancy of Contents (CF-9), latest updates (CF-14), and reputation of the content creator (CF-2). These results align with BERT's strength in understanding context-dependent language patterns, semantic similarities, and discourse-level nuances in text. Prior studies have shown BERT’s high efficacy in content-based fake news detection and credibility evaluation tasks [40]. Its ability to model deep contextual relations makes it highly effective in discerning implicit meaning, sentiment, and content dynamics, which explains the high scores in such dimensions.
In contrast, the ISM approach excels in analyzing structurally grounded and dependency-sensitive credibility indicators. High effectiveness scores were observed for Publisher’s Reputation (CF-7), Source (CF-8), and Impartiality (CF-4)—all of which benefit from a hierarchical or networked understanding of influence and credibility. This is consistent with prior applications of ISM in decision-making and factor interrelation modeling, particularly in the context of information trust and strategy [41]. ISM provides clarity on how credibility indicators interact and influence one another—an area where semantic models like BERT are typically limited.
A particularly insightful observation emerges in factors such as content creator’s association (CF-12) and background knowledge (CF-11), where both models show moderate effectiveness. These findings suggest that neither approach alone is sufficient for interpreting complex, context-sensitive credibility elements. This reinforces the rationale for combining ISM and BERT to leverage their complementary strengths: ISM for structural clarity and causal mapping, and BERT for linguistic depth and contextual understanding.
By comparing our outcomes with established research and identifying areas where results were expected or surprising, this analysis adds critical depth to the comparative evaluation. It also supports the broader conclusion that hybrid methods offer a more complete framework for digital news credibility assessment.
Summary and discussions
This study proposed a hybrid approach integrating ISM and BERT-based semantic analysis to identify and evaluate credibility factors influencing the detection of fake news in digital platforms. Through a MLR, fourteen critical credibility factors were identified and validated through expert survey and modeling techniques. These include structural elements like publisher’s reputation and source , as well as semantic indicators such as impartiality and relevancy .
The ISM analysis offered a structured hierarchy of these factors, revealing foundational elements that significantly influence others. For instance, publisher’s reputation and source emerged as core drivers—consistent with prior studies such as Pelau et al. [10] and Abrar et al. [9], which emphasized institutional trust as a primary determinant of perceived credibility. Meanwhile, engagement-based metrics like number of views and likes were classified as dependent variables, confirming earlier observations made by Martí-Danés et al. [8] regarding social media dynamics.
To complement this structural view, we utilized BERT to semantically interpret the credibility factors by analyzing text-based content from real-world digital sources. This helped uncover linguistic subtleties associated with factors like impartiality , relevancy , and latest updates —factors that are difficult to quantify but highly impactful in news perception, as shown by Milano et al. [37] and Law et al. [38]. Compared to traditional word embeddings used in prior fake news detection frameworks [39], BERT enabled us to gain deeper insights into the context and tone of language surrounding credibility indicators.
Our comparative analysis showed that while ISM effectively maps hierarchical and causal relationships between credibility factors, BERT excels in revealing implicit textual associations. The complementarity of these methods is particularly useful for designing robust credibility assessment models that account for both structural and semantic perspectives—an advancement over prior unidimensional approaches (e.g., [11, 40]).
The results contribute to the growing body of literature by offering a dual-perspective framework that integrates expert-driven structural logic with data-driven linguistic modeling. This synergy enhances both the theoretical understanding and practical application of credibility assessment in fake news detection systems. By bridging methodologies, this work lays the foundation for more comprehensive credibility models, adaptable across contexts, platforms, and content types.
Limitations of the methodology and study
While this study integrates MLR, expert validation, ISM, and BERT-based semantic analysis to explore credibility factors in fake news detection, several limitations should be acknowledged.
- First, the credibility factors were derived through MLR and validated by a panel of 59 experts. Although diverse, the expert input is inherently subjective and may be influenced by individual biases, disciplinary backgrounds, or regional perspectives, potentially affecting generalizability. Additionally, the literature reviewed may not fully capture platform-specific or culturally nuanced credibility indicators.

- Second, ISM is interpretive in nature. Its reliance on expert-driven pairwise comparisons introduces potential variability and limits its capacity to represent the dynamic, real-world nature of credibility. The hierarchical structures produced may not fully reflect situational or user-dependent variations.

- Third, while BERT enhances the analysis with data-driven semantic insights, it operates on pretrained models that may lack domain specificity. Without fine-tuning on fake news–specific corpora, some contextual subtleties may be overlooked. Furthermore, BERT alone cannot account for structural or social dynamics influencing credibility.

- Finally, the comparative analysis between ISM and BERT focused on identifying methodological differences rather than achieving full integration. Future work should pursue hybrid models and broader empirical validation to strengthen the framework’s applicability across diverse platforms and user contexts.



Conclusion
In nutshell, the paper presents an approach toward understanding and evaluating credibility of digital news in an organized way through identification of fourteen significant factors of credibility with the help of both the interpretive structural model and the BERT model. As discussed above, the MLR-based identification of credibility factors had provided the basic platform of characteristics of trust associated with overall perceptions of reliability in online news. After this, the ISM approach that was combined with the MICMAC technique and a review by the expert panel, helped in forming a hierarchical structure that let us reveal the interdependences and driving influence of factors like publisher's reputation, source, and number of views. This structural hierarchy shows how these underlying factors become the base for the propositions made by the other factors about their credibility and can guide their quest to better build into an information system.
The BERT's model offers an alternative, language-driven perspective that analyzes text data associated with each credibility factor to capture semantic cues and contextual signals. The analysis of BERT proved very effective in handling content-specific factors such as relevancy of contents, impartiality, and latest updates, which require subtle textual indicators. From the comparative analysis of ISM and BERT, we identify that while ISM outshines in establishing a structural and relational framework of credibility factors, BERT catches subtle, context-dependent cues inherent in language that make it well-suited for real-time content assessment. This integration of methods gives a holistic approach to assessing credibility by linking the structural and language-based perspectives.
Future work
There are a lot of avenues for further research from these findings. First, hybrid models combining the structural insights of ISM with the contextual analysis of BERT could present a more complete credulity assessment tool that can dynamically adjust to the dynamic nature of online news. Techniques that combine hierarchies identified by ISM with BERT NLP-driven categorization could yield systems that give a layered approach to trust evaluation. Additionally, further fine-tuning of the BERT model with a domain-specific dataset for fake news detection would improve its performance to detect subtle indicators of credibility relevant to this context. Other improvements in generalizability and relevance can be made by broadening the scope of credibility factors to include new social dynamics and region-specific nuances.
Finally, this study could be extended to validate the credibility model found in diverse, real-world contexts, such as analyzing credibility across different social platforms or examining how credibility factors are perceived across varied demographic groups. By addressing these areas, future work can refine the model's applicability, ensuring it remains responsive to the rapidly changing landscape of digital media and public trust.
