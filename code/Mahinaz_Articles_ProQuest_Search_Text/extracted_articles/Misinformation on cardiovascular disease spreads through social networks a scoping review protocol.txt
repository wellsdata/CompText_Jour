STRENGTHS AND LIMITATIONS OF THIS STUDY
- To the best of our knowledge, this is the first review to focus on misinformation on cardiovascular disease (CVD) prevention and control.

- Use of the Joanna Briggs Institute guideline for scoping reviews ensures a rigorous and transparent approach that can be easily replicated.

- There are no restrictions on study design, methodology and publication date, and broadens the scope of this review, providing a comprehensive overview of the topic.

- Use of grey literature adds important information from non-peer-reviewed sources, such as policy documents, reports and guidelines.

- Despite the comprehensive search strategy designed to cover a wide range of terms related to CVD misinformation, variations in terminology and definitions used in different contexts might lead to the omission of relevant studies.


Introduction
Despite the large scientific and therapeutic advances in the prevention and management of cardiovascular disease (CVD), its burden remains the leading cause of disability-adjusted life-years globally. 1 Several barriers, including the pervasive spread of misinformation online, hinder the effective adoption of evidence-based health practices by patients. 2 3 With the advent of the digital age, 4 the internet offers a vast amount of information, often including misinformation. This misinformation can significantly impact patients’ understanding of their health conditions, leading to delayed treatment, poor adherence to medical advice and the adoption of ineffective or harmful practices. The ubiquity of social media and the ease of information dissemination have led to scenarios where misinformation can spread more rapidly than factual information, 5 a phenomenon starkly highlighted during the COVID-19 pandemic. 6
A preliminary search of MEDLINE and the Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews revealed no current or underway systematic reviews or scoping reviews explicitly addressing the scope of misinformation on cardiovascular health. This gap underscores the need for a comprehensive scoping review to map the landscape of misinformation in this critical area of public health. Given the complexity and heterogeneity of the information sources and the broad nature of the topic, a scoping review is the most appropriate methodological approach to synthesise the existing evidence and identify the key characteristics and sources of cardiovascular health misinformation.
This scoping review aims to describe and synthesise the existing evidence on misinformation related to cardiovascular health, from prevention to treatment, and to assess its prevalence, sources and impact, spread through social networks. By examining the literature, this review will identify the methods used to study cardiovascular misinformation, the limitations of these methods and the results they have yielded. The overarching objective of this scoping review is to assess the extent of the literature on cardiovascular health misinformation and understand its implications for public health. This will enable the identification of research gaps and inform future studies to mitigate the consequences of misinformation.
Methods
The proposed scoping review will follow the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) methodology for scoping reviews. 7 The JBI guideline for scoping review consists of five sequential stages: (1) developing research questions, (2) identification of relevant studies, (3) study selection, (4) charting the data and (5) collating, summarising and reporting the results. 8
Stage 1: developing research questions
The research questions for this scoping review are
- What is the extent and nature of misinformation related to cardiovascular health?

- What are the primary sources and types of cardiovascular health misinformation?

- What methodologies have been used to study cardiovascular health misinformation?

- What are the limitations of current research methodologies on cardiovascular health misinformation?

- How does cardiovascular health misinformation impact public health understanding, behaviours and outcomes?

- What strategies have been proposed or implemented to combat cardiovascular health misinformation, and how effective are they?


Stage 2: identification of relevant studies
Inclusion criteria
The inclusion criteria for this review are developed according to the Population–Concept–Context framework: 8
Population
This scoping review will include studies involving individuals of any age, gender or ethnicity who are exposed to or affected by misinformation related to cardiovascular health. Studies will be considered if they examine the impact of misinformation on the prevention, management or understanding of CVD or its risk factors. The scoping review will consider a wide range of study designs, including
- Experimental and quasi-experimental studies.

- Observational studies (prospective and retrospective cohort studies, case–control studies and cross-sectional studies).

- Qualitative studies.

- Systematic reviews that meet the inclusion criteria will be considered to identify primary studies.

- Text and opinion papers that provide insights into cardiovascular health misinformation.


There will be no restrictions based on participant characteristics, and specific exclusion criteria will include editorials, protocols, conference abstracts and books/book chapters. In the case of notes, commentaries and letters to the editor will be considered if they present original data. However, reviews will be used as sources of potentially includable primary studies.
Concept
This review focuses on misinformation about cardiovascular health disseminated via social networks. This encompasses a range of false or misleading information, including but not limited to rumours, fake news, conspiracy theories and general misinformation and disinformation. According to WHO, 9 health misinformation is defined as false or misleading information about health that spreads, regardless of intent to mislead. Disinformation is false information deliberately created and disseminated with harmful intent. The concept of ‘infodemic’—an overabundance of information, both accurate and false, that spreads rapidly during disease outbreaks—will also be included. This is crucial for understanding the dynamics of misinformation spread during health crises such as the COVID-19 pandemic.
Context
The context of this review encompasses a broad range of environments where cardiovascular health misinformation may spread and exert its impact. These environments include online platforms, healthcare settings, public health campaigns and other relevant environments. By considering such diverse contexts, we aim to capture the various cultural, subcultural, geographic or setting-specific factors that influence the dissemination and effects of misinformation. Studies will be selected without geographic limitations to ensure a global understanding of the issue. There will be no study design, methodology or publication year restrictions. The search will be limited to the English language.
Search strategy
An initial limited search of databases will include MEDLINE (PubMed), Web of Science, Global Index and EMBASE. The last date of search was January 2025. The keywords in the search will include “misinformation”, “disinformation”, “cardiovascular health”, “patient education”, “nutritional and metabolic disorders”, “fake news”, “infodemic”, “public health”, “risk communication”, “rumour” and “health policy”. A preliminary search strategy is presented in online supplemental file 1. Grey literature will be explored on Google and Google Scholar. We will use simplified keyword combinations derived from our main search strategy (eg, ‘cardiovascular misinformation’, ‘heart disease myths’, ‘CVD prevention fake news’). For each distinct search query, the first 20 results, sorted by relevance (the default for these search engines), will be screened for potential inclusion.
Stage 3: study selection
All records identified through the search will be imported into Covidence systematic review software (Veritas Health Innovation, Melbourne, Australia), where duplicate entries will be automatically detected and removed. The study selection process will be conducted in two main steps:
- Title and abstract screening:
- Two authors will screen titles and abstracts against predefined inclusion criteria.

- A pilot test with 15–20 studies will be conducted to refine and streamline the screening process.

- Discrepancies will be resolved through discussion or consultation with a third reviewer.



- Full-text review:
- For records identified as potentially relevant during the initial screening, the same two reviewers will assess the full texts to determine final eligibility.

- A pilot test with 10–15 studies will be conducted to refine and validate the selection criteria.

- Reasons for exclusion at this stage will be documented and reported.

- Discrepancies will be resolved through discussion or consultation with a third reviewer.




Documentation and reporting
The entire study selection process, including the number of studies identified, screened and included or excluded at each stage, will be documented narratively and summarised in a Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses flow diagram. The study selection process will be reported following the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses extension for Scoping Reviews. 10 This will ensure clarity and reproducibility of our review methodology, as well as provide readers with a transparent account of how the included studies were identified and selected for inclusion in our review.
Stage 4: charting the data
Two reviewers will develop and preliminarily test a comprehensive data extraction form using Covidence on 5% of the included articles. Key data points to be captured will include specific details about the participants, concept, context, study methods and key findings relevant to the review questions (table 1). The draft data extraction tool will be modified and revised as necessary during the process to ensure robustness and relevance.

*Rumours: unverified and often false pieces of information spread informally, typically without reliable sources. It is often designed to generate ad revenue or influence public opinion. 12 Conspiracy theories: Explanations for events or situations that invoke a conspiracy by sinister and powerful groups, often without credible evidence. These theories can undermine trust in public institutions and spread fear or mistrust. 13
Data extraction
A data extraction form will be developed and preliminarily tested on 5% of the included articles by two reviewers using Covidence. Subsequently, data extraction will be conducted by two reviewers, with each paper assigned to two independent extractors. Key data points to be captured include study objectives, design, timing and location, sampling strategy, data sources, the method used to assess misinformation, characteristics of misinformation, sources, impact on public health and strategies used to combat misinformation.
Piloting the data extraction process
The data extraction process will be piloted on a subset of included studies to refine the methods and ensure they effectively address the review’s objectives. The pilot phase will inform any adjustments to the data extraction techniques and the narrative summary approach.
Stage 5: collating, summarising and reporting the results
The data analysis and presentation for this scoping review will be structured to address the objective and questions directly, ensuring a clear and comprehensive synthesis of the evidence related to cardiovascular health misinformation.
The process will involve several key steps:
- Systematic display of essential information:
- Essential information from the included studies, such as year of publication, research design, sample size, study population demographics, geographic location and related outcomes, will be systematically displayed in a table. This structured approach will facilitate easy reference and comparison across studies



- Descriptive summary:
- A descriptive summary of the extent and nature of cardiovascular health misinformation will be provided for the primary objectives. This summary will include details on the prevalence, types and sources of misinformation and the methods used to study them.

- Given the included studies’ diverse methodologies and reporting practices, the review will avoid aggregating data into quantitative metrics, such as combined prevalence proportions. Instead, findings will be synthesised using descriptive tables, visuals and a narrative summary, as recommended for scoping reviews. This approach will offer a comprehensive overview of cardiovascular health misinformation, reflecting the varied contexts, methods and reporting practices documented in the literature.



- Content analysis:
- Both inductive and deductive content analysis methods will be employed for objectives related to the impact and strategies to combat misinformation.

- The authors will discuss any factor not captured by existing frameworks to determine whether it should be merged into existing categories or established as a new category. This comprehensive method allows for an in-depth exploration of the complex factors influencing cardiovascular health misinformation and the effectiveness of different strategies to address it.



- Pilot testing:
- The data analysis and presentation methods will be piloted on a subset of included studies to refine the techniques and ensure they effectively address the review’s objectives. This pilot phase will inform any adjustments to the data presentation techniques and the narrative summary approach.



- Final presentation:
- The final presentation of the data will provide a detailed description that helps the reader understand the current state of the literature on cardiovascular health misinformation and its implications for public health. The findings will be disseminated using a combination of descriptive tables, visual aids (such as charts and graphs) and a narrative summary to communicate the results effectively.




Patient and public involvement
Patients and/or the public were not involved in the design, or conduct, or reporting, or dissemination plans of this research.
Ethics and dissemination
Given the nature of this study, which involves synthesising data from existing published literature, ethical approval is optional as no primary data collection involving human subjects will be conducted. The findings of this scoping review will be disseminated through multiple channels to reach a broad audience. Specifically:
- Academic dissemination:
- The results will be presented at international conferences, where they will be discussed with researchers and practitioners in the fields of public health, cardiovascular health and health communication.

- A comprehensive report of the review will be published in a peer-reviewed journal, ensuring the findings are accessible to the academic community and contribute to the existing body of knowledge.



- Public and policy dissemination:
- The findings will also be shared with public health organisations, organisations and policymakers through targeted reports, policy briefs and presentations to inform strategies and interventions aimed at combating misinformation related to cardiovascular health.

- Summaries of the review’s findings will be prepared for non-academic audiences, including public health advocates and the public, and disseminated through online platforms, collaborations with advocacy groups and easily digestible formats (eg, infographics) to raise awareness about the impact of misinformation on cardiovascular health.



- Digital and social media:
- Key insights from the review will be disseminated through digital platforms and social media channels to reach a wider audience and facilitate public engagement.

- To communicate the findings in an accessible and engaging manner, infographics, blog posts and short videos will be created.
