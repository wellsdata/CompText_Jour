1. Introduction The largest pandemic in the past century has raised numerous questions regarding its direct and indirect effects on children and young people. Among student populations, severe dependencies were identified, manifesting as behavioral disorders, a loss of motivation, and even complete disengagement from distance learning [1,2]. Furthermore, the online environment experienced an increase in cyberbullying, the emergence and diagnosis of conditions associated with excessive Internet use, and other adverse outcomes directly linked to the prevalence of false information and misinformation [3,4]. These not only shaped the opinions of young people [5] but also significantly influenced educators, who frequently rely on online resources [6].
Additionally, during the pandemic, most children globally experienced extended interruptions in their regular education, with some being out of school for a year or more. This was largely due to challenges and limited access to alternatives for in-person learning [7,8]. Despite these difficulties, the shift to online education also fostered innovation, leading to the development of new digital learning tools and expanded access to educational resources.
The rapid digitalization of education during the COVID-19 pandemic introduced unprecedented opportunities and challenges within the educational landscape. One notable opportunity was, for example, the accelerated adoption of digital learning tools, which helped expand access to education [9], particularly for learners in remote or underserved areas [10]. Additionally, the shift to online learning fostered greater flexibility in how students could engage with content and allow for more personalized learning experiences [11]. Another key opportunity was the rise of collaborative online platforms, which facilitated peer-to-peer learning and allowed educators to connect with students in new, interactive ways [12]. Furthermore, the pandemic highlighted the potential for educational content to be delivered asynchronously, providing students with greater control over their learning pace [13]. These positive developments paved the way for innovative approaches to education that continue to evolve. We can see that significant consequences emerged from the time individuals spent online during this period [14].
Similarly, online media has become the primary source of information for a large portion of the population [15,16,17], often shaping educational practices and access to knowledge. However, the sheer volume of available content makes it increasingly difficult to distinguish between credible sources and misinformation. The rapid spread of false or misleading educational materials has significantly influenced student learning outcomes and educators’ reliance on digital tools. In many cases, the prevalence of misinformation has led educators to question the reliability of online resources, adversely affecting their trust in digital tools and the educational materials available through these platforms. As a result, online media has undermined the relevance of online sources that were previously trusted for accessing knowledge. Moreover, online media has amplified the risks of misinformation influencing online learning and its outcomes.
For example, one of the less explored repercussions of the pandemic was the rapid normalization of unverified or misleading educational resources, which found fertile ground in the haste to transition to digital learning. Under time pressure and with limited digital literacy skills—the ability to navigate, evaluate, and create information using digital technologies safely and effectively—many teachers and students often turned to online platforms lacking credibility or peer-reviewed content. Studies have shown that this issue is widespread, with many educators selecting educational technologies without fully assessing their credibility or reliability. For example, a report from the University of Chicago and New York University highlights how schools often choose online platforms, which may also lack credibility and peer-reviewed content [18]. Similarly, the U.S. Department of Education provides guidelines that warn against the use of unverified online educational services [19]. Additionally, another research found that the surge in the use of unverified online platforms significantly impacts the quality of education, as educators and students increasingly rely on platforms that do not offer peer-reviewed or credible materials [20]. This proliferation of low-quality information exacerbated existing problems using unreliable educational resources, leaving vulnerable populations at risk. Consequently, the phenomenon of misinformation in online learning not only influenced academic outcomes but also undermined trust in digital tools and pedagogical approaches [21].
Moreover, alongside these platforms, sources of disinformation and conspiracy theories proliferated. These sources proved not only unreliable but also dangerous, as they were linked to a heightened potential for influencing users’ health-related behaviors and were associated with increased levels of negative effects, for example, public anxiety [22].
This entry focuses on misinformation in online learning, specifically exploring its types, spread, and effects in the post-pandemic period. It is conceptual in nature and does not rely on applied empirical research. Instead, it synthesizes existing theoretical frameworks, scholarly discussions, and documented observations to analyze the challenges posed by misinformation in online learning.
The paper explores how misinformation manifests within digital educational environments, the factors contributing to its spread, and the consequences of misinformation on learning, critical thinking, and the broader educational ecosystem. The objective is to analyze the effects of misinformation on education in the aftermath of the COVID-19 pandemic and to identify effective strategies for mitigating its impact.
Let us add, in this entry, that the terms “educators” and “learners” are intentionally used to reflect the diverse roles and participants within the educational ecosystem. The term “educators” encompasses not only traditional classroom teachers but also a broader group of professionals involved in the design, facilitation, and support of learning, including lecturers or trainers. This choice highlights the multifaceted nature of education, particularly in the digital age, where roles extend beyond traditional teaching to include mentorship, content creation, and technological facilitation. Similarly, the term “learners” is employed to represent individuals engaged in the process of acquiring knowledge, regardless of age, formal enrollment, or learning environment. By using “learners” rather than “students”, the study acknowledges the inclusivity of modern education, which spans formal settings, online platforms, and self-directed learning pathways. We believe these terms collectively emphasize the dynamic and interconnected nature of education, particularly in the context of online learning in the post-pandemic era.
2. Defining Misinformation and Disinformation in the Digital Age: Implications for Online Learning Misinformation refers to false or inaccurate information that is disseminated, regardless of any intent to deceive [23]. It often emerges from misunderstandings or a lack of verification and can be shared unintentionally by individuals who genuinely believe it to be true, making it a pervasive issue in the digital age. Misinformation can take various forms, including incorrect data, misleading headlines, or distorted interpretations of facts [24,25,26].
Unlike misinformation, disinformation refers to deliberately creating and spreading false information with the intent to deceive, manipulate, or achieve a specific goal, such as political, economic, or societal objectives [27]. Misinformation, as a broad term, encompasses various types of false information, ranging from intentionally crafted conspiracy theories to satirical content mistakenly perceived as true [28].
Disinformation includes deceptive news, fake news, or fabricated information that is blatantly false (which may be intentionally false, i.e., disinformation, or unintentionally misleading, i.e., misinformation), hoaxes, rumors (misleading information with an undefined purpose), misleading satire (using humor, irony, and exaggeration to ridicule a subject, often a politician), clickbait (content designed primarily to attract attention and encourage users to click on a link), gossip, and propaganda [27,29]. Disinformation is considered a threat to democracy, journalism, and freedom of speech, as it undermines public trust in democracy and governments and may have a potential impact on controversial solutions [30].
Historically, misinformation spread through traditional media such as newspapers, radio, and television. While these channels were influential, their reach and speed of dissemination were constrained by technological limitations [31,32]. Today, misinformation often circulates through social media and other online platforms, where fact-checking is frequently absent or insufficient. The Internet and digital platforms have revolutionized the way information is shared, creating an environment in which misinformation can flourish [33].
Thus, former readers, viewers, and listeners of traditional media have evolved into not only active distributors of media content in the online space but also content creators. Unfortunately, much of the content produced can often be considered manipulative or even misinformative [31,32]. In this context, research confirms that individuals’ willingness to share information (including false content) is related to their attitudes, which are often linked to their values, emotions, social trust, and social identity [34], or as a response to personal needs, such as the desire to shape one’s identity, feelings of belonging, the need for attention, or the desire to maintain communication with peers [35], among others.
Misinformation has long been a pervasive issue, affecting various domains, including education. Social media, blogs, and online forums serve students and teachers often as primary sources of information, making it easier for falsehoods to spread widely and rapidly [36,37]. This phenomenon is further amplified by the growing reliance on informal, user-generated information sources such as Wikipedia, Facebook, YouTube, blogs, forums, and Q&A websites [38]. In addition, while social media has democratized access to information, it often lacks rigorous editorial oversight, allowing unverified and potentially inaccurate content to circulate unchecked.
The widespread dissemination of misinformation presents unique challenges also within education. Students, often inexperienced in evaluating sources, are particularly vulnerable to accepting and sharing inaccurate information. This has highlighted the importance of integrating media literacy and critical thinking skills into educational curricula. The reason for this is clear—the volume of content we consume, both online and offline, is increasing, while the amount of misinformation is also rising. Meanwhile, our ability to assess the truthfulness of content is decreasing. In other words, the more we engage with the rich, fast-paced, and financially accessible offerings of online content, the more we experience information overload [39,40], as well as limited attention [41,42,43]. This diminishes our ability to judge the veracity of the content we consume and also impacts our ability to concentrate, process complex information, and think critically—all of which naturally affect education.
The increasing prevalence of misinformation is closely linked to the growing popularity of social media among young people. In the United States, nearly 40% of children aged 8–12 use social media, and up to 95% of teens aged 13–17 report using a platform, with more than a third indicating they engage with social media “almost constantly” [44]. Furthermore, between 2019 and 2021, daily screen time for tweens in the United States rose from over four hours to nearly five and a half, while for teens, it grew from more than seven hours to almost nine. This increase occurred at a significantly faster pace over two years compared to the preceding four years [45].
Therefore, many experts are studying social media audiences to understand the factors influencing the acceptance or rejection of false information among users, including students or teachers. Studies generally suggest that susceptibility to disinformation is influenced by individuals’ prior beliefs, a phenomenon known as confirmation bias [46,47], or factors such as age, gender, education, or socio-economic status [48,49].
Moreover, experts suggest that, despite these challenges, effective strategies to reduce the impact of disinformation include flagging false information and consulting professionals for evaluation. While these approaches are important, studies indicate that efforts to combat misinformation and disinformation—even with credible information and expert guidance—often have only limited success [50]. Consequently, researchers stress the need for proactive intervention by professionals, particularly in sectors like healthcare and education.
3. Manifestations of Misinformation in Online Learning Drawing on the existing literature and critical discourse, this entry employs a theoretical framework to examine misinformation in online learning environments. Given the dynamic nature of digital education and the multifaceted challenges posed by misinformation, a conceptual analysis enables a comprehensive exploration of its underlying causes, implications, and potential mitigation strategies.
As mentioned earlier, studies indicate a significant rise in the dissemination of false content in online learning, further exacerbated by the pandemic’s impact. Misinformation, whether related to health or other fields, can severely impair students’ ability to distinguish accurate information. As a result, various studies underscore the importance of critical digital literacy skills in addressing these challenges. Furthermore, belief systems, demographic factors, and prior experiences play crucial roles in how misinformation is accepted and internalized, further complicating the issue within online learning environments.
Consequently, misinformation in online learning manifests in various forms, including the following:
- False educational content—Unverified resources shared through digital platforms, often presented as credible instructional materials;

- Misinterpreted scientific data—The misuse or oversimplification of research findings, leading to incorrect conclusions;

- Misleading sources—Manipulated videos, images, and infographics created to deceive;

- Misattributed quotes—Statements falsely credited to experts or reputable sources, which can mislead learners about the authenticity of information;

- Pseudoscientific theories—Claims that lack empirical evidence but are presented in scientific language to appear credible;

- Clickbait titles—Headlines or abstracts that exaggerate or distort the content to draw attention, often leading to misinformation;

- Skewed visual data—Manipulated graphs or charts that alter facts to align with a specific narrative;

- Propagation of discriminatory or biased narratives—Content that promotes stereotypes or prejudices under the guise of educational material;

- Fraudulent online courses—Programs that promise knowledge or certification but deliver misleading or irrelevant information.



4. Factors Contributing to the Proliferation of Misinformation The spread of misinformation in online learning is driven by several interrelated factors that shape its pervasive influence. One of the primary contributors is the excessive reliance on online sources. The shift to digital learning has significantly increased dependency on readily available yet often unreliable online materials, making it easier for misinformation to circulate unchecked. Closely related to this issue is the lack of adequate media literacy skills among both learners and educators. Many individuals struggle to critically evaluate the credibility of online content, such as recognizing bias in news articles or identifying false claims in blogs [33]. This deficiency is particularly problematic when contrasted with scientific knowledge’s dynamic and evolving nature, where misinformation can be easily mistaken for legitimate information.
Emotional susceptibility further exacerbates the problem, especially in times of crisis. Heightened anxiety and uncertainty make individuals more prone to believing and sharing unverified information. Emotional reactions contribute to the rapid spread of misinformation, as emotionally charged content tends to be more engaging and shareable within online communities [51]. Similarly, confirmation bias plays a significant role, as learners may actively seek and trust content that aligns with their preexisting beliefs, often disregarding contradictory yet accurate information [52]. This selective exposure reinforces misinformation and hampers critical engagement with alternative perspectives, perpetuating a cycle that strengthens false beliefs [53].
The formation of echo chambers further intensifies the problem. Online communities and discussion groups can create environments where users predominantly encounter viewpoints that mirror their own, reinforcing misinformation and making it difficult for accurate information to gain traction [54]. The marginalization of opposing perspectives, the lack of shared understanding, and the resulting stagnation in political, administrative, and social decision-making all contribute to the persistence of misinformation in such isolated networks [55,56]. This self-reinforcing cycle facilitates the unchecked dissemination of misinformation and deliberate disinformation [57,58].
Algorithmic amplification on digital platforms further complicates efforts to combat misinformation. Personalized algorithms, particularly on social media and video-sharing platforms, prioritize content that captures users’ attention and engagement, even when such content is misleading or inaccurate. This system inadvertently promotes sensationalized misinformation, making it more visible and influential within online learning spaces. Once misinformation becomes deeply entrenched, correcting it poses significant challenges. Although refutation texts in academic environments can help students overcome misconceptions [59], research suggests that individuals often persist in their incorrect beliefs even when confronted with accurate information [60].
Additionally, online education’s lack of content verification facilitates the spread of inaccuracies. Educational materials are frequently shared on platforms without proper fact-checking or validation, allowing misinformation to proliferate. Time constraints further exacerbate this issue, as both educators and students often prioritize speed and convenience over thorough research, leading to the unintentional use of unreliable sources. Finally, the poor regulation of online learning platforms contributes to the persistence of misinformation. Many platforms lack adequate moderation and regulatory measures, creating an environment where misleading content can spread unchecked, further complicating efforts to ensure accurate and reliable information in digital education.
5. The Impact of Misinformation on the Educational Ecosystem The consequences of misinformation go beyond individual classrooms and affect the integrity of the broader educational system. As students increasingly rely on social media for news [15,16,17,18] as well as artificial intelligence tools that often amplify the creation and dissemination of misinformation [61,62,63], fostering media literacy has become essential to developing critically informed learners. In other words, the negative role of misinformation in shaping digital learning dynamics underscores the urgency of equipping students with the necessary skills to critically assess online content.
Exposure to and influence by misinformation weaken students’ ability to critically evaluate and analyze information, stunting intellectual growth [64,65]. This disruption of critical thinking development makes it difficult for learners to distinguish credible sources from misleading ones. Additionally, the cognitive overload caused by the need to discern credible sources adds to the mental burden of learners [66,67,68]. Misinformation can create confusion in classrooms, where students may bring inaccurate or distorted facts into discussions, further complicating the learning process.
The dissemination of harmful practices is another major repercussion of misinformation. False information about health, safety, or social issues can lead students to adopt dangerous practices or beliefs, such as disregarding expert advice or engaging in risky behaviors [69,70,71]. Moreover, misinformation contributes to the polarization of viewpoints, dividing learners into opposing groups [72] based on false beliefs and fostering conflict [73], which hinders collaborative learning and the development of shared understanding.
Educators face an increased burden due to misinformation, as they must devote more time to addressing false claims, debunking misconceptions, and guiding students toward accurate sources [74]. This creates an environment that detracts from the learning experience and makes it difficult to focus on the curriculum. Similarly, the constant challenge of addressing misconceptions in the classroom can hinder the maintenance of a productive and focused learning environment [75].
Misinformation disrupts the educational ecosystem by eroding trust, which is crucial for academic integrity. Trust fosters honest collaboration, the sharing of ideas, and the advancement of genuine learning [76]. When misinformation infiltrates educational environments, it breeds skepticism toward the credibility of academic content, teachers’ expertise, and the reliability of academic institutions. This ultimately undermines students’ and researchers’ ability to build upon existing work and collaborate effectively.
The broader educational ecosystem is significantly affected by misinformation, particularly as it undermines the credibility of academic institutions and research. When false information circulates widely, it can erode public trust in educational systems, especially in fields such as science, health, and history [28]. Misinformation has long-term consequences for the integrity of the educational ecosystem, affecting academic integrity and sustaining public confidence in scholarly research, teaching, and the value of education. As mentioned earlier, misinformation’s impact extends beyond individual classrooms, posing a substantial challenge to the broader educational framework.
6. Strategic Interventions to Combat Misinformation in Online Learning after the Pandemic While social media can facilitate the spread of false content, it also offers potential avenues for combating misinformation, particularly when coupled with strong digital literacy skills that enable users to rate information critically. In light of these challenges, this entry presents possible strategic interventions addressing the proliferation of misinformation in online learning.
One crucial approach is the integration of digital literacy programs [77]. Developing curricula that focus on identifying credible sources, fact-checking, and responsibly navigating digital platforms is essential. To enhance effectiveness, digital literacy should be incorporated across all school subjects rather than being confined to specialized lessons. This cross-disciplinary approach enables students to apply digital skills in various contexts, promoting responsible and informed content creation and evaluation [78]. Furthermore, implementing systems and artificial intelligence tools that automatically cross-check facts in educational materials and suggest verified sources to educators and learners can improve the reliability of online learning [79,80]. Collaboration among schools and educational organizations might also strengthen the availability of authentic resources for educators and students.
Another fundamental strategy is reinforcing critical thinking pedagogy [81]. Encouraging analytical skills helps learners question and validate information, fostering a more discerning approach to digital content. Inquiry-based learning models that prioritize evidence-based understanding should be emphasized, alongside promoting the use of peer-reviewed articles, academic journals, and other credible sources in online learning materials. Providing students with real-world media stories and examples of misinformation and disinformation, coupled with classroom discussions, enhances their ability to critically assess digital content [68].
Equipping students and teachers with access to real-time fact-checking tools, such as Google Fact Check Explorer or Rumour Guard, is also essential. Such platforms allow users to search for claims or rumors and verify their accuracy, promoting active engagement with information and responsible media consumption [82]. Additionally, implementing the “humanization” of journalism—where professional journalists take an active role in the educational process—can strengthen the connection between media education and media practice. This approach provides an authentic perspective on journalistic work, its challenges, and ethical standards [81].
Promoting active social interaction in online learning is another key intervention. Encouraging collaborative learning strategies enables students to engage in discussions and debates regarding the credibility of sources and the accuracy of information. Creating virtual spaces for peer-to-peer interaction allows learners to share insights, question assumptions, and collectively cross-check information, fostering critical thinking in a safe environment [83]. Group projects and discussions that analyze and correct misinformation contribute to developing students’ social and cognitive skills in identifying incorrect content. Synchronous online sessions can also be leveraged to facilitate guided discussions, role-playing, and group activities that explore how misinformation spreads under different circumstances [84]. Educators should be trained to implement and facilitate these strategies effectively, ensuring respect and support for active social interaction in online learning environments.
Another important measure is the removal of misleading content. Schools should collaborate with educational technology companies to remove deceptive online material and work with established academic institutions and expert organizations, such as FactCheck.org or Snopes, to provide reliable, evidence-based content that counters misinformation. News literacy projects play a vital role in combating misleading content by reducing the harmful effects of misinformation, encouraging responsible sharing, and supporting fact-based online discussions. Additionally, launching awareness campaigns can educate learners about the risks of misinformation and the importance of seeking verified information [85,86,87].
Supporting research on the effects of digital media on education is also crucial [75,78,88]. Schools and universities should partner with research organizations and technology companies to conduct long-term studies that assess the positive and negative impacts of digital media on learning and mental health. Research teams composed of experts in education, psychology, technological innovation, and information technology should analyze algorithms, platform designs, and their content to evaluate their effects on the learning process. The findings should offer specific recommendations for developing effective online learning strategies that minimize students’ exposure to misinformation while enhancing their critical thinking skills. These research conclusions should be publicly accessible in a clear and applicable format to ensure that educators, educational technology developers, and other professionals can utilize them effectively. Financial support for research projects examining the intersection of digital media use in education and its long-term implications for teaching and learning should be strengthened.
Finally, post-pandemic approaches to misinformation in online learning require action from digital platforms. Digital platforms should establish content moderation guidelines that prioritize reliable, evidence-based sources and use automated systems to flag or remove inaccurate content, thereby reducing the spread of misinformation among students [89]. Integrating digital citizenship education into online learning programs can teach students responsible online behavior, including respecting intellectual property and engaging in ethical content sharing [90]. To further combat misinformation, platforms should improve algorithm transparency, allowing educators and students to better understand how content is curated. Collaboration with independent researchers can help ensure that algorithms amplify reliable content while minimizing misinformation. Educational content platforms should involve experts and peer-reviewed academic work in the creation and validation of online learning materials to maintain high standards of accuracy and foster trust in educational resources. Public awareness campaigns also play a critical role in educating communities about the risks of misinformation [25,85,91,92,93]. Schools, parents, and local communities must collaborate to provide tools and resources for identifying and avoiding misleading content, thereby promoting shared responsibility in combating misinformation.
7. Conclusions Understanding misinformation necessitates examining its historical trajectory and its varied applications across contexts, including education. While misinformation itself is not a novel phenomenon, its scale and impact have been significantly amplified by the advent of digital platforms and social media, making it a critical area of study within modern educational systems.
The transition to online learning has further compounded these challenges, as digital platforms, despite their advantages, have become primary sources of educational content. These platforms, while offering numerous benefits, have also facilitated the dissemination of unverified and misleading information, posing significant obstacles for both educators and learners.
In contrast, the post-pandemic era offers a unique opportunity to critically address the pervasive issue of misinformation within online learning environments, fostering a renewed focus on building resilient, informed, and digitally literate educational communities. This entry, therefore, underscores how the swift shift to digital education during the pandemic intensified vulnerabilities in the consumption and dissemination of information, exposing both educators and learners to the risks of unverified content.
Within this entry, strategic interventions—ranging from the integration of digital literacy programs and the promotion of critical thinking pedagogy to fostering active social interaction and eliminating misleading content—present a comprehensive framework for combating misinformation and empowering learners by equipping students, educators, and institutions with the necessary tools to critically engage with and challenge misinformation. As we navigate the evolving landscape of online education, prioritizing these efforts will foster a culture of informed, responsible, and engaged learners, thereby strengthening the foundation of education in a digitally connected world.
In conclusion, the study underscores the importance of collective efforts from educators, policymakers, and technology providers in creating a trustworthy digital learning environment. Better-regulated platforms, effective tools for detecting misinformation, and a strong emphasis on digital literacy are essential for equipping learners with the skills needed to navigate an increasingly complex information landscape. By fostering collaboration and innovation, the educational community can protect the integrity of online education and empower students to thrive in the digital age. Ultimately, our shared responsibility is to adapt and respond proactively to the evolving digital environment, ensuring that online education remains a platform for truth, growth, and empowerment.
