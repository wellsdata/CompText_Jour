Introduction and Research Problem: Disinformation on Electoral Processes by Political Authorities
In recent years, the issue of disinformation has garnered significant attention across multiple domains, including academic research, international organizations such as the United Nations, public policy updates, and comparative law developments. This growing interest is closely linked to the widespread and continuous use of social networks by billions of users for entertainment, content sharing, and information gathering. These platforms have become the primary form of social communication in the digital era, offering both advantages and risks. While they provide citizens with unprecedented access to information, they also enable the rapid production and dissemination of false content, often amplified by Artificial Intelligence (AI), which can attract greater attention and credibility than factual information (Vaccari & Chadwick, 2020).
Although academia, the media, legislative bodies, and international organizations are intensely debating this issue today, the problem of fake news is not exclusive to the era of social media, smartphones, and post-truth. On the contrary, historical evidence shows that concerns about disinformation have long existed across different contexts. For instance, nearly a century ago, in October 1925, Harper’s Magazine published Edward McKernon’s article Fake News and the Public: How the Press Combats Rumor, the Market Rigger, and the Propagandist . The text denounced how technological advancements of that time complicated the distinction between rumors and facts, leading to political confusion among readers.
In Chile, disinformation has an extensive regulatory history. In 1925, amid intense political tension and on the eve of a constitutional process, a government junta issued a decree that, for the first time, criminalized “the publication or reproduction of fake news” (Art. 17 DL 425, 1925). The context was critical. Enacted during a de facto government, this measure was part of broader efforts to impose press censorship and suppress political dissent (Silva, 1975). Over the years, the regulation of fake news underwent multiple amendments and remained in force until 2001. These modifications reflected a clear historical pattern: during authoritarian periods, restrictions were severe and curtailed free speech, whereas democratic governments implemented frameworks more aligned with free speech principles (Charney, 2025).
Fake news has a long history. The so-called yellow journalism of the late nineteenth century, characterized by exaggerated and false reporting, played a key role in shaping public opinion and even contributed to major geopolitical events, such as the Spanish-American War in 1898. What distinguishes the contemporary era is not the existence of fake news itself but its unprecedented scale, reach, and technological sophistication. Social media platforms and AI-driven algorithms have enabled disinformation to spread at an accelerated pace, while microtargeting techniques have ensured that specific disinformation campaigns reach vulnerable audiences with high precision.
2016 marked a tipping point in the historical evolution of fake news. Three key elections took place that year—two referendums and one presidential—each significantly impacted by disinformation. Baptista and Gradim (2022), in a systematic review of the state of the art from 2017 to 2021, highlighted a fundamental shift in the focus of scholars following 2016. Before this period, research primarily associated fake news with political satire and emphasized its presence on television. However, the 2016 U.S. presidential election of Donald Trump and the Brexit referendum that same year signaled a turning point. Collins (2019) underscores how, in the aftermath of these events, terms such as “fake news” and “post-truth” became central to the political discourse, with “post-truth” even being named as the Oxford Dictionary’s “Word of the Year”. Marshall and Drieschova (2020) similarly identify the Brexit referendum as a paradigmatic example of post-truth politics, with social networks playing a decisive role in shaping public perception.
That same year, Colombia held the Referendum for Peace , which posed a binary choice—Yes or No—on the agreement between the government and the FARC guerrillas. The campaign was heavily influenced by widespread disinformation, particularly from supporters of the “No” campaign. Several factors contributed to this phenomenon, including the specific design of the peace process. As Amaral (2021) explains, “the exclusive character of negotiations, coupled with their confidentiality, contributed to the political opposition’s capacity to influence public opinion against the peace process and to reject the peace agreement in the 2016 referendum”. Disinformation was widely disseminated via social media (Arroyave & Romero-Moreno, 2022; Dajer, 2021), particularly regarding contentious topics such as the so-called “gender ideology” (Beltrán & Creely, 2018; Guerrero, 2024). The extent of this disinformation was so significant that the State Council of Colombia ( Consejo de Estado de Colombia ) later scrutinized the referendum’s outcome. In a ruling of December 19, 2016, the Council stated as follows: “the existence of a generalized deception is concluded that annulled the freedom of the electorate to choose autonomously among the existing options in the plebiscite in question, since, as demonstrated, lies were exposed in a massive and systematic manner (...) due to the irregularities that occurred in the election process, inasmuch as psychological violence was exercised on the electorate during the campaign, this Office considers at this procedural stage that the act of declaring the result of the plebiscite is flawed” (State Council of Colombia, 2016).

Since 2016, disinformation has become a central focus of social research, particularly in the context of electoral campaigns. Concerned by the impact of disinformation during the Brexit referendum, the British Parliament issued a 2017 report clarifying key concepts. This document rejected the broad use of the term “fake news,” advocating instead for the distinction between ‘disinformation’ and ‘misinformation.’ That same year, the Council of Europe expanded this conceptual framework by introducing a third category, ‘malinformation’ (Wardle & Derakhshan, 2017). Together, these terms define what is now recognized as an environment of information disorder.
- Disinformation : False information that is knowingly disseminated with the intent to harm a person, social group, or organization (Dan et al., 2021).

- Misinformation : False information that is shared without awareness of its inaccuracy and without intent to cause harm (Vaccari & Chadwick, 2020).

- Malinformation : Information based on real facts but distorted, exaggerated, or decontextualized in a way that misleads and harms a person, social group, or organization (Mendoza et al., 2022).



The specialized literature reflects a growing consensus on the role of AI-driven disinformation in social networks, particularly regarding three key aspects: - Disinformation is especially intense during crises—whether social or natural—and electoral campaigns (United Nations, 2022).

- National and international institutions recognize it as one of the greatest challenges to modern democracies (McKay & Tenove, 2021).

- Social networks serve as the primary medium for its rapid propagation (Di Domenico et al., 2021).


Building on these points, recent studies have explored the intersection of disinformation, crises, and electoral processes (Calvo & Aruguete, 2021; González-Bailón et al., 2023; Guess et al., 2023; Rondero, 2023). However, two critical yet underexplored dimensions remain central to this study: - The specific obligations of political authorities concerning disinformation, particularly when they are the primary source of such falsehoods.

- Disinformation in constitional electoral processes , where the focus is not on electing authorities but on approving a new constitution. As noted, constitutional processes involve deliberations on issues that are highly sensitive and critical to a nation’s political future.


As Sunstein (2021) underscores, any analysis of disinformation must prioritize its “origin” and the “obligations incumbent on certain sectors,” particularly political authorities. This principle is echoed in international legal frameworks. For example, the Joint Declaration on Freedom of Expression and Fake News (2017), adopted in Vienna by the United Nations (hereinafter “UN”) Special Rapporteur, the Organization for Security and Co-operation in Europe (hereinafter “OSCE”), the Organization of American States (hereinafter “OAS”), and the African Commission on Human and Peoples’ Rights (hereinafter “ACHPR”), asserts: “State actors should not make, sponsor or disseminate statements that they know or ought reasonably to know are false (disinformation) or that show a manifest disregard for verifiable information (propaganda)”.

International law reinforces this duty of care for state actors, particularly in the context of heightened social conflict, public disorder, or political polarization, where such disinformation poses risks to individuals and groups (Inter-American Court of Human Rights, 2009).
Guided by this framework, this study examines the obligations of political authorities in electoral contexts, specifically regarding their role in the dissemination of disinformation . While this research intersects with broader studies on electoral disinformation, it distinguishes itself by focusing on two underexplored aspects: disinformation occurring during a constitutional referendum and the deliberate spread of falsehoods by political authorities within this process.
Conceptual Discussion
Disinformation and Democratic Health
Disinformation is widely recognized by institutions, experts, and international bodies as one of the most critical threats to democratic governance. The UN has addressed this issue through multiple resolutions (United Nations, 2022), while the United Nations Educational, Scientific and Cultural Organization (hereinafter “UNESCO”) has produced in-depth reports on its impact (Unesco, 2022, 2023). The Organization for Economic Co-operation and Development (2022) also established an expert group dedicated to analyzing misinformation and disinformation.1 In 2017, this global concern culminated in the adoption of the Joint Declaration on Freedom of Expression and Fake News, Disinformation, and Propaganda , signed by representatives of the UN, the OSCE, the OAS, and the ACHPR. The Inter-American Court of Human Rights (2009) has also addressed the issue, as explored below.
The legal response to disinformation has had a significant impact on comparative legal frameworks. A growing number of countries have introduced electoral regulations that explicitly criminalize the dissemination of false information during election periods. For instance, Colombia (Art. 103, Electoral Code), Brazil (Art. 323, Electoral Code), and Mexico (Art. 8, General Law of Electoral Institutions and Procedures) have enacted legislation penalizing fake news that targets candidates or electoral processes. Although these regulations do not explicitly address disinformation propagated by political authorities during elections, they reflect the severity with which electoral disinformation is regarded.
In Chile, both academia and state institutions have increasingly focused on the issue. Researchers have extensively examined disinformation and its effects (e.g., Bachmann & Valenzuela, 2021; Cárcamo-Ulloa et al., 2023; Grassau et al., 2019, 2020; Halpern et al., 2019; Kloss & Louit, 2024; Núñez-Mussa et al., 2024; Mendoza et al., 2022; Santana & Huerta, 2019; Valenzuela et al., 2022; Weidenslaufer & Roberts, 2023a). Bachmann and Valenzuela (2021) observed that “Chile is experiencing an information disorder, with most Chileans acknowledging that they have recently received, believed, and even shared information that was later disproved.”
At the governmental level, Chile has taken institutional measures to address the problem. In 2023, the Ministry General Secretariat of Government established the Ministerial Advisory Commission against Disinformation (hereinafter “MACD”). That same year, the Commission published two reports warning of the “negative impacts” that disinformation spaces could have on the “political and social stability of the country” (MACD, 2023, p. 29), explicitly identifying disinformation as a “threat” to Chile’s democratic stability. The Library of the National Congress has also conducted extensive research on the subject. As of this writing, nine bills addressing “fake news and disinformation” are under parliamentary review (Weidenslaufer & Roberts, 2022, 2023a, 2023b).
Disinformation and Constitutional Referendums
Between 2019 and 2023, Chile underwent a constitutional process that included three constitutional referendums, during which a pronounced environment of information disorder emerged. The significance of constitutional referendum requires heightened vigilance against disinformation, not only due to the political weight of the decisions at stake but also because of the unique vulnerabilities of such referendums.
Certain structural characteristics make constitutional referendums particularly susceptible to propaganda and disinformation. By reducing a highly complex constitutional text to a binary choice—approval or rejection—these referendums simplify decision-making processes in ways that facilitate disinformation (Gargarella, 2023). Voters may strongly support certain provisions while opposing others, leading to situations in which aspects of the text provoke greater resistance than others. This dynamic allows for the strategic design of disinformation campaigns aimed at exploiting voter apprehensions. In this context, substantive debates over the proposal’s provisions are often overshadowed by electoral communication strategies that rely on misinformation (Chambers, 2001).
Constitutional referendums are rare—Chile had not held a national referendum for over 30 years prior to this process—and they require the electorate to engage with legal and political issues that, as a general rule, are unfamiliar to them. This contributes to electoral volatility, shifting the focus away from the substantive content of the proposed text and toward external influences, such as media campaigns and agenda-setting efforts. As a result, constitutional referendums may become referendums on the government in power or the constitutional body itself, creating fertile ground for disinformation (Gargarella, 2023; LeDuc, 2002).
The exit referendum, which determines whether a proposed constitution is approved or rejected, introduces an additional function that distinguishes the constitutional process from other electoral exercises. By granting voters the power to veto a constitutional proposal that fails to meet their expectations, the referendum incentivizes the drafting of a text that aligns with public demands (Verdugo, 2022). Political parties involved in Chile’s constitutional process saw the referendum as a mechanism for fostering consensus within the drafting body, thereby increasing the likelihood of producing a constitution that reflected the electorate’s political diversity (Tschorne, 2023). However, this moderating function can be undermined when media structures are controlled by actors with predefined agendas who leverage their influence to shape public discourse, as occurred in Chile (Santander et al., 2022; MACD, 2023). Under such conditions, the incentives to create and sustain an environment of information disorder are significantly magnified.
The Constitutional Process in Chile (2019–2023) and the Dissemination of Disinformation
The constitutional process that occurred between 2019 and 2023 was marked by significant political and social upheaval. During this period, Chile held one entrance referendum (October 25, 2020), in which citizens voted on whether to replace the existing constitution, and two exit referendums (September 4, 2022, and December 17, 2023) to approve or reject proposed new constitutions. These proposals aimed to replace the 1980 constitution, a document drafted and approved during the dictatorship of Augusto Pinochet. Both drafts were prepared by elected bodies created specifically for this purpose.
This extraordinary process was triggered by the social outburst ( estallido social ) of October 2019 (Jiménez-Yañez, 2020; Morales, 2020), a period of mass protests and civil unrest that lasted for months, nearly toppled the government of President Sebastián Piñera, and represented the most significant crisis for Chilean democracy since its restoration in 1990. In response, political parties with parliamentary representation devised an institutional solution to address the crisis: the initiation of a constitutional process.
Since the existing constitution contained no provisions for its total replacement, a major constitutional amendment was enacted in December 2019 to establish the framework for the constitutional process. This reform led to the initial referendum , in which voters were asked whether they supported drafting a new constitution. The result was decisive: 78.2% voted in favor.
Following this decision, a Constitutional Convention was established, comprising 155 members elected by popular vote and tasked with drafting a new constitution over the course of one year (July 2021 to July 2022). The final draft was submitted to an exit referendum , where voters decided whether to approve or reject the proposal. The outcome was clear: 61.9% rejected the draft, while only 38.1% approved it. This result not only nullified the Convention’s proposal but also left the 1980 constitution in force.
Although another constitutional process took place in 2023, which similarly resulted in the rejection of a proposed text, this study focuses on the first process. This initial process unfolded amid a particularly acute environment of information disorder. Evidence of this phenomenon includes journalistic investigations, academic studies, and hundreds of complaints filed before state agencies concerning the dissemination of disinformation, as will be examined in the following section.
The Importance of a Constitutional Referendum and the Gravity of Disinformation Spread
Constitutional referendums are exceptional electoral events that fundamentally differ from other voting processes. In such referendums, the electorate is entrusted with the final decision on establishing a new political order. This involves determining the future structure and guiding principles of governance, including the form of government (e.g., presidential or parliamentary), the form of the state (e.g., unitary or federal), the limits of power (through fundamental rights), and the objectives of state action (e.g., national security, public order, and the common good) (Holmes, 2012). Furthermore, redefining the foundational structures of a political system directly impacts the identity of the sovereign entity itself. As Tierney (2012) argues, such referendums do not merely establish a new political order but also reshape the identity of the people who collectively affirm it.
In Chile’s first constitutional process, the proposed text explicitly articulated key elements of national political identity. For instance, plurinationalism was presented as a defining characteristic of the Chilean people, a concept that directly influenced other proposals regarding the organization of state power, institutional structure, and fundamental rights (Charney & Nuñez, 2024).
Beyond their intrinsic constitutional significance, the referendums held in Chile requires an examination within their specific political context. These referendums emerged as an institutional response to a profound social and political crisis, were conducted within a relatively short timeframe, and addressed matters of constitutional significance. This combination rendered them events of exceptional political, legal, sociological, and communicational importance.
Compounding these factors was a phenomenon rarely seen in Chilean electoral history: an acute and widespread environment of information disorder. Unprecedentedly, political authorities—some directly involved in drafting the constitutional proposal—played a central role in creating, disseminating, and amplifying disinformation. The widespread circulation of false and misleading narratives during the campaigns for and against the new constitution was extensively documented and became a defining feature of the process (Mendoza et al., 2022; MACD, 2023).
The proliferation of disinformation through social networks and traditional media, often driven by political authorities—as will be illustrated in the case discussions below—compelled state agencies to take unprecedented institutional measures. During the first constitutional process in 2022, the Electoral Service ( Servicio Electoral , hereinafter “ Servel ”), an autonomous body of constitutional rank with legal personhood and independent assets responsible for administering, overseeing, and supervising electoral and referendum processes, received over 200 complaints concerning the dissemination of fake news. This number represented a quarter of all complaints submitted to the agency at the time.
The pervasive environment of information disorder during the referendum campaign prompted Servel to establish a specialized team tasked with “detecting and disproving publications with false information on social networks through constant monitoring of the digital conversation.” To support this effort, Servel launched a Repository of Fake News ,2 a public platform designed to refute misinformation identified by this newly created team. This initiative marked a significant step in institutional responses to the disinformation crisis.
Similarly, the National Television Council (2021) (hereinafter “NTVC”), another autonomous institution of constitutional rank whose mission, under Law 18.838, is “to watch over the correct functioning of all television services operating, or operating in the future in the national territory,” received—for the first time in its history—hundreds of citizen complaints regarding the broadcasting of disinformation on Chilean television. During the referendum campaign, the NTVC recorded 674 complaints from viewers against content aired by Chilean open television channels, relating to cases considered by complainants as disinformation within the context of the constitutional process.
The magnitude of the observed disinformation led the Chilean government to establish the MACD in 2023. At the legislative level, the National Congress is currently debating various regulatory proposals to address disinformation in electoral campaigns. However, at present, neither Servel , the NTVC, nor any other Chilean agency has the authority to control or sanction disinformation—unlike regulatory bodies in neighboring countries such as Brazil—. As a result, all complaints regarding electoral disinformation remain merely recorded rather than acted upon.
When Disinformation Comes from the Guardians of the Public Interest
While specialized literature and international organizations have long warned that electoral campaigns provide fertile ground for disinformation, its presence during Chile’s constitutional process was not entirely unexpected. What was striking, however, was the scale of the information disorder and, more critically, the fact that much of the disinformation originated from political authorities—including members of Parliament and even of the Constitutional Convention, individuals directly involved in drafting the constitution that was to be put to a plebiscite—.
The democratic stability of a nation and its institutions is particularly vulnerable when information disorder emerges within a constitutional process, especially when its source includes political authorities. These figures bear the highest responsibility for preventing such disruptions. As the Inter-American Court of Human Rights (2009) has stated. “State actors, consistent with their national and international legal obligations and their public duties, should endeavor to disseminate reliable and trustworthy information, including on matters of public interest, such as the economy, public health, security, and the environment”.

Moreover, the Court emphasizes that public officials, as guarantors of individuals’ fundamental rights, must ensure that their statements neither violate these rights nor constitute direct or indirect interference that hinders public deliberation. Sunstein (2021) similarly argues as follows: “Those who hold public office (...) enjoy freedom of expression, but it is not equivalent to that of private citizens or even the media. For them, lying is especially reprehensible, and the obligation to refrain from falsehoods can be regulated, particularly given the consequences false statements may entail”.

Given that, as previously discussed, the constitutional referendums were conceived as a political solution to address a severe social crisis, the dissemination of disinformation by political authorities becomes even more egregious. Such actions not only undermined the public trust necessary for a legitimate and inclusive constitutional process but also exacerbated the very instability the process was designed to resolve.
The Special Situation of Free Speech for Members of Deliberative Bodies.
In Chile, members of Congress are classified as public officials ( lato sensu), according to the broad definition provided in Article 260 of the Penal Code. Similarly, Article 23(7) of the General Regulations of the Constitutional Convention (published in the Official Gazette on October 13, 2021) stipulates that members of the Constitutional Convention must “comply with the obligations established in Law No. 20,880, on probity in public office and prevention of conflicts of interest, as applicable.” As public officials, their free speech is subject to certain constraints (Silva Major, 2024). Under Article 1, second paragraph of Law No. 20,880, the principle of probity in public service requires “impeccable official conduct, an honest and loyal performance of the function or position, with preeminence of the general interest over private interests.” Furthermore, the third paragraph of this article states that a conflict of interest arises “when the general interest inherent in the exercise of the functions coincides with a particular interest, whether economic or otherwise, of the person exercising those functions or of third parties linked to them, or when circumstances detract from impartiality in the exercise of their powers.”
International law also classifies members of deliberative bodies as public officials. The United Nations Convention Against Corruption defines a public official as including. “(i) any person holding a legislative, executive, administrative, or judicial office of a State Party, whether appointed or elected, permanent or temporary, paid or honorary, irrespective of that person’s seniority in office; (ii) any other person who performs a public function, including for a public agency or public enterprise, or provides a public service, as defined in the domestic law of the State Party (...)” (Art. 2).

The legal status of members of Parliament and of the Constitutional Convention presents a unique situation. As public officials, they serve as guarantors of fundamental rights, particularly of free speech, and their primary duty is to ensure that the norms they draft do not infringe upon these rights. However, members of Parliament also benefit from a privilege that exempts them from criminal liability for statements made in the exercise of their functions. Article 61 of the Chilean constitution (hereinafter “CPR”) establishes that members of Parliament cannot be prosecuted for opinions expressed during congressional sessions, even if such statements constitute offenses such as libel or slander.
This protection, known as parliamentary immunity , was extended to members of the Constitutional Convention through the constitutional reform that enabled the constitutional process (Art. 134 CPR). Recognized across contemporary constitutional systems (Bradley & Pinelli, 2012), parliamentary immunity originated during the rise of the modern state as a means to limit royal prerogatives (Bodin, 1962). Today, its primary justification lies in safeguarding the integrity of parliament against interference from other branches of government and ensuring the independence of its members (Rules of Procedure, European Parliament, Rule 5.2). Moreover, parliaments are regarded as fundamental spaces for political debate, and democratic systems generally require compelling justifications to restrict freedom of expression within these institutions ( A v. United Kingdom , 35,373/97 ECHR).
However, parliamentary immunity does not equate to impunity. Legislative bodies establish internal rules to regulate a member’s conduct and enforce accountability mechanisms. In the case of the Constitutional Convention, these rules were particularly rigorous. The Convention’s Ethics Regulations (hereinafter “ER”) explicitly identified disinformation as a violation of the duty of truthfulness, defining it as “the expression through any physical or digital means of a fact that is presented as real, knowing or having a duty to know that it is false” (Art. 24, ER). The regulations prohibited “disinformation in any space or social network, including chamber or committee sessions” (Art. 37(i), ER). To enforce these provisions, the ER established an Ethics Committee responsible for investigating, adjudicating, and sanctioning breaches, including those related to disinformation. This committee ruled on multiple complaints concerning Convention members found in violation of the prohibition against disinformation (MacClure, 2023).
Methodological Considerations: Selection Criteria and Case Analysis
As previously outlined, this research examines the role of incumbent political authorities—specifically members of Parliament and the Constitutional Convention—in fostering an environment of information disorder during the constitutional process and its corresponding electoral campaign. To this end, we selected representative cases involving such authorities that became subjects of public debate, as documented in national media and social networks.
Approximately 300 cases were analyzed in the selection process. Nearly 200 correspond to complaints filed with Servel (2022); 21 relate to complaints submitted to the Ethics Committee of the Constitutional Convention (MacClure, 2023); and 76 involve cases reported in media outlets such as BBC (Molina, 2022), CIPER (Herrera et al., 2022), and elDesconcierto (Cancino, 2023).
The selection process followed the principles of criteria-based selection (Goetz & LeCompte, 1988), a qualitative methodological approach that requires defining specific attributes for study units in advance. The key selection criteria were as follows:
- Variable of Context: Information disorder occurring within the electoral campaign period. For this study, the campaign period is defined as spanning from May 30, 2022, to September 4, 2022. The starting date corresponds to the release of the finalized and harmonized draft of the constitutional proposal, incorporating the norms approved by the Plenary of the Constitutional Convention (2022) and structured according to agreements adopted in the harmonization commission during session No. 11 on May 28, 2022.3 This draft served as the official text submitted for the referendum and formed the basis for public statements by political authorities. Prior to this, extensive discussions took place regarding the content of the proposal, making it difficult to determine whether statements about the draft could be classified as false or true. The endpoint marks the exit referendum held to approve or reject the text. Selected cases must fall within this time frame.

- Variable of Origin of Disinformation: Instances of information disorder disseminated by political authorities, specifically sitting members of Parliament or the Constitutional Convention.

- Variable of Consequences: Disinformation with significant public impact, evidenced either through widespread public debate or formal complaints filed with entities such as NTVC or Servel .

- Variable of Content: Information disorder addressing core aspects of the proposed constitutional project, including fundamental rights, the organization of the state, and constitutional identity, as reflected in the text submitted for referendum.



This purposive sampling approach, distinct from probability or random sampling, guided the identification and selection of three representative cases for detailed analysis. The cases submitted to Servel were excluded primarily because they involved instances of information disorder unrelated to the proposed constitutional text (e.g., misinformation about voter eligibility for the referendum). Similarly, cases presented to the Ethics Committee of the Constitutional Convention were excluded because they occurred before the final constitutional proposal was completed.
The vast majority of identified cases originated from political authorities opposing the proposal. While further research is needed, this pattern may be explained within the broader context of constitutional referendums, where opponents have strong incentives to discredit the proposal—sometimes through disinformation, misinformation, or malinformation—. Conversely, political authorities who supported the text had little motivation to undermine its content, instead focusing their criticism on the 1980 constitution. This dynamic explains why one of the few identified cases involved a statement by the President of the Convention, Elisa Loncón: "The constitution of 1980 has so many locks that it does not allow reform, no reform is possible"(Padilla, 2022).

This case was excluded from our analysis for two reasons: first, it does not pertain to the proposed new constitution; second, it does not meet the normative threshold of credibility or plausibility required to cause harm, as will be discussed below. Indeed, it is widely known that the 1980 constitution can be and has been reformed on multiple occasions.
The three selected cases highlight instances of information disorder involving highly sensitive and politically charged issues: abortion, indigenous rights, and property rights. These topics were widely identified as core reasons for rejecting the constitutional proposal (CADEM, 2022). For a significant portion of the electorate, these issues were deeply controversial, often invoking fear or rejection, and they dominated the media agenda at the time. As we will see, the first case exploited societal concerns about expanding legal grounds for abortion, framing the proposal in an exaggerated and misleading way. The second case amplified fears about the potential weakening of property rights, invoking narratives of unfair treatment favoring indigenous peoples. The third case distorted provisions related to indigenous justice systems, portraying them as threats to public security and legal consistency.
For the analytical and normative framework, we adopted the taxonomy proposed by Sunstein (2021), with modifications to incorporate instances of malinformation, as outlined in our complementary approach. Sunstein’s (2021) typology seeks to balance the need to sanction disinformation with the protection of free speech. It classifies information disorder—disinformation and misinformation—based on four criteria: the author’s intent, and the magnitude, likelihood, and imminence of harm associated with the falsehood’s dissemination. [See Table 1]. Each criterion is assessed on a scale that differentiates falsehoods by their severity:
- Most severe : Falsehoods knowingly disseminated by the author (lies) that are certain to cause serious and imminent harm.

- Least severe : Falsehoods that the author could reasonably have believed to be true and that cause no harm.

Between these extremes lie various combinations of the typology’s elements, allowing for nuanced categorization. To safeguard freedom speech, Sunstein (2021) advocates for restricting sanctions to the most severe cases—lies that meet the dual criteria of being knowingly false and causing serious and imminent harm—. If either criterion is not met, sanctions should not be applied to preserve open debate and the free exchange of ideas.
This typology provided the foundation for our analysis of the selected cases, ensuring a systematic approach to evaluating the seriousness of information disorder within the context of Chile’s constitutional process.
Criterion (1) corresponds to what the Anglo-American legal tradition refers to as mens rea or the mental element (Herring, 2022), while criteria (2), (3), and (4) fall under actus reus or the conduct element (Herring, 2022). The inclusion of both subjective and objective criteria helps mitigate judicial arbitrariness in determining which cases warrant punitive measures.
The concept of magnitude (of harm) has a quantitative dimension, whereas seriousness (of harm) has a qualitative dimension. In the context of information disorder, magnitude relates to the volume of data involved (e.g., limited, moderate, or extensive). By contrast, seriousness pertains to the nature of the data (e.g., highly relevant, moderately relevant, or of little relevance). Seriousness is also linked to the interests at stake, as will be discussed below. Meanwhile, an event is considered probable if it is highly likely to occur or to be true.
Furthermore, the last three criteria are directly tied to the harm to others principle (Feinberg, 1984), which serves as an essential condition for imposing sanctions. More specifically, punishment depends on the existence of a recognizable social harm (Brown, 2016), which in turn presupposes that the message in question is both plausible and credible. Consequently, statements that are universally or overwhelmingly recognized as false do not meet this requirement.
Former U.S. Supreme Court Justice Holmes’ well-known example—“falsely shouting fire in a theatre and causing a panic”—illustrates the application of criteria (2), (3), and (4).4 In this scenario, the harm is serious because it endangers a large number of people; it is certain because the likelihood of harm occurring is extremely high; and it is imminent because, upon hearing the message, a significant portion of the audience will attempt to flee, potentially triggering a stampede that results in injury or death.
In this example, the protected interests involve human life and physical integrity. By contrast, information disorders related to a constitutional vote threaten other fundamental interests, such as the integrity of the electoral process and the stability of the democratic system itself.
Sunstein’s (2021) typology refines the broad categories of disinformation and misinformation by reserving sanctions exclusively for the most severe cases. However, this framework does not address malinformation, as its criteria presuppose that all instances involve falsehoods. To bridge this conceptual gap and integrate disinformation, misinformation, and malinformation within Sunstein’s (2021) framework, an additional category must be incorporated—one that accounts for the content of the information. This criterion also integrates the actus reus or conduct element.
Unlike disinformation and misinformation—defined by the dissemination of falsehoods with and without intent to mislead, respectively—malinformation involves the use of factual content that is distorted or decontextualized to mislead its audience. Consistent with Sunstein’s (2021) guarantor principle , we limit judgments of reproachability to the most serious cases, specifically those involving intentional falsehoods (Zimmermann, 2025). From this perspective, cases of malinformation—where information is factually accurate but decontextualized or exaggerated—should not be subject to sanction. This approach ensures a balanced evaluation, preserving free speech while addressing the harms caused by deliberate information disorder.
For example, if a person claims that the 1980 constitution allows hate speech because it does not explicitly prohibit it, they may be distorting the constitutional text, but they are not necessarily making a false statement. Therefore, such a case should not be subject to sanction.
Although the normative framework applied in this study restricts reproachability to the most severe cases, any proposal aimed at combating disinformation must be carefully scrutinized and tolerated only when it aligns with human rights protections and is strictly necessary for the proper functioning of a democratic system. Otherwise, there is a significant risk of discouraging free speech ( chilling effect ), thereby undermining democratic dynamics. The international human rights system, as well as domestic constitutional frameworks, contain safeguards to prevent such overreach. Article 10 of the European Convention on Human Rights permits restrictions on free speech only when “necessary in a democratic society.” Similarly, Article 19 of the International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights and Article 13 of the American Convention on Human Rights allow for restrictions solely when required to ensure, among other things, “respect for the rights or reputation of others.”
Such normative guarantees ensure that any regulation of free speech remains consistent with a democratic state governed by the rule of law. Commonly associated with the principle of proportionality , they require a balance between restricting free speech and achieving legitimate aims (Webber, 2021). This principle demands that any restrictive measure must be suitable for achieving its objective, must be the least restrictive option available, and must strike a reasonable balance between its purpose and the extent to which it limits expression.
A crucial element of proportionality is the severity of the sanction imposed—in this case, penalties for disinformation by political authorities during constitutional electoral processes. In our view, such cases should not be punished with prison sentences, which should be reserved for serious crimes. Instead, sanctions should take the form of fines or symbolic penalties, such as mandatory public corrections (Baade, 2019). These conclusions align with the principle of ultima ratio , which holds that criminal sanctions, particularly those involving deprivation of liberty, should only be applied when no other adequate and less restrictive measures are available (de la Torre García, 2023). By focusing punitive measures on the most severe cases, other mechanisms—such as improving voter education, enhancing media literacy, and increasing accountability for platforms that amplify false messages—can be implemented for less serious cases.
Finally, any sanctioning system designed to counter disinformation in constitutional electoral processes must be administered by an independent and impartial entity. This is essential to prevent political authorities from exploiting these mechanisms to target their opponents. In principle, Courts should be responsible for adjudicating these matters. Moreover, to prevent other relevant actors from engaging in strategic lawsuits against public participation (hereinafter “SLAPPs”), comparative legal frameworks offer useful mechanisms. For example, in the United Kingdom, the Economic Crime and Corporate Transparency Act of 2023 provides that: “A claim may be struck out before trial where the court determines (a) that the claim is a SLAPP claim.”

According to Sect. 195, a claim qualifies as a SLAPP claim if, among other criteria: “(a) the claimant's behavior in relation to the matters complained of in the claim has, or is intended to have, the effect of restraining the defendant's exercise of the right to freedom of speech.”

Section 194 further establishes that a SLAPP claim exempts the defendant from court costs. Mechanisms of this type have gained prominence in comparative law (Coe et al., 2025) and serve as effective tools to limit the strategic use of litigation to suppress disinformation in constitutional processes.
Cases and Origin of the Disinformation
First case: In June 2022, a member of Parliament stated as follows in a campaign radio advertisement regarding the proposed constitution: “The Constitutional Convention allows abortion up to nine months of pregnancy. This not only violates the human rights of the unborn child (…) we would be the only country that promotes and allows abortion up to nine months of pregnancy. This does not protect women, because in fact what mother would want to have an abortion at nine months. So it is really a lack of respect for women.” (El Mostrador, 2022).
Second case : In July 2022, a pamphlet titled “Practical Guide to the Lies of the Constitution of the Convention,” signed by incumbent Convention members was distributed across several cities. The pamphlet included statements such as: “They promise property rights, but under a regime of expropriation of lands to give them to the indigenous people, without the need to justify public utility and paying you whatever the politicians want.” (Alday, 2022).
Third Case : On August 24, 2022, following the arrest of indigenous ( Mapuche ) leader and Arauco Malleco Coordination’s (hereinafter “CAM”) spokesperson Héctor Llaitul, a member of Parliament remarked as follows: “If Mr. Llaitul had been arrested with the new text proposed by the constitutional, he would have been tried by the Temucuicui justice system and probably released within minutes.” (Araya & Pérez, 2022).
Case Analysis: Characterizing Cases and Possible Sanctions (Court determined Fines or Duty to Rectify)
Sunstein’s taxonomy (2021) posits that it is often impossible to determine whether an individual disseminating information was aware of its falsity. However, this premise does not fully apply to the present analysis, particularly concerning members of the Constitutional Convention. These actors actively participated in the deliberation and drafting of the proposed constitution during a period of intense political debate and profound historical significance. Given their direct involvement, any subsequent statements—whether in advertising campaigns, promotional materials, or public declarations—that openly and blatantly contradicted the content of the proposed constitution may reasonably be presumed to involve malice.
Accordingly, the selection of cases focused on instances with the potential to constitute significant breaches. When prominent political figures, wielding substantial influence over public discourse and media narratives, disseminate disinformation on critical issues during an electoral campaign to approve or reject a new constitution, it becomes plausible to conclude that serious harm to the integrity of public debate is likely to ensue. However, uncertainties remain regarding the extent to which disinformation influenced the election outcome. This issue lies beyond the scope of this study and requires further investigation to establish any causal relationship.
First Case: “The constitution Allows Abortion Up to Nine Months”
This case, involving a sitting member of Parliament, exemplifies the concept of malinformation . While the member of Parliament did not explicitly state falsehood, their framing of the proposed constitution was so distorted that it had the potential to mislead the electorate. Article 61.2 of the proposed text guaranteed the right to voluntary termination of pregnancy but deferred its regulation to subsequent legislation. Although it is theoretically possible that such legislation could permit abortion up to nine months, this scenario is entirely implausible. As the member of Parliament himself acknowledged, no country in the world allows abortion up to nine months through legislative authorization.
The implausibility of this claim is further underscored by the historical evolution of the abortion debate in Chile. After more than a decade of parliamentary deliberation, a limited framework for the decriminalization of abortion was enacted in 2017. This legal framework introduced a system of indications , whereby the permissibility of abortion depended on the verification of strictly defined conditions, independent of the pregnant woman’s sole discretion. Specifically, the three permissible circumstances—known in comparative law as therapeutic indication (risk to the woman’s life or health), embryopathic indication (fetal inviability or severe malformations), and ethical-social , criminological , or humanitarian indication (pregnancy resulting from an illicit act)—were narrowly defined and rigorously regulated (Hernández, 2016). The introduction of these provisions followed years of legislative attempts and robust societal debate, underscoring the entrenched resistance to more permissive approaches.
Consequently, the absence of explicit time limits in the constitutional proposal on pregnancy termination cannot reasonably be interpreted as “allowing abortion up to nine months.” Approval of the constitutional proposal would not have automatically sanctioned such a measure. Achieving such a radical shift would have required a profound transformation of societal moral and political values—an outcome far removed from the prevailing context—.
Although the member of Parliament’ statements were biased in addressing a highly sensitive issue and thus contributed to information disorder, they do not constitute falsehoods. Interpretations as misleading as this one, even when their severity risks distorting public opinion, should be countered through rigorous public debate that exposes their flaws and inconsistencies. Legal sanctions should be reserved for the gravest infractions, as applying them too broadly risks undermining both the integrity of public discourse and the principle of free speech.
Second Case: “Land Will Be Expropriated and Given to the Indigenous People”
This case is a textbook example of disinformation perpetrated by Constitutional Convention members during the electoral campaign. Despite their direct involvement in drafting the constitutional proposal, these individuals disseminated blatant falsehoods regarding the regulation of property rights and the expropriation framework—some of which directly contradicted explicit provisions of the proposed text—. They claimed that the proposal established a “regime of expropriation of lands to give them to the indigenous people without the need to justify public utility and paying you whatever the politicians want.”
The first falsehood concerned the alleged absence of a public utility requirement for expropriation. This claim is unequivocally refuted by Article 78.3 of the proposed text, which clearly stipulated that “no person may be deprived of his property, except by virtue of a law authorizing expropriation for public utility or general interest declared by the legislator.” Furthermore, the expropriation regime outlined in the proposal mirrored the general framework established in the 1980 constitution. Under both regimes, expropriation could only be authorized by law for reasons of public utility or general interest, with compensation based on “the fair price of the expropriated property” (Art. 78.4). Thus, the assertion that the new regime was specifically designed “to give it to the indigenous people” is demonstrably false.
While the proposal did recognize the restitution of indigenous lands as a preferred mechanism of reparation (Art. 79.3), its implementation depended on negotiated agreements between the State and indigenous peoples, facilitated by the Indigenous Territorial Commission, as outlined in the twenty-eighth transitory provision. Only after such agreements could legislators consider expropriation for restitution purposes. However, such actions were already permissible under the existing constitutional framework, making the claims put forth by Convention members wholly misleading.
Another falsehood asserted that compensation for expropriation would be determined arbitrarily by politicians. In reality, the proposal explicitly guaranteed the right to “the fair price of the expropriated property” and provided affected parties with judicial recourse to challenge both the legality of the expropriation and the determination of compensation (Art. 78.5). Therefore, compensation was subject to judicial review rather than political discretion.
This case represents one of the most egregious instances of disinformation, aligning with the highest intensity categories of Sunstein’s taxonomy (2021). The falsehoods were presented in written form, directly contradicting the constitutional proposal’s provisions—provisions that the authors themselves had helped craft—. It is therefore indisputable that they were aware of the falsehoods, making these deliberate lies intended to mislead the electorate.
Moreover, these statements targeted some of the most sensitive and politically charged topics of the constitutional debate—indigenous rights, property rights, and expropriation—. By distilling the electorate’s deepest fears into deceptive narratives, these falsehoods posed a significant and immediate threat to the democratic process. Unlike extreme interpretations, which can be addressed through public debate, outright lies of this nature undermine informed deliberation and democratic legitimacy. According to Sunstein’s taxonomy (2021), such disinformation requires legal intervention.
Third Case: Privileged Judicial Treatment for Indigenous Peoples
This case aligns with the concept of malinformation , assuming the member of Parliament employed a half-truth . While not directly stating a falsehood, the member of Parliament presented information in a manner that misleads through omission or exaggeration. The proposed new constitution, like the existing one, included provisions for detention as a precautionary measure in cases of flagrante delicto or through court order (Art. 110.2). The primary difference lay in the detention period: the proposal limited it to 24 h, whereas the current constitution permits detention up to 48 h, extendable up to 10 days for terrorist acts, such as those attributed to CAM’s leader Héctor Llaitul. In this context, the member of Parliament’ remarks could be seen as hyperbolic, emphasizing the difference in detention limits between the two frameworks.
Another interpretation of the member of Parliament’ statement suggests an insinuation that indigenous individuals committing crimes would receive preferential treatment compared to non-indigenous individuals. However, such an implication is unsustainable under the provisions of the proposed constitution. The proposal stated that “The jurisdictional function is defined in its structure, integration and procedures according to the principles of plurinationality, legal pluralism and interculturality” (Art. 322.1) (Charney & Nuñez, 2024). However, this provision does not establish privileged treatment for indigenous individuals facing criminal prosecution.
The proposal also recognized indigenous legal systems: “The State recognizes the legal systems of indigenous peoples and nations, which, by virtue of their right to self-determination, coexist on an equal footing with the National Justice System. These shall respect the fundamental rights established by this constitution and the international treaties and instruments on human rights to which Chile is a party” (Art. 309.1).

This provision must be read alongside two others: the principle of jurisdictional unity , which states: “The courts of justice are structured according to the principle of jurisdictional unity as the basis of their organization and operation and are subject to the same legal status and the same principles” (Art. 308).

And, also the rule that considers the role of the Supreme Court as the ultimate authority to ensure the correct application of the law and to unify its interpretation (Art. 328.1). Therefore, if a Mapuche individual committed a crime, the Supreme Court would retain the authority to adjudicate the matter. Moreover, indigenous justice was explicitly required to respect human rights, including the enforcement of sanctions for serious offenses impacting non-indigenous individuals, such as terrorism. Crucially, the constitutional proposal did not mandate that criminal cases be resolved exclusively by indigenous legal systems; this decision would remain at the discretion of Parliament. It is implausible to assume that the Chilean Parliament would establish a legal system that privileges indigenous individuals who commit crimes.
The member of Parliament’ “ half-truth ” hinges on the phrasing of his statement. He declared, “If Mr. Llaitul had been arrested with the new text proposed by the constitutional, he would have been judged by the justice of Temucuicui and probably released in minutes.” The conditional phrasing— "would have" —does not constitute a definitive assertion that can be classified as true or false. However, the statement’s underlying message insinuates privileged treatment for Mapuche individuals, a premise neither explicitly nor implicitly supported by the constitutional proposal.
According to Sunstein’s taxonomy (2021), despite its gravity, the member of Parliament’ statement does not meet the criteria for legal sanctions. While contributing to information disorder, it does not constitute a direct falsehood but rather a tendentious interpretation of the proposal. As with Case 1 , such instances should not be addressed through legal means but rather through alternative mechanisms that promote informed public discourse.
Final Remarks and Proposals
In the context of an electoral campaign, the creation of an atmosphere of information disorder—such as that one that occurred during Chile’s constitutional process—has complex origins and significant consequences. The global disinformation crisis is often attributed to the role of social networks and their algorithms, as was the case in the Colombian referendum. However, this study focuses on a less-explored dimension: the disinformation generated by key political authorities and their role in fostering an environment of information disorder during a constitutional referendum campaign.
Understanding the origins of disinformation and assessing the information disorder constructed in such a scenario requires attention to the specific responsibilities of political authorities. This includes sitting member of Parliament, who legislate on critical issues such as free speech and disinformation, as well as members of the Constitutional Convention, who actively draft constitutional proposals yet disseminate falsehoods about the very process in which they participate. These roles warrant heightened scrutiny, as they raise pressing legal, political, and investigative concerns.
Constitutional processes are inherently unique, involving the drafting and approval of a new constitution—a document that establishes the foundational rules of a political system—. In this context, political authorities are expected to act as facilitators of accurate and reliable information. Instead, as demonstrated in several cases, certain authorities engaged in disinformation or malinformation, undermining the electorate’s capacity to make informed decisions.
While political authorities, like all citizens, are entitled to free speech—a right that any democratic system must protect vigorously—this freedom is not without limits. Sunstein’s taxonomy (2021) provides a useful framework for determining when restrictions are appropriate, based on four criteria: (1) the mental disposition of the perpetrator, (2) the magnitude of harm, (3) the probability of harm, and (4) the timing of harm. We propose an additional criterion: (5) the accuracy of the information, which encompasses falsehoods, misrepresentations, half-truths, or factual statements taken out of context. This addition broadens the analysis of information disorder by incorporating cases of malinformation, thereby enhancing the understanding of how such behaviors contribute to public misinformation.
On this basis, we conclude that a political system committed to free speech must also recognize the obligations of political authorities in a constitutional process. Authorities who, during an electoral campaign, deliberately disseminate false messages that are certain to cause serious and immediate harm to the accuracy of information available to voters should face sanctions. Conversely, cases that do not meet these stringent criteria must remain unpunished to preserve the space for political expression and debate.
To safeguard free speech, we argue that sanctions for the dissemination of disinformation should be limited to fines and mandatory corrections, and should never result in imprisonment. Furthermore, the body responsible for imposing sanctions must be independent and impartial. To prevent strategic lawsuits against public participation (SLAPPs), we recommend adopting mechanisms developed in comparative law to minimize their impact on free speech. Specifically, granting courts the power to dismiss frivolous lawsuits preemptively and imposing litigation costs on those who engage in strategic legal actions could serve as effective preventive measures.
Applying this framework, we examined three cases widely disseminated through social networks and traditional media, in which political authorities publicly and conspicuously contributed to information disorder during Chile’s constitutional campaign. Our analysis determined that only one case met the criteria for sanctions (fines or mandatory corrections). The other two cases, while problematic, fell within the realm of political opinion. Although misleading, they did not constitute outright falsehoods and should therefore remain outside the scope of punitive measures. For these cases, measures such as media literacy, voter education, and the promotion of counter-speech provide more effective responses.
