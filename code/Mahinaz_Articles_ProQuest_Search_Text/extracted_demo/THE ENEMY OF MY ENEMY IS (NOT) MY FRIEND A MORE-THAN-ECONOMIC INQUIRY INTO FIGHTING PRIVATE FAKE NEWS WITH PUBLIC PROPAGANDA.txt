Abstract
The rapid proliferation of private fake news has revived calls for stronger public intervention in the governance of information. Yet the institutional response often raises a paradox of its own: can decentralized falsehood legitimately be countered through centralized truth management without reproducing the very pathologies it seeks to eliminate? This scientific essay addresses that dilemma by examining the economic, ethical, and epistemological foundations of the relationship between private fake news and public propaganda. Rather than treating them as simple opposites, it argues that they represent competing institutional responses to the persistent problem of epistemic uncertainty. Building upon contributions from Austrian Economics, Information Economics, Public Choice, Institutional Economics, and Behavioural Economics, the paper develops an "Economics+" framework for observing information disorders. It further reviews a series of internationally recognized empirical indicators and databases capable of approximating the institutional environments within which truth and falsehood, as well as unreliable plots and official narratives compete for public legitimacy. The analysis suggests that economics cannot determine what truth is, but it can illuminate the knowledge constraints, informational asymmetries, incentive structures, institutional arrangements, and behavioural mechanisms, which shape its production and consumption. The conclusion is that combating falsehood should not imply manufacturing official verity, open societies being advised to preserve institutional arrangements capable to correct error without monopolizing speech, thence avoiding both the "informational anarchy" (of freely floating falsities) and the "epistemic bastille" (of authoritarian ministries of truth).
Keywords: fake news, public propaganda, truth management, epistemic governance, Information Economics, Austrian Economics, Public Choice Theory, media pluralism, informational resilience, fact-checking.

Introduction
"Truth or dare... to (re)search for the truth"
Truth has always had quite of an image problem. Everyone invokes it, few truly seek it, and even fewer are willing to bear the full consequences of its discovery, even up to the payment of the ultimate price - life -, for instance, as Plato's (ca. 399 BCE) Socrates did it by daring to link the truth to mind rather than to mobs, or as Galileo (1632) risked it when he democratized the (very laws of the) universe, severing it from entrenched geo(-God-)centric dogmas. By contrast, error, illusion, fraud, lies, manipulation, or propaganda have always enjoyed a not inconsiderable competitive advantage: they are often cheaper, more attractive, more comfortable, and more profitable. Far from representing an exclusively contemporary pathology, the conflict between truth and its substitutes runs through the entire human history, while, in economics parlance, producing, distributing and consuming the good/bad called (")truth(") have been since always a matter of culture (maybe more than nature) and of institutions (maybe more than technology), where decentralized (free) markets cohabit and collide with centralized (fierce) hierarchies.
From founding myths and sacralised catechisms to ideological propaganda, strategic public relations, and algorithms personalizing beliefs, societies have had to constantly deal with the tension between what is, what is believed to be, and what one wants to be perceived as being. Nietzsche (1887) once decried the humans' "slave morality" that ends up poisoning democratic and Christian landmarks (their values, virtues, verities), Freud (1921) disclosed the impulsive resignation of critical faculties by massified individuals to various lords and Lords, while Saramago's (1995) "blindness pandemic" reads as disinformation breaking empathy, and governmental isolation institutionalizing barbarity. Brought to the present, the phenomenology of fake news proliferation and the growing concern for combating it (at any cost?!) justify not only a canonical SWOT (Strengths, Weaknesses, Opportunities, Threats) analysis, but, metaphorically speaking, even a SWAT-type intervention. However, before any kind of intervention, a less spectacular, but more sober question is in need to be asked: who establishes the nature of the info-threat and who authorizes its neutralization since the cure and the disease seem, sometimes, disturbingly indistinctive?
To answer, even partially, such a query - one that, in a sense, is much more than a simple research question, featuring as a societal-existential conundrum, resonant, inter alia, to Hobbes's (1651) vs. Kant's (1795) (dis)trust in human kind(ness) and reason(ability) -, we propose two "idealized benchmarks" against which the frailties of the "real world" - among which those of news generation and of truth seeking - can be evaluated satisfactorily. The first is represented by the informed, cultivated and epistemically prudent individual/agent, evolving from data hunter-gatherer to a knowledgeand, eventually, a wisdom-artisan. The second is represented by the open society/environment, characterized by the freedom, openness and sincerity at the level of public communication, in which the competition of ideas prevails over the monopoly of narratives. However, reality definitely remains far from both ideals: on the one hand, individuals are troubled by ignorance, biases, emotions, interests; on the other hand, institutional and informational environments are vulnerable (even if unevenly) to capture, manipulation, propaganda and power concentration.
In fact, both private fake news and public propaganda can be interpreted as different manifestations of the same fundamental difficulty: the imperfect management of truth in a world of imperfect knowledge. And this is not (breaking) news. For instance, Hayek (1945) had warned against (socialist systems') central planning, praising (free market) prices for their "coordinative power" over extremely dispersed personal and proprietary bits of information about preference scales and resource scarcity estimates. Then Kuhn (1962) pointed to the perils of too rigid and routinely problem-solving, within obsolete, outdated scientific "paradigms" (by which he understood the epoch-dependent, even if deemed epochal in their golden ages, models of explaining or predicting reality). As for Taleb (2007, 2012; 2018), he drew attention to the epistemic arrogance (the overestimation of the known and the underestimation of the uncertainty), a shortcoming to be treated with via negativa approaches (fighting the wrong before embracing the good), by devising antifragile (shockconverting) setups, while adding, if possible, advisors'/experts' skin-in-the-game.
Various streams of literature - in social sciences, broadly speaking - have examined both the mechanisms of production and dissemination of disinformation and the institutional instruments designed to combat it. Only if we recount two "classics" of the field, we observe that what for many seems a present-day digital disease is in fact an ancient or at least anticipable outcome. Le Bon's Psychologie des foules (1895), written long before the advent of the internet, is a masterpiece that explains how - bottom-up - disinformation is born, goes viral and gets certified by contagion, suggestibility and anonymity, while Lasswell's Propaganda Technique in the World War (1927) shows how belligerent governments used the monopoly on communication channels (press, radio, posters) to inject simplified messages, stimulate hatred towards the enemy and mobilize the population - top-down. Much less attention has been paid, still, to the paradox that arises when the remedy borrows features of the disease, while the battle against private falsehood risks legitimizing increasingly sophisticated forms of public management of truth.
It is precisely this "grey area", located at the intersection of economics, ethics and the epistemology of information, that constitutes the main space investigated by this essay. Its originality does not lie in proposing a new technique for measuring disinformation, nor in a normative plea favouring one of the conflicting camps, but in stressing out and scrutinizing an otherwise persistent dilemma of modern societies: whether the enemy of truth is lies, can the enemy of lies become an unconditional friend of truth? To examine this question, the analysis proceeds as follows: first, we clarify the defining aspects of the opposition between fake news and propaganda; second, we recollect a series of historical experiences germane to the institutional failure in the search for and administration of truth; third, we mobilize conceptual tools derived mainly from economics and related disciplines; fourth, we identify empirical indicators useful for observing contemporary information conflicts; all this undertaken in order to conclude the inroad with a dialectical rumination on the limits of both information anarchy and epistemic monopoly.
1. Definitional issues in the media domain: private fake news vs. public propaganda
Whether originating in academic (research, educational) and cultural (ideological, religious), corporate or civic, governmental/establishment versus political opposition settings, informational contents - including fake news and propaganda, as competing pathologies of public communication - scarcely exert significant influence in the absence of institutional mechanisms capable of disseminating, amplifying, legitimizing, or contesting them. In this respect, mass media - broadly understood, from traditional press and broadcasting organizations to contemporary digital and social platforms - occupies a privileged position as the archetypal intermediary between producers and consumers of information. Media institutions constitute the principal channels through which, alongside certified information, contaminated stories strive to circulate, mutate, and occasionally manage to get normalized.
1.1. Notes on the ideology of mass media (or how some put pressure on the press)
Back in the 1950s, three American communication scholars - Fred S. Siebert, Theodore Peterson, and Wilbur Schramm (1956) - identified four "theories of the press". In reality, these are better understood as four enduring perspectives on the media: perspectives that have remained surprisingly contemporary, continue to coexist across the world (even within the same country), and gain prominence depending on who is in charge with switching, in "print houses", the "lights of the da(il)y" on and off.
The first perspective is the authoritarian one. It derives from the statist fetishism. Mass communication, being essential for the dissemination of centralized wisdom, must be protected from heresies. Structures of social control require propaganda and instruction on the supply side, as well as conformity and docility on the demand side. The journalist is entrusted with an almost martial mission, acting as a combat arm against mentally subversive elements. The press is subordinated either through ownership arrangements (public property) or through a combination of "sticks"/censorship and "carrots"/subsidies.
The second perspective is the libertarian one. Its roots can be traced back to post-Magna Carta England, later flourishing even more vigorously in the American New World. Freedom of expression is seen as both sustaining the search for truth and facilitating its discovery. Pluralistic and competitive, the press cultivates common sense and acts as a custodian of societal checks and balances within the classical Montesquieuian triad of executive, legislative, and judicial powers. Somewhat idealistic and idyllic, this is the press typically tied to liberal democracy, market economy, capitalism, and laissez-faire.
The third perspective is the communist interpretation. It may appear obsolete, yet its persistence in the world's second most populous country (People's Republic of China) alone is sufficient to keep it relevant. Here, criticism is acceptable solely when directed at failures in implementing commitments, never at the commitments themselves. The role of the press is not to provide transparency regarding mundane realities, but to offer examples and guidance in the realization of the grand project of the New Man. The ethical standard for journalists is nothing short of blunt survivalism: "better bend than break".
The fourth perspective is the doctrine of social responsibility. Often presented as progressive, politically correct, and occasionally even prudish, it shifts from "freedom of" to "fairness in" expression. It is not the media consumer who disciplines the press via market mechanisms, but the citizen. Particular attention is devoted to condemning linguistic offenses, inappropriate attitudes, problematic representations, or asymmetric visibility granted to certain categories of messages (commercial ones included). This angle is usually associated to activist, egalitarian, inclusive, and environmentally-friendly narratives.
More than half a century after their formulation, these four perspectives remain less a matter of history than of contemporary institutional choice. They continue to shape the ways in which societies understand the relationship between truth, power, public opinion, and media legitimacy. Most importantly, they remind us that disputes concerning fake news and propaganda are inseparable from broader disagreements regarding who should be entitled to speak, who should be allowed to decide what counts as truth, and who should ultimately supervise the supervisors of public discourse.
1.2. Notes on the socio/techno/logy of a switch - from mass media to social media
An insightful observation published some years ago by The Economist (Jora, 2020a; 2020b) suggested that news was becoming social again, much as they had been before the rise of mass, industrial journalism in the nineteenth century - only vastly more so. The remark remains remarkably relevant. What we are witnessing today is not simply a technological rupture, but also as a sociological return to a much older mode of information circulation, one based on social interaction rather than on professionalized institutions controlling a massproduction process of news. Information has remained massified, although communication itself has become increasingly socialized.
Prior to the nineteenth century, no technology existed that could disseminate information rapidly and simultaneously to large audiences. Conversations in taverns, personal correspondence, pamphlets, sermons, and local networks functioned as the newspapers, radio stations, television channels, and internet platforms of their age. Even after the invention of the printing press facilitated the reproduction of written texts, distribution continued to rely heavily on interpersonal connections. Reformers such as Martin Luther or revolutionaries such as Thomas Paine operated within informational environments where producers and consumers of ideas were often indistinguishable. In this sense, media were inherently social long before the unfolding of social media.
The industrialization of communication fundamentally altered this pattern. The steampowered press, mass newspapers, radio broadcasting, and television transformed news production into the domain of professional organizations capable of distributing information vertically, from specialized elites to mass audiences. As communication scholar Walter Lippmann (1922) observed almost a century ago, modern citizens increasingly came to know the world through mediated representations rather than direct experience. Throughout much of the twentieth century, media institutions acted as gatekeepers, editors, and verifiers of public reality, concentrating significant informational power in the hands of journalists, publishers, broadcasters, and media groups.
The internet has profoundly disrupted this industrial model. Blogs, social networks, videosharing platforms, instant messaging applications, and citizen journalism have reopened the gates of information production to virtually anyone with a digital connection. As argued by Castells (2009), communication increasingly unfolds within networked structures characterized by decentralized participation and continuous interaction. Yet this apparent democratization has not eliminated intermediaries; it has merely replaced traditional editorial filters with algorithmic ones. Search engines, recommendation systems, social platforms, and, more recently, generative artificial intelligence increasingly determine what information becomes visible, credible, viral, or forgotten.
This transformation carries profound implications for both fake news and propaganda. On the one hand, digital technologies have dramatically lowered barriers to entry, facilitating unprecedented informational diversity and participation. On the other hand, they have also accelerated the dissemination of misinformation, coordinated influence operations, botgenerated content, deepfakes, and synthetic media. As noted by Wardle and Derakhshan (2017), while the decline of traditional gatekeepers has weakened certain forms of centralized informational control, it has simultaneously created new opportunities for both decentralized falsehoods and seasoned forms of narrative management. The battlefield has changed, but the struggle over truth remains oddly familiar.
1.3. Notes (bey)on(d) phraseology: being "all ears" on fake news and propaganda
The notion of fake news remains contested despite its widespread usage. For Allcott and Gentzkow (2017), fake news consists of intentionally and verifiably false news articles capable of misleading readers, while Wardle and Derakhshan (2017) prefer the broader framework of information disorder, distinguishing among misinformation, disinformation, and malinformation according to intentionality and harm. The role of informational cascades, echo chambers, and reputational shortcuts in facilitating the diffusion of falsehoods is emphasized by Sunstein (2018), McIntyre (2018) associates fake news with the broader phenomenon of "post-truth" politics, and Jankowicz (2020) highlights its strategic usage within contemporary information warfare. Although differing in emphasis, these approaches converge on several elements: factual inaccuracy; deceptive or manipulative intent; rapid dissemination; and the capacity to distort individual or collective judgement. For the purposes of this essay, fake news will be understood as intentionally or negligently misleading informational content disseminated mainly through competitive and decentralized channels in pursuit of political, ideological, reputational, or economic gains.
The concept of propaganda is considerably older and arguably more complex. For Lasswell (1927), propaganda consists in the management of collective attitudes through the manipulation of significant symbols, whilst Bernays (1928) famously described it as the conscious and intelligent manipulation of organized habits and opinions. It is Ellul (1973) who viewed propaganda as a systemic feature of technological mass society rather than merely an instrument of authoritarian regimes, with Arendt (1967) linking propaganda to the political management of factual reality. Uncomfortably for mainstream stances, Chomsky and Herman (1988) demonstrated how propaganda may also operate within formally democratic settings via mechanisms of consent manufacturing. Taken together, these perspectives suggest that propaganda employ mixtures of truths, half-truths, omissions, emotional framing, selective visibility, symbolic narratives, or strategic silences. For the purposes of this essay, propaganda is seen as the systematic and institutionally supported management of information, symbols, and narratives intended to shape perceptions and behaviours in accordance with specific "establishment" objectives.
Although both phenomena seek to influence beliefs and behaviour, they differ substantially in their institutional ecology. Private fake news tends to be disordered, competitive, decentralized, and polycentric, emerging from multiple actors pursuing heterogeneous political, ideological, reputational, or economic objectives. Public propaganda, by contrast, tends to be coherent, institutionalized, resource-backed, and potentially monopolizing, benefiting from organizational continuity, privileged access to communication channels, and, at times, the authority of the state itself. One thrives through informational fragmentation; the other through informational concentration.
The resulting dilemma may therefore be translated (economically and institutionally) as a (deeply perverted) confrontation between the "free market" of ignorance, error, and fraud and the official, legal, and occasionally coercive monopoly (or "central planning") of truth.
2. Histories and hysterias of institutional failure in truth-seeking and truth-speaking
Truth may be approached in two fundamental ways: philosophically and socially. Philosophical theories of truth traditionally revolve around correspondence, coherence, and utility. As established in the literature, correspondence theory links truth to factual reality, coherence theory to consistency within a system of beliefs, while pragmatism associates it with practical effectiveness (Capps, 2019). The question of social truth, at its turn, becomes inseparable from that of the source of its legitimacy, which may be either individual or collective. Yet both sources raise difficulties: individual truth can hardly be separated from opinion, illusion, or falsity, while collective truth remains vulnerable to influence, diversity, and bias. The history of truth-seeking and truth-speaking may therefore be read as a continuous struggle between rivalrous sources of epistemic legitimacy (or to say authority?).
2.1. My truth - your truth - our truth - their truth
This distinction between individual and collective truths has even brought about different concepts for truth in logic, as individual truth is called certitude, while the term "truth" is allotted to collective and objectively verifiable truth. As it is, verification seems to be a serious option in favour of objective or collective truth, and the same goes for its opposite, falsifiability, both of them being, at conceptual and social levels, fundamentally collective.
Popper argued that scientific knowledge is never a collection of verified facts, but a set of provisional theories open to critical scrutiny and potential refutation, with falsifiability operating as a collective methodological standard rather than an individual one (Veronesi, 2014). Besides, after Wittgenstein's rejection of "private language" - which demonstrates that the criteria for correct language use must be public and shared within a community, thence making individual private meaning impossible (Tully, 2014) - and despite Chomsky's argument that human beings possess an innate universal grammar - a biological endowment that pre-structures language acquisition a priori (Al-Ahdal, 2021) -, as well as Fodor's related claim that thought itself operates in a structured mental language called "mentalese" - a system of inner symbolic representations with its own syntax and compositionality (Rescorla, 2023) -, it is hard to be a partisan of individual truth (in social matters).
A relevant side of the quarrel between individual and social types of truth is related to holistic - or context-dependent, or interconnectedly - and atomistic - or constituent-based, or indivisibly - attitudes towards truth. For the objectives of the present paper, it suffices to say that, in scientific and philosophical realms, holism has allegedly won; when it comes to religion, ethics, politics, and social issues in general, things seem to be quite different. For instance, persuasion does not necessarily depend on numbers - in ethics, this particular principle lies at the core of the role-model concept. From a moral point of view, a single individual can be right, recognized as such, and subsequently followed by many others who alter their perspectives and conduct in accordance with that role model.
Consequently, within a social subsystem, truths may be shaped by a few individuals, while the vast majority holds little to no influence over them. Following Kuhn's framework, experts are accepted primarily in the case of "puzzle-solving" tasks - which possess technical solutions akin to chess games -, whereas social issues admit no genuine experts; they are non-technical, relying instead upon interests, biases, and decision-making systems that readily morph into systems of social truth. Indeed, Kuhn demonstrated that the notion of scientific truth, at any given moment, cannot be established exclusively by objective criteria; rather, it is defined by the consensus of a scientific community, meaning that what counts as knowledge represents always, in part, the product of collective human judgement (Kuhn, 1962, as pointed in Kaiser, 2016).
Therefore, unlike science and philosophy, society possesses mechanisms that render holism far more powerful than atomism. If in science one may accept, following Kuhn, the idea of a paradigm (meaning a scientific achievement that functions as an exemplar of accurate research), which could be interpreted in favour of atomism, in social issues an atomistic standard still requires further societal validation through mechanisms such as voting. The tension between atomistic and holistic approaches to knowledge reflects a broader ideological divide with significant implications for how truth is collectively constructed and legitimated (Birhane et al., 2022).
As a brief intermediary conclusion, social truth requires a system of legitimation driven by both rational and non-rational factors, whereas scientific and philosophical truth relies strictly on logic and empirical reason. There are cases in which these two domains interfere, leading to both positive and negative consequences that are further explored below.
2.2. Secu(la)r(iz)ing the truth: from "God" to "Gov"
For thousands of years, truth was conceived sub specie aeternitatis, as, starting with Aristotle, the observable world was the realm of becoming and corruption in a philosophical sense. In his Metaphysics, Aristotle formulated one of the earliest and most enduring definitions of truth, asserting that a statement is true when it accurately reflects the state of affairs in reality: what is, is, and what is not, is not (Rasmussen, Internet Encyclopedia of Philosophy; David, 2022).
Starting with Hegel, truth gains a diachronic side which does not really contradict, but rather completes, the synchronic side. In fact, as Yeomans (2022) has shown, the synchronicdiachronic dialectics of the issue is born precisely when truth acquires this temporal dimension in Hegel's system, historicity proving to be just as much synchronic as diachronic, as much about the complex nature of the present as about an arc of change linking past and future.
Historical evolution has brought about different systems of legitimation for truth that have passed through intriguing diachronic changes. One of the longest-lasting systems seems to be religion, which for millennia held a monopoly on truth and, indeed, on all other values that were socially relevant. This religious truth needs to be absolute and to reject all other types of truth, tolerance and nuance being out of the question. If this presents no significant problem when held within the truth itself, tensions appear when it spreads throughout society, generating irreconcilable positions that have led to wars and other forms of violence, such as pogroms, excommunication, anathemas, witch-hunts, or burnings at the stake.
It is quite reasonable to see the history of civilization (or, at least, Western civilization) as an evolution towards the separation of religion and state, as religious truth cannot, per se, sustain societies of mutual understanding and moral consideration, since all religions rely upon gods that are absolute (and, therefore, all-knowing and all-powerful), and there is not enough room within the limits of the Cosmos - and indeed within the limits of human societies - for more than one such absolute claim. Furthermore, the fusion of religious and political authority has historically served as a mechanism through which states extract legitimacy, with religious communities functioning as competing centres of authority that simultaneously validate and constrain political power (Saperstein, 2019).
A long period of human history was dominated by the close connection between state and religion. In some areas of present-day society, this is still true. The state derives its power also from holding a serious (quasi-religious) claim to truth and legitimacy. This is how the official standpoint arises as the one and only tool for imposing collective truth as opposed to individual truth. Nevertheless, reliability is the hermeneutical key to the official sociopolitical discourse, and it can be obtained through a vast range of methods and instruments, ranging from violence and the elimination of opponents to manipulation and mind control - by means of media or technology in general.
The heretics in this case are called dissidents - persons who oppose the official side on a certain issue, either by attempting to impose their individual option or by supporting a different holistic or collective interpretation. The former would then be heroes (similar to martyrs in religions and usually considered social role models), while the latter are usually called traitors. As for the tools used, they depend on the historical stage, although some could be considered suitable throughout human history: propaganda, myths, censorship, manipulation, and so on - all adaptable to successive stages of scientific and technological evolution.
Legitimacy, in most cases, seems to be provided by the raison d'État - and so is truth. States need legitimacy in order to exert power, and one way to obtain it is to claim a monopoly on truth and build reliability from this official perspective. Censorship functions as an excellent tool for suppressing individual interpretations of truth, while propaganda is the instrument used to spread official truth throughout society, primarily through mind control and the elimination of people's access to alternative narratives (Brown, 2018).
Still, propaganda - more or less mythologically-infused -, is more commonly used in less developed states. For the most evolved ones, the preferred tool is manipulation, which is similar to propaganda when objectives are considered, but different when the role and capacities of the individual come into play. If propaganda creates mental reflexes mainly associated with a lack of critical thinking - by deepen mental dependency on authority -, manipulation can function quite well even in freedom-friendly spaces.
In fact, manipulators feel more comfortable in democracies, as there is room for spreading rumours and applying tools that generate and maintain social stereotypes or trends favourable to their objectives. Moreover, manipulation may be regarded as a technique directed towards emancipated individuals which does not rely on censorship, although it often appeals to the raison d'État, frequently presented as the authorities' concern for the "public good" / "national interest". Manipulation allows alternative interpretations and accepts critical thinking, whilst authorities attempt to encourage individuals to believe that they are masters of their own decisions. Thus, through manipulation, criticism is getting avoided softly rather than violently.
Algorithmic communications deepen this dynamic considerably, as filtering, hyper-nudging, and microtargeting can polarize an electorate and generate the illusion of autonomous opinion while, in fact, steering individuals towards predetermined conclusions (Christiano, 2021). Still, which is more effective, manipulation or propaganda? Through propaganda, on the one hand, states control minds, but are forced to build and maintain a costly system that keeps all, or most, windows of society closed; there is always the danger that external forces may attempt to break these strong boundaries. Manipulation, on the other hand, feeds on the juices of emancipated societies and individuals; state authorities still rely on the raison d'État, but simply use what is already at hand to stir in the minds of people the illusion of "own opinion".
Long story short, if propaganda tends to become delusional, manipulation prefers to be illusional.
2.3. Truth sagas: dramas = cases, people = numbers
A natural consequence of the historical attempts to monopolize truth is represented by the twentieth-century totalitarian regimes - Nazism and Communism. Despite being placed at opposite ends of the political spectrum, their methods were strikingly similar: propaganda, mind control, fanaticism, and violence. In both cases, truth belonged exclusively to the ruling party, while the ideal subject of totalitarian rule was, as Arendt observed, not the convinced Nazi or committed Communist, but the person for whom the distinction between fact and fiction, between true and false, had ceased to exist altogether (Arendt, 1951/1968). Any alternative explanation was treated as ideological opposition, generating enemies, "final solutions" and "statistical purges".
The historical response to these excesses (euphemism for outrages) was liberal democracy, consolidating after World War II. However, the rise of individualism threatened social cohesion. With violence and propaganda dismissed, persuasion became the primary instrument for sustaining shared truth - though its darker side, manipulation, was never far behind. Persuasion implies a voluntary dimension: truth is the product of research, and the individual retains the right to disagree. Yet truth became ethically biased by utilitarianism - any truth deemed harmful was suppressed. When utility fully overcomes truth, standards fail. Everything becomes negotiable - and truth gets monetized.
Technology offered a refuge: the digital era. Artificial intelligence, with its capacity to process information without human bias, seemed better equipped than humans to identify truth. However, the deeper cause of this shift was the "post-truth" era - truth lost its existential relevance, becoming tribal or individual. The proliferation of misinformation in this context is not simply a technical problem, but a political and epistemic one: it distorts public debate, undermines shared knowledge, and erodes the informational foundations upon which democratic life depends (Lewandowsky et al., 2023). AI occupied this empty space, raising a clear danger: it may replace more than anticipated.
Yet truth resurged. Faced with an epidemic of fake news, society developed virality as a response mechanism. Through social media, individuals spread their own perspectives, generating a new dialectic between truth and falsity. Conspiracy theories multiply, but so does fact-checking. Verification, supported by AI, becomes the essential tool. The "posttruth" phenomenon thus reflects not merely a proliferation of false claims, but a deeper transformation at the very level of trust relations, in which digital media, the attention economy, and political polarization converge to destabilize the very standards by which truth is acknowledged and evaluated (Lewandowsky et al., 2017).
The historical record suggests that truth has never found a definitive "institutional guardian". What changes are the actors, the instruments, and the mechanisms through which competing truth claims seek legitimacy. Understanding such processes is precisely where economics and related social sciences become relevant, offering analytical tools for observing informational pathologies whose ultimate resolution remains, nevertheless, political.
3. Economics+ tools for observing (still not for obliterating!) informational maladies
The fake news economy is not just about "bad" broadcasters interested in maximizing their audience and naïve receivers who no longer distinguish truth from falsehood, but an increasingly well-oiled mechanism, a "marketplace for truth" whose institutional architecture has evolved to accommodate so-called "fact-checking organizations". Fact-checking is vulnerable in its own account: it cannot function as a universally accepted epistemic arbiter replacing the market's self-regulating mechanism, and while its promise is to provide a lucid, verdict between reality and fiction, not infrequently it exceeds its remit. Several economic, multi-paradigmatic theses, grouped here under an "Economics+" framework, may offer useful analytical tools for understanding the socio-humane, "supply and demand" processes, of both "fake news" and "fact-checking" whenever the latter tends to become manipulative.
3.1. The knowledge problem: dispersed truths vs. centralized truth
Fact-checking's attempt to position itself as a guardian of truth contradicts, at least in part, one of the central insights of the Austrian School. Building on Mises' (1990/1920) critique of central planning, Hayek (1937, 1945) argued that the knowledge necessary for a rational economic order is never concentrated, but exists only dispersed, belonging to distinct individuals. It is precisely the possibility for each consumer or entrepreneur to possess his own information, not possessed by others, that encourages innovative spirit and risk-taking, each trying, in his own way and with his own strategies, to use the information he possesses to maximize his benefits. For Hayek, this freedom to possess one's own information distinguished capitalism from planned, socialist economies.
In this Austrian logic, the existence of a centralized fact-checker may become a rigid filter that distorts the market if it privileges certain sources, imposes certain styles of thinking, value judgments, or analytic methodologies, and claims an artificial or privileged position as intermediary between issuer and receiver. Contrary to the freedom of the information market advocated by Hayek and, in a broader libertarian register, by Rothbard (1970), such a filter may block the natural flow of data, influence public opinion, and, most importantly, contribute even more to disinformation than if the market were left free and the analysis of information entirely up to the receivers.
It must be said, however, that fact-checkers should not be seen as the ultimate evil in the market. There are organizations that perform their tasks with the rigor and objectivity specific to journalism. The problem arises if or when such organizations manifest claims to epistemic monopoly. Uscinski (2015) pointed out that certain groups of journalists have arrogated to themselves the position of arbiters of "truth". Even if journalists objectively verify the news, the problem does not necessarily lie there, but begins at the moment of selecting the statements to be verified. If there are prejudices from that moment on, then the "truth" obtained from the "audit" is already influenced or distorted.
Here, a subtle tension also arises with the ideas of Hayek and Rothbard, both of whom asserted that the free exchange of information and unrestricted expression are economic necessities for increasing social welfare. Except that these ideas were expressed in a presocial media and pre-fake news era. If we were to follow the line of thought of these two Austrian School thinkers, we would be discussing today the free exchange of false information and unfettered expression on social media (the TikTok-ization of judgment), which can lead - as countless election campaigns across the globe over the past decade have shown - to dire results for democracies.
Hayek argues that one of the strengths of markets is their ability to aggregate decentralized information and, building on that, to coordinate effectively. The problem does not lie in this capacity of markets - which remains unchanged -, but in the sources of information that reach the markets. If markets are flooded with fake news, then what they aggregate may turn into a massive falsehood. Given that Hayek places trust in decentralized information, truth risks being reduced to what is not real, but merely popular. A/the solution is a competitive market for journalistic reputation/credibility, acting as an authenticator and aggregator of decentralized information, before releasing it to the market.
The result, in this old-fashion, pre-"social", media, was quality information whose ethical standards protected democracy. Today, selection, analysis, and verification have all but disappeared or migrated toward new institutions of epistemic certification. Ironically, salvation might come from Hayek himself, who argued that human knowledge, due to its vastness, is dispersed and that no one can possess absolute truth - which means there will continue to be people who know "real reality", distinguishing it from that within fake news bubbles. Except that, even here, the first group tends to shrink, and then we return profitably marketable hoaxes (a.k.a. "infodemic").
3.2. The information problem: when truth becomes a lemon
The phenomenon of fake news may also be interpreted through the lens of Information Economics. George Akerlof's (1970) theory of the "market for lemons" provides a useful framework for understanding how information asymmetries arise when those supplying information know more about its quality than those consuming it. Traditionally, professional journalists possessed privileged access to information sources and, through established editorial standards, reduced such asymmetries. Consumers were exposed to multiple sources of the same piece of news, allowing them to compare, evaluate, and exercise critical scrutiny.
The gradual tabloidization of the press, followed by the rise of online social media and the "attention economy", has profoundly altered this equilibrium. As traditional media channels lose ground to digital platforms, consumers become increasingly exposed to simplified, emotionally charged, and often unverified content. Overwhelmed by an abundance of information, they find it progressively more difficult to distinguish truth from falsehood. Pennycook et al. (2018) show that even information explicitly flagged as false often continues to be believed, while Rose (2020) warns that even rational consumers may gradually lose the ability to detect deception.
Fake news thus becomes a market failure generated by growing information asymmetries. As consumers' discernment weakens, high-quality journalism risks being misunderstood or undervalued, while sensational and simplified narratives become increasingly attractive. In Akerlof's terms, good information is gradually crowded out by inferior information, as consumers become unwilling - or unable - to recognize and reward quality. The value of information increasingly lies in its shareability rather than its veracity (Epstein et al., 2023), while the pursuit of clicks and shares degenerates the info infrastructure of the media market.
Under such circumstances, the emergence of fact-checking organizations appears as a market response to informational failure. Their role is to certify the quality of information in a marketplace where consumers can no longer reliably distinguish between truth and falsehood. Yet this solution is itself imperfect. As Shin (2026) argues, even when factcheckers perform their task professionally, the very selection of which claims deserve verification may introduce subtle forms of bias and hidden manipulation. The challenge lies not merely in correcting informational asymmetries, but in doing so without replacing one market failure with another.
3.3. The incentive problem: who profits from truth?
The previous discussion naturally raises a Public Choice question - in the spirit consecrated by Buchanan and Tullock (1962): who verifies the verifiers? If fact-checking organizations become participants in the information market rather than neutral observers, then they inevitably operate under incentives similar to those affecting all other institutions. They control resources, build reputations, establish public or private relationships, and pursue organizational objectives. As Public Choice theory thoroughly reminds us, institutions and organizations should not be assumed to act differently from the people who generate, populate, and maintain them.
The emergence of fact-checking organizations reflects the growing inability of both information suppliers and receivers to perform their traditional roles. Yet, however independent and objective these organizations aspire to be, their members inevitably operate within particular intellectual traditions, professional cultures, and institutional environments. Moreover, opinion manipulators may infiltrate such organizations, while ideological or financial alignments may influence the selection of topics submitted for verification. The issue is therefore not necessarily the correctness of individual verdicts, but the very incentives which empower the professional assessors/verifiers.
The pursuit of economic rents is already visible among producers of fake news, who transform facts - or invent them - into sensational stories designed to maximize attention and revenues. The tabloidization of the media, the race for clicks and shares, and the continuous search for "breaking news" illustrate how competition for attention may replace competition for accuracy. The same logic may, under certain circumstances, affect organizations entrusted with certifying truth. If so, fact-checking itself risks becoming another participant in the quest for influence, legitimacy, and visibility.
A free market for ideas presupposes a largely unmediated relationship between information suppliers and receivers. Fact-checking undoubtedly performs an important corrective function whenever it validates truthful information or exposes falsehoods. Yet, if verification gradually evolves into an institutional privilege or an epistemic monopoly, one layer of informational intermediation may simply be replaced by another. The problem is therefore less the existence of fact-checking than the incentives under which it operates and the institutional safeguards capable of preventing truth (or pseudo-truth) from becoming yet another object of political or economic rent seeking.
Rather than offering a universal detector of truth, "Economics+" (that is economic science disciplinarily and disciplinedly interwoven, yet not epistemologically entangled with other social scholarships coming from philosophy, sociology, psychology, legal studies etc.) provides a plurality of analytical lenses for observing informational pathologies. 4. Empirical proxies for scrutinizing info-wars (indicators, databases, organizations)
While truth and falsehood remain inherently resistant to direct measurement, the informational environments within which they compete are not. Although no indicator can quantify "truth" itself, a growing body of empirical research provides useful indexes for observing the institutional, technological, and behavioural conditions under which fake news and propaganda emerge, spread, and acquire legitimacy. Rather than measuring informational content, these indicators capture the openness, resilience, vulnerability, and governance of information ecosystems, this being the reason for considering them reliable proxies for whatever empirical research that might follow up from this theoretical essay. They offer valuable empirical complements to the conceptual lenses discussed in the previous section and provide a promising foundation for future comparative and quantitative research.
4.1. Media freedom and informational pluralism
Media freedom and informational pluralism constitute one of the most frequently used proxy dimensions for assessing the quality of an information ecosystem. Rather than measuring the truthfulness of information directly, they evaluate the institutional conditions under which truthful and false narratives compete. Among the most comprehensive initiatives are the Media Pluralism Monitor (Centre for Media Pluralism and Media Freedom, 2025), Freedom in the World and Freedom on the Net (Freedom House, 2025), and UNESCO's (2008) Media Development Indicators. Although they differ in methodology, these frameworks converge around several recurring dimensions: freedom of expression, editorial independence, ownership concentration, political influence, media diversity, journalists' protection, and media literacy. Collectively, they provide indirect evidence regarding the openness, competitiveness, and resilience of national information environments, making them valuable proxies for the institutional conditions under which both fake news and official propaganda operate.
The Media Pluralism Monitor (MPM) (Centre for Media Pluralism and Media Freedom, 2025) illustrates particularly well the multidimensional nature of media freedom. Rather than relying on a single index, it assesses media pluralism through four complementary areas - fundamental protection, market plurality, political independence, and social inclusiveness - covering issues such as freedom of expression, ownership transparency, editorial autonomy, media viability, political independence, and media literacy. The 2025 edition reports an overall medium-low risk for the European Union, while identifying market plurality and social inclusiveness as the most vulnerable dimensions, largely reflecting ownership concentration and the growing influence of digital intermediaries. Such findings suggest the fact that informational disorders are closely linked not only to content itself, but also to the very institutional architecture through which information is produced, distributed, and consumed.
4.2. Misinformation and disinformation vulnerability
A second family of empirical proxies focuses on societies' exposure and vulnerability to misinformation and disinformation. As digital communication increasingly amplifies informational fragmentation and cognitive contagion, several organizations have developed instruments for monitoring the spread and impact of false narratives. Representative examples include the UNHCR Information Integrity Toolkit, EU DisinfoLab's Impact-Risk Index, and the comparative framework proposed by the International Panel on the Information Environment (IPIE) (Broda & Strömbäck, 2024; UNHCR, 2024; EU DisinfoLab, 2025). Comparable evidence is also provided by the Reuters Institute's Digital News Report (Reuters Institute, 2025), which documents long-term changes in news consumption, media trust, and exposure to digital information ecosystems. These initiatives monitor variables such as fake news exposure, virality, coordinated campaigns, bot activity, algorithmic amplification, engagement, online polarization, and behavioural effects. Rather than identifying truth itself, they estimate the probability that informational disorders will emerge, circulate, and influence public opinion.
The rapid proliferation of misinformation has also stimulated the development of increasingly sophisticated measurement frameworks. Beyond simple indicators of fake news exposure, recent initiatives monitor virality, coordinated campaigns, bot activity, algorithmic amplification, behavioural change, and offline effects. Particularly noteworthy is the comparative review conducted by the IPIE (International Panel on the Information Environment, 2026), which systematizes ten major international indexes measuring resilience and vulnerability to misinformation and disinformation. Despite their methodological diversity, these instruments converge in treating misinformation as a multidimensional phenomenon shaped by institutional quality, media ecosystems, digital literacy, political polarization, and public trust.
4.3. Governmental propaganda and narrative management
A third category of indicators captures the institutional capacity of governments to shape, regulate, or monopolize public narratives. Recent years have witnessed a significant expansion of legislation addressing misinformation, strategic communication structures, and public fact-checking initiatives. Comparative evidence provided by the State Media Monitor, Freedom House, and recent studies on anti-fake-news legislation suggests that the institutionalization of official narrative management has accelerated worldwide - Lim & Bradshaw (2023), Media and Journalism Research Center (2025). Relevant proxies include state ownership or influence over media, public communication expenditures, contentremoval mechanisms, strategic communication infrastructures, and the degree of institutionalization of fact-checking. Such indicators do not measure propaganda directly; rather, they approximate the capacity of public authorities to influence the informational environment through legal, organizational, or technological means.
Empirical evidence also points to the growing institutionalization of governmental narrative management. According to recent comparative research (Lim & Bradshaw, 2023), 78 countries adopted legislation targeting misinformation and fake news between 2011 and 2022, although many of these initiatives have been criticized for favouring restrictive approaches over press freedom. Similarly, the State Media Monitor 2025 (Media and Journalism Research Center, 2025) reports that 85% of state and public media outlets worldwide are either controlled or captured, concluding that financial dependence, political influence, and strategic communication increasingly transform public media into instruments of geopolitical and domestic narrative competition rather than pluralistic journalism.

Conclusion: combating falsehood without crafting truth - against "anarchy over bastille"
Throughout history, societies have repeatedly oscillated between abandoning truth to the nihilism of competing narratives or entrusting it to leviathan structures (bastilles included) of centralized authority. Both responses emerge from legitimate concerns. One fears censorship; the other fears deception. Yet neither offers a satisfactory institutional equilibrium. Left unchecked, informational anarchy corrodes public trust; unchecked epistemic authority risks replacing falsehood with officially sanctioned truth. The dilemma is therefore not how to eliminate one by embracing the other, but how to resist both simultaneously, substantively.
The contribution of economic science lies precisely in discouraging institutional hubris: Austrian Economics reminds us that knowledge is dispersed; Information Economics that information is imperfect; Public Choice that institutions pursue incentives; Behavioural Economics that individuals remain cognitively bounded; Institutional Economics that rules shape outcomes without guaranteeing them. Rather than deciding what truth is, these perspectives explain why no actor - whether market or state - should be presumed capable of administering truths without errors. Their common lesson is one of "epistemic humility".
Combating falsehood does not require crafting an official truth. It requires preserving institutional arrangements capable of correcting error without suppressing dissent, encouraging verification without monopolizing interpretation, and protecting free inquiry without surrendering it to informational chaos. Between bastilles and anarchy lies the fragile space of an open society, a "market for ideas" (Jora et al., 2022), one that accepts truth as an object of continuous discovery rather than of permanent administration. And perhaps the greatest safeguard of truth is not that one governs or guards it, but that no one ever fully can.
