Abstract
In the context of the intensification of misinformation on climate change and the importance of public financing for the energy transition, this article analyses whether informational factors influence the allocation of climate funds in the European Union. The existing literature has mainly examined the social, political, and communication-related effects of climate misinformation, devoting less attention to its macroeconomic implications for public financing decisions. Starting from this gap, the study investigates the relationship between climate misinformation, economic capacity, greenhouse gas emissions, and energy transition financing across the Member States of the European Union. The analysis uses a panel dataset for the EU-27 covering the period 2014-2023, which integrates indicators related to climate financing, real GDP per capita, greenhouse gas emissions, and climate misinformation derived from the Climate FEVER dataset. The methodology combines panel econometric models, dynamic estimations, spatial analyses, and machine learning predictive techniques. The results show that climate misinformation does not exert a statistically significant effect on climate financing in the econometric specifications tested, whereas economic capacity and the institutional persistence of previous allocations remain the main determinants of financing. The spatial analysis does not confirm the existence of significant regional effects of misinformation, while the predictive modelling indicates the dominant role of economic factors in shaping future climate financing. The study's original contribution lies in integrating the informational dimension into the analysis of the determinants of climate financing and in demonstrating that European institutional mechanisms for resource allocation appear relatively resilient to the direct influence of misinformation. The findings extend the literature on the economics of climate policy by highlighting the relationship between economic, institutional, and informational factors in the financing of the energy transition.
Keywords: Fake news, climate finance, energy transition, climate misinformation, greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions, Instrumental Variables Two-Stage Least Squares (IV2SLS), Extreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost), Climate FEVER dataset.
JEL Classification: Q54, G18.
Introduction
The European Union is one of the main global actors in promoting climate policies, the energy transition, and sustainable finance. Nevertheless, significant differences persist among Member States in terms of economic capacity, levels of greenhouse gas emissions, and the scale of financial commitments allocated to climate policies. The specialised literature has traditionally explained climate finance through economic, fiscal, institutional, and environmental factors, such as real GDP per capita, budgetary capacity, national public policy priorities, and the pressure generated by greenhouse gas emissions.
In recent years, climate disinformation has become an important factor in shaping public perceptions and political discourse on climate change. False or misleading narratives may reduce trust in the scientific consensus, fuel social polarisation, and diminish public support for energy transition measures. Although these effects have been analysed primarily from communicational, behavioural, and sociopolitical perspectives, the macroeconomic implications of climate disinformation for public financing remain insufficiently investigated.
Starting from this gap, the study analyses whether the prevalence of online disinformation on climate change influences the allocation of public funds dedicated to renewable energy, energy efficiency, and decarbonisation technologies in the Member States of the European Union. The research uses a panel dataset for the EU-27 covering the period 2014-2023, which integrates indicators on climate finance, real GDP per capita, greenhouse gas emissions, and climate disinformation derived from the Climate FEVER dataset. Methodologically, the analysis combines panel econometric models, dynamic IV2SLS-GMM estimations, Moran's I spatial analysis, and predictive modelling through the XGBoost algorithm.
The contribution of the study is threefold. First, the article introduces climate disinformation as an informational determinant in the analysis of public financing for the energy transition. Second, it empirically tests the relationship between disinformation, economic capacity, greenhouse gas emissions, and climate finance in the EU-27 context. Third, it combines econometric, spatial, and predictive tools to assess whether disinformation has an independent explanatory or predictive role in relation to economic and environmental factors.
The article is structured as follows: the first section presents the specialised literature and the research hypotheses; the second describes the methodology and data used; the third analyses the empirical results; and the final section synthesises the discussion, conclusions, and implications for public policies.
1. Literature Review
The literature on climate finance and the energy transition has developed around the question of which factors explain cross-country differences in the capacity and willingness to finance climate policies. Existing research shows that climate finance is influenced primarily by economic, institutional, and environmental factors, to which recent literature has increasingly added informational factors, such as climate disinformation, digital polarisation, and public narratives that may affect social and political support for climate action (Hornsey and Lewandowsky, 2022; Gertrudix et al., 2024; Moreno-Cabanillas et al., 2024).
From an economic perspective, climate finance is frequently explained by fiscal capacity, the level of economic development, and the institutional capacity to absorb funds. Countries with a higher gross domestic product generally have more substantial budgetary resources for investment in renewable energy, energy efficiency, and decarbonisation technologies. Previous studies show that GDP per capita, the structure of the economy, and institutional capacity play an important role in shaping public allocations for climate-related purposes (Buchner et al., 2021; Karakosta et al., 2025). This perspective is also supported by research highlighting the relationship between economic development, renewable energy consumption, and countries' capacity to support energy transition policies (Sahlian et al., 2021; Tudor and Sova, 2021; Lenz and Fajdetić, 2022).
However, the relationship between economic development and climate finance is not automatic. Recent literature shows that countries with similar levels of economic development may display different behaviours in relation to climate finance, suggesting that economic prosperity alone does not explain the level of public allocations (Buchner et al., 2021; Dembicka-Niemiec et al., 2023). Political priorities, institutional stability, governance quality, budgetary architecture, and the strategic orientation of public policies may amplify or limit the effect of economic capacity on climate finance (Canal Vieira et al., 2022; Gajdzik et al., 2024; Ma and Jiang, 2025).
A second relevant strand of the literature concerns the role of greenhouse gas emissions in determining climate finance. From a normative perspective, countries with high emission levels should demonstrate a stronger financial commitment to policies aimed at reducing climate impact. Research on climate responsibility and the distribution of financial efforts suggests that emission levels may provide a rationale for increasing public contributions allocated to the energy transition (ÓhAiseadha et al., 2020; Zou et al., 2024). Nevertheless, the empirical evidence remains nuanced, as environmental pressure does not automatically translate into financial action but is mediated by economic, political, and institutional factors.
In this respect, climate finance should be understood not only as a response to the need to reduce emissions, but also as the outcome of countries' capacity to translate climate objectives into concrete budgetary commitments. Green budgeting instruments and institutional mechanisms for integrating climate objectives into national fiscal processes become relevant for explaining cross-country differences (Battersby et al., 2021; OECD, 2022). However, these mechanisms do not fully eliminate the influence of political priorities and economic constraints on the actual level of climate finance.
In parallel, the literature on climate disinformation has expanded significantly, particularly in the context of the development of digital platforms and the growing visibility of campaigns that challenge the scientific consensus on climate change. Recent studies show that climate disinformation can influence public perceptions, intensify ideological polarisation, and reduce trust in the institutions responsible for designing environmental policies (Lewandowsky, 2021; Hornsey and Lewandowsky, 2022; Treen et al., 2022). False or misleading narratives do not function merely as informational distortions, but also as mechanisms for obstructing climate policies, by reducing public support for green investments and increasing the political costs of the energy transition (Farrell, 2015; Gertrudix et al., 2024).
Research on digital ecosystems shows that online platforms, social media networks, and organised actors may contribute to the production, dissemination, and amplification of obstructionist narratives concerning climate policies (Falkenberg et al., 2022; Moreno- Cabanillas et al., 2024). Similarly, studies on think tanks and networks of actors involved in climate communication show that disinformation can be used strategically to influence the public agenda, increase polarisation, and diminish the legitimacy of public interventions in the climate domain (Gertrudix et al., 2024; Moreno-Cabanillas et al., 2024; EU DisinfoLab, 2025).
Nevertheless, most research on climate disinformation focuses on the communicational, social, and political effects of the phenomenon. Studies mainly analyse how disinformation influences public attitudes, climate scepticism, ideological polarisation, or the digital strategies of actors opposing climate policies (Lucas et al., 2021; Hornsey and Lewandowsky, 2022; Treen et al., 2022). Less investigated is the question of whether climate disinformation can effectively influence the level of public finance allocated to climate action and the energy transition.
This limitation indicates the existence of a separation between two streams of literature. On the one hand, the climate finance literature explains budgetary allocations through economic, institutional, and environmental factors, such as GDP per capita, fiscal capacity, greenhouse gas emissions, and public policy architecture (Buchner et al., 2021; OECD, 2022; Coldrey et al., 2024). On the other hand, the climate disinformation literature explains the effects of false information on public opinion, institutional trust, and support for climate policies (Farrell, 2015; Lewandowsky et al., 2017; Lewandowsky, 2021; Hornsey and Lewandowsky, 2022). What remains insufficiently tested is how an informational factor, such as climate disinformation, interacts with the economic and environmental determinants of climate finance.
In the context of the European Union, this relationship must be analysed with caution. On the one hand, the sociopolitical literature suggests that climate disinformation may reduce the public acceptability of environmental policies and fuel opposition to the energy transition (Lewandowsky, 2021; Kulin and Johansson Sevä, 2024; Xie et al., 2024). On the other hand, the economic literature indicates that climate finance is influenced particularly by economic capacity, institutional commitments, and environmental pressures (Buchner et al., 2021; Zou et al., 2024; Karakosta et al., 2025). Therefore, it is not sufficient to state that disinformation affects climate policies; it is necessary to empirically test whether this effect also manifests itself at the level of public financial allocations.
The use of the Climate FEVER dataset allows climate disinformation to be operationalized with reference to verified claims and the scientific evidence associated with them (Aly et al., 2021; Diggelmann et al., 2020). The literature on automated fact-checking and climate claim verification shows that such databases enable a more systematic measurement of content that is incompatible with scientific evidence, even if they do not fully capture the complexity of the digital information ecosystem (Shao et al., 2018; Guo et al., 2022; Leippold et al., 2025). By integrating these data into an econometric, spatial, and predictive framework, the present study connects the analysis of climate disinformation with the economics of financing the energy transition.
Based on the literature reviewed, the study formulates the following research hypotheses:
H1 - Climate disinformation and climate finance - Higher levels of climate change disinformation are associated with lower levels of public finance for climate action and the energy transition in the Member States of the European Union.
H2 - Economic capacity and climate finance - Real GDP per capita has a positive effect on the level of public finance for climate action and the energy transition.
H3 - Greenhouse gas emissions and climate finance - Higher levels of greenhouse gas emissions are associated with higher levels of climate finance.
H4 - Spatial heterogeneity of climate disinformation - Climate change disinformation rates differ significantly across European countries, indicating the possible existence of spatial heterogeneity.
H5 - Predictive capacity of the integrated model - Climate finance can be predicted through econometric and machine learning models that integrate climate disinformation, real GDP per capita, and greenhouse gas emissions.
The empirical results of these hypotheses contribute to a better understanding of the relationships between economic, informational, and environmental factors that influence climate finance in Europe. 2. Research Methodology
To analyse the relationship between climate disinformation and the financing of the energy transition, the study uses a country-year panel dataset for the Member States of the European Union, covering the period 2014-2023. The database integrates indicators related to climate finance, real GDP per capita, greenhouse gas emissions, and the rate of climate disinformation. The choice of the analytical period is determined by the comparable availability of data on climate finance at the level of the European Union Member States.
The informational dimension of the study is constructed based on the Climate FEVER dataset, developed within the FEVER methodology, and used to assess claims about climate change by reference to scientific evidence (Diggelmann et al., 2020; Aly et al., 2021). The dataset includes climate-related claims associated with evidence sentences classified into three categories: "supports the claim" (support), "refutes the claim" (refute), and "not enough information." In this study, the climate disinformation indicator is constructed by aggregating claims labelled as refute, which are considered incompatible with the available scientific evidence. Claims labelled as support are not included in the disinformation indicator, while those marked as not enough information are excluded from the main indicator, in line with practices used in the literature on automated fact-checking and climate claim verification (Shao et al., 2018; Guo et al., 2022; Leippold et al., 2025).
The economic and environmental variables are drawn from European and international statistical sources. Real GDP per capita is used as a proxy for the economic capacity of Member States and is expressed in constant euros. Net domestic greenhouse gas emissions are expressed in metric tonnes of CO2 equivalent and reflect the environmental pressure associated with economic activity. Climate finance is measured through annual public allocations directed towards climate programmes, renewable energy, energy efficiency, and decarbonisation technologies, expressed in millions of euros. The data are drawn mainly from Eurostat, the World Development Indicators, and the budgetary reports of the European Union (European Commission, 2023; Eurostat, n.d.; World Bank, 2024).
The empirical analysis was carried out in several stages. In the first stage, the economic, financial, and environmental indicators were integrated into a unified panel dataset structured along the country-year dimension. In the next stage, the claims classified as refuted in Climate FEVER were aggregated to construct the annual climate disinformation rate, which was subsequently correlated with the economic database. This procedure enables the relationship between the dynamics of climate disinformation and variation in public financing for the energy transition in the European Union Member States to be tested.
To test the research hypotheses, panel-data econometric models were used, first with countryfixed effects and subsequently with both countryand year-fixed effects. These specifications allow for the control of unobserved heterogeneity across states and common time shocks. The baseline model treats climate finance as the dependent variable, while the climate disinformation rate, real GDP per capita, and greenhouse gas emissions are treated as explanatory variables. The choice between fixed-effects and random-effects models was assessed using the Hausman test, while the appropriateness of using fixed effects was verified through the data pooling test.
To address potential endogeneity and the temporal persistence of climate finance, the analysis was extended through a dynamic IV2SLS-GMM specification. Within this specification, lagged climate finance is introduced as an explanatory variable, while lagged values of the relevant variables are used as instruments. This approach allows the path dependence of climate allocations to be assessed and the robustness of the relationship between disinformation, economic capacity, emissions, and public climate finance to be tested.
In addition to the econometric models, the study includes a spatial analysis based on Moran's I statistic, used to identify possible forms of spatial autocorrelation in climate disinformation rates across European states. This stage enables the verification of the hypothesis regarding the existence of regional patterns or neighbourhood effects in the distribution of climate disinformation.
In the final stage, the Extreme Gradient Boosting algorithm, XGBoost, was applied to assess the predictive capacity of the integrated model. This method allows the relative importance of the explanatory variables in forecasting climate finance to be estimated and the predictive role of climate disinformation to be compared with that of real GDP per capita, greenhouse gas emissions, and previous climate finance. The integration of econometric models, spatial analysis, and machine learning techniques provides a multidimensional assessment of the relationship between informational, economic, and environmental factors in the financing of the energy transition.
The resulting methodological framework combines panel econometric models, spatial analysis, and machine learning techniques, providing an integrated perspective on the interactions between misinformation dynamics, economic capacity, and environmental commitment within the Member States of the European Union (Figure no. 2).
Although the methodological framework employed enables an integrated analysis of the relationship between climate misinformation, economic indicators, and climate finance in the Member States of the European Union, the study presents certain limitations that should be considered when interpreting the results. First, the climate misinformation indicator is constructed based on the Climate FEVER dataset, which focuses on claims verified through fact-checking procedures and the evidence associated with them. Although this approach provides a rigorous framework for identifying statements that are incompatible with scientific evidence, it does not fully capture the complexity of the digital information ecosystem, nor the total volume of misinformation content circulating on online platforms. Furthermore, the empirical analysis relies on country-year aggregated data, which allows the identification of structural relationships between variables, but limits the ability to capture subnational variations or differences across specific media platforms. Consequently, the results should be interpreted as reflecting aggregated trends at the European level. Another limitation relates to the availability of climate finance data, which are comparable across the Member States of the European Union only for the period 2014-2023. Restricting the estimation window to this interval reduces the possibility of analysing long-term dynamics.
Although the use of econometric models with instrumental variables and the Generalised Method of Moments (GMM) helps mitigate endogeneity issues, it cannot completely eliminate all potential bidirectional relationships between the analysed variables. In particular, the relationship between public discourse on climate change and financing decisions may be influenced by political and institutional factors that are difficult to quantify within econometric models. Finally, the predictive modelling component based on the XGBoost algorithm provides robust estimates of future trends, yet the results should be interpreted with caution, as the performance of machine learning models depends on the structure and stability of the historical data series used. Despite these limitations, the combination of econometric analysis, spatial diagnostics, and machine learning techniques provides a solid analytical framework for investigating the relationships between misinformation dynamics, economic capacity, and climate finance in the European Union.
3. Analysis of Empirical Results
The annual rates of misinformation regarding climate change generally remained below the threshold of 20% of the total claims analysed, with the exception of 2017 and 2018, when the proportion of claims classified as misinformation exceeded this level. The temporal evolution indicates an increase in misinformation rates during the 2015-2017 period, followed by a decline between 2017 and 2020, a decrease in 2022, and a slight rebound in 2023. These developments confirm the fluctuating nature of climate misinformation and are consistent with the literature showing that the intensification of activity by certain political and organisational actors can amplify the polarisation of climate discourse and the visibility of misinformation narratives (Falkenberg et al., 2022). The described trends are illustrated in Figure no. 3, which presents the evolution of annual climate misinformation rates during the analysed period.
At the level of the analysed countries, France, Cyprus, Serbia, and Finland record the highest rates of climate misinformation. Each of these countries exhibits distinct and relatively complex temporal variation patterns, which differ from the general trend observed at the European level. These variations are illustrated in Figure no. 4 and indicate the existence of country-specific dynamics that may influence the intensity and evolution of climate misinformation. Consequently, these findings are of particular analytical interest and suggest the need for further investigation into the factors that drive these cross-country differences.
The analysis of the scatter plot between the misinformation rate and the average annual level of climate finance indicates the existence of a weak positive relationship between the two variables. The observations suggest that years characterised by higher average misinformation rates tend to be associated with higher levels of climate finance. However, the observed relationship is neither strong nor strictly linear. The relatively high dispersion of the points in the graph indicates that the variation in climate finance cannot be explained solely by the dynamics of misinformation, suggesting the presence of other economic, political, or institutional factors that may influence this relationship. The corresponding scatter plot is presented in Figure no. 5.
The scatter plot between the misinformation rate and real GDP per capita also indicates the existence of a weak positive relationship between the annual misinformation rate and the average annual level of real GDP per capita over the analysed period. Years characterised by higher average misinformation rates tend to coincide with years in which higher levels of real GDP per capita are recorded.
For hypothesis testing, an OLS model was initially estimated using panel data with countrylevel fixed effects, in which climate finance was treated as the dependent variable, while the misinformation rate, real GDP per capita, and greenhouse gas emissions were included as explanatory variables. The results indicate that the misinformation rate does not have a statistically significant effect on climate finance. On the contrary, real GDP per capita exhibits a positive and statistically significant effect, suggesting that countries with higher economic capacity tend to allocate greater resources to climate policies. Greenhouse gas emissions do not show a statistically significant relationship in this specification. The results of the Panel OLS estimation with country fixed effects are presented in Table no. 1.
The Hausman test results support the use of the fixed-effects model, while the data aggregation test indicates the existence of significant heterogeneity across states. These results confirm the need to control for structural differences among the countries analysed. However, the low explanatory power of the model suggests that climate finance is influenced by a broader set of institutional, political, and budgetary factors that are not fully captured by the baseline specification.
In the second stage, the model was extended by including country and year fixed effects. In this specification, neither the rate of misinformation, nor the real GDP per capita, nor greenhouse gas emissions exhibit statistically significant relationships with climate finance. This result indicates that, after simultaneously controlling for cross-country differences and common time shocks, the variables included in the baseline model do not sufficiently explain the variation in climate-related financial allocations. Therefore, hypothesis H1, according to which higher levels of climate misinformation are associated with lower levels of public climate finance, is not supported by the fixed-effects models.
To address potential endogeneity and the temporal persistence of climate finance, the analysis was extended by means of a dynamic IV2SLS-GMM specification. The results indicate that lagged climate finance has a positive and highly statistically significant effect on current finance. The estimated coefficient, close to the value of 1, suggests pronounced path dependency, indicating that current climate allocations are strongly influenced by prior financial commitments. The misinformation rate remains statistically insignificant, whereas real GDP per capita and greenhouse gas emissions exhibit marginally significant positive effects. These results show that climate finance is explained primarily by the institutional continuity of allocations, economic capacity, and environmental pressure, rather than by the direct dynamics of climate misinformation. The detailed results of the estimation are presented in Table no. 3.
The comparison of the three econometric specifications confirms the robustness of the main result: the climate misinformation rate does not exert a statistically significant effect on climate finance in the models tested. Therefore, H1 is not confirmed. H2 is partially supported, as real GDP per capita has a positive and significant effect in the country fixedeffects model and a marginally significant positive effect in the IV2SLS-GMM model. H3 is only partially supported, since greenhouse gas emissions become marginally significant only in the dynamic specification. By contrast, the results support the idea that climate finance exhibits strong institutional inertia, reflected in the substantial influence of lagged finance on current allocations.
The spatial analysis conducted using Moran's I statistic does not indicate the presence of significant spatial autocorrelation in climate misinformation rates. The negative value of the indicator rather suggests a dispersed pattern than a systematic regional concentration. This result does not support hypothesis H4 regarding the existence of significant spatial heterogeneity in climate misinformation in European countries. From this perspective, climate misinformation appears to be shaped more by national and digital dynamics than by geographic neighbourhood effects.
This interpretation is also supported by the cartographic representation of average misinformation rates, which shows significant variation across countries without a clear regional concentration. Countries such as Finland, Serbia, Switzerland, Lithuania, and Spain record relatively higher levels of misinformation, yet neighbouring states display considerably different values, confirming the absence of a coherent geographical pattern. In addition, Ireland and Iceland appear as relatively isolated geographical units, with limited potential to influence neighbouring countries; in the case of Iceland, the level of misinformation is relatively higher compared to other European states. Overall, the results of the spatial analysis suggest that variations in misinformation rates across European countries are not determined by geographical proximity. This finding indicates that the dynamics of misinformation is more likely influenced by domestic factors or transnational information networks rather than spatial contiguity, highlighting the importance of institutional and informational factors in understanding the relationship between misinformation and climate finance.
Based on historical data, the main factors influencing climate finance were analysed using the Extreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost) machine learning technique. This method provides a significant advantage over traditional linear regression, as it can capture both linear and nonlinear relationships between variables, thereby revealing complex underlying patterns that may influence financing dynamics. The results indicate that real GDP per capita is the most influential factor in determining climate finance, followed by greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions and, finally, by the misinformation rate. Notably, the influence indices for GHG emissions and misinformation are nearly identical, suggesting that both exert a comparable - although secondary - impact on financing outcomes. Consequently, the findings indicate that real GDP per capita is likely to remain the primary determinant of future climate finance, while misinformation is expected to play a relatively minor role.
4. Discussion
The results of the study provide a nuanced perspective on the relationship between climate misinformation and the financing of the energy transition in the European Union. Although previous literature shows that climate misinformation can influence public perceptions, reduce trust in scientific consensus, and amplify social and political polarisation regarding climate policies (Lewandowsky, 2021; Hornsey and Lewandowsky, 2022; Treen et al., 2022), the findings obtained in this study indicate that these effects do not automatically translate into a direct and statistically significant reduction in climate finance at the macroinstitutional level. Thus, hypothesis H1 is not confirmed, and the relationship between misinformation and climate finance appears to be rather indirect, mediated by political, social, and institutional processes.
This finding does not contradict the literature on the effects of climate misinformation, but rather complements it. Previous studies have shown that false or misleading narratives can reduce the social acceptability of climate policies, fuel opposition to decarbonisation measures, and increase the political costs of the energy transition (Farrell, 2015; Gertrudix et al., 2024; Moreno-Cabanillas et al., 2024). However, the results of the present study suggest that, in the case of the European Union, these communicational and socio-political effects are not strong enough to directly alter aggregate public allocations for climate over the period analysed. Therefore, the contribution of the study lies in distinguishing between the discursive vulnerability of climate policies and the institutional-budgetary resilience of financing mechanisms.
With regard to the literature on climate finance, the results confirm the importance of economic capacity in supporting public investment for climate action and the energy transition. The positive effect of real GDP per capita, observed in the country fixed-effects model and partially supported in the IV2SLS-GMM specification, is consistent with research showing that countries with higher levels of economic development have greater fiscal capacity to finance climate policies (Buchner et al., 2021; Karakosta et al., 2025). The result is also compatible with studies highlighting the link between economic development, renewable energy consumption, and the capacity of states to support the energy transition (Sahlian et al., 2021; Tudor and Sova, 2021; Lenz and Fajdetić, 2022).
Nevertheless, the results show that economic development does not fully explain climate finance. This conclusion is consistent with the literature emphasising the role of political priorities, governance quality, budgetary architecture, and institutional capacity in shaping climate-related allocations (Canal Vieira et al., 2022; Dembicka-Niemiec et al., 2023; Gajdzik et al., 2024; Ma and Jiang, 2025). Therefore, H2 is partially supported: economic capacity influences climate finance, but its effect depends on the institutional environment and the continuity of public commitments.
An important result is the strong effect of lagged climate finance on current climate finance. This result indicates the existence of path dependency and suggests that climate allocations in the European Union are influenced by previous budgetary commitments, multiannual programmatic frameworks, and stable institutional mechanisms. This finding is in line with the literature on climate governance and green budgeting, which shows that financing the energy transition is often embedded in mechanisms of multiannual planning, reporting, and institutional coordination (Battersby et al., 2021; OECD, 2022). From this perspective, the results suggest that European climate finance has a high degree of institutional inertia, which may reduce its immediate sensitivity to fluctuations in public discourse or to informational pressures generated by misinformation.
Regarding greenhouse gas emissions, the results only partially support hypothesis H3. The positive and marginally significant effect observed in the IV2SLS-GMM model suggests that higher emission levels may be associated with higher climate allocations; however, this relationship is not robust across all specifications. This conclusion is compatible with the literature showing that environmental pressure may constitute an argument for increasing public contributions aimed at the energy transition, but does not automatically translate into effective financing (ÓhAiseadha et al., 2020; Zou et al., 2024). Therefore, the relationship between emissions and climate finance appears to be mediated by each state's administrative capacity, political priorities, and budgetary constraints.
The spatial analysis does not confirm the existence of significant spatial autocorrelation in climate misinformation rates, leading to the rejection of hypothesis H4. This result suggests that climate misinformation is not distributed across the European Union according to a simple geographical pattern based on regional proximity. The finding is relevant in relation to the literature on digital ecosystems, which shows that misinformation is amplified primarily through online networks, digital platforms, and organised actors rather than through geographical proximity (Falkenberg et al., 2022; Moreno-Cabanillas et al., 2024). Thus, the absence of spatial clustering does not refute the existence of misinformation, but indicates that its diffusion mechanisms may be more digital and transnational than strictly regional.
The results of predictive modelling using XGBoost partially support hypothesis H5. The integrated model has predictive capacity regarding climate finance; however, this capacity derives mainly from previous financing and economic variables, rather than from the climate misinformation rate. This finding is compatible with recent approaches that use machine learning methods to analyse complex climate and economic phenomena, but it shows that the predictive power of an integrated model depends on the relevance of the structural variables included (Xie et al., 2024; Ali et al., 2025). In the present case, economic and institutional factors have greater predictive importance than the informational factor measured through Climate FEVER.
In relation to the broader literature, the study contributes to connecting two research streams that have largely evolved separately: the literature on climate misinformation and the literature on financing the energy transition. The former has shown that misinformation can affect perceptions, polarisation, and public support for climate policies (Lewandowsky et al., 2017; Hornsey and Lewandowsky, 2022; Treen et al., 2022), whereas the latter has explained climate finance through economic, institutional, and environmental factors (Buchner et al., 2021; OECD, 2022; Coldrey et al., 2024). The results of the present study show that, in the context of the European Union, climate misinformation does not have a significant direct effect on aggregate public financing, but may remain relevant through indirect effects on public opinion, the political agenda, and the social acceptability of the energy transition.
These results have important implications for public policy. Combating climate misinformation remains necessary, as the literature shows that it can affect public trust, the legitimacy of climate policies, and social support for the energy transition (Lewandowsky, 2021; Hornsey and Lewandowsky, 2022; Gertrudix et al., 2024;). However, the results of the study suggest that measures to combat misinformation must be complemented by policies aimed at strengthening the financial and institutional capacity of Member States. In the absence of stable budgetary mechanisms, high administrative capacity, and coherent multiannual commitments, reducing misinformation is not sufficient to guarantee an increase in climate finance.
The interpretation of the results must be approached with caution. The absence of a statistically significant effect of misinformation on climate finance does not imply the absence of social, political, or electoral effects of misinformation. Rather, the results indicate that these effects are not detectable as a direct influence on aggregate climate finance over the period and through the variables analysed. It is possible that misinformation operates indirectly, by altering public perceptions, influencing the political agenda, increasing polarisation, or generating electoral changes-mechanisms that are not fully captured by the empirical specifications used in this study.
Overall, the study shows that climate finance in the European Union is more strongly determined by economic capacity, environmental pressure, and institutional persistence than by measurable fluctuations in climate misinformation. This conclusion extends the literature on climate governance and sustainable finance by integrating the informational dimension into a macroeconomic empirical framework and by demonstrating that the effects of misinformation on climate policies may be indirect rather than directly budgetary.
Conclusions
The study analysed the relationship between climate misinformation and the financing of the energy transition in the European Union, using a panel dataset for EU Member States covering the period 2014-2023. The research integrated indicators related to the climate misinformation rate, real GDP per capita, greenhouse gas emissions, and public funding allocated to climate policies, through a methodological approach combining panel econometric models, dynamic IV2SLS-GMM estimations, Moran's I spatial analysis, and XGBoost predictive modelling.
The results show that the climate misinformation rate does not exert a statistically significant direct effect on climate finance in the tested specifications. Instead, lagged climate finance and the economic capacity of Member States explain the dynamics of current allocations more consistently. Moreover, greenhouse gas emissions have a positive effect only in certain specifications, while the spatial analysis does not indicate the existence of a significant regional pattern of climate misinformation. The predictive modelling confirms the dominant role of economic and institutional variables in forecasting climate finance.
The main contribution of the study lies in integrating the informational dimension into the analysis of climate finance at the macro-institutional level. By empirically testing the relationship between misinformation, economic capacity, environmental pressure, and climate-related financial allocations, the research highlights that European mechanisms for financing the energy transition appear to be more strongly influenced by the continuity of budgetary commitments and economic capacity than by measurable fluctuations in climate misinformation.
From a practical perspective, the results suggest that climate finance policies should rely on stable, predictable, and multiannual mechanisms capable of reducing disparities among Member States in terms of their capacity to support the energy transition. At the same time, combating climate misinformation remains relevant for strengthening public trust, the legitimacy of climate policies, and social support for the green transition, even though its direct effect on aggregate financing is not statistically confirmed.
The study also presents several limitations. The climate misinformation indicator, constructed on the basis of the Climate FEVER dataset, provides a rigorous measure of claims that are inconsistent with scientific evidence, but it does not fully capture the volume, diversity, and circulation of misleading content within digital ecosystems. Furthermore, the use of country-year aggregated data and the 2014-2023 analysis period limits the possibility of examining subnational, sectoral, or long-term dynamics. In addition, the set of explanatory variables does not include all political, institutional, and social dimensions that may influence climate finance.
Future research could extend the analysis by including indicators related to governance quality, political stability, ideological polarisation, institutional trust, exposure to digital platforms, and public attitudes towards climate change. Moreover, the use of regional, microeconomic, or cross-platform media data could contribute to a more detailed understanding of the mechanisms through which climate misinformation indirectly influences the public agenda, political support, and the sustainability of energy transition financing in the European Union.
