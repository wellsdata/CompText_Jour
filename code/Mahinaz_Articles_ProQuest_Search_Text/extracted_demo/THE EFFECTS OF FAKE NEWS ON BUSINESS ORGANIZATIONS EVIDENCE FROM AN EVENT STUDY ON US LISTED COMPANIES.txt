Abstract
This study examines the market impact of fake news on publicly traded companies in the U.S., using an event study analysis applied to a sample of 30 confirmed cases of misinformation that affected U.S. companies between 2020 and 2025. From the perspective of the economy of information, fake news constitutes a major source of information asymmetry, with direct effects on market value, company reputation, and the rationality of investor decisions. Using a single-factor market model estimated over a 252-day period, with the SandP 500 index as the benchmark, events are classified into five categories: companyspecific (A), contagion (B), market-wide systemic (C), null/control cases (D), and SEC- documented pump-and-dump schemes (E). The results show that fake news generally generates significant abnormal returns on the day of the event, with a predominantly negative directional trend, suggesting that disinformation rapidly erodes investor confidence in the targeted organisations. Contagion effects prove to be the most systematic and powerful, indicating that disinformation can extend beyond the target company and affect entire sectors. The inclusion of null cases validates the methodology, confirming that in the absence of an active disinformation mechanism, no measurable effects are recorded. The proposed multicategory framework demonstrates that aggregating heterogeneous types of disinformation can mask relevant specific effects, with important methodological implications for future research. For business organisations, investors, and regulators, the findings underscore that information risk is not uniform and requires differentiated management strategies depending on the mechanism and context of disinformation.
Keywords: fake news, event study, abnormal returns, capital market, contagion, business organisations, information asymmetry.

Introduction
The proliferation of false information in financial markets represents a major challenge to market integrity, investor protection, and the effectiveness of regulatory mechanisms. From fabricated corporate announcements to AI-generated deepfake images, the ways in which misinformation influences asset prices have diversified significantly in the age of social media. At the same time, the implications of this phenomenon extend beyond the strictly financial sphere, affecting business organisations through reputational damage, the erosion of stakeholder trust, and increased costs associated with managing information risk. The World Economic Forum (2024) has identified misinformation as one of the most severe global risks, while the U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC) has intensified its actions against market manipulation through false online content.
Despite growing concerns among regulators, the academic literature on the impact of fake news on financial markets remains limited. Arcuri et al. (2023) conduct the first comprehensive event study on the effects of fake news on U.S. and EU markets (2007-2019), analysing 19 events. Clarke et al. (2021) investigated pump-and-dump schemes documented by the SEC on the Seeking Alpha platform. Louchez (2025) provided a detailed analysis of a single event highlighting contagion effects within the pharmaceutical oligopoly.
However, several relevant gaps persist. First, the post-2019 period remains insufficiently investigated, although it is characterised by the emergence of new forms of misinformation, including impersonation facilitated by digital platforms and content generated by artificial intelligence. Second, the existing literature frequently treats fake news as a homogeneous phenomenon, without distinguishing between the heterogeneous mechanisms through which different types of misinformation affect markets and business organisations. Third, previous studies do not systematically include null cases - situations in which verified misinformation has no effect on prices - as a tool for internal validation of the methodology. Fourth, the relationship between fake news, organisational vulnerability, and the rationality of economic decisions remains insufficiently explored empirically.
This paper addresses these gaps by constructing a dataset comprising 30 confirmed fake news events targeting U.S.-listed companies between 2020 and 2025. The events are classified into five categories based on mechanism and target: company-specific direct impact (Category A, n = 14), contagion and propagation effects (Category B, n = 3), market-wide systemic shocks (Category C, n = 3), null/control cases (Category D, n = 4), and SEC-documented "pumpand-dump" schemes (Category E, n = 6). Each event is validated through regulatory actions, peer-reviewed publications, or established fact-checking organisations.
The empirical analysis uses the standard event study methodology (MacKinlay, 1997), applying a unifactorial market model estimated over 252-day windows. Seven hypotheses are tested, addressing the existence, direction, and persistence of effects, heterogeneity across categories, contagion mechanisms, cross-sectional determinants, and validation via null cases. From a broader perspective, the results show that misinformation disrupts evaluation and decision-making processes in the economic environment, affecting not only market efficiency but also the ability of organisations to protect their credibility and reputational capital in an increasingly volatile information environment.
By introducing a multi-category classification framework, the paper demonstrates that the effects of fake news are not homogeneous but depend on the propagation mechanism and market context, thus offering a relevant contribution to both the academic literature and practitioners and policymakers.
1. Review of scientific literature
1.1. Efficient market hypothesis and information processing
The theoretical foundation of this study is based on the Efficient Market Hypothesis (EMH), formulated by Fama (1970), according to which asset prices fully reflect available information. In its semi-strong form, prices should adjust quickly and accurately to public information, including news regarding corporate fundamentals. However, this hypothesis assumes that investors can distinguish true information from false information, a premise that is increasingly difficult to uphold given the speed and scale of information dissemination on social media (Obadă and Dabija, 2022). In this regard, Vosoughi et al. (2018), analysing 126,000 rumour chains on Twitter, show that false information spreads faster and more widely than true information, undermining the assumption of rational information processing. Behavioural finance provides the explanatory mechanisms for these deviations. Kahneman and Tversky (1979) highlight loss aversion, Shiller (2000) describes her behaviour, and De Bondt and Thaler (1985) demonstrate overreaction to dramatic news. Together, these mechanisms suggest that false information, particularly negative information, can generate immediate and amplified price reactions, followed by partial corrections as the information is clarified.
The literature often uses terms with distinct meanings interchangeably. Wardle and Derakhshan (2017) distinguish between misinformation (false information without manipulative intent), disinformation (false information deliberately disseminated), and malinformation (true information used harmfully). In the financial context, fake news falls predominantly under disinformation (Allcott and Gentzkow, 2017). Market manipulation involves the intentional distortion of prices (Clarke et al., 2021; Kogan et al., 2023), reporting error is an unintentional inaccuracy with reversible effects (Palmrose et al., 2004), and a rumour is characterised by a lack of confirmation from authoritative sources (Vosoughi et al., 2018). The sample of this study deliberately covers this spectrum, justifying the need for the proposed multi-categorical classification framework.
1.2. Fake news and financial markets
Against this theoretical background, the empirical literature on the effects of fake news on financial markets remains relatively limited. Arcuri et al. (2023) represent the main methodological benchmark, demonstrating through an event study applied to 19 cases (U.S. and EU, 2007-2019) the existence of significant negative abnormal returns, which are more pronounced for small firms and the financial sector. Other recent research highlights the role of social media in amplifying investor reactions and the dynamics of price adjustments following misinformation (Hirshleifer et al., 2023; Zhong et al., 2024). However, their analysis does not cover the recent period and treats the phenomenon as homogeneous.
Clarke et al. (2021) examine SEC-documented pump-and-dump schemes on the Seeking Alpha platform, highlighting positive abnormal returns during the promotional phase, followed by corrections. Although relevant, the study focuses on a single manipulation mechanism. Louchez (2025) makes an important recent contribution by analysing the effects of the fake Eli Lilly tweet regarding insulin and demonstrating the existence of contagion within the pharmaceutical oligopoly. The present study extends these results by integrating the case into a multi-category framework, which allows for comparisons between different types of misinformation, including events with no effect and alternative mechanisms.
1.3. Social media, information asymmetry and market manipulation
The spread of fake news is closely linked to the role of social media in price determination. Jiao et al. (2020) show that sentiment on social media can predict short-term returns, and Kogan et al. (2023) demonstrate that fraudulent information directly influences trading activity and prices, while exposure to fraud reduces trust in the entire information flow. Allcott and Gentzkow (2017) highlight the scale of the fake news phenomenon in the digital ecosystem. These effects can be explained by the theory of information asymmetry (Kyle, 1985). Misinformation creates temporary asymmetries between informed and uninformed investors, and the speed at which these are corrected determines the persistence of abnormal returns.
Beyond the impact on prices, these mechanisms directly affect business organisations. The rapid spread of false information implies additional costs for monitoring, communication, and reputation management, turning fake news into an issue of information governance and organisational sustainability (Sariarei et al., 2024).
1.4. Contagion and spillover effects
A key mechanism through which misinformation affects markets is informational contagion, whereby false news targeting one firm also influences the prices of related firms. King and Wadhwani (1990) provide the theoretical framework for cross-market contagion, while Forbes and Rigobon (2002) refine the distinction between contagion and interdependence. In the context of fake news, Louchez (2025) provides empirical evidence of contagion in an oligopolistic market, demonstrating that competing firms are affected even in the absence of direct exposure. Hong et al. (2023) extends the analysis to the COVID-19 context, highlighting systemic risks generated by misinformation.
Within this framework, our analysis contributes by testing contagion across multiple sectors and comparing it to direct effects and null cases.
2. Research methodology
2.1. Research design and data
This study (Figure 1) uses the event study methodology developed by MacKinlay (1997) to measure the market impact of 30 confirmed fake news events on U.S.-listed companies between January 2020 and December 2025. To investigate these effects, the literature predominantly employs the event study methodology, which has been the standard in financial economics since Fama et al. (1969), who established the framework for market models and the testing of abnormal returns. Brown and Warner (1985) analyse the statistical properties of these tests, including under conditions of non-normality and cross-sectional dependence. Boehmer et al. (1991) and Patell (1976) develop the statistical tests used in the present analysis. The present study utilises these established methods, adapting them to the specific context of confirmed disinformation events.
The dataset includes adjusted daily closing prices for 25 unique tickers and the SandP 500 ETF (SPY), used as a market benchmark, resulting in 43,748 firm-day observations for the period January 2019-March 2026. The data comes from Yahoo Finance for all securities except Twitter (TWTR), which was delisted following its acquisition in October 2022; for this security, historical data is sourced from Investing.com.
For each event, the estimation window covers 252 trading days [-257, -6] prior to the event, and the event window covers 11 days [-5, +5], resulting in a total analysis window of 263 days per event. Daily logarithmic returns are calculated as rt = ln(Pt/Pt-1 ). Events are classified into five categories based on mechanism and target, as shown in Table no. 1.
The inclusion of each event meets four criteria: (i) the information has been confirmed as false by authoritative sources (SEC filings, peer-reviewed publications, or established factchecking organisations such as Reuters, AP, Snopes, or PolitiFact); (ii) the security in question is listed on a U.S. stock exchange, with data available; (iii) the date of the event is precisely identifiable; (iv) the event generated or had the documented potential to generate temporary information asymmetry in the market, regardless of the issuer's intent-a criterion that justifies the inclusion of both disinformation and misinformation cases, as well as false disclosures, under the common analytical umbrella of fake news, understood broadly as informational disorder (Wardle and Derakhshan, 2017). The type of disinformation follows this taxonomy: cases #9, #13, and #23 represent misinformation (unintentional error without manipulative intent); cases #10 and #14 represent false disclosure (intentionally misleading disclosure in official reports). All other events represent disinformation (deliberately distributed false information). These five cases are deliberately included to test the robustness of the methodology by contrasting it with disinformation proper and to reflect the actual spectrum of information disorder in financial markets. The source column provides an abbreviated verification reference.

2.2. Statistical tests
The significance of individual events is assessed using Patell's t-statistic (1976): t = ARi0/σi^, where σi^ is the residual standard error from the OLS regression of the estimation window. Aggregate significance across events is assessed using four complementary tests: (i) the standardised cross-sectional BMP test (Boehmer et al. 1991); (ii) the binomial sign test for directional dominance; (iii) the signed Wilcoxon rank test; and (iv) cross-sectional t-tests on category means. The cross-sectional regression of CAR [-5, +5] on firm and event characteristics (log volume in dollars, category dummy variables, stock exchange) uses HC3 robust standard errors (White, 1980).
Based on the literature reviewed above, we formulate the following hypotheses.
* H1: Fake news events generate statistically significant abnormal returns on the day of the event.
* H2: The effects of fake news persist beyond Day 0, producing significant cumulative abnormal returns over the [-5, +5] window.
* H3: Company-specific fake news (Category A) produces higher absolute CARs than market-level shocks (Category C).
* H4: Fake news generates significant contagion abnormal returns in competing firms that were not directly targeted.
* H5: SEC-documented pump-and-dump schemes (Category E) generate significant abnormal returns consistent with the direction of the manipulation.
* H6: Firm-level and event-level characteristics moderate the market impact of fake news.
* H7: Null-event cases (Category D) do not produce significant abnormal returns, serving as a placebo control.
3. Results and discussion
This section presents the empirical results organised by hypothesis. For events in Category C, the DIA is used as the test title. In relation to the broader theme of the paper, the results should be interpreted not only as market reactions to misinformation, but also as indicators of business organisations' vulnerability to informational shocks, with implications for corporate credibility, the rationality of investor decisions, and the stability of competitive ecosystems. Before testing the hypotheses, two characteristics of the dataset are worth highlighting.
3.1. Individual event results (H1)
Table no. 2 reports the Day-0 abnormal returns and the cumulative abnormal returns over the [-5, +5] window for all 30 events.
The results indicate substantial heterogeneity. Twelve events (40%) show statistically significant ARs on day 0 at the 5% level, and 16 (53%) at the 10% level. Under the null hypothesis, approximately 1.5 events would have been expected to be significant at the 5% level by chance alone. Therefore, the observed rate of 40% is eight times higher than expected, suggesting that fake news events generate abnormal returns as a class.
The sign test also confirms a negative directional bias: 20 out of 30 events (66.7%) produce negative ARs on day 0 (p = 0.049). Excluding SCWorx, the proportion rises to 69.0% (p = 0.031). In contrast, the standardised cross-sectional BMP test for Day 0 is not significant (t = 0.16, p = 0.875), a result explained by the deliberate inclusion of heterogeneous types of events. Categories A and B tend to generate negative standardised ARs, while Category E frequently produces positive standardised ARs. MacKinlay (1997) shows that aggregate tests lose power when event-induced effects differ in sign. Following the same logic, a comparison with Arcuri et al. (2023), who report significant aggregate effects in a more homogeneous sample of negative events, suggests that the sample structure decisively influences the outcome of the aggregate test.
Consequently, H1 is partially confirmed. The sign test (p = 0.049) and the high proportion of individually significant events (40%, compared to the expected 5%) provide robust evidence that fake news generates abnormal market reactions. The predominantly negative direction is consistent with loss aversion, and the fact that the BMP test is insignificant reflects intercategory heterogeneity, not the absence of effects.
3.2. Category-level patterns and persistence (H2, H3)
Table 3 summarises the statistics on abnormal returns by event category and serves as the primary evidence supporting the central argument of this paper: different types of fake news produce systematically different market effects.
The strongest result is found in Category B (contagion). The Day-0 AR mean of -4.48% is significant at the 1% level (t = -10.10, p = 0.010), and all events in this category have a negative sign. The standardised BMP test independently confirms this result (t = -7.23, p = 0.019), making Category B the only category that is significant when aggregated using both parametric approaches.
Category A exhibits the highest intra-category variance (SD = 34.99%), reflecting the heterogeneity of direct misinformation mechanisms. After excluding the SCWorx outlier, the CAR mean shifts to -4.89%, and 69.2% of events remain negative, although the aggregated statistics do not reach the significance threshold. Category C records low magnitudes (mean CAR = -1.05%), with no statistical significance. Category E exhibits the highest dispersion (SD = 68.37%), suggesting the coexistence of opposing pump-and-dump dynamics.
Regarding H2, seven events (23%) exhibit CARs significant at the 5% level. The analysis of alternative windows, presented below in Table 4, indicates a clear pattern of attenuation: the [-1, +1] window produces 15 significant events (50%), a number that drops to 7 for the [-5, +5] window. This result is consistent with the existence of information shocks concentrated around day 0, followed by partial corrections. The pattern of gradual attenuation is consistent with Brown and Warner (1985), who demonstrate that informational effects are concentrated around the event date and dissipate over broader time windows. The high intra-category variance in Category A is consistent with Arcuri et al. (2023), who report similar heterogeneity based on firm size and industry.
Regarding H3, the absolute CAR average for Category A, after excluding SCWorx, is 15.5%, compared to 1.1% for Category C. The ratio of approximately 14:1 suggests that firm-specific information shocks produce much stronger effects than systemic market-level shocks. This finding is consistent with Kogan et al. (2023), who show that targeted fraudulent information produces stronger and more persistent effects than diffuse informational shocks.
3.3. Contagion effects (H4)
The fake Eli Lilly tweet about insulin, dated November 11, 2022, provides a natural experiment on information contagion. All three pharmaceutical companies show significant negative Day-0 ARs, with Eli Lilly (the direct target) at -5.29% (t = -3.32, p < 0.01), Novo Nordisk at -4.22% (t = -2.14, p < 0.05), and Sanofi at -3.87% (t = -2.54, p < 0.05) (Figure no.2).
The magnitude of contagion accounts for 73-80% of the target firm's AR, suggesting nearly complete pass-through within the insulin oligopoly. The result is consistent with Louchez (2025). The UnitedHealth Group event extends this logic to the health insurance sector: the Day-0 AR of -5.34% (t = -3.40, p < 0.01) is followed by a persistent CAR of -16.29% (p < 0.01). It is also relevant that AbbVie, although affected on the same day by a Twitter Blue impersonation but outside the insulin market, shows no significant effect (-0.17%, t = -0.13). This contrast suggests that mere temporal co-occurrence is not sufficient for contagion; proximity within the product market appears to be a necessary condition. The significance of the BMP test for Category B (t = -7.23, p = 0.019), in contrast to the non-significant result obtained on the full sample (t = 0.16, p = 0.875), directly supports the paper's central methodological argument: aggregating heterogeneous types of events can hide category-specific effects that are relevant both statistically and economically. Therefore, H4 is confirmed. All contagion events exhibit significant negative ARs, and transmission reaches 73-80% within the insulin oligopoly. The results indicate that product market proximity functions as a threshold condition for the occurrence of contagion.
3.4. Pump-and-Dump, Cross-Sectional Analysis, and Null Cases (H5-H7)
Two of the six events in Category E exhibit significant Day-0 effects: GameStop (+87.48%, t = 11.72, p < 0.01) and TWTR "deal on hold" (-12.81%, t = -4.65, p < 0.01). The opposite directions reflect distinct mechanisms: classic pump schemes generate positive ARs, while Musk's tweet worked as a sell signal, producing a negative AR. Consequently, H5 is only partially confirmed: there are significant individual effects, but the category-level means remain insignificant (p = 0.362) due to sign heterogeneity. The pump-and-dump dynamics identified for GameStop are consistent with Clarke et al. (2021), who document positive abnormal returns during the promotional phase followed by corrections, and with Desiderio et al. (2025), who confirm the causality between Reddit posts and price movements. The negative effect of the tweet reflects a distinct manipulation mechanism that warrants separate investigation in future research.
Regarding H6, the cross-sectional regression of CAR[-5, +5] on log-volume in dollars, category, and exchange dummy variables does not identify significant predictors (all p > 0.30; R2 = 0.106; Adj. R2 = -0.127). Given the small sample size (N = 30) and the presence of outliers ranging from -136% to +108%, statistical power is insufficient to detect moderating effects. Therefore, H6 is not confirmed.
Category D, used as the null set, clusters around zero, as predicted: the mean Day-0 AR is -0.74% (t = -0.51, p = 0.645), and the mean CAR is +1.10% (p = 0.725). Three of the four events produce individually insignificant Day-0 ARs and less than 2% in absolute value. The exception, Berkshire Hathaway (-4.57%, t = -4.91), coincides with the market crash on April 4, 2025 (SPY -6.03%), and the insignificant CAR of -0.26% indicates a complete reversal.
By showing that events for which no effects are expected do not produce systematic effects, the analysis provides important internal validation of the methodology, which is largely absent from previous literature on fake news. Consequently, H7 is confirmed. The absence of effects in null cases is consistent with Jiao et al. (2020), who show that market reactions to social media content are determined by the perceived relevance and credibility of the source.
3.5. Robustness checks
Table no. 4 presents the aggregated significance tests for all specifications and summarises the main robustness checks.
First, the standardised BMP test applied at the category level yields a different picture from the non-significant result obtained on the full sample. Category B reaches significance on day 0 (t = -7.23, p = 0.019), while no other category does, which directly supports the proposed multi-category framework. Second, the decreasing number of significant events as the windows widen-from 15 in [-1, +1] to 7 in [-5, +5]-confirms that the effects are concentrated around day 0 and subsequently attenuate, although the rate of this attenuation differs across events. Third, excluding the SCWorx observation strengthens the sign test, from p = 0.049 to p = 0.031, and shifts the BMP on the CAR toward marginal significance (t = -1.42, p = 0.165). Fourth, substituting DIA for Category C events avoids regressing SPY on itself, while maintaining a high correlation with the benchmark (ρ ≈ 0.97). Fifth, the BRK- B event, disrupted by the tariff crash on April 4, 2025, is properly identified and documented: the significance of Day-0 AR disappears over wider windows, suggesting market noise rather than effects attributable to fake news.
Conclusions
This study provides a multi-category event study analysis of confirmed fake news events in U.S. financial markets, covering the period 2020-2025 with a sample of 30 verified misinformation events. Beyond its contribution to the empirical finance literature, the paper highlights that fake news poses a real risk to business organisations, as it simultaneously affects market value, stakeholder confidence, and the quality of decision-making processes in an economic environment increasingly dependent on the speed of information flow. First, fake news generates economically and statistically significant abnormal returns at the individual event level. Twelve out of 30 events (40%) produce Day-0 abnormal returns that are significant at the 5% level, eight times the expected rate under the null hypothesis. The sign test confirms a significant negative directional trend (66.7% negative, p = 0.049), consistent with the loss aversion and overreaction mechanisms predicted by behavioural finance theory. This result suggests that misinformation rapidly erodes confidence in the targeted companies and amplifies market participants' irrational reactions. Second, the nonsignificant aggregate cross-sectional tests, combined with the significance of the tests at the category level, directly support the multi-category framework proposed in this study. Different types of fake news produce systematically different market effects that cancel each other out in aggregate analyses. This finding has methodological implications for future research, as well as practical implications for business organisations, as it shows that the risk of misinformation cannot be managed uniformly; it must be understood in terms of mechanism, sectoral proximity, and market structure. Third, the inclusion of null cases (Category D) provides internal methodological validation absent from most previous research. Confirming that events where no effect is expected produce no measurable effect strengthens the causal interpretation of significant findings in the other categories and demonstrates that the methodology does not generate systematic false positives.
The study expands the existing literature in several dimensions. Compared to Arcuri et al. (2023), it covers a more recent period, a larger sample, and introduces the multi-category framework and validation through null cases. Compared to Louchez (2025), it integrates the insulin contagion case into a broader cross-sectional analysis that allows for comparison across misinformation mechanisms. Compared to Clarke et al. (2021), it includes SEC- documented pump-and-dump schemes as one of five categories, not as the sole focus.
Several limitations should be acknowledged. Small sample sizes at the category level limit statistical power for cross-category comparisons and cross-sectional regression. As confirmed fake news events continue to accumulate, future studies with larger samples per category may detect cross-sectional determinants that are undetectable in the current setting. Furthermore, the single-factor market model, while standard, may underestimate extreme market movements in contexts of information shock.
For practitioners and regulators, the findings highlight that the risk of misinformation is not uniform. Contagion effects in oligopolistic markets pose the most systematic threat, while large-cap firms with high institutional ownership demonstrate relative resilience. The documented contagion transmission rate of 73-80% within the insulin oligopoly suggests that misinformation can rapidly spread beyond the target company and affect entire sectors, with implications for competition, reputation, and stability. From a broader perspective, the results show that fake news affects the efficient functioning of markets by distorting the informational signals on which investors and other stakeholders base their decisions. In this sense, combating disinformation is not only about protecting investors, but also about strengthening organisational sustainability, communicational transparency, and an economic environment based on trust and rational decision-making.
