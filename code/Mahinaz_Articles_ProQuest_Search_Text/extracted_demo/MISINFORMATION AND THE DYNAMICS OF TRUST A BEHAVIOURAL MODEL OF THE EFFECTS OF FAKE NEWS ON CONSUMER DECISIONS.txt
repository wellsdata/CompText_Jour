Abstract
The study analyses the impact of exposure to fake news on consumer behaviour in the digital environment by investigating the relationships among information credibility assessment, brand trust, brand loyalty, and purchase intention, with a focus on media literacy as a cognitive protection mechanism. The research is grounded in the information economy and the Stimulus-Organism-Response framework and uses an explanatory quantitative approach. The data were collected via questionnaire from 294 active online consumers and analysed using PLS-SEM structural modelling.
The results highlight that exposure to fake news significantly influences the assessment of information credibility and media literacy, suggesting the activation of mechanisms of cognitive vigilance and selective verification of brand information. Assessing the credibility of information and media literacy contributes positively to building trust in the brand, and trust in the brand is emerging as a central determinant of loyalty and purchase intent. The study contributes to the literature by highlighting that exposure to fake news can activate adaptive cognitive responses, and that media literacy plays an essential role in filtering information and strengthening consumer resilience.
Keywords: fake news, misinformation, brand trust, consumer behaviour, information credibility, purchase intention.

Introduction
The expansion of the digital environment has transformed the information infrastructure of the contemporary economy, reducing the costs of producing and distributing information, but has also increased the vulnerability of markets to information distortions. In this context, the phenomenon of fake news - defined as the deliberate or accidental dissemination of false information presented as legitimate news - has gone beyond the political and media sphere, becoming a variable with direct economic implications (Farhoudinia et al., 2025). Recent literature shows that exposure to disinformation alters the structure of informational judgments, trust in institutions, and individuals' decision-making behaviours (Sharif et al., 2022; Van Zoonen et al., 2024). In consumer markets, where decisions are essentially based on informational signals, the distortion of these signals can generate systemic effects on the functioning of economic mechanisms, leading to lower investor confidence, destabilisation of the market structure, and increased operational costs for market participants (Ayanbode and Stanley, 2025).
Efficient markets depend on the credibility, utility, quality, and accuracy of information flows, as well as on economic actors' ability to assess the quality of the information available to them (Sabiri and Tahiri, 2025). When the information environment is influenced by fake news, consumers face increased uncertainty, higher verification costs, difficulties in processing market signals, and suboptimal decision-making (Lewandowsky et al., 2017). These conditions affect trust, an essential element of economic exchanges and the stability of relations between organisations and consumers (Lim et al., 2024). Trust functions as a mechanism for reducing perceived risk and as a relational infrastructure of markets (Chaudhuri and Holbrook, 2001). Therefore, disinformation is not just a communication problem, but an economic distortion that affects consumer behaviour and organisational performance (Di Domenico and Ding, 2023).
In this context, research highlights that fake news can influence consumers' behavioural attitudes and intentions through cognitive mechanisms related to the assessment of information credibility (Visentin et al., 2019; Sharif et al., 2022; Verma and Nayak, 2024). When brand information is perceived as inaccurate or manipulative, the negative transfer of credibility undermines trust in the economic actors associated with it (Mohit et al., 2026). This dynamic has direct consequences on purchase intent and brand loyalty, generating volatility in market behaviour (Cham et al., 2024). In an information ecosystem characterised by information overload and the rise of manipulative content, consumers' ability to critically evaluate information becomes essential to reduce vulnerability to disinformation (Hu and Krishen, 2019). Thus, repeated exposure to fake news can activate adaptive cognitive responses, leading consumers to become more selective in evaluating sources and attributing credibility to information. At the same time, media literacy serves as a cognitive protection mechanism, supporting the verification of information and the decision-making process in an ever-changing environment (Maksl et al., 2015; Fendt et al., 2023). Although research on fake news is numerous, most studies focus on the political and social impact of disinformation, while the economic and behavioural implications remain insufficiently explored. There is a clear need for integration between the information economy and consumer behaviour research to explain how information distortions influence concrete economic decisions. Starting from these premises, the purpose of this article is to empirically analyse the economic implications of fake news on consumer behaviour by developing and testing an integrative behavioural model that explains how exposure to disinformation influences the assessment of information credibility, brand trust, brand loyalty, and purchase intention, with media literacy as a mediating mechanism of cognitive reactions generated by informational distortions. The novelty of the study lies in the approach to fake news as an economic phenomenon with systemic effects on the functioning of markets, by integrating the information economy with the literature of consumer behaviour, and by highlighting the fact that exposure to an unstable information environment can activate adaptive mechanisms of verification, filtering, and selective attribution of credibility. The results of the study provide relevant insights for organisations, digital platforms, and public decision-makers interested in protecting consumers in an unstable information environment. The article is structured as follows: the next section presents the theoretical framework and hypotheses of the research, followed by the methodology used, the analysis of the empirical results, and the conclusions of the study.
1. Theoretical Framework - Presentation of the Conceptual Model
The expansion of the digital environment has amplified the circulation of information about brands and products, but simultaneously increased consumers' vulnerability to misinformation. The digital transformation has facilitated access to information, but it has also predisposed consumers to manipulation through fake news. In this digital ecosystem, the results of the management process can also be quantified by the extent to which it uses digital tools to provide a safe and authentic information environment (Banciu et al., 2023). The concept of fake news refers to fabricated content or misleading information deliberately presented under the guise of legitimate sources, imitating journalistic forms while failing to meet the standards of accuracy and verification specific to professional media (Lazer et al., 2018). Fake news is not just incorrect information, but a structural distortion of the information ecosystem, as it exploits the trust mechanisms associated with institutional communication (Armeen et al., 2025). Tandoc et al. (2018) note that fake news reproduces the style and structure of authentic media content precisely to undermine the public's ability to distinguish between verified information and manipulative content. The presentation of fake news in forms associated with legitimate sources diminishes consumers' ability to critically evaluate information and distorts the cognitive processes of credibility assessment, thereby increasing vulnerability to disinformation in the digital ecosystem (Sitaula et al., 2020).
In this context, the credibility of the source becomes a central factor in evaluating online information about brands. Studies on quality journalism highlight that the reputation of media outlets is built on consistency, accuracy, and editorial responsibility (Kovach and Rosenstiel, 2014), and consumers use the name and visual identity of sources as implicit benchmarks for assessing the credibility of information (Kümpel, 2019). The expansion of online disinformation is one of the most significant challenges for the contemporary information ecosystem, drawing the attention of researchers and public institutions to its social and economic effects (Lazer et al., 2018; Nedelcu and Balaban, 2021). The literature analyses the negative effects of fake news on consumer trust and behaviour. Beyond these disruptive effects, the present study proposes a complementary perspective, arguing that repeated exposure to fake news can activate adaptive mechanisms of cognitive vigilance, leading consumers to become more selective in assessing the accuracy, authenticity, and verifiability of brand information.
H1a. Exposure to fake news (FN) positively influences the assessment of the credibility of online brand information (CRED) by activating cognitive vigilance, leading consumers to selectively credit information perceived as accurate, authentic, and verifiable.
Despite the degradation of information credibility, information filtering and verifying skills are becoming strategic cognitive resources for consumers, highlighting the importance of media literacy in decision making and in reducing vulnerability to disinformation (Berger et al., 2025). Media literacy is an integrated set of knowledge and skills that enables individuals to access, analyse, and critically evaluate media messages, understanding the conditions under which they are produced and distributed (Zhao et al., 2023). In this regard, organisations can contribute to strengthening the information ecosystem by supporting educational programs carried out in collaboration with digital platforms aimed at developing media literacy and critical information verification skills among consumers (Moisescu et al., 2026). In the proposed model, media literacy is not treated solely as a general protective feature, but as a cognitive mechanism through which exposure to fake news drives more intense information-checking and filtering behaviour.
H1b. Exposure to fake news (FN) has a positive effect on media literacy (ML) by stimulating behaviours of verification and critical evaluation of online information.
This effect does not imply a deterministic relationship, but reflects a process of cognitive adaptation through which repeated exposure to problematic content can stimulate verification and critical evaluation behaviours.
The credibility of information is a key determinant of brand trust in digital environments, where consumers rely on it to reduce the uncertainty associated with purchase decisions (Dandona et al., 2025). In the absence of clear verification mechanisms, message credibility serves as a proxy for direct experience, shaping evaluations of brand quality and integrity (Kothari et al., 2025). When information is perceived as consistent, verifiable, and from legitimate sources, it reinforces perceptions of predictability and safety, central elements in the formation of trust (Visentin et al., 2019).
H2. The assessment of the credibility of online brand information (CRED) has a positive effect on brand trust (TRUST).
In this context, media literacy plays an important intermediary role, as consumers with stronger critical appraisal skills are better able to distinguish between credible information and manipulative content, thereby influencing trust in brands (Shakir et al., 2025). The credibility of the information not only affects the evaluation of the message itself but also shapes the perception of the organisation or the associated brand, contributing to the formation of relational trust capital (Kemeç and Yüksel, 2021). Research shows that media literacy integrates cognitive and reflective dimensions, including both understanding the structures of the media industry and the ability to critically interpret content (Ashley et al., 2013). At the same time, it is not limited to self-perceived competence, but involves the development of complex critical thinking, manifested through habits of questioning, verifying, and contextualising information (Schilder and Redmond, 2019). High levels of media literacy contribute to reduced vulnerability to manipulative content (Jones-Jang et al., 2019) and are associated with a more in-depth analysis of information and a more sceptical attitude towards the apparent credibility of sources (Maksl et al., 2015). Within the proposed model, media literacy is not conceptualised as a substitute for brand trust, but rather as a facilitator of critical evaluation of information, which, in turn, indirectly influences the formation of trust judgments and behavioural intentions.
H3a. Media literacy (ML) has a positive effect on the evaluation of the credibility of online information about brands (CRED).
H3b. Media literacy (ML) has a positive effect on brand trust (TRUST).
Media literacy favours the assumption of a reflective stance towards commercial messages, allowing consumers to integrate information into their own system of values and preferences (Park and Martuza, 2026). This cognitive internalisation transforms the consumer from a receiver into a deliberative actor, and purchasing decisions become the result of a conscious evaluation rather than a reaction to superficial informational stimuli (Arsalani et al., 2025). From the perspective of planned behaviour theory, cognitive competencies do not directly influence behaviour but are translated into judgments of trust and attitudes that mediate the relationship between knowledge and action (La Barbera and Ajzen, 2021). In digital markets, where exposure to contradictory content is constant, media literacy supports behavioural consistency and increases the likelihood that positive brand reviews will translate into real purchase intentions.
H3c. Media literacy (ML) has a positive effect on purchase intention (PI).
Brand trust is a central mechanism of exchange relationships in digital markets, reducing perceived risk and facilitating the transformation of cognitive assessments into concrete behaviours. The relational marketing literature shows that trust is a direct predictor of purchase intent (Popa et al., 2022), as consumers tend to prefer brands, they perceive as predictable, responsible, and interest-oriented (Nagalakshmi et al., 2025). At the same time, trust has a long-term relational dimension that strengthens attachment to the brand and stimulates loyalty behaviours, including recommending and maintaining the commercial relationship (Kaur et al., 2025). Brand loyalty should be understood as an evolutionary process, derived from the accumulation of trust capital built through repeated, satisfactory experiences and the maintenance of a coherent, predictable relationship with consumers (Parris and Guzmán, 2023; Uzir et al., 2025). In this sense, loyalty supports consumer retention and contributes to organisations' long-term performance (Agu et al., 2024). Looking at both the short-term and long-term dimensions of consumer behaviour, connections mediated by brand trust provide a better explanation than the direct one (Popa et al., 2022). In informational contexts marked by uncertainty and misinformation, the role of trust becomes even more pronounced, as it functions as the main filter through which consumers validate brands.
H4a. Brand trust (TRUST) has a positive effect on purchase intention (PI).
H4b. Brand trust (TRUST) has a positive effect on brand loyalty (LOY).
H5. Brand loyalty (LOY) has a positive effect on purchase intention (PI).
The relationships between exposure to fake news, the assessment of information credibility, and consumer behaviour involve intermediate cognitive mechanisms, as media literacy reduces vulnerability to disinformation by developing critical analysis capacity (Jones-Jang et al., 2019), and information credibility influences purchase intent by internalising brand trust as a mechanism for transforming cognitive evaluations into consumer behaviours (Angmo et al., 2025), a process accentuated in digital social media, where the transferability of credibility facilitates the formation of trusting relationships between consumers and brands (Idrees et al., 2022). Therefore, both media literacy and brand trust function as intermediate factors that explain how information influences the consumption decision, justifying the testing of mediation relationships in the proposed model, namely:
H6. Media literacy (ML) mediates the relationship between exposure to fake news (FN) and the evaluation of the credibility of online brand information (CRED). (FN ML CRED).
H7. Brand trust (TRUST) mediates the relationship between the evaluation of brand information credibility (CRED) and purchase intention (PI). (CRED TRUST PI).
To systematise the direct relations elaborated within the theoretical framework previously presented, the research proposes the conceptual model illustrated in Figure no. 1, which captures the mechanisms by which fake news influences consumer perceptions and behaviours. The proposed conceptual model thus captures a complex causal chain, in which informational distortions propagate through successive cognitive mechanisms, affecting trust and consumer behaviour.
The proposed conceptual model integrates perspectives from information economics, relational marketing and media literacy studies to explain how disinformation influences consumption decisions in the digital environment. The relationships among exposure to fake news, assessing the credibility of information, media literacy, and trust in the brand outline a complex mechanism by which cognitive processes are translated into economic behaviours. The model suggests that consumer reactions to misinformation are not exclusively passive but include adaptive and informational filtering strategies that shape purchase intent and brand loyalty. In this sense, the theoretical framework provides an integrated basis for understanding the interaction between information skills and the dynamics of trust in digital markets. To empirically evaluate these relationships and test the formulated hypotheses, a methodological approach is needed that allows simultaneous analysis of the direct and indirect effects among latent variables.
2. Research Methodology
The present study adopts a quantitative, explanatory approach to test a behavioural model analysing the effects of exposure to fake news on consumer decisions. The research is based on the information economy paradigm and the Stimulus-Organism-Response (SOR) theoretical framework, which explains how stimuli in the information environment influence individuals' internal cognitive processes and economic behaviour. The SOR model describes consumer behaviour as a dynamic process in which stimuli from the external environment are filtered through internal cognitive mechanisms and transformed into behavioural responses, providing an integrative framework for understanding the psychological processes underlying economic decisions (Jacoby, 2002). In this logic, exposure to fake news is treated as a disruptive informational stimulus; the assessment of information credibility and media literacy are internal cognitive mechanisms, and brand trust, brand loyalty, and purchase intention express the resulting behavioural responses. The proposed model investigates the relationships among exposure to fake news, the assessment of brand information credibility, brand trust, purchase intention, brand loyalty, and the role of media literacy as a mechanism for cognitive protection and information filtering.
The data were collected through a structured questionnaire, based on scales validated in the literature on disinformation, information credibility, and consumer behaviour. The tool contains items formulated on a 7-point Likert scale (1 = total disagreement; 7 = total agreement), allowing for the gradual measurement of respondents' perceptions. Within this research, constructs are operationalised at the perceptual and self-evaluative level. Exposure to fake news (FN) reflects the perceived frequency of contact with false, contradictory, distorted, or unverified information about brands online, without necessarily implying acceptance of such information as true. The information credibility assessment (CRED) captures the degree to which respondents value online brand information as accurate, authentic, consistent, verifiable, and trustworthy. Media literacy (ML) refers to an individual's perceived ability to verify sources, compare information, and critically evaluate online content. This operationalisation allows the interpretation of relationships among variables from the perspective of cognitive mechanisms of vigilance, filtering, and selective attribution of credibility in an information environment perceived as unstable.
The constructs used in the research are exposure to fake news (FN), information credibility assessment (CRED), trust in the brand (TRUST), purchase intention (PI), brand loyalty (LOY), and media literacy (ML), (Table no. 1).
Data collection was conducted online by uploading the questionnaire to the google forms platform and distributing the fillable link in the digital environment. Participation was voluntary and anonymous, and respondents were informed of the research's exclusively academic purpose. No personally identifiable data was collected, and the results are reported at an aggregate level. The sample consisted of consumers active in the online environment, selected through convenience sampling, appropriate for exploratory and explanatory studies in behavioural research. Although the convenience sampling method limits the generalisability of the results, it is appropriate to the study's explanatory objectives and is frequently used in research on consumer behaviour in the digital environment.
The sample size (294 respondents) meets the methodological requirements for PLS-SEM structural modelling, which recommends a significantly higher number of observations than the maximum number of structural links oriented towards an endogenous construct. The PLS- SEM method was chosen due to the model's predictive nature, the high number of structural relationships, and its suitability for medium-sized samples. The demographic structure of the sample includes variables such as age, gender, average income, level of education, and frequency of use of the online environment for information and shopping, these are used to describe the respondents' profile (Table no. 2).
The sample structure indicates a relatively balanced distribution by age groups, with a higher concentration in the 25-34 age group, suggesting a significant representation of digitally active consumers. From a gender perspective, the sample is dominated by female respondents, a common feature of studies on online consumer behaviour. The high educational level of the respondents - most of whom have university studies - indicates a cognitive profile favourable to the evaluation of digital information, relevant for the analysis of media literacy and exposure to disinformation. The distribution of income reflects a diversified economic structure, allowing the investigation of consumption behaviour in different socioeconomic contexts. Regarding the frequency of online shopping, a significant proportion of respondents report regular purchasing behaviour, confirming the integration of the digital environment into the consumption routine. The main sources of information are dominated by social media and influencers, highlighting increased exposure to unfiltered information flows and underscoring the relevance of the study on the impact of fake news on purchasing decisions.
Before the structural model analysis, the data were checked for the consistency and completeness of the responses. No significant missing values or invalid responses were identified, which confirms the quality of the database used in the analysis. The characteristics of the sample and the distribution of the responses support the robustness of the PLS-SEM estimation procedure, a suitable method for predictive models with multiple latent variables. The assessment of the collinearity and overall adequacy of the model indicates VIF values below the recommended critical thresholds, ranging from 1.554 to 4.859, falling below the threshold of 5 recommended in the PLS-SEM literature, suggesting that common method bias does not significantly affect research results (Kock, 2015). At the same time, to assess the overall adequacy of the model, specific PLS-SEM indicators were analysed. The SRMR (Standardised Root Mean Square Residual) value for the saturated model is 0.63, indicating an acceptable level of model fit (Henseler et al., 2016). 3. Results and Discussion
The results section presents the evaluation of the measurement and structural models, following the standard procedure for PLS-SEM analysis. Data analysis was performed using SmartPLS software (SmartPLS GmbH, Germany), version 4.1.1.7 (Ringle et al., 2024). In the first stage, the quality of the research tool was analysed by testing the reliability and validity of the latent constructs. The evaluation of the measurement model is essential to confirm that the indicators used adequately reflect the theoretical variables under investigation and enable the correct interpretation of the structural relationships between them. The results presented in Table no. 3 indicate a high level of internal consistency for all the constructs analysed. Cronbach's alpha and composite reliability exceed the recommended threshold of 0.70 for all constructs, and the AVEs are above 0.50, supporting the reliability and convergent validity of the scales.
The discriminant validity of the constructs is confirmed by the heterotrait-monotrait ratio (HTMT) and the Fornell-Larcker method; the values obtained indicate adequate differentiation between the latent constructs (Table 4). The HTMT values in Table no. 4 are below the critical threshold of 0.90 recommended in the literature, indicating adequate separation among constructs and the absence of significant conceptual overlap (Hair et al., 2022).
The relatively high correlations observed between trust, loyalty, and the assessment of information credibility are consistent with existing literature, which emphasises the interdependence of these dimensions in consumer behaviour (Visentin et al., 2019). After validation of the measurement model, the PLS-SEM structural model was estimated to investigate the causal relationships among the latent constructs in the conceptual framework. As shown in Figure no. 2, the path coefficients and R2 values confirm the model's consistency, indicating a moderate to high explanatory capacity for the endogenous variables (R2 between 0.521 and 0.693).
Exposure to fake news significantly affects the assessment of information credibility, suggesting that a distorted information environment activates cognitive processes of analysis, filtering, and validation of brand messages. This result does not necessarily indicate a widespread erosion of trust in information, but rather an intensification of the way consumers selectively attribute credibility to content perceived as accurate, authentic, and verifiable. The credibility assessment is then transferred to brand trust, confirming the existence of a cognitive mechanism through which information processing influences the perception of the associated economic actor. In turn, brand trust is proving to be a central predictor of consumer behaviour, influencing both loyalty and purchase intent. In this framework, media literacy plays a significant intermediary role, explaining how exposure to fake news can lead to a more rigorous process of verification, source comparison, and critical evaluation of information. From the perspective of the Elaboration Likelihood Model (ELM), the perception of a high informational risk favours the activation of central cognitive processing, characterised by careful assessment of information credibility (Petty and Cacioppo, 1986). Therefore, consumer reactions to disinformation reflect not only passive vulnerability, but also adaptive cognitive mechanisms that help stabilise trust, loyalty, and purchase intent in a digital environment marked by uncertainty.
Table no. 5 presents the path coefficients estimated by the bootstrapping procedure, along with the associated statistics (sample mean, standard deviation, t-values, and p-values) used to test the structural model assumptions. The analysis indicates that all proposed relationships are statistically significant, as the p-values are below the 0.05 threshold and the t-values exceed the 1.96 critical value, confirming the empirical validity of the formulated hypotheses.
Regarding the relationships between variables, exposure to fake news (FN) exerts a positive and significant effect on the credibility assessment (CRED) (β = 0.656; t = 7.849; p < 0.001), supporting the H1a hypothesis. This result suggests that the frequency of contact with false, contradictory or distorted information about brands may activate increased cognitive alertness and a more rigorous process of assessing the credibility of subsequent information. This result complements studies that highlight the negative effects of fake news on trust and information evaluation by introducing an adaptive perspective on the cognitive processing of messages (Lazer et al., 2018; Sitaula et al., 2020; Di Domenico et al., 2021). Also, exposure to fake news positively influences media literacy (ML) (β = 0.722; t = 19.146; p < 0.001), which confirms the H1b hypothesis. The result suggests that the experience of contact with a problematic information environment can activate the behaviours of verification and critical evaluation of informational content, being empirically supported by research that highlights the role of exposure to manipulative content in the development of critical analysis and filtering skills. online information (Jones-Jones et al., 2019; Maksl et al., 2015).
The assessment of the credibility of online brand information (CRED) has a significant positive effect on brand trust (TRUST) (β = 0.581; t = 9.334; p < 0.001), confirming the H2 hypothesis and indicating that the perception of the accuracy, authenticity and verifiability of information contributes to strengthening the relationship of trust between consumers and brands. The literature highlights the fact that the perception of credibility and quality of information directly influences the level of trust of consumers and the development of longterm relationships between consumers and brands. Consequently, brands perceived as credible and transparent tend to generate higher levels of trust and loyalty among consumers (Açikgöz et al., 2024; Ali et al., 2025). In this sense, the assessment of the credibility of information functions as a mechanism for reducing the perceived risk and as a cognitive infrastructure of exchange relations in the digital environment.
The results also highlight the relevant role of media literacy (ML) in the model. This positively influences the assessment of information credibility (β = 0.175; t = 1.994; p = 0.046), purchase intent (PI) (β = 0.180; t = 2.545; p = 0.011) and trust in the brand (TRUST) (β = 0.329; t = 5.466; p < 0.001), confirming the hypotheses H3a, H3b and H3c. The magnitude of the coefficients suggests that media literacy exerts the strongest influence on brand trust, while the effect on the assessment of information credibility is more moderate. These findings indicate that the level of media literacy contributes not only to the processing of information online, but also to the formation of consumer attitudes and behavioural intentions. The results are consistent with recent studies highlighting the role of media literacy in strengthening consumer resilience to disinformation and developing more effective mechanisms for assessing digital content (Song and Kim, 2025; Dwivedi and Sen, 2025).
In terms of relationships associated with consumer behaviour, brand trust (TRUST) exerts the strongest effect on brand loyalty (LOY) (β = 0.775; t = 22.261; p < 0.001), supporting the H4b hypothesis and indicating that trust is a key determinant of sustainable relationships between consumers and brands. Also, brand trust positively influences purchase intent (PI) (β = 0.328; t = 4.233; p < 0.001), confirming the H4a hypothesis. At the same time, brand loyalty (LOY) has a positive and significant effect on purchase intent (PI) (β = 0.384; t = 4.896; p < 0.001), supporting the H5 hypothesis and highlighting the fact that stable and repeated brand relationships contribute to strengthening future consumer behaviours. Recent literature confirms that trust-based relationships support consumer behavioural stability and reduce the likelihood of negative reactions in uncertain informational contexts (Moisescu et al., 2026). Overall, the results of the structural model confirm the empirical consistency of the proposed conceptual framework, demonstrating that exposure to fake news, media literacy, information credibility assessment, and brand trust are interconnected in an explanatory mechanism that significantly influences consumer loyalty and purchase intention. Also interpreted from an ELM perspective, these results suggest that the perception of a high informational risk can lead to a more intense level of cognitive processing, favouring the verification of sources, the comparison of information, and the more careful evaluation of the credibility of content about brands.
Table no. 6 presents the specific indirect effects estimated by the bootstrapping procedure, highlighting the mediation mechanisms by which model variables influence consumer behaviour. The results indicate that the relevant indirect effects analysed are statistically significant (p < 0.05), confirming the existence of mediation relationships between the model variables.
Exposure to fake news indirectly influences purchase intent through media literacy, the relationship between FN ML PI being significant (β = 0.130; p = 0.011). Media literacy also mediates the relationship between exposure to fake news and brand trust (FN ML TRUST; β = 0.237; p < 0.001), highlighting the role of critical appraisal skills in shaping perceptions about brands. The results also confirm the role of trust in the brand as a mechanism for transmitting cognitive effects on consumer behaviour. The ML TRUST PI relationship is significant (β = 0.108; p = 0.002), and more complex causal chains, such as FN ML TRUST LOY PI, indicate that the effects of disinformation gradually propagate towards loyalty and purchase intent.
This interpretation is also supported by the recent literature on the effects of disinformation in the digital environment. Specifically, the model's results confirm that exposure to fake news significantly influences how consumers evaluate brand information. Although numerous studies show that disinformation can erode consumers' trust in online information and affect the way messages about products and organisations are evaluated, these effects should not be understood exclusively in the sense of a linear decrease in credibility, but also in the sense of activating cognitive mechanisms of vigilance and filtering (Di Domenico et al., 2021; Lim and Schmälzle, 2024). In addition, the assessment of the credibility of the information indirectly influences the purchase intention through trust in the brand (CRED TRUST PI, β = 0.190; p < 0.001). Overall, the results confirm the existence of multiple mediation mechanisms, demonstrating that the impact of fake news on consumer behaviour is transmitted through interdependent cognitive processes involving media literacy, the degree of evaluation of information credibility and trust in the brand. In today's information ecosystem, dominated by digital platforms and social media, disinformation is becoming an important source of distortion of market signals, influencing consumer perceptions of brands and products and increasing the need for a more careful evaluation of available information (Burcă-Voicu et al., 2025).
The research results also highlight the significant influence of brand trust on loyalty and purchase intent. Trust helps to strengthen the relationship between consumers and brands, reducing perceived uncertainty, and facilitating purchasing decisions. In this context, a high level of trust favours the development of loyalty and increases the likelihood of maintaining the relationship with the brand in the long term. In addition, the analysis of indirect effects highlights the fact that the relationships between variables are not only manifested directly, but also through mediation mechanisms. Media literacy and brand trust function as intermediate variables that convey the effects of exposure to fake news on consumer behaviour. Thus, the influence of misinformation on purchase intention and loyalty is propagated through a relational and cognitive mechanism that involves assessing the credibility of the information and strengthening the relationship of trust with the brand. Overall, the results of the study confirm recent theoretical perspectives according to which disinformation influences consumer behaviour through a complex mechanism that involves assessing the credibility of information, building trust in the brand, and developing loyalty relationships. The effects of fake news on markets are not limited to the informational dimension but extend to the cognitive processes through which consumers filter, interpret and capitalise on information in the adoption of economic decisions.
Conclusions
The empirical analysis of the structural model indicates that the influence of fake news on consumer behaviour primarily operates through intermediate cognitive and relational mechanisms rather than through isolated direct effects. The results suggest that exposure to fake news activates a process of critical evaluation of information credibility, supported by media literacy, which functions as a mechanism of protection and cognitive filtering. Consumers with a higher level of competence tend to process online content more rigorously, compare sources, and selectively attribute credibility to information, thereby reducing decision-making uncertainty generated by a distorted information environment. This evaluation process then transfers to trust in the brand, which influences loyalty and purchase intent. At the same time, brand loyalty acts as a mechanism to strengthen purchase intention, reducing the vulnerability of consumer behaviour in conditions of informational risk. Therefore, the results confirm the stratified and interdependent nature of the decision-making process, highlighting that the impact of disinformation propagates through a succession of cognitive and relational relationships, rather than through simple linear effects.
From a theoretical perspective, the study makes relevant contributions by conceptualising fake news as an economic distortion and by integrating the information economy with the literature of consumer behaviour, relational marketing, and ELM. The proposed model expands the Stimulus-Organism-Response framework by including media literacy as an intermediate cognitive variable and by highlighting that the perception of high informational risk can activate deeper cognitive processing. In addition, the research shows that the relationships between information credibility assessment, brand trust, and consumer behaviour are supported by layered cognitive processes, providing a more nuanced perspective on the dynamics of market decisions in an unstable information ecosystem.
The practical implications of the results are relevant for organisations, digital platforms, and public decision-makers. For organisations, the conclusions underline that the management of information credibility must be treated as a strategic element of the relationship with consumers, not just as a dimension of commercial communication. In concrete terms, organisations can counter the effects of disinformation by systematically monitoring false brand information in the digital environment, reacting quickly and transparently to misleading content, publishing verifiable clarifications and evidence on official channels, as well as by ensuring the consistency of messages conveyed on the website, social media, and other points of contact with consumers. At the same time, developing myth-busting communication formats, up-to-date FAQ sections, and working with credible external sources can help build trust and reduce uncertainty during the buying process. For digital platforms and public institutions, the results support the relevance of media literacy programmes and fact-checking mechanisms as tools to reduce information risks and protect consumers.
At the same time, the study has certain limitations that must be taken into account when interpreting the results. The use of a convenience sample is an important limitation of the study, as it can affect the representativeness of the results and the ability to generalise to a large population of digital consumers. In addition, voluntary participation in research may introduce self-selection bias, and respondents who are more interested in disinformation or more familiar with the digital environment may have been over-represented in the sample. This can influence reported levels of media literacy, cognitive alertness, and critical evaluation of information. At the same time, the cross-cutting nature of the research limits the analysis of the evolution over time of the investigated relationships, and the perceived measurement of exposure to fake news can introduce subjective distortions associated with self-reporting. Future research can address these limitations by using probabilistic samples and longitudinal or experimental designs, which allow the analysis of the evolution over time of the investigated relationships and a more precise identification of the causal mechanisms underlying the relationship between exposure to fake news and consumer behaviour. At the same time, differentiating the types of information sources and expanding the model by including contextual, cultural, or demographic variables could increase the generality of the results and deepen the understanding of how disinformation influences consumer behaviour across different economic contexts.
