Headnote Abstract
The article extends transaction cost economics (TCE) into artificial intelligence (AI)- mediated information markets, specifically AI-regulated fake news. It reframes AI-generated fake news a s a transaction-cost asymmetry problem in which AI compresses production and coordination costs for deceptive actors while raising evaluation and compliance burdens for legitimate ones. Regulatory responses introduce additional imposed costs through compliance, auditing, and transparency requirements, but these are distributed unevenly across actors. A conceptual comparative analysis of the United States, China, and the European Union demonstrates that different AI governance architectures generate distinct market transaction costs (MTCs)-arising from voluntary exchange under uncertainty-and imposed transaction costs (ITCs)-generated by regulatory intervention. It is argued that using the two categories of transaction costs is a useful prerequisite for developing AI regulations that address AI-mediated fake news, particularly in the EU's more legalist and bureaucratic framework, compared with the US's liberal and competitive or China's stateled, dual-use regulatory frameworks. It is further argued that, from a TCE perspective, EU AI regulatory development would benefit from a greater focus on reducing market transaction costs rather than increasing imposed transaction costs. It concludes that effective AI governance, which affects fake news, depends not on regulatory intensity alone but on how institutional mechanisms distribute transaction costs among market actors. The study is designed as a qualitative analysis, grounded in a conceptual and institutional-comparative approach, suited to a field in which normative architectures and governance mechanisms are more analytically relevant than aggregate quantitative measurement. The analytical framework rests on transaction cost economics, in which the distinction between market transaction costs (MTCs) and imposed transaction costs (ITCs) functions as the central methodological instrument. The review was conducted by consulting secondary sources and the specialized literature at the intersection of artificial intelligence, fake news, and transaction costs, and the bibliographic mapping revealed that analysing AI-mediated fake news from a transaction-cost perspective is an under-investigated field, which grounded the necessity of the present study.
Keywords: fake news, transaction cost economics, governance, artificial intelligence, regulation, globalization, international relations.
JEL Classification: F53, F55, F59.
Introduction
Artificial intelligence (AI) has transformed digital information markets, specifically AI- mediated fake news, by drastically reducing the marginal costs of producing, replicating, and targeting content. The same infrastructure that enables automation, personalisation, and large-scale coordination also facilitates the creation of AI-generated fake news at unprecedented speed and scale. This article argues that AI-mediated information markets, of which AI-mediated fake news is a core category, are characterised by a structural reallocation of transaction costs rather than a general reduction, as the existing literature suggests. AI does not simply lower transaction costs for AI-generated fake news across the board. AI compresses market transaction costs (MTCs) for content generation and coordination, lowering the costs of producing and disseminating deceptive narratives. At the same time, it inflates the costs of search, verification, monitoring, and evaluation for users, journalists, platforms, and markets. Efforts to counter disinformation introduce imposed transaction costs (ITCs) through documentation requirements, auditing obligations, liability regimes, and administrative oversight. These imposed costs are unevenly distributed and are often more burdensome for smaller or public-interest actors than for large platforms that can internalise compliance infrastructure. The persistence of AI-mediated fake news is therefore not merely a technological or cognitive phenomenon; it is the result of a shifting cost structure. Against this background, the article enquires into how AI regulatory architectures reshape the allocation of market and imposed transaction costs associated with AI-mediated fake news.
The analytical framework is grounded in transaction cost economics (TCE), particularly the distinction between market transaction costs-arising from voluntary exchange under uncertainty-and imposed transaction costs-generated by regulatory and bureaucratic intervention. The article proceeds in three stages. First, it clarifies the MTC/ITC distinction and situates it within the broader TCE framework. Second, it applies this classification to AI- mediated information environments, identifying how digital infrastructures simultaneously compress production costs and inflate verification costs. Third, it conducts a comparative analysis of AI regulatory regimes in the United States (US), China, and the European Union (EU), examining how different institutional architectures redistribute transaction costs across actors. For each, it looks at how AI rules affect: (1) market transaction costs (production, coordination, verification), (2) imposed transaction costs (regulatory compliance), and (3) incentives around disinformation and moderation. The article focuses on the United States, China, and the European Union because they embody the three most influential and contrasting AI regulatory architectures-market-led, stateled, and legalistic rights-based -whose distinct allocations of market and imposed transaction costs critically shape the global conditions under which AI-mediated fake news is produced, amplified, and governed. Since AI-mediated fake n ews is governed by AI regulatory frameworks, this directly motivates and justifies a comparative method that contrasts those frameworks.
The article is organised into five sections, followed by conclusions. Section 1 presents the review of the literature on transaction cost economics and clarifies the distinction between market transaction costs (MTCs) and imposed transaction costs (ITCs). Section 2 analyses AI-mediated fake news as a subcategory of disinformation and how digital infrastructures reconfigure, for example, the collection and evaluation of open-source information (opensource intelligence - OSINT). Section 3 addresses the regulation of artificial intelligence in the United States, China, and the European Union from an institutional perspective, then highlights how the different architectures redistribute MTCs and ITCs. Section 4 describes the research methodology, explaining the use of the MTC/ITC distinction as a conceptual and comparative instrument, as well as the need to analyse the three regulatory models. Section 5 presents the results and discussion, focusing on public-policy implications and the calibration of governance mechanisms. The conclusions synthesise the article's main contributions and indicate future research directions.
The central claim is that AI governance should be evaluated not by regulatory intensity but by cost allocation. The US model concentrates imposed costs in security-sensitive domains while maintaining relatively low domestic compliance burdens; China centralises imposed costs and aligns coordination incentives with state objectives; and the EU distributes broad risk-based compliance obligations that may unintentionally favour incumbents capable of absorbing regulatory burdens. These regimes generate distinct configurations of market and imposed transaction costs, with differing implications for information pluralism, innovation, and democratic resilience.
The article contributes to three respects. First, it extends transaction cost economics into AI- mediated information markets, showing that digital infrastructures reconfigure the internal composition and distribution of transaction costs rather than merely lowering them. Second, it reframes fake news as a transaction-cost asymmetry problem in which AI compresses production and coordination costs for deceptive actors while raising evaluation and compliance burdens for legitimate ones. Third, it provides a comparative institutional analysis of how major AI regulatory architectures redistribute market and imposed transaction costs, offering a structured framework for calibrating AI governance. By focusing on cost redistribution rather than regulatory volume, the article provides a novel lens for understanding AI-mediated fake news and for designing institutional arrangements that mitigate disinformation without entrenching dominant intermediaries or suppressing beneficial experimentation. The research is explicitly delimited to AI-generated fake news as a specific subcategory within the broader fake-news and misinformat ion domain. It does not seek to cover all forms of misinformation or non-AI- mediated disinformation, but focuses on instances where the creation, targeting, and diffusion of fake news are materially shaped by AI systems and AI governance. This delineation is important because AI-mediated fake news shares the core features of fake news -strategic deception and information asymmetries-yet operates under a distinct technological and regulatory environment As a limitation, the analysis therefore abstracts from fake-news dynamics that are weakly affected by AI and from purely psychological or media-effects explanations, concentrating instead on the institutional and transaction-cost consequences of AI's involvement in the fake-news subset.
1. Review of the scientific TCE literature
Hicks (1935), Baumol (1952), and Arrow (1974) treated transaction costs as the costs of running the economic system, while Demsetz (1964) and Allen (1991) shifted attention to the costs of trading and of establishing and protecting ownership titles. Coase (1937) linked these costs to the existence and size of the firm and to the legal system, formalising the famous counterfactual, the Coase Theorem. Williamson (1979, 2010) then consolidated TCE, focusing on asset specificity, uncertainty, and frequency as determinants of governance structures, and interpreting transaction costs as ex post adaptation and safeguarding costs within and between organisations. North (1990, 1992) placed transaction costs at the core of institutional change by defining them as the costs of measuring attributes and enforcing rights. Marinescu (2012) argues that mainstream institutionalism often deploys transaction costs as an efficiency criterion without fully incorporating the subjective, ex ante opportunity-cos t logic emphasised initially by Menger (1994), Buchanan (1969, 1985), Mises (1966), and Rothbard (1997), thereby blurring the line between analytical convenience and empirically meaningful indicators. Empirical work-from Wallis and North's (1986) macro estimates to sectoral studies in finance, agriculture, environmental policy, and planning (Colby, 1990; Collins & Fabozzi, 1991; Shahab, 2021; Shahab et al., 2018a, 2018b; Whittington, 2012)-has expanded rapidly but remains methodologically heterogeneous and prone to conflating distinct categories of cost. This revelation is relevant to the application of TCE theory to AI-driven fake news, which is discussed further.
Within this evolving literature, Hart (2008) revisits Coase's agenda by introducing aggrievement costs and shading, offering a micro-foundation for firm boundaries that emphasises contractual reference points and perceived entitlements. Rindfleisch (2020) and Bylund (2021) call for de-homogenising Coasean and Williamsonian theories, while Benkler (2017, 2019) re-reads TCE in the context of digital technologies, arguing that collapsing communication and coordination costs enable peer production and new corporate power strategies that a single cost-minimi sing equilibrium cannot capture. These extensions are especially relevant for analysing fake news as a coordination and governance problem in digital media, where platforms, algorithms, and user communities reshape both the level and the distribution of transaction costs in information production and verification. Institutional and property-rights approaches distinguish several cost categories ti ed to how property rights are used and transformed. Transaction costs are the costs of using the price system in market exchanges-information gathering, negotiating, drafting, monitoring, and enforcing contracts needed to uphold exclusive rights in decentralised trade (Coase, 1937, 1960).
Marinescu (2012) proposes a particular taxonomy of transaction costs, based on the differentiation between Market Transaction Costs (MTCs) - resulting from voluntary exchange under natural conditions of uncertainty - and Imposed Transaction Costs (ITCs) - arising from legislative, bureaucratic, and administrative interventions. Within this framework, some reductions in transaction, coordination, or organisation costs can be interpreted as entrepreneurial investments justified by expected profit opportunities (Foss & Foss, 2005; Furubotn, 2002; Kim & Mahoney, 2006; Kirzner, 1997), a perspective that becomes especially salient where digital technologies reconfigure the costs of information acquisition and verification, as in online discourse on fake news. Authors such as Menger (1994), Mises (1966), Buchanan (1969, 1985), Rothbard (1970, 1997), and Hülsmann (2004) underline that cost- including transaction cost-is fundamentally an opportunity-cost category linked to individual expectations and choices, which raises deep problems for empirical operationalisation. Hart's (2008) introduction of aggrievement costs and shading, together with Rindfleisch's (2020) and Bylund's (2021) insistence on distinguishing Coasean ex ante "marketing costs" from Williamsonian ex post adaptation costs, refines this picture by showing how relational breakdowns and perceived unfairness generate new transaction costs in organisations and networks, including digital platforms that host or amplify fake news.
The paper builds on the distinction between MTCs and ITCs, essential for assessing the role of regulation, including in the information space. The paper differentiates between Market Transaction Costs (MTCs), which arise naturally from voluntary contracting under uncertainty, and Imposed Transaction Costs (ITCs), which are generated by state regulation and bureaucratic procedures. Market transaction costs include the effort required to search for information, negotiate, draft, and monitor contracts, and to build reputational assets that support exchange; they are embedded in entrepreneurial processes and can signal beneficial deepening of specialisation and coordination. Imposed transaction costs, by contrast, encompass the monetary and non-monetary bur dens associated with complying with laws and administrative rules-tax procedures, licensing, registration, compulsory use of monopolistic state services such as public notaries, and time spent interacting with bureaucrats (Marinescu, 2013). Because these costs are anchored in formal procedures, they are often easier to measure and thus become primary targets of institutional reform aimed at improving the business environment and expanding economic freedom, for example, via Doing Business-type indicators and cross-country governance metrics. High levels of imposed transaction costs-stemming from heavy regulation, unstable legislation, high taxation, and corruption-characterise interventionist institutional arrangements and tend to depress entrepreneurship and economic performance. In contrast, high market transaction costs do not automatically signal institutional failure and may reflect richer exchanges and more sophisticated contractual arrangements. This article adopts a conceptual and institutional approach rather than an econometric one. It does not attempt to measure aggregate transaction costs, estimate the prevalence of misinformation, or provide a comprehensive theory of misinformation psychology, welfare optimisation, or geopolitical AI competition. Instead, it identifies the mechanisms through which AI regulatory architectures redistribute market and imposed transaction costs across actors pertaining to AI-generated fake news. By isolating this cost-redistribution dimension, the analysis clarifies the effects of institutional design.
2. Fake news mediated by artificial intelligence in the context of transaction costs
This article treats AI-mediated fake news as a specific subcategory within the broader misinformation and disinformation landscape. By focusing on AI-generated fake news, the analysis targets the segment in which actors deliberately exploit digital infrastructures and AI tools to compress production and coordination costs while shifting verification and compliance burdens onto others and allows the article to identify what is analytically novel about AI: the reconfiguration of the transaction-cost structure underlying the production, diffusion, and correction of false content. Misinformation-false or misleading content without intent to deceive-is treated as a context in which users face high search and verification costs while producers face low production and dissemination costs. Disinformation, by contrast, is framed as the strategic use of information for manipulation, allowing the analysis to focus on purposive actors who exploit institutional and technological frictions to lower their own transaction costs while raising others'.
Fong's (2021) model of investor overreaction and subsequent underreaction is read as evidence that fake news increases uncertainty about information veracity, thereby altering the cost-benefit calculus of information acquisition and processing for market participants and raising the opportunity cost of attention. Mignot et al. (2024) argue how polluted information environments raise the effective costs of fundamental analysis, shift trading toward cheaper heuristic-based strategies, and ultimately destabili se prices. Alvestad (2026) shows how AI-generated disinformation reduces the marginal costs of producing credible-looking content, expands the strategic repertoire of propagandists through appeals to authority and values, and complicates linguistic detection, thereby increasing verification costs. Gondwe (2025), by contrast, characterises the countervailing increase in computational, organisational, and regulatory costs associated with deploying high-performing detection models, especially when tools such as L IME and SHAP, as well as high-quality training data (for example, PolitiFactbased corpora), are requir ed. These contributions operationalise AI as both an amplifier of fake news-by compressing production and coordination costs for deceptive content-and a mitigation tool that generates substantial new imposed transaction costs, in line with the paper's central argument about the rise in transaction costs in AI-mediated information environments.
AI-enhanced open-source intelligence (OSINT) provides a particularly clear illustration of how AI technologies and regulatory architectures jointly reshape transaction costs in digital information markets. Once a specialised capability of military and intelligence agencies, OSINT has become widely used in journalism, business, law enforcement, and humanitarian work, relying on social media and other public digital traces as core data sources (Hijazi & Hassan, 2018). Decentralised communities of analysts use geolocation techniques, satellite imagery, and user-generated videos to reconstruct events in quasi-real time, demonstrating the collapse of coordination and aggregation costs. AI-assisted image recognition, automated translation, and large-scale data parsing further reduce production and synthesis costs (MTCp) for investigative actors by lowering the marginal cost of assembling credible evidence from dispersed digital traces.
However, the same infrastructure that lowers verification costs for sophisticated actors simultaneously enables new forms of deception. The visual rhetoric of OSINT can be repurposed for sophisticated disinformation, as observed in conflicts such as Ukraine and Gaza. AI-generated or mislabelled images framed as "open-source proof" circulate through the same decentralised networks, while machine-learning tools reduce the marginal cost of aggregating data, profiling audiences, and laundering misleading narratives through apparently authoritative visual artefacts. The result is that the cost of producing crediblelooking disinformation declines for skilled actors even as the informational surface area expands.
This dynamic inflates verification and evaluation costs (MTCv) for traditional media, factcheckers, and ordinary users. Authenticating claims now requires not only textual scrutiny but also technical analysis of imagery, metadata, and contextual consistency. The burden of contextualisation and privacy protection rises, and disputes over what constitutes legitimate OSINT, intrusive surveillance, or propaganda generate reputational conflict and moderation frictions. In Hart's (2008) terms, contestation over reference points and perceived aggrievement adds further coordination costs within digital communities.
For non-state actors-journalists, NGOs, civil-society groups, and independent investigators-the absence of clear and binding legal frameworks means that OSINT practice is often governed by soft-law instruments and voluntary commitments (Millett, 2023). These arrangements require investments in ethical reflection, training, and self-regulation, but they do not provide the predictable and enforceable constraints associated with mature statutory regimes. Soft-law and evolving hard-law frameworks therefore function as uneven substitutes for formal ITCs, amplifying heterogeneity in the cost structures faced by different actors engaged in AI-enabled information production, verification, and investigation. Taken together, the OSINT example illustrates the layered cost structure identified in the model. AI reduces production and coordination costs, lowers barriers to aggregation for sophisticated actors, and enables scalable deception. Verification costs rise for less technically equipped users, while legal grey zones and soft-law regimes keep formal imposed costs relatively low in some domains and unpredictable in others. Regulatory responses introduce additional compliance burdens that are unevenly distributed. The result is not a simple expansion or contraction of transaction costs, but a redistribution that deepens asymmetries between sophisticated information producers and ordinary users, reinforcing the structural conditions under which AI-mediated fake news can persist.
3. The regulation of artificial intelligence in the US, China, and the EU: an institutional analysis
The article adopts a comparative design because AI-mediated fake news is governed not in isolation, but through broader AI regulatory architectures that differ systematically across jurisdictions. The US, Chinese, and EU models are treated as three archetypal systems- market-led, stateled, and legalistic rightsbased -that organise incentives, constraints, and enforcement in distinct ways. Comparing these approaches along a common transaction-cost lens makes it possible to show how each reallocates. US approaches to AI have evolved from a largely market-driven model to a more explicitly securityoriented framework that still relies heavily on private-sector dynamism (Horowitz, 201 8; Horowitz et al., 2018; Schmidt, 2022). Federal initiatives such as the Obama-era AI reports, the National AI Research & Development (R&D) Plan, and Project Maven programs aim to integrate AI into defence and intelligence and to experiment with new organisational arrangements (Horowitz et al., 2018). At the same time, Washington is tightening controls on technology flows to rivals through export restrictions, investment screening, and debates over "purposeful decoupling" from China in sensitive AI-related supply chains (Schmidt, 2022). The National Security Commission on Artificial Intelligence calls for a comprehensive strategy that couples large-scale federal R&D and microelectronics investment with immigration policies to retain top AI talent, as well as stronger cybersecurity and data-protection measures (Schmidt, 2022). From a regulatory standpoint, this produces a hybrid regime: light ex ante regulation of most commercial AI, targeted security instruments for dual-use and military AI (export controls, Committee on Foreign Investment in the United States reform, classified programs), and early moves toward standards and norms for safety, robustness, and human control in sensitive applications (Horowitz, 2018; Horowitz et al., 2018; Schmidt, 2022).
China's state-led AI strategy fuses regulatory design and industrial policy, with an explicit goal of technological leadership by 2030 (Horowitz et al., 2018; Horowitz, 2018). The New Generation AI Development Plan and follow-on initiatives mobili se central and provincial actors around building a "premier AI innovation centre", designating firms such as Baidu, Alibaba, Tencent, and iFlytek as national platform leaders (Horowitz et al., 2018). Through "military-civil fu sion", the Chinese Communist Party channels advances in commercial AI into the modernisation of the People's Liberation Army (PLA), targeting warfare, joint operations command systems, and AI-enabled decision support (Horowitz et al., 201 8). T his produces a regulatory framework in which data access, platform governance, and research priorities are subordinated to party-state objectives, including social control via integrated surveillance systems. In this context, AI regulation operates less as a constraint and more as a steering mechanism, balancing the promotion of innovation with tight political oversight of platforms, data flows, and cross-border technology transfer.
The EU approaches AI primarily as a governance and rights challenge, leveraging regulatory power to shape global norms while struggling to build competitive AI industries. Europe is depicted as a normative power through General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) and ethics-driven debates on autonomous weapons, human control, and algorithmic discrimination, casting AI regulation as an extension of human rights and humanitarian law (Cummings et al., 2018; Parakilas & Bryce, 2018). This orientation is being entrenched through the AI Act and the Council of Europe's Framework Convention on Artificial Intelligence, which seeks to impose risk-based obligations on highrisk uses, safeguard fundamental rights, and export European standards via the "Brussels Effect." At the same time, exemptions for national security, the abandonment of the AI Liability Directive, and tensions around EuroHPC and compute allocation reveal frictions between principled regulation and industrial or strategic imperatives (Csernatoni, 2025). Debates on "technological sovereignty" illustrate this tension: the EU remains dependent on U.S. cloud and AI platforms and Chinese hardware, yet uses regulation and industrial initiatives (EuroStack, AI Factories, export-control alignment) to reduce dependency while preserving a rights-centric regime (Csernatoni, 2025). Schmidt (2022) notes that fragmentation, multiple languages, and regulatory activism complicate the rise of home-grown platforms, forcing the EU to choose between alignment with U.S. tech governance and a balancing position between U.S. and Chinese spheres.
4. Methodology
The present paper seeks to answer the research question: How do the artificial intelligence regulatory models of the US, China, and the EU influence the structure of the transaction costs (MTCs/ITCs) associated with AI-mediated fake news? The study is designed as a qualitative investigation, grounded in a conceptual and institutional-comparative approach, suited to a field in which normative architectures, the distribution of incentives, and governance mechanisms are more analytically relevant than aggregate quantitative measurement.
The article's analytical framework is grounded in transaction cost economics (TCE), more precisely in the distinction between market transaction costs (MTCs) - arising from voluntary exchange under conditions of uncertainty - and imposed transaction costs (ITCs), generated by legislative and bureaucratic/administrative interventions. In the article, this distinction serves as the central conceptual-methodological instrument, in continuity with a previously formulated theoretical contribution. The conceptual analysis also draws on the taxonomy of transaction costs (Marinescu, 2012), which provides the basis for classifying the relevant dimensions examined in the study.
In the preliminary stage of the research, documentation was carried out by using academic search engines and bibliographic databases, in order to identify the relevant specialised literature at the intersection of artificial intelligence, fake news/digital disinformation, and transaction cost economics. This bibliographic mapping revealed that analysing AI-mediated fake news from a transaction-cost perspective is a relatively under-investigated field, which grounded the necessity of the present study. The research relies predominantly on secondary sources, consisting of academic literature, public-policy documents, institutional reports, and normative texts relevant to the regulation of artificial intelligence and the issue of disinformation.
The study follows three successive stages. In the first stage, the MTC/ITC distinction is clarified and situated within the broader context of TCE, to build the conceptual framework of the analysis. In the second stage, this classification is applied to AI-mediated information environments, with a view to identifying how digital infrastructures reduce production and coordination costs but raise the costs of verifying and evaluating information. In the third stage, a comparative analysis is carried out of the regulation of artificial intelligence in the United States, China, and the European Union, examining how different institutional architectures redistribute transaction costs among the actors involved in producing, propagating, and limiting AI-mediated fake news.
For the United States, federal reports on AI, strategic documents, and public-policy initiatives concerning security, export controls, and the use of AI in sensitive domains are used, complemented by the specialised literature on technological governance in the American context. For China, national plans on AI development, documents on civil-military fusion, regulations on the governance of digital platforms, and academic studies analysing the techno-nationalist model and information-control mechanisms are considered - sources that are less available owing to China's control over research.
For the European Union, the analysis draws on the relevant legal and policy texts - particularly the Artificial Intelligence Act, the General Data Protection Regulation, the instruments of the Council of Europe, and documents on combating disinformation - as well as on the literature addressing the EU's role as a normative power. The comparative analysis considers not only the normative frameworks concerning AI, but also the general institutional-economic orientation of the United States, China, and the European Union, since the distinct relationship between market, state, and rights influences both the philosophy of regulation and the distribution of transaction costs among the actors involved. Given the accelerated dynamics of regulation in the AI field, these sources are treated as benchmarks for the purposes of analysis, not as an exhaustive inventory.
The analysis of each legal framework follows three dimensions: (1) the effects on market transaction costs, especially in relation to the production, coordination, and verification of information; (2) the effects on imposed transaction costs, such as documentation, auditing, liability, and administrative-oversight requirements; and (3) the institutional incentives associated both with producing and propagating disinformation and with reducing it. In operational terms, the approach consists of extracting from the TCE literature the relevant dimensions of transaction costs, using these as a grid for analysing the selected documents, and synthesising the results in a comparative matrix. The article focuses on the US, China, and the EU because they represent the most influential and contrasting architectures of AI regulation - market-led, state-imposed, and rights-based, respectively - and the distinct allocation of market and imposed transaction costs corresponding to each system fundamentally influences the conditions for producing, propagating, and governing AI-mediated fake news. The subject under investigation is explicitly delimited to fake news generated, targeted, and disseminated with the help of artificial intelligence, without seeking to cover all forms of misinformation and digital disinformation. This delimitation is necessary to maintain analytical coherence and to allow a direct comparison between AI regulatory architectures and the associated transaction-cost structure.
The main limitation of the research derives from its qualitative and conceptual nature, which offers an explanatory and comparative interpretation of institutional mechanisms, without seeking the direct econometric measurement of transaction costs - a dimension which, as shown in Section 1, is not the object of such an analysis and could not bring additional information relevant to the research question. Moreover, the rapid dynamics of regulation in the field of artificial intelligence and the heterogeneity of normative sources mean that the results have primarily explanatory and comparative value, rather than constituting an exhaustive quantification. Nevertheless, the chosen approach is suited to the study's objective and offers a useful analytical framework for future empirical research.
5. Results and discussion
The analysis shows that artificial intelligence does not lead to a simple aggregate reduction of transaction costs, but to their structural reallocation among different categories of actors. In digital information markets, the use of AI lowers production and coordination costs for manipulative actors, while raising verification and compliance costs for legitimate actors, including the media and civic projects oriented toward the public interest.
A comparison of the regulatory architectures of the US, China, and the European Union shows that each model distributes both market transaction costs (MTCs) and imposed transaction costs (ITCs) differently, with differing implications for information pluralism, innovation, and the capacity to limit the spread of AI-mediated fake news. Together, these findings suggest that the effectiveness of AI governance depends less on the intensity of regulation and more on how costs are allocated among market actors.
Benkler's (2017, 2019) account of the collapse of communication and coordination costs in digitally networked environments complements this view by showing how AI-enabled platforms support new organisational forms-peer production, influencer networks, decentralised campaigns-that undermine the centrality of traditional media hierarchies. Nevertheless, while these forms reduce some coordination costs for AI-mediated fake news content producers, they also diffuse responsibility and complicate collective governance, raising the costs of monitoring, verifying, and disciplining harmful behaviour across distributed networks. A further implication is the emergence of "algorithmic bargaining": creators and platforms continuously adapt to AI-driven ranking and recommendation systems, engaging in trial-anderror strategies (content optimi sation, timing, engagement tactics) that effectively function as ongoing search and adaptation costs. Such algorithmic bargaining is itself a form of transaction cost, generated by the need to infer and respond to machine-mediated governance signals in the absence of clear, enforceable contracts. Reference points, aggrievement, and shading suggest that incomplete and ambiguous contracts between platforms, content creators, advertisers, and users-regarding moderation criteria, demonetisation, algorithmic ranking, and appeals-may give rise to disputes and feelings of unfairness that manifest as additional transaction costs. Content removals, shadow-banning, or sudden changes to recommendation algorithms can trigger costly cycles of contestation, reputational battles, lobbying, and potential litigation, especially when AI-driven moderation decisions about fake news are opaque and difficult to challenge. From a transaction-cost perspective, these aggrievementdriven processes appear as organi sation and coordination costs internal to platforms and their broader ecosystems, reflecting the need to manage expectations and renegotiate reference points in an environment of rapid technological change.
The literature on business regulation warns that indiscriminate attempts to reduce "transaction costs" can be misleading, because high observed costs may reflect not only obstacles but also productive investments in coordination, specialisation, and safeguards from fake news (Benham & Benham, 2001; Shahab et al., 2018a, 2018b; Wallis & North, 1986). In the context of fake news, this suggests that institutional design should selectively reduce ITCs that block credible journalism, fact-checking, and publicinterest AI, while preserving or even encouraging specific MTCs that sustain verification, reputation-building, and contractual experimentation in media governance. From a policy perspective, the calibration problem plays out differently across the three major regulatory models.
In the US, relatively light ex ante regulation of most commercial AI is combined with a dense layer of security-oriented instruments such as export controls, investment screening, and classified defence programs (Horowitz, 2018; Horowitz et al., 2018; Schmidt, 2022). This configuration keeps domestic ITCs for platform innovation comparatively low, enabling rapid deployment of AI for both content production and moderation, while concentrating higher ITCs on cross-border technology flows and securitysensitive applications. In the context of fake news, the marginal cost of using AI for both disinformation and detection on US-based platforms remains low, and ITCs are focused mainly on external security and strategic concerns rather than on routine information-governance tasks.
China's technonationalist model integrates AI regulation, industrial policy, and military-civil fusion under party-state leadership, subordinating platforms, data flows, and research priorities to political objectives, including social control via smart-city infrastructures and integrated surveillance systems (Horowitz et al., 2018; Prestes, 2022). This generates high and highly centralised ITCs for firms and users in the form of mandatory compliance, data-sharing, and alignment with state goals, even as industrial support and planning lower certain MTCs for strategic AI applications. For fake news and broader information operations, such an arrangement lowers the effective costs of state-aligned communication and raises the MTCs, and ITCs associated with oppositional or foreign narratives, so that AI and regulation function less as neutral safeguards and more as steering mechanisms within a politically defined information order.
The European Union, by contrast, approaches AI primarily as a governance and rights challenge and relies on regulation as a tool of normative power. Instruments such as the GDPR, the AI Act, and the Council of Europe's framework convention on AI impose risk-based obligations, documentation, auditing, and fundamentalrights safeguards, especially for high-risk uses that intersect with profiling, content ranking, and politica l communication (Csernatoni, 2025; Parakilas & Bryce, 2018). This model imposes ITCs on deploying AI systems in domains relevant to fake news, affecting both harmful uses and protective moderation and transparency tools.
The model risks favouring large incumbents able to absorb compliance burdens while unintentionally squeezing smaller media outlets and civic-tech projects (Csernatoni, 2025; Martens, 2024). Singh et al. (2025) underline that AI deployment in security and surveillance contexts raises particularly acute questions about transparency, explainability, accountability, bias, and privacy, and that robust legal frameworks must address these dimensions explicitly to sustain public trust. They also note that fragmented definitions of AI and heterogeneous national standards complicate effective governance, creating legal uncertainty and uneven compliance burdens that can inhibit responsible innovation. From a TCE perspective, these observations reinforce the concern that high, diffuse ITCs in the EU risk over-burdening smaller or public-interest actors, even when they are motivated by legitimate rights-protection goals. EU AI regulatory development would therefore benefit from a rebalancing toward reducing MTCs that support verification, credible journalism, and public-interest AI, rather than primarily increasing ITCs through complex compliance regimes.
Against this backdrop, the MTC/ITC distinction offers a useful compass for designing AI and platform regulations that tilt the cost structure toward trustworthy information production and verification, rather than simply raising overall transaction costs in the digital public sphere. Because AI systems can tailor narratives characteristic of fake news through appeals to authority and values across multiple linguistic and cultural contexts, the heterogeneity of cues increases, making rule-ofthumb detection more costly and less reliable. In this sense, AI and fake news jointly raise market transaction costs by inflating the ex-ante costs of search, evaluation, and belief formation, even as they economise on the production and coordination costs of disinformation campaigns.
A plausible implication is that ITCs associated with AI-based detection and compliance are unevenly distributed. Large platforms may internalise and even weaponise them as barriers to entry, whereas independent media, civic-tech projects, and publicinterest AI initiatives struggle to comply, potentially reducing pluralism even as systems ostensibly target fake news. Layered on top of already asymmetric MTCs, these ITCs shape who can participate in governing AI-mediated information flows and anticipate a calibration problem.
Conclusions
The article contributes to three respects. First, it extends transaction cost economics into AI- mediated fake news, showing that digital infrastructures reconfigure the internal composition and distribution of transaction costs rather than merely lowering them. Second, it reframes fake news as a transaction-cost asymmetry problem in which AI compress es production and coordination costs for deceptive actors while raising evaluation and compliance burdens for legitimate ones. Third, it provides a comparative institutional analysis of how major AI regulatory architectures redistribute market and imposed transaction costs, offering a structured framework for calibrating AI-mediated fake news governance.
Additionally, the MTC/ITC distinction offers a novel approach to calibrating AI regulations that tilts the cost structure toward trustworthy information production and verification, rather than simply raising overall transaction costs in the digital public sphere to combat fake news. Methodologically, the article adopts a conceptual, transaction-costeconomics approach: it clarifies and applies the MTC/ITC distinction to AI-mediated fake news, and then uses a comparative institutional analysis of US, Chinese, and EU AI regulatory architectures, which impact AI-mediated fake news-treated as market-led, stateled, and legalistic rights-based models -to show how these frameworks redistribute MTCs and ITCs in ways that directly shape the incentives and capacities for disinformation and its mitigation.
By focusing on cost redistribution rather than regulatory volume, the article provides a novel lens for understanding AI-mediated fake news and for designing institutional arrangements that mitigate disinformation without entrenching dominant intermediaries or suppressing beneficial experimentation. From a policy perspective, for the EU in particular, a TCE lens implies shifting emphasis from continually adding new ITCs through dense ex ante regulation toward targeted reductions in MTCs that burden smaller media outlets, civic-tech projects, and independent AI initiatives.
Liability and transparency regimes should internalise the costs of clearly negligent amplification of fake news disinformation-such as repeated promotion of demonstrably false content without meaningful moderation-without imposing ITCs so heavy that smaller or non-profit actors are driven out of the market. Further research is needed to operationalise and measure MTCs and ITCs in AI-mediated information environments-such as verification, moderation, compliance, and privacy-protection costs-using observable proxies that remain faithful to the subjectivist nature of cost emphasised in TCE. Substantively, extending the comparative framework beyond the US, China, and the EU, and beyond fake news to other AI-mediated informational failures, would test the generality of the proposed distinctions and refine the implications these have for public policies aimed at digital governance.
