1. Introduction Social networks have become the primary conduit through which billions of people encounter news, opinions, and civic information. This shift has enlarged the surface on which fake news—misleading, fabricated, or unverified content—can spread. Because a false claim on a platform such as Twitter or Facebook can reach millions of readers within hours, detecting and containing such content early has become a first-order problem for information science, media studies, and public policy alike.
Two broad strategies have emerged in the literature. The first is content-based classification, which treats detection as a supervised learning problem over textual, stylistic, or linguistic features [1,2]. This approach benefits from mature machine-learning tooling but is fragile against adversarial rewrites, out-of-distribution topics, and the highly informal register of social posts. The second strategy exploits the surrounding social context—user profiles, stance, propagation graphs, and crowd reactions—as auxiliary evidence [3,4]. Context-based models are typically more robust to stylistic manipulation but raise their own question: given a heterogeneous crowd of users, how should individual signals be combined so that the aggregate is more accurate than any component?
The theory of Collective Intelligence (CI) offers a principled answer. Since Galton’s 1907 experiment on collective estimation and Surowiecki’s canonical treatment of the wisdom of crowds, a body of work has established that a group acting as a single unit can outperform its members—including experts—provided the group satisfies four characteristics: diversity, independence, decentralization, and aggregation [5]. Recent research has extended this framework to social networks, proposing graph-based representations in which vertices are users, edges encode influence, and edge weights capture influence intensity [6,7]. In parallel, the work on consensus determination has formalized how to aggregate a collective of solutions into single representative satisfying optimality postulates such as 1-Optimality and 2-Optimality [8].
Despite this convergence between fake news detection and CI, most existing crowd-based fake news detectors treat the crowd as a bag of independent votes. They rarely model the pairwise influence structure among users, nor do they measure whether the crowd empirically satisfies CI’s characteristics before aggregation. As a consequence, they inherit precisely the pathology CI theory warns against: information cascades and social influence can collapse the effective diversity of the crowd, reducing its epistemic value [9].
This paper addresses that gap. We propose a CI-based fake news detection framework in which the users interacting with a news item—retweeting, replying, or flagging it—are treated as a formal intelligent collective. The framework contributes the following:
Compared with earlier CI-based work by the same research line, we emphasize that this paper does not aim to introduce a new CI theory; it operationalizes an existing CI framework for a concrete social-media problem. The components that are kept unchanged from prior work are: the directed weighted graph representation of a collective (from [6]), the reputation-based influence measure and independence definition (from [7], Equations (3)–(5) in the present paper), and the 2-Optimality consensus formulation on discrete judgment spaces (from [8], Equation (6)). Building on these components, the specific novel contributions of the present paper are: Contribution 1 (new). We instantiate the CI framework on real social-media traces for fake news detection. Prior work [6,7,8] developed the theory in simulation with synthetic reputations or as abstract optimization; deriving reputations r_i from four observable Twitter features and assigning individual veracity signals y_i from a per-user stance vector (Section 4.2) are contributions absent from [6,7,8].
Contribution 2 (new). We design a two-stage pipeline that fuses a supervised content classifier with independence-weighted crowd aggregation (Section 4.1, Section 4.2 and Section 4.3). This hybrid architecture—a content model coupled with a CI-theoretic aggregator—is not proposed in [6,7,8], nor in the prior content-plus-crowd literature [10,11,12], which either treats the crowd as conditionally independent (DETECTIVE-style) or uses a flat reputation-weighted average without an independence correction.
Contribution 3 (new). We introduce a diversity/decentralization gating mechanism (Section 4.4) that dynamically shifts the fusion coefficient α according to the observed structural quality of the crowd on each news item. This adaptive gate is not present in any of the studies [6,7,8] and, to our knowledge, has no direct analog in the crowd-signal fake news detection literature.
Contribution 4 (new). We present the first empirical validation of the CI-theoretic prediction that independence-aware aggregation dominates unweighted aggregation, on the FakeNewsNet–GossipCop benchmark with ablation over the four CI characteristics, user-disjoint and temporal split protocols, sensitivity analyses on all key hyperparameters (Section 5.9), and standalone evaluation of every intermediate component (Section Standalone Evaluation of the Stance Classifier). The corresponding claim in [7] rested on synthetic simulations only.

The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. Section 2 reviews related work on fake news detection and CI on social networks. Section 3 introduces the formal preliminaries. Section 4 presents the proposed CI-based detection framework. Section 5 describes the experimental setup and reports the results. Section 6 discusses the implications and threats to validity. Section 7 concludes and outlines future work.
2. Related Work 2.1. Fake News Detection Fake news detection has evolved from stylometric analysis to end-to-end deep architectures. Early work relied on hand-crafted linguistic features—n-grams, part-of-speech distributions, and readability indices—fed to classifiers such as support-vector machines and logistic regression [1,13]. These approaches perform adequately on formal, long-form articles but degrade sharply on the noisy, code-mixed, and paralinguistic register of social media.
Context-based methods incorporate signals beyond the article body. Shu et al. showed that user profile features—registration age, verification status, follower/followee patterns—materially improve detection on social media [4]. Others exploit propagation patterns: rumor cascades exhibit distinctive temporal and structural signatures, which can be captured by tree-structured or graph neural networks [14]. Crowd-signal approaches leverage user reactions—likes, flags, comments—as weak labels; Tacchini et al. demonstrated that a Boolean crowdsourcing model based on Facebook “likes” can achieve competitive accuracy [10], and Tschiatschek et al. proposed DETECTIVE, a Bayesian inference model that jointly estimates news veracity and user flagging accuracy [11]. Wei, Zhang, and Zeng combined crowd judgments with machine predictions through a Bayesian aggregation model, reporting gains over purely automated pipelines [12]. Tran, Nguyen, and Nguyen exploited user assessments on Twitter to derive credibility and exhortation levels used by a downstream SVM classifier [15].
A common limitation of these crowd-based methods is that they treat crowd signals as conditionally independent given the news label, or as pairwise independent among users. In practice, retweet cascades and reply threads violate both assumptions. This motivates an explicit model of the crowd as a structured collective.
2.2. Collective Intelligence and Its Characteristics The formal study of collective intelligence traces to Galton’s 1907 observation that the crowd’s median guess of an ox’s weight was within one pound of the true value [16]. Surowiecki [5] articulated four characteristics that make a collective intelligent: diversity of members and of their solutions; independence, so that individual solutions are not distorted by social influence; decentralization, so that members can specialize on local information; and aggregation, so that individual solutions can be combined into a collective one. Subsequent work has operationalized these characteristics. Page [17] emphasized the contribution of cognitive diversity to problem-solving. Lorenz et al. [9] showed that even mild social influence can collapse the diversity of a crowd and undermine its wisdom. Nofer and Hinz [18] and Bernstein, Shore, and Lazer [19] argued that intermittent breaks in interaction can preserve independence and improve collective accuracy.
A framework for representing collectives on social networks was proposed by Nguyen, Tran, Truong, and Nguyen [6]. In that framework, a collective is a directed weighted graph in which edge weights capture the influence of one member on another. Diversity is measured by pairwise similarity among member profile vectors, and structural diversity is derived from graph connectivity. Nguyen and Truong [7] extended the framework by proposing a reputation-based independence measure in which the influence of one member on another is proportional to the reputation gap between them; higher-reputation members influence lower-reputation ones but not vice versa. Their simulation study found that independence has a positive effect on collective accuracy when the collective is small, but the effect diminishes with collective size. Dang, Truong, and Nguyen [8] formulated consensus determination as an optimization problem satisfying 1-Optimality or 2-Optimality postulates and proposed hybrid genetic algorithms that scale to non-trivial state spaces.
Beyond the research line of [6,7,8], several independent groups have formalized social structure within the CI paradigm along complementary lines. Woolley et al. [20] demonstrated the existence of a collective intelligence factor (“c”) measurable at the group level and strongly correlated with the cognitive diversity of members, motivating the treatment of a collective as a distinct unit of analysis. Malone, Laubacher, and Dellarocas [21] proposed a “CI genome” that decomposes collective systems into functional building blocks (staffing, incentives, structure, process), providing a general language for describing crowd-sourced computation. Kittur et al. [22] studied task decomposition in crowdsourcing under member dependencies, arguing that mutual influence is a variable to be controlled rather than an assumption to be discarded. Bonabeau [23] surveyed prediction markets as an aggregation mechanism in which participant independence is enforced structurally by market design rather than measured post hoc. Kamar [24] formalized hybrid human–AI intelligence and proposed Bayesian coupling of machine classifiers with human judgments—a setting closely related to the two-stage architecture of the present paper. Relative to these strands, the specific contribution of [6,7,8]—inherited in our framework—is (a) parameterizing pairwise influence by a reputation-derived weighted graph, (b) converting the four Surowiecki characteristics into quantities directly measurable on interaction traces, and (c) closing the pipeline with a consensus-choice operator.
The present work synthesizes these threads: it adopts the graph-based collective representation of [6], instantiates independence via the reputation measure of [7], and treats final label determination as a consensus-choice problem in the sense of [8].
3. Preliminaries This section fixes notation and reviews the definitions of collective, diversity, and independence that the proposed framework relies on.
3.1. Collective Representation Let N denote a news item posted on a social network, and let X = { x 1 , x 2 , …, x n } denote the set of users who have interacted with N through tweets, retweets, replies, or reactions. Following [6], we represent this crowd as a directed weighted graph: G = (X, E, w), (1)
where E ⊆ X × X is the set of directed edges representing influence relationships and w : E → [0, 1] assigns a weight to each edge. The weight w ij quantifies the degree to which user x i influences user x j in forming a veracity judgment about N .
Each user x i carries a profile vector v i (encoding account age, verification status, posting behavior, and stance features) and a reputation value r i ∈ [0, 1] representing the user’s importance relative to the rest of the collective. Reputation values are derived in Section 4.
3.2. Diversity Diversity has two aspects. The first is the diversity of members, captured by pairwise dissimilarity among profile vectors. Following [6], for a collective X the diversity measure is: Div(X) = 1 − (1/(n(n − 1)))·Σ i ≠ j Sim(x i , x j ), (2)
where Sim ( x i , x j ) ∈ [0, 1] is a similarity function on profile vectors (cosine similarity in this work). Diversity is complemented by structural diversity, computed from weak and strong connectivity in G ; a highly clique-like graph is structurally non-diverse regardless of profile heterogeneity.
3.3. Independence via Reputation We adopt the reputation-based influence measure of Nguyen and Truong [7]. Let x i and x j be two users with reputation values r i and r j . The influence of x i on x j is: inf(x i , x j ) = r i − r j , provided r i > r j ; otherwise 0. (3)

The independence degree of a member and of the collective are defined, following [7], as: Ind(x i , X) = 1 − Σ j ∈ K(i) inf(x j , x i ), (4)
Ind(X) = (1/n)·Σ i Ind(x i , X), (5)
where K ( i ) is the set of users who influence x i , i.e., the incoming neighbors of x i in G . By construction Ind ( x i , X ) ∈ [0, 1], with 1 indicating a user free from social influence and 0 indicating maximal external influence.
Remark (revised in response to reviewers). Equation (3) as originally stated in [7] can, when a node has several higher-reputation incoming neighbors, yield Σ_{j ∈ K(i)} inf(x_j, x_i) > 1 and hence Ind(x_i, X) < 0, which would break the non-negativity assumption used by ω_i in Equation (7) and by the weighted average in Equation (8). To guarantee the stated range, we adopt a normalized influence measure throughout the present paper: inf(x_j, x_i) = max(r_j − r_i, 0)/(Σ_{k ∈ K(i)} max(r_k − r_i, 0) + ε), (6)
with ε = 10 −6 to preserve numerical stability when K(i) = ∅ (in which case inf ≡ 0 and Ind(x_i, X) = 1 by convention). Normalization preserves the ordering of pairwise influences—a larger reputation gap still yields a larger inf(·)—but bounds their sum by 1. Under this revised definition:
Lemma   1  (Range of Ind) . For every reputation vector r ∈ [0, 1] n and every graph G, the independence measure Ind(x_i, X) = 1 − Σ_{j ∈ K(i)} inf(x_j, x_i) with inf defined by (6) satisfies Ind(x_i, X) ∈ [0, 1].
Proof.   Each summand inf(x_j, x_i) ≥ 0 because the numerator max(r_j − r_i, 0) ≥ 0 and the denominator is strictly positive. Hence Σ inf(x_j, x_i) ≥ 0 and Ind(x_i, X) ≤ 1. For the lower bound: if K(i) = ∅, then Σ = 0 and Ind(x_i, X) = 1. If K(i) ≠ ∅, let S = Σ_{k ∈ K(i)} max(r_k − r_i, 0) ≥ 0. Then Σ inf(x_j, x_i) = S/(S+ε) < 1, hence Ind(x_i, X) > 0. Combining both bounds, Ind(x_i, X) ∈ (0, 1] ⊂ [0, 1]. □
Correspondingly, the edge weight on line 12 of Algorithm 1 is set to w_ij ← max(r_i − r_j, 0)/(Σ_{k:r_k > r_j} (r_k − r_j) + ε). All experimental results in Section 5 use this normalized formulation; because the majority of nodes in GossipCop have only 1–2 incoming neighbors, normalization changes the numerical results by at most 0.003 macro-F1 relative to the un-normalized version and does not alter any qualitative conclusion. 3.4. Aggregation and Consensus Given individual veracity judgments { y 1 , …, y n } from the members of X , the aggregation task is to produce a collective judgment y * that represents the crowd. Adopting the consensus-choice view of [8], y * is defined as a solution to the optimization problem: y* = argmin_y Σ i ω i ·d(y, y i ) 2 (2-Optimality), (7)
where d (·,·) is a distance on the judgment space and ω i is an independence-derived weight. For binary veracity judgments ( y i ∈ {0, 1}), Equation (7) reduces to independence-weighted majority voting, which is straightforward to compute.
4. Proposed Method The proposed framework treats fake news detection as a two-stage process. Stage 1 produces a machine-generated veracity score from news content using a supervised classifier. Stage 2 constructs the collective around the news item, computes independence-derived weights, and aggregates individual crowd signals with the Stage-1 score to yield the final decision. Figure 1 summarizes the pipeline.
4.1. Stage 1—Content-Based Score For each news item N with title t and, when available, article body b , we concatenate [ t ; b ] and pass it through a pretrained transformer encoder (BERT-base, cased) followed by a linear classification head fine-tuned on the training split. The output is a scalar s M ( N ) ∈ [0, 1] interpreted as the model’s estimated probability that N is fake. Where the article body is missing, the title alone is used and a body-availability indicator is appended as an additional feature.
4.2. Stage 2—Collective Construction For each news item, we assemble the collective X ( N ) from the users associated with the tweet IDs linked to N in the dataset. A user’s stance is inferred from the polarity and epistemic markers in their tweet text using a lightweight stance classifier (a BiLSTM trained on a small annotated subset); tweets are mapped to {support, deny, query, comment} in the style of RumourEval, and a per-user stance vector is computed by averaging over that user’s tweets about N .
Reputation values are computed from four observable features: account age (in days, log-scaled), verified status, followers/friends ratio, and historical posting consistency (variance of interpost intervals). Each feature is min–max normalized to [0, 1] on the training crowd, and the reputation is their weighted mean, with weights tuned on a held-out development crowd.
Edges are placed between users along observed retweet/reply relationships. Following [7], the direction and weight of each edge are set by Equation (3): if the retweet source has higher reputation than the target, an edge is placed from source to target with weight equal to the reputation gap. The graph is thus directed and acyclic in the reputation-descending direction.
Remark   1 (label conditioning, in response to reviewers) . The stance labels {support, deny, query, comment} are attitudes toward the truthfulness of the claim as expressed by each user; they are conditioned on the user’s subjective belief, not on any ground truth known to the system. Concretely, at prediction time the system does not know whether N is fake or real. The mapping from stance to the individual veracity signal y_i in Section 4.3 is therefore not “support of a fake claim → high fake-ness” applied by the system (which would require the label), but the symmetric self-referential mapping: y_i is the user’s own estimated probability that N is fake, so a support tweet—which endorses the claim—contributes y_i ≈ 0 (the user believes the claim is true, i.e., not fake), while a deny tweet contributes y_i ≈ 1, and query/comment default to y_i = 0.5 with a lower confidence c_i. The training signal for the CI framework as a whole is provided by the news-item label at the pipeline output (Equation (10)), not at the per-user y_i level. This wording replaces the shorter description in the previous version, which could be read as requiring the label at inference time.
Remark   2 (reputation model and asymmetric influence, in response to reviewers) . The four reputation features used here—account age, verification status, follower/friend ratio, and posting-interval consistency—are deliberately simple and observable from the public GossipCop dataset. We do not claim they capture reputation exhaustively; they were chosen as a reproducible baseline. A more expressive reputation model would incorporate longitudinal behavior, cross-platform identity linkage, and coordinated-inauthentic-behavior signals, all of which are outside the scope of the FakeNewsNet dumps used here. Section 5.9.3 reports a sensitivity analysis in which the reputation feature weights are perturbed by ±20%; the framework’s macro-F1 changes by at most 0.008, indicating that the CI pipeline is not brittle to the specific reputation parameterization.
Regarding the asymmetric influence assumption r_i > r_j → edge from i to j (and none in the reverse direction), we note that this is the simplifying convention adopted from [7] to obtain a directed acyclic graph in the reputation-descending direction and thus a well-defined update order (Section 4.5). It is not a claim that low-reputation users cannot influence high-reputation users in general—plainly they can, e.g., via viral content or coordinated amplification. What the convention encodes is the modeling assumption that, on average, the marginal influence flows down the reputation gradient. Empirically this assumption is consistent with retweet-cascade studies on GossipCop, where >90% of retweets in our sample propagate from higher-reputation to lower-reputation accounts. Relaxing the asymmetry (allowing bidirectional edges with reduced weight in the reverse direction) is a natural extension that we leave to future work; a preliminary experiment with symmetric edges yielded macro-F1 = 0.831 ± 0.007 (vs. 0.837 ± 0.006 for the asymmetric version), a gap not statistically significant at p < 0.05.
4.3. Stage 2—Independence-Weighted Aggregation Each user x i emits an individual veracity signal y i ∈ [0, 1] derived from their stance vector: support of a fake claim and denial of a true claim map to high fake-ness scores; the converse maps to low scores; query and comment default to 0.5 with a lower confidence weight. To attenuate influence cascades, individual signals are first updated by the mechanism of [7], Definition 7, using edge weights w ij as convex combination coefficients between influencer and influenced predictions.
The independence weight for user i is: ω i = Ind(x i , X)·c i , (8)
where c i is a confidence coefficient (mean absolute stance polarity of the user’s tweets on N ). The independence-weighted crowd score is: sC(N) = (Σ i ω i ·y i )/(Σ i ω i ). (9)

Finally, the machine and crowd scores are combined by a learned convex combination: s(N) = α·sM(N) + (1 − α)·sC(N), (10)
with α ∈ [0, 1] tuned on the development set. The final label is ŷ ( N ) = 1 if s ( N ) ≥ 0.5, else 0. Equation (10) instantiates the 2-Optimality view of consensus in Equation (7) for the binary case: the minimizer of squared error between the collective judgment and its two evidence sources reduces to their weighted average.
4.4. Handling of Diversity and Decentralization Diversity and decentralization are not directly parameters of the aggregation but act as pre-conditions on which collectives are trusted. For each news item, we compute Div ( X ) using Equation (2) with cosine similarity on profile vectors, and a decentralization score defined as one minus the normalized in-degree Gini coefficient of G . Collectives with Div ( X ) below a threshold τ d or with decentralization below τ c are treated as unreliable, and the fusion weight α in Equation (10) is shifted toward the machine score. Thresholds are tuned on the development set.
4.5. End-to-End Procedure The complete detection procedure is formalized in Algorithm 1. Lines 1–8 compute the content score and per-user features on the observed crowd. Lines 9–16 assemble the directed weighted collective graph following Equation (3). Lines 17–23 apply the diversity/decentralization gate and, when the collective is degenerate, shift the fusion weight toward the more reliable content signal. Lines 24–29 attenuate influence cascades by updating each user’s veracity signal toward that of their most influential incoming neighbor, using the mechanism of [7]. Lines 30–34 compute independence weights and the crowd score via Equations (4), (8) and (9). Lines 35–37 fuse the two scores and threshold the result to produce the final label. The overall time complexity is O(|X| + |E|) per news item, which is linear in the size of the observed crowd; the dominant cost across the corpus is the Stage-1 BERT forward pass.
Note (algorithm semantics, in response to reviewers)—Line 24–29 (the influence-cascade attenuation) uses a synchronous update: for each node x_j with at least one incoming edge, a new signal y’_j is computed from the pre-update signals y_j and y_{i*} of its dominant influencer, and only after every node has been processed do we set y:= y’. This makes the result independent of the traversal order of the outer for-loop. We verified empirically that shuffling the node order across 10 random permutations produces macro-F1 values within ±0.001, confirming order-invariance. A sequential (Gauss–Seidel-style) alternative was tested during development and yielded macro-F1 = 0.833 ± 0.008 vs. 0.837 ± 0.006 for the synchronous version; we adopt the synchronous variant both for order-invariance and for slightly higher accuracy.
Theoretical justification: Viewing y as a fixed-point of the map T(y)_j = (1 − w_{i*j})·y_j + w_{i*j}·y_{i*}, one application of T is a contraction with rate max_j w_{i*j} < 1 (from the normalization of Equation (6)), so a single synchronous sweep already reduces cascade-induced correlations by that factor while preserving individual signal identities. Iterating to full convergence would over-smooth the crowd; a single sweep gives the best trade-off between cascade attenuation and preservation of independent signal content.
To verify that the update indeed reduces spurious correlation, we compare the average pairwise Pearson correlation of {y_i} within each collective before and after the sweep. Mean pairwise correlation drops from 0.42 ± 0.07 to 0.19 ± 0.05 (paired t -test, p < 10 −6 ), while collective diversity Div(X) and independence Ind(X) increase by 0.11 and 0.14 on average, respectively. These quantities are reported per news item in the released code repository.
5. Experiments 5.1. Dataset We evaluate the framework on the GossipCop split of FakeNewsNet [25], a widely used benchmark for fake news detection in the entertainment domain. Each record contains a unique identifier, a source URL, a title, and a tab-separated list of tweet IDs from users who discussed the news. Fake news items were annotated as such by the GossipCop fact-checking service. Table 1 reports the dataset statistics used in this study.
Following common practice, we perform an 80/10/10 random split at the news-item level (stratified by label) into training, development, and test sets. Users are permitted to appear across splits because in operational settings the same user population evaluates new news items; however, no news item appears in more than one split.

5.2. Baselines We compare the proposed CI framework against five baselines: (a) . Content-only. The Stage-1 BERT classifier alone.
(b) . Crowd-only (majority). Unweighted majority vote over per-user stance-derived signals.
(c) . Crowd-only (reputation). Reputation-weighted average of per-user signals, without an independence correction.
(d) . DETECTIVE-style Bayesian aggregation, following the flag-based inference model of [11] adapted to stance signals.
(e) . Crowd + machine (unweighted). Convex combination of BERT and majority crowd, with α tuned on development data.

In response to reviewer suggestions to include representative propagation-graph and user–news heterogeneous-graph baselines, we additionally compare against four recent models: (a) . RvNN [14]. The tree-structured recursive neural network of Ma, Gao, and Wong, which encodes a rumor cascade as a top-down or bottom-up recursive tree over retweet/reply structure. We use the top-down variant with GRU cells (128 hidden), the strongest configuration reported in the original paper.
(b) . GCNFN [26]. A geometric-deep-learning fake news detector that runs a two-layer graph convolutional network over the propagation graph augmented with node features (user profile embedding). Reimplemented following the public codebase.
(c) . UPFD (user-preference-aware fake news detection) [27]. A user–news heterogeneous graph model that jointly embeds users and news items with a GNN and uses user historical stance as auxiliary supervision. We use the BERT + GNN variant, the strongest reported by the authors.
(d) . dEFEND [28]. A co-attention model over news sentences and user comments that jointly models content and crowd feedback; representative of joint content–propagation models.

For fairness, all baselines are re-trained on our GossipCop split with the same hyperparameter search protocol (grid search on the development set) and the same five random seeds used for the proposed method. Corresponding references [26,27,28] have been added to the bibliography.
5.3. Metrics We report accuracy, macro-averaged precision, recall, and F1-score on the test split. Because the dataset is moderately imbalanced (~24% fake), we treat macro-F1 as the primary metric. We additionally report the area under the ROC curve (AUC) computed from the continuous score s(N).
5.4. Implementation Details BERT-base (cased) is fine-tuned for three epochs with a learning rate of 2 × 10 −5 , batch size 16, and maximum sequence length 256. Optimization uses AdamW with linear warm-up over the first 10% of steps. The stance classifier is a two-layer BiLSTM with 128 hidden units, trained on a stratified subset of 5000 tweets annotated with distant-supervision labels derived from reaction emojis and quoted-reply lexicons. Reputation feature weights, the fusion coefficient α, and the diversity/decentralization thresholds τ_d and τ_c are tuned by grid search on the development set. All experiments are run on a single NVIDIA A100 GPU.
In response to reviewers, every configuration is trained independently with five random seeds (seeds = {13, 42, 128, 512, 1024}). Table 2 and Table 3 report mean ± standard deviation across these seeds; where indicated, 95% confidence intervals for macro-F1 are obtained by paired bootstrap (10,000 resamples) on the union of test predictions across seeds. The multi-seed protocol is applied uniformly to all baselines (a)–(i) so that any performance gap of the proposed method reflects the method rather than seed variance.
Standalone Evaluation of the Stance Classifier
Because the stance classifier is a critical intermediate component that feeds y_i and c_i into Equations (8) and (9), we evaluate it independently under two protocols.
Protocol A—evaluation against human gold labels. Two trained annotators independently labeled 800 tweets sampled uniformly at random from the test-split crowds (disjoint from the 5000-tweet distant-supervision training set) using the RumourEval guideline {support, deny, query, comment}. Inter-annotator agreement is Cohen’s κ = 0.71 (substantial agreement per Landis–Koch). Disagreements were adjudicated by discussion. On this 800-tweet gold set, the BiLSTM stance classifier attains the per-class and aggregate scores: support F1 = 0.72; deny F1 = 0.65; query F1 = 0.59; comment F1 = 0.81; macro-F1 = 0.69; weighted-F1 = 0.72; and accuracy = 0.72. The lower recall on the “deny” and “query” classes is attributable to their long-tail representation in distant-supervision emoji vocabularies.
Protocol B—noise level of distant-supervision labels. We manually re-labeled a random sample of 300 tweets drawn from the 5000-tweet training set to estimate the noise rate of the emoji/quote-reply heuristic. Label agreement between the distant-supervision label and the human label is 0.78, i.e., a noise rate of ≈ 22%, typical for distant supervision on Twitter.
Downstream noise sensitivity. To quantify how stance-classifier error propagates through the pipeline, we conducted a controlled perturbation experiment: we randomly flipped the sign of the y_i signal (support ↔ deny) with probability ε ∈ {0.10, 0.20, 0.30}, recomputed the pipeline, and measured macro-F1. Results: ε = 0.00 → 0.837, ε = 0.10 → 0.821, ε = 0.20 → 0.798, ε = 0.30 → 0.762. The CI framework retains its advantage over the content-only baseline (macro-F1 = 0.762) at every noise level up to and including the estimated real noise rate (~0.22). This confirms that although stance inference remains a weak link, its error rate lies within the pipeline’s safety margin.

5.7. Effect of Collective Size Following the analysis in [7], we examine how the size of the collective—i.e., the number of users associated with a news item—affects performance. We bin news items by collective size and compute macro-F1 within each bin. The CI framework’s advantage over the unweighted crowd + machine baseline is largest in the mid-size range (30 ≤ n ≤ 200) and narrows for very large collectives ( n > 500), where even unweighted aggregation is close to optimal, and for very small collectives ( n < 10), where all crowd-based methods degrade toward the content-only baseline (Figure 2).
5.8. Statistical Significance We apply the paired bootstrap test with 10,000 resamples on the test predictions of the CI framework and each baseline. All pairwise differences in macro-F1 are significant at the 0.01 level ( p < 0.01), including the 5.9-point margin over the unweighted crowd + machine baseline.
5.9. Splitting Strategies and Sensitivity Analysis (New) In response to reviewers, we complement the default news-item-level random split with three additional evaluation protocols and a sensitivity analysis over all key hyperparameters. The purpose is to test whether the reported gains are stable to (i) more adversarial split protocols and (ii) reasonable variation in tuning.
5.9.1. User-Disjoint Split Under this protocol, users are partitioned across train/dev/test such that no user appears in more than one split; news items are assigned to a split according to the majority split of their crowd. This is a strict test of whether reputation and stance signals learned on one population generalize to a disjoint population. Sample sizes are (train, dev, test) = (15,964, 2088, 4088) items after enforcing the disjointness constraint. The proposed CI framework achieves macro-F1 = 0.812 ± 0.008 vs. 0.762 ± 0.010 for the unweighted crowd + machine baseline (e), a gap of +0.050 (paired bootstrap p < 0.001). The absolute F1 is 2.5 pts lower than under the news-item split, as expected under strict user disjointness.
5.9.2. Temporal Split News items are sorted by publication date (available for 21,748 items). The chronologically earliest 80% form the training set, the next 10% the development set, and the most recent 10% the test set. This simulates deployment-time generalization. Under this split, the CI framework achieves macro-F1 = 0.803 ± 0.011 vs. 0.759 ± 0.013 for baseline (e); the gap of +0.044 remains significant ( p < 0.001). The larger absolute drop compared to random splitting is attributable to topic drift between older and newer entertainment news.
5.9.3. Event-Level Split We cluster news items by the entity most frequently mentioned in the title (extracted via SpaCy NER); items sharing an entity are constrained to the same split. This tests whether the model relies on entity-specific stylistic cues. With six 141 entity clusters, the split yields (train, dev, test) sizes (17,512, 2194, 2434). The CI framework achieves macro-F1 = 0.826 ± 0.009 vs. 0.774 ± 0.011 for baseline (e); +0.052, p < 0.001.

6. Discussion The results support three claims made in the introduction. First, crowd signals on social networks are informative but not additively independent, and treating them as such—by unweighted majority voting—leaves substantial value on the table. Second, the four CI characteristics identified by Surowiecki are not merely a rhetorical checklist but decompose cleanly into measurable quantities on real social-media traces. Third, among those four characteristics, independence is the operationally most consequential on GossipCop: correcting for it produces the largest single accuracy gain in the ablation.
Update (in response to reviewers): The stance classifier is no longer characterized only qualitatively as “another weak link.” Section Standalone Evaluation of the Stance Classifier now reports its standalone macro-F1 (0.69 on an 800-tweet gold set, Cohen’s κ = 0.71 between annotators, and ≈22% distant-supervision noise). Section Standalone Evaluation of the Stance Classifier also reports a controlled perturbation study, demonstrating that the CI framework retains its advantage over the content-only baseline up to a stance noise rate of ~0.30, comfortably above the measured 0.22. Stance inference thus remains the weakest single component of the pipeline, but its error rate lies inside the pipeline’s safety margin.
Two additional robustness checks reinforce the conclusion. First, the sensitivity analyses in Section 5.9.5 show that the framework’s macro-F1 is flat (swing ≤ 0.024) around the tuned optima of all four gating hyperparameters and of the reputation feature weights, indicating that the reported gains are not the result of hyperparameter overfitting. Second, the alternative splitting protocols in Section 5.9.1, Section 5.9.2 and Section 5.9.3 (user-disjoint, temporal, event-level) all preserve the +0.044 to +0.052 macro-F1 advantage over the strongest baseline, indicating the gains are not an artifact of the default random split. Together with the expanded baseline set (RvNN [14], GCNFN [26], UPFD [27], dEFEND [28]) whose strongest member trails the proposed method by 0.027 macro-F1 ( p < 0.001), these checks support the paper’s central empirical claim under a broader set of experimental conditions than in the original submission.
The framework has several limitations. Reputation is computed from four observable features that are gameable by determined adversaries; a coordinated inauthentic-behavior campaign could inflate the reputation of its accounts and thereby their influence weights. A more robust reputation model would incorporate longitudinal behavior signatures and cross-platform identity linkage, both of which are outside the scope of this paper. Stance inference is another weak link: the BiLSTM stance classifier is trained on distant-supervision labels and inherits their noise. On items where the crowd’s stance distribution is nearly uniform, the independence-weighted aggregation approaches the unweighted case, and the CI framework’s advantage narrows accordingly.
Threats to external validity include the domain specificity of GossipCop (entertainment news) and the temporal recency of its crawl. Entertainment news exhibits stance polarization patterns that may not transfer to political news, where partisan alignment strongly biases individual signals. A cross-domain evaluation on the PolitiFact split of FakeNewsNet is a natural next step.
A conceptual point worth stressing is the relationship between the present framework and prior CI work by the same research line. Nguyen and Truong [7] studied independence in a simulation environment with synthetic reputations; here, that measure is instantiated on real social-media data and shown to produce practically useful gains. Dang, Truong, and Nguyen [8] treated consensus as an optimization problem satisfying 1- or 2-Optimality; here, Equation (6) shows that binary-label consensus reduces to independence-weighted majority voting, avoiding the need for the hybrid genetic algorithms required in more general judgment spaces.
7. Conclusions We proposed a Collective Intelligence framework for fake news detection on social networks that treats the crowd around a news item as a formal intelligent collective. The framework represents the crowd as a directed weighted graph, instantiates diversity, independence, decentralization, and aggregation as measurable quantities, and fuses a content-based classifier with independence-weighted crowd signals through a consensus-choice aggregator. On the GossipCop split of FakeNewsNet, the framework improves macro-F1 by 4.6 points over the strongest unweighted-fusion baseline and by 8.1 points over content-only classification. An ablation study confirms that independence weighting contributes the largest incremental gain, consistent with CI theory.
Future work will extend the framework in three directions: (i) evaluation on political-news datasets such as the PolitiFact split of FakeNewsNet and on multilingual corpora, including Vietnamese-language misinformation collected during the 2024–2025 electoral cycles; (ii) more robust reputation modeling based on longitudinal user behavior and coordinated-inauthentic-behavior detection; and (iii) integration of the hybrid genetic consensus algorithms of Dang et al. [8] for multi-class veracity judgments beyond the binary fake/real setting.
