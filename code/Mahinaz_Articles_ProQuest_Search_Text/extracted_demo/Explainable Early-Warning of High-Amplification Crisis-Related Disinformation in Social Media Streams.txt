1. Introduction Social media platforms have become part of the communication infrastructure used during crises. Crisis informatics describes emergencies as networked events in which citizens, responders, journalists, and public institutions exchange information through digital channels while the situation is still evolving [1]. This communication environment can support disaster risk reduction and crisis management, but it also creates ethical and operational challenges when information is incomplete, emotionally charged, or difficult to verify [2]. The result is a dual role in which platforms provide situational awareness and public communication, while also creating conditions for uncertainty and unverified claims to circulate rapidly.
Recent disaster risk research has emphasized that social media streams can be transformed into actionable information for natural hazard management when data collection and interpretation are carefully designed [3]. Other work on disaster risk management stresses that social media data remain affected by noise, bias, representativeness problems, and dependence on platform access conditions [4]. These limitations are especially important in crisis communication, because monitoring systems must operate before the event has stabilized and before factual verification is complete.
The same channels that support response can also amplify misleading claims. In natural disasters, misinformation may affect risk perception, protective behavior, institutional trust, and the allocation of attention or resources [5]. The 2024 DANA crisis in Spain provides a recent example in which false claims, misleading narratives, and debunking activity appeared during a period of severe social and institutional pressure [6]. The same episode has also been analyzed as an information disorder in which falsehoods circulated together with legitimate calls for help and institutional messages [7]. This type of crisis shows that misinformation is not only a matter of incorrect content. It is also a problem of visibility, timing, and operational prioritization.
The broader misinformation literature indicates that the harm of an information unit cannot be reduced to factual status alone. False news can diffuse differently from true news in online environments, which makes diffusion part of the risk profile of unreliable information [8]. User engagement practices also contribute to the circulation of disinformation, because users may react to fake news in ways that extend its visibility [9]. Automated accounts can further amplify low credibility content and distort the apparent reach of unreliable information [10]. Platform mechanisms can also affect the visibility of political content, showing that amplification is partly mediated by the communication infrastructure itself [11]. These findings suggest that an operational warning system should not only estimate whether a unit is factually risky. It should also estimate whether that unit is likely to become visible enough to require attention.
This view is consistent with the social amplification of risk framework. Risk signals are transformed as they move through social, institutional, and communication channels, so public impact depends on amplification processes as well as on the original hazard [12]. Later developments of this framework emphasize its relevance for contemporary communication environments in which social and institutional channels interact dynamically [13]. In platform societies, public visibility is shaped by infrastructures that organize interaction, recommendation, and attention rather than by neutral transmission channels [14]. Recent work on recommendation algorithms and disinformation networks reinforces the need to consider platform mediated amplification when defining operational risk [15].
Machine learning research has developed many methods for misinformation and rumor detection. Fake news detection is commonly organized around content evidence, source evidence, social context, and propagation evidence, which shows that the problem is broader than text classification [16]. General surveys of fake news research also emphasize the role of propagation and source credibility in addition to textual content [17]. Rumor research separates detection, stance analysis, veracity assessment, and resolution, which are related but distinct modeling tasks [18]. Early neural approaches to rumor detection show that temporal information can be useful before the full conversation has unfolded [19]. Propagation path models also support early detection by exploiting partial diffusion patterns observed before the final outcome [20].
A related line of work addresses the early prediction of information cascades from partial diffusion signals. Partial diffusion histories and early structural features can contain information about later cascade size [21]. This distinction is central for crisis misinformation. One question is whether an item is factually risky. Another question is whether it will attract sufficient attention to become operationally important. A system that answers only the first question may overload responders with low visibility cases. A system that answers only the second question may prioritize viral but factually harmless content.
Recent advances in automatic detection have expanded the range of possible models. Large Language Models (LLMs) have been incorporated into fake news detection research and may enrich semantic representation when used under appropriate validation protocols [22]. Recent survey work also discusses how LLMs influence fake news detection pipelines and evaluation choices [23]. Multimodal fake news detection extends the evidence space to text, image, and cross modal signals when such data are available [24]. At the same time, recent reviews identify recurring methodological problems related to bias, preprocessing, class imbalance, and evaluation design [25]. These developments show that detection technology is advancing, but they also reinforce the need for carefully defined targets and validation protocols.
The gap addressed in this paper follows from this literature. Crisis monitoring does not only require a factual classifier. It requires a way to prioritize information units that are both factually risky and highly amplified by a forecast horizon. This joint target is especially relevant when verification capacity is limited. In such a setting, the goal is not to label every item in a stream, but to rank early observed units so that a small alert set contains a higher concentration of cases that combine factual-risk and future visibility.
This paper proposes the Disinformation Diffusion Virality Risk Index (DDVRI) as a formal response to this gap. DDVRI estimates the probability that an adjudicated information unit belongs to the intersection of a factual-risk class and a high-amplification class. The formulation uses only evidence available before the decision time. It defines observation windows, forecast horizons, amplification thresholds, block-structured early representations, calibrated risk scores, and capacity-constrained alert rules. The block representation separates content, source, temporal diffusion, graph structure, platform, and crisis context evidence when those blocks are available. This allows the framework to evaluate which evidence classes are useful under a given validation regime.
The contribution of the paper is methodological and empirical. Methodologically, the paper defines a joint early-warning target for high-amplification crisis-related disinformation and distinguishes it from factual classification alone and from virality prediction alone. It also provides a block-structured representation and a calibrated ranking protocol for capacity-constrained alerting. Empirically, the framework is evaluated on two open crisis-related datasets. The first evaluation uses an event-structured rumor benchmark to test event-level generalization. The second uses a COVID-19 health misinformation dataset to test whether the same joint target can be instantiated in an external crisis setting with social engagement signals. The empirical evaluation also compares the direct and factorized DDVRI implementations with representative content-based, early-count, and nonlinear joint-risk baselines under common held-out folds and common target definitions. A qualitative case analysis further examines how content, source, temporal, and graph evidence support individual high-risk scores.
The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. Section 2 formalizes the early-warning task, including the adjudicated monitoring population, the observation window, the forecast horizon, and the joint factual-risk and amplification target. Section 3 introduces the multilevel early representation used to encode content, source, temporal diffusion, graph structure, platform, and crisis context evidence when such blocks are available before the decision time. Section 4 defines the DDVRI score and describes direct, factorized, calibrated, and explainable joint-risk estimators. Section 5 presents the learning protocol, the alerting rules, and the evaluation metrics. Section 6 reports the numerical experiments on two open crisis-related datasets and analyzes event-level ranking, external validation, alert-budget sensitivity, block ablations, task-specific baseline comparisons, qualitative block-level explanations, and computational time performance. Section 7 summarizes the main findings, limitations, and future research directions.
2. Preliminaries and Problem Formulation This section formalizes the early-warning task. The unit of analysis is an information unit observed during a crisis-related communication episode. The objective is to estimate, from evidence available in an early observation window, whether the unit is both factually risky and likely to reach high-amplification within a later horizon.
2.1. Target Population and Adjudication Let P ★ denote the target distribution over crisis-related information units, early histories, factual labels, adjudication indicators, and amplification outcomes. All probability statements are defined with respect to this distribution unless stated otherwise.
Factual labels are normally available only for a selected subset of a social media stream. This reflects the usual setting of rumor and misinformation benchmarks, where factual or verification labels are attached to adjudicated claims, threads, or information units rather than to the complete platform stream [17,18]. We therefore introduce the adjudication indicator (1) O i ∈ { 0 , 1 } .

The value O i = 1 means that information unit u i has an available factual or verification label under the adopted annotation protocol. Supervised learning is performed on the conditional population O i = 1 .
Assumption   1. The supervised model is trained, calibrated, and evaluated on units satisfying O i = 1 . Probability statements involving factual labels refer to this adjudicated population unless an explicit model for the selection mechanism is introduced.
This assumption does not imply that adjudicated units are representative of the complete platform stream. It defines the population for which supervised factual-risk estimation is identifiable from the available labels. The relation between adjudicated-population risk and full-stream risk is formalized in Section 4.1.
2.2. Information Units and Early Histories Let E denote a set of crisis-related events. For each event e ∈ E , let (2) U e = { u i : i = 1 , … , N e }
be the observed collection of information units. Each unit is represented as (3) u i = x i , s i , t i , e i ,
where x i denotes the observed content, s i denotes a source, account, thread, or channel identifier, t i is the first observation time, and e i ∈ E is the event or episode to which the unit belongs. Additional modalities or platform fields can be included when they are observed before the decision time.
The early history of unit u i up to time t ≥ t i is denoted by (4) H i ( t ) = u i , R i ( t ) , N i ( t ) .

Here, R i ( t ) contains time-stamped engagement observations available up to t , and N i ( t ) denotes the partial diffusion structure observed up to t . The exact content of these objects is data-dependent. The formulation only requires that they are transformed into features measurable at the decision time. This representation follows the common separation between content, engagement history, and diffusion structure used in rumor detection, fake news detection, and cascade prediction studies [16,18,21].
2.3. Observation Window and Forecast Horizon Let Δ o > 0 be the early observation window and let Δ f > Δ o be the forecast horizon. The decision time is (5) t i o = t i + Δ o .

The outcome evaluation time is (6) t i f = t i + Δ f .

At t i o , the model has access only to H i ( t i o ) and to auxiliary platform or crisis records released no later than t i o , as formalized in Section 3.1. This temporal separation prevents retrospective use of diffusion evidence that would not be available at the time of alerting. Early detection studies in rumor and fake news modeling adopt the same principle, since features used at prediction time must be available before the outcome period being evaluated [19,20,26].
Let M i ( t ) be a nonnegative cumulative amplification measure for unit u i at time t . It may represent replies, reposts, cumulative engagement, exposed communities, or another platform-consistent measure. The future increment is (7) Δ M i = M i ( t i f ) − M i ( t i o ) .

Both the final cumulative amplification M i ( t i f ) and the future increment Δ M i are admissible amplification quantities. The former asks whether a unit reaches high visibility by the horizon. The latter asks whether the unit grows substantially after the early observation window. The use of early growth and cascade structure as predictors of later visibility is also consistent with cascade prediction work, where partial diffusion histories are used to estimate future cascade size [21].
Assumption   2. For each unit u i , M i ( t ) is cumulative and nonnegative. If raw platform counts are nonmonotone because of deletion, moderation, or measurement updates, M i ( t ) is replaced by its observed cumulative maximum up to t.
2.4. Factual-Risk, Amplification, and the Joint Target The factual-risk label is denoted by (8) D i ∈ { 0 , 1 } .

The value D i = 1 indicates that the unit belongs to the factually risky class under the adopted annotation protocol. The value D i = 0 indicates the complementary adjudicated class.
Let Q i denote the amplification quantity used to define high-amplification. Amplification is treated as an operational dimension of risk because prior work has shown that unreliable content, bot activity, platform mechanisms, and user engagement can affect the reach of misinformation [8,10,11]. In applications, Q i can be M i ( t i f ) , Δ M i , a normalized growth measure, or another prespecified future amplification outcome. Let q ^ 1 − α t r be a ( 1 − α ) -level reference threshold estimated only from training data or from a reference population available before evaluation. To avoid treating minimally diffused units as high-amplification cases, an absolute amplification floor m min ≥ 0 may also be specified. The operative amplification threshold is (9) γ α t r = max q ^ 1 − α t r , m min .

The high-amplification label is (10) A i α , Δ f , m min = I Q i ≥ γ α t r .

When m min = 0 , this reduces to a pure reference-quantile definition. When the floor is active, α controls the reference tail only above a minimum operational level of amplification.
The joint high-risk label is (11) Y i α , Δ f , m min = D i A i α , Δ f , m min .

A positive unit is therefore both factually risky and highly amplified under the selected reference definition. The threshold γ α t r must be estimated without using future outcomes from the evaluated units.
The floor is a target-definition parameter rather than a regularization parameter of the predictor. For a fixed training fold, we define γ α t r ( m ) = max ( q ^ 1 − α t r , m ) . If 0 ≤ m 1 ≤ m 2 , it follows that (12) γ α t r ( m 1 ) ≤ γ α t r ( m 2 ) , A i α , Δ f , m 2 ≤ A i α , Δ f , m 1 , Y i α , Δ f , m 2 ≤ Y i α , Δ f , m 1 .

The positive sets are therefore nested as the floor increases. If m min ≤ q ^ 1 − α t r , the floor is inactive and has no effect on the labels or fitted model in that fold. Once m min exceeds the training quantile, it changes the target prevalence and may change both ranking difficulty and probability calibration.
An absolute count should not be transferred unchanged across platforms with different engagement scales. A scale-normalized implementation can be obtained from a positive training-only reference scale s Q t r . For a dimensionless floor m ˜ min , define (13) m min t r = s Q t r m ˜ min , γ α t r = max q ^ 1 − α t r , s Q t r m ˜ min .

The scale s Q t r may be a prespecified robust amplification statistic computed only from the training-fold or an operational platform reference. This reparameterization separates the dimensionless strictness of the floor from the native units of the platform. It does not remove differences in crisis dynamics, so sensitivity must still be reported for each deployment setting.
Equation (11) defines early-warning at the intersection of factual risk and future high-amplification. Under this definition, a unit is positive only when both conditions hold for the selected horizon and amplification threshold.
2.5. Early-Warning Risk Using the early representation introduced in Section 3, the early-warning risk is (14) R i ( t i o ) = P ★ Y i α , Δ f , m min = 1 ∣ z i ( t i o ) , O i = 1 .

The output is a risk score that can be ranked, calibrated, thresholded, and explained. The target is the intersection of factual-risk and future high-amplification. This differs from standard veracity classification and from cascade size prediction, which are usually treated as separate modeling tasks [17,18,21]. It is not a factual-veracity score alone and it is not an amplification forecast alone.
For a threshold τ ∈ [ 0 , 1 ] , a basic alerting rule is (15) π τ ( u i , t i o ) = I R ^ i ( t i o ) ≥ τ .

The value π τ = 1 indicates that the unit is selected for additional monitoring, verification, or escalation.
3. Multilevel Early Representation The framework uses block representations. Each block corresponds to a distinct class of early evidence. The block structure follows prior fake news and rumor detection literature, where content, source, social context, temporal, and propagation evidence are often analyzed as complementary signal families [16,17,18]. This structure allows the model to be instantiated with different data sources while retaining a common risk definition and a common explanation interface.
Let B denote the set of possible feature blocks. A general implementation may include content, source, temporal diffusion, graph structure, platform, and crisis context blocks, see Table 1. An applied model uses only the blocks observed before t i o .
3.1. Early Observability Let A i o denote the collection of admissible auxiliary platform and crisis records whose release times do not exceed t i o . Let F i o be the information available at the decision time, generated by H i ( t i o ) and A i o . Every feature block used by the model must be F i o -measurable. This condition rules out features derived from future diffusion, later moderation outcomes, revised crisis measurements released after the decision time, or post-horizon labels. This restriction is essential in early detection settings, where post-outcome information would convert an operational prediction task into retrospective classification [20,26].
Assumption   3. For every included block b ∈ B , the feature map F b satisfies (16) z i b ( t i o ) = F b H i ( t i o ) , A i o ,
and z i b ( t i o ) is measurable with respect to F i o . A feature map may ignore either argument when the corresponding information is not required.
This assumption is required for early-warning validity. It is independent of the particular model family used for prediction.
3.2. Core Blocks The content block is (17) z i c ( t i o ) = F c ( x i , H i ( t i o ) ) ∈ R d c .

It contains textual, semantic, or media information available during the early window. The encoder can be a linear representation, a set of hand-crafted descriptors, a learned language representation, or another modality-specific representation, provided that it uses only early observable information. Such content representations are standard in misinformation detection pipelines, from lexical and semantic descriptors to learned language representations [16,22,23].
The source block is (18) z i s ( t i o ) = F s ( s i , H i ( t i o ) ) ∈ R d s .

It contains descriptors of the source, account, channel, thread, or initial posting context. Its interpretation must be audited because source variables may encode event-specific, platform-specific, or historically contingent effects. Source and credibility related variables have been widely used in fake news detection, but their stability across events and platforms remains an empirical question [17,25].
The temporal diffusion block is (19) z i t ( t i o ) = F t ( R i ( t i o ) ) ∈ R d t .

It captures early growth. Basic descriptors include early cumulative amplification, velocity, acceleration, inter-arrival times, and logarithmic growth measures. Temporal descriptors are motivated by early rumor detection and cascade prediction studies, where early growth patterns contain information about later diffusion [19,21].
The graph block is (20) z i g ( t i o ) = F g ( N i ( t i o ) ) ∈ R d g .

It contains early cascade structure. Examples include breadth, depth, branching, component counts, and other topology descriptors computed from the partial diffusion graph available at t i o . Graph and propagation descriptors are also common in rumor and fake news studies, especially when the structure of replies, reposts, or diffusion paths is available [16,20].
3.3. Optional Platform and Context Blocks The platform block is denoted by z i p . It may contain channel-level variables such as visibility regime, forwarding affordances, content format, or moderation state, provided that these variables are available at the decision time. Platform variables are relevant because platforms shape visibility, recommendation, and interaction conditions rather than acting as neutral transmission channels [14,15,27].
The crisis-context block is denoted by z i k . It may contain event phase, severity level, hazard type, geographical scope, public communication stage, or other contextual variables available before alerting. Crisis context variables are motivated by crisis informatics and disaster risk management research, where event phase, hazard type, and public communication conditions affect the interpretation of social media signals [1,3,28].
A concrete event-time construction of the crisis-context block can be defined as follows. Let e i denote the crisis episode associated with unit i . Let (21) C e i ( t ) = X e i ( t ) , D e i ( t )
be the external context record released by time t . The structured component X e i ( t ) contains official measurements and declarations. The document component D e i ( t ) contains official bulletins or reports. The record C e i ( t i o ) is included in the admissible auxiliary information A i o as its crisis-specific component.
The context block at the decision time is (22) z i k ( t i o ) = z e i sev ( t i o ) , z e i stage ( t i o ) , z e i comm ( t i o ) , z e i text ( t i o ) = F k C e i ( t i o ) .

The same context vector may be shared by units observed in the same crisis interval and may change when new measurements or documents are released.
For a structured severity indicator x e j ( t ) , let a e j ( t ) ∈ { 0 , 1 } indicate whether the value is available by time t . Let r e j ( t ) denote a transformed released value and set r e j ( t ) = 0 when a e j ( t ) = 0 . When a e j ( t ) = 1 , the transformation is fixed before evaluation and may be log ( 1 + x e j ( t ) ) for a nonnegative count, x e j ( t ) / E e j ( t ) for a positive exposure measure E e j ( t ) released by time t , or x e j ( t ) for an official standardized index. For a prespecified lag ℓ , the severity encoding can include (23) ϕ e j ( t ) = r e j ( t ) , a e j ( t ) a e j ( t − ℓ ) r e j ( t ) − r e j ( t − ℓ ) ℓ , a e j ( t ) , a e j ( t ) a e j ( t − ℓ ) .

The availability indicators distinguish an unavailable measurement from an observed zero. Continuous components are standardized using training-fold statistics. The severity block concatenates the encodings of the indicators available in the deployment setting.
Crisis stage can be represented by an online state (24) S e ( t ) = G X e ( t ) ∈ onset , escalation , stabilization , recovery ,
where G uses only measurements and declarations released by t . The stage block can contain a one-hot encoding of S e ( t ) , elapsed time since the first official alert, and elapsed time since entry into the current state. The transition rules and thresholds must be fixed from training data or operational policy before evaluating the held-out crisis. A retrospective stage label based on the completed event trajectory is not admissible.
Unstructured crisis conditions can be encoded from contemporaneous bulletins. The communication block can contain the bulletin arrival rate over a fixed trailing window, elapsed time since the latest bulletin, and active official declarations. The text block can contain h e ( t ) , b e ( t ) , cosine novelty relative to the previous available window, and a prespecified uncertainty-marker rate. Encoder parameters, lag lengths, trailing windows, and decay rates must be fixed without using outcomes from the held-out crisis. The cosine-novelty component is computed only when embeddings are available in both the current and preceding windows. Otherwise, it is set to zero and paired with an availability indicator.
For a sudden hazard, severity inputs available at the decision time can include the latest official intensity or magnitude, affected population or area, infrastructure outage counts, geographical spread, and emergency declarations. Stage inputs can include hours since the first alert and the state produced by prespecified escalation and stabilization rules. Recent official bulletins provide the unstructured component.
For a prolonged public-health event, structured inputs can include the latest released incidence rate, hospital occupancy, recent growth, number of affected regions, and current policy level. The online stage can be derived from prespecified combinations of trend, occupancy, geographical spread, and policy transitions. Health-authority bulletins released during a trailing daily window provide the unstructured component. In both settings, release time rather than a later revised value determines availability at t i o .
These blocks are optional. If they are unavailable, they must not be reconstructed from future information. Missingness can be represented explicitly by block availability indicators when the absence of a block is itself informative.
3.4. Unified Representation with Block Availability Let m i b ∈ { 0 , 1 } indicate whether block b is available for unit i at the decision time. The available block set is (26) B i = { b ∈ B : m i b = 1 } .

For b ∉ B i , the corresponding feature vector is set to z i b ( t i o ) = 0 d b . The indicator m i b then distinguishes an unavailable block from an observed zero vector. The full early representation is (27) z i ( t i o ) = z i b ( t i o ) , m i b : b ∈ B .

The primary additive specification in Equation (34) omits unavailable blocks by summing over B i . If block absence is instead treated as predictive information, each block function can be written as ψ b ( z i b , m i b ) and the sum can be taken over the complete block set B . The reported direct DDVRI implementations use the block-omission convention. For compactness, later sections omit the explicit dependence on t i o . All features remain early-window features.
4. Explainable Joint-Risk Model This section defines the DDVRI and its estimators. The model estimates the joint probability of factual-risk and future high-amplification from the early representation.
4.1. Disinformation Diffusion Virality Risk Index Definition   1. For unit u i observed at time t i o , the DDVRI is (28) DDVRI i ( t i o ) = P ★ D i = 1 , A i α , Δ f , m min = 1 ∣ z i ( t i o ) , O i = 1 .

The index is a joint-risk. It estimates the probability that a unit belongs to the intersection of the factual-risk class and the high-amplification class under the selected horizon and amplification definition.
To relate Definition 1 to deployment on the complete stream, we use the shorthand Y i = Y i α , Δ f , m min and define (29) p str ( z ) = P ★ Y i = 1 ∣ z i = z , p adj ( z ) = P ★ Y i = 1 ∣ z i = z , O i = 1 , s y ( z ) = P ★ O i = 1 ∣ Y i = y , z i = z , y ∈ { 0 , 1 } .

Definition 1 satisfies DDVRI i ( t i o ) = p adj z i ( t i o ) .

For feature values satisfying P ★ ( O i = 1 ∣ z i = z ) > 0 , Bayes’ rule gives (30) p adj ( z ) = s 1 ( z ) p str ( z ) s 1 ( z ) p str ( z ) + s 0 ( z ) 1 − p str ( z ) .

When s 0 ( z ) and s 1 ( z ) are positive, we define the selection-odds ratio (31) ρ ( z ) = s 1 ( z ) s 0 ( z ) .

If adjudication is conditionally independent of the joint target given the early representation, then ρ ( z ) = 1 and p adj ( z ) = p str ( z ) . If ρ ( z ) = c for a positive constant c , Equation (30) is a strictly increasing transformation of p str ( z ) . The alert ranking is then preserved, although the numerical probabilities are not calibrated for the complete stream unless c = 1 or the transformation is corrected. If ρ ( z ) varies with z , both ranking and calibration can change. If positivity fails in a region of the feature space, full-stream risk in that region is not identifiable from the adjudicated sample.

For an additive logit model, (34) η i = η 0 + ∑ b ∈ B i ψ b ( z i b ) .

A linear block contribution has the form (35) ψ b ( z i b ) = θ b ⊤ z i b .

Equation (34) does not impose marginal or conditional independence among the feature blocks. Correlated content, temporal, and graph variables may enter the predictor simultaneously. The restriction concerns the form of the conditional log-odds. Each block contributes through its own function and no cross-block interaction term is present. Let z i j b denote coordinate j of block b . Where the block functions are differentiable, the additive specification implies (36) ∂ 2 η i ∂ z i j b ∂ z i k b ′ = 0 , b ≠ b ′ .

This equality is a functional restriction on the fitted logit and not a probabilistic independence statement.
If interactions are required, let ≺ denote a fixed ordering of the block labels. An interaction-augmented logit can be written as (37) η i int = η 0 + ∑ b ∈ B i ψ b ( z i b ) + ∑ b , b ′ ∈ B i b ≺ b ′ ψ b b ′ ( z i b , z i b ′ ) .

A content–graph interaction is represented by ψ c g ( z i c , z i g ) . Such a term can capture cases in which a content pattern becomes informative only when it occurs together with a particular early diffusion structure. The interaction functions can be estimated with prespecified tensor products, splines, trees, or neural modules. Regularization and held-out validation are required because rare joint targets provide limited information for estimating high-dimensional interactions.
The additive estimator is the primary transparent specification rather than a requirement of the DDVRI definition. Equation (37) preserves explanations at the block and block-pair levels. More general nonlinear predictors can be explained with the block-level Shapley construction in Equation (45). An interaction or nonlinear model should be retained when its gain under the same held-out protocol is operationally material.

4.3. Ranking Invariance and Calibration For fixed alert budgets, the ranking induced by the score is central. If g is a strictly increasing function, then R ^ i and g ( R ^ i ) induce the same ranking and the same top- K alert set, up to ties. Probability calibration changes the numerical interpretation of scores but should preserve ranking when the calibration map is monotone. The distinction between ranking quality and probability calibration is standard in supervised classification, since a classifier may rank examples well while producing poorly calibrated probabilities [29,30].

with C F P > 0 and C F N > 0 , the Bayes-optimal alerting rule is (42) π ★ ( u i ) = I R ^ i ≥ C F P C F P + C F N .

Proof.   Conditioning on z i , alerting has expected loss C F P ( 1 − R ^ i ) . Not alerting has expected loss C F N R ^ i . Alerting is optimal when C F P ( 1 − R ^ i ) ≤ C F N R ^ i , which is equivalent to Equation (42). □
The proposition justifies cost-sensitive thresholding only when scores are well calibrated and the costs are meaningful for the operational setting. It does not determine the appropriate alert budget when response capacity is fixed.

The total logit satisfies (44) η i = η 0 + ∑ b ∈ B i C b ( i ) .

Positive contributions increase the log-odds of joint-risk. Negative contributions reduce them. Because contributions are on the logit scale, they should not be interpreted as additive probability changes.
For nonadditive models, block attributions can be defined using the Shapley value from cooperative game theory, which has become a common basis for model explanation methods [31,32]. Let f ( S ; i ) be the prediction obtained when only blocks S ⊆ B i are available. The Shapley contribution of block b is (45) ϕ b ( i ) = ∑ S ⊆ B i ∖ { b } | S | ! ( | B i | − | S | − 1 ) ! | B i | ! f ( S ∪ { b } ; i ) − f ( S ; i ) .

This is a model-based attribution. It explains how the fitted prediction changes when blocks are included in different coalitions. It is not a causal attribution and does not prove that changing a block would change the future outcome.
For empirical case analysis, the additive decomposition is complemented by a block-isolation profile. For each evidence block, a model is refitted under the same outer validation fold using that block alone, and its calibrated score is displayed next to the full DDVRI score. The isolated scores are not additive probability contributions. They provide a directly observable diagnostic of whether each evidence class can support a high-risk ranking on its own while preserving the held-out-event protocol.

5.1. Supervised Sample Construction Fix α , Δ o , Δ f , m min , and the amplification quantity Q i . Each supervised instance is constructed from one information unit. The input is z i ( t i o ) . The target is Y i α , Δ f , m min . The supervised sample is (46) D Δ o , Δ f α , m min = z i ( t i o ) , Y i α , Δ f , m min : O i = 1 .

The reference amplification threshold is recomputed from the outer development fold and never uses test-fold outcomes. Test units are not used to estimate the amplification threshold, fit any data-dependent feature transformation, fit the predictive model, fit the probability-calibration map, or choose conformal thresholds.
5.2. Splitting Protocol For each outer validation fold, the adjudicated indices are first divided into an outer development subset I d e v and a test subset I t e . The fold-specific amplification threshold is estimated using only amplification outcomes from I d e v . In this notation, the superscript t r attached to the amplification threshold denotes the complete outer training or development fold rather than only the model-fitting subset.
After constructing the fold-specific amplification and joint labels, the development subset is partitioned into model-training, probability-calibration, and conformal-calibration subsets, (47) I d e v = I t r ∪ ˙ I p c ∪ ˙ I c f ,
where ∪ ˙ denotes a disjoint union. The predictive model and all data-dependent feature transformations are fitted on I t r . The probability-calibration map is fitted on I p c . The conformal threshold, when used, is computed on I c f . Final metrics are computed only on I t e .
For event-level generalization, complete events are assigned to the test split. This choice is motivated by rumor and crisis datasets in which posts from the same event share narratives, sources, and diffusion conditions, so random instance-level splits can overestimate generalization [18,33]. This prevents leakage from event-specific narratives, repeated sources, or diffusion patterns that would otherwise appear in both training and testing. When event labels are unavailable, the same principle can be applied using time periods, source groups, claim clusters, or other non-overlapping deployment units.
5.3. Probability Calibration Let R ^ j 0 be an uncalibrated score. A calibration map T produces (48) R ^ j = T ( R ^ j 0 ) .

The map may be Platt scaling, isotonic calibration, or another monotone method fitted without using test labels. Platt scaling and isotonic calibration are standard post hoc methods for transforming classifier scores into calibrated probability estimates [29,34]. Calibration is meaningful only relative to the population and target definition used during fitting.
Calibration quality can be summarized with the expected calibration error (49) ECE = ∑ m = 1 M c | B m | n e v a l 1 | B m | ∑ j ∈ B m Y j α , Δ f , m min − 1 | B m | ∑ j ∈ B m R ^ j ,
where B m are nonempty score bins and n e v a l is the number of evaluated instances. ECE is widely used as a summary diagnostic for probabilistic calibration in modern classification models [30].
5.4. Alerting Rules A fixed-budget rule selects the K highest-scored units in the evaluation batch or operational queue. Let rank R ^ ( i ) be the descending score rank of unit i , with deterministic tie-breaking. The top- K rule is (50) π K ( u i , t i o ) = I rank R ^ ( i ) ≤ K .

This rule is appropriate when verification or response capacity is fixed.
The fixed value K is an evaluation operating point and not a universal deployment constant. Let U t new denote the units entering the system during a trailing interval of width w . Define N t = | U t new | , v t = N t w .

Let B t be the number of unresolved units at the beginning of the interval. Let Q t denote the current review queue after the new arrivals have been added, so that | Q t | = N t + B t .

Let C t be the maximum number of units that the available verification team can review before the next update.
One capacity-aware policy defines the velocity-dependent review fraction as (51) β t = clip β 0 + β v log 1 + v t v 0 , β min , β max ,
where 0 ≤ β min ≤ β max ≤ 1 , 0 ≤ β 0 ≤ 1 , β v ≥ 0 , v 0 > 0 .

The clipping operator is defined by clip ( x , a , b ) = min b , max a , x .

The volume-dependent review demand is (52) K t req = β t | Q t | .

The feasible adaptive budget is (53) K t = min C t , | Q t | , max K min , K t req .

The parameter K min provides a requested minimum budget whenever the queue size and available capacity permit. The capacity term C t prevents the rule from assigning more alerts than the team can process. The unresolved queue evolves as (54) B t + 1 = | Q t | − K t .

Let rank R ^ , t ( i ) denote the descending score rank of unit i among the units in Q t . The corresponding alert rule is (55) π t ( u i ) = I rank R ^ , t ( i ) ≤ K t .

All policy parameters must be fixed from historical workload or operational policy without using future labels. The rule changes the operating point and not the DDVRI ranking. If arrivals persistently exceed C t , backlog grows and no ranking rule alone can restore capacity. Surge staffing or a shorter review protocol is then required.
The cost-sensitive threshold from Proposition 1 is (56) τ ★ = C F P C F P + C F N .

It is appropriate when calibrated probabilities and explicit false-alert and missed-risk costs are available.
Conformal methods provide distribution-free calibration principles under exchangeability and have recently been extended to risk control settings [35,36,37]. A positive conformal threshold can be computed on positive calibration examples. Let (57) I c f + = { j ∈ I c f : Y j α , Δ f , m min = 1 }
and let n + = | I c f + | . For j ∈ I c f + , define the nonconformity score (58) S j = 1 − R ^ j .

For a desired missed-positive level δ ∈ ( 0 , 1 ) , let (59) k = ( n + + 1 ) ( 1 − δ ) .

If k ≤ n + , let S ( k ) be the k -th order statistic of the positive calibration scores and define (60) τ δ = 1 − S ( k ) .

Under exchangeability of positive calibration and positive test examples, the rule I [ R ^ i ≥ τ δ ] controls the marginal missed-positive probability at level δ . If n + is too small or k > n + , the threshold is unstable or degenerates to alerting nearly all units. For rare joint-risk targets, n + and the conformal status must therefore be reported. This limitation is especially relevant when the positive class is rare, and cross-validation variants of conformal risk control have been proposed to improve data efficiency in small calibration regimes [38].
When cost-sensitive and conformal thresholds are combined, the conservative recall-preserving threshold is (61) τ o p = min ( τ ★ , τ δ ) .

This choice can increase the alert rate. Capacity-constrained alerting remains preferable when operational capacity is the binding constraint.
5.5. Evaluation Metrics Ranking quality is evaluated by AUROC, AUPRC, precision at K , and recall at K . AUROC summarizes threshold-independent ranking discrimination [39]. AUPRC and precision-based summaries are particularly informative under rare positive classes, where AUROC alone can obscure performance in the high score region [40]. Precision at K and recall at K are used because the proposed alerting setting is capacity-constrained and only the top-ranked units can be reviewed [41]. Operational alerting is evaluated by precision, recall, missed high-risk rate, false-alert rate, alert rate, and ECE.
For an alert rule π , precision and recall are (62) Precision = ∑ i ∈ I t e I [ Y i = 1 , π ( u i ) = 1 ] ∑ i ∈ I t e I [ π ( u i ) = 1 ] ,
(63) Recall = ∑ i ∈ I t e I [ Y i = 1 , π ( u i ) = 1 ] ∑ i ∈ I t e I [ Y i = 1 ] .

The missed high-risk rate is (64) MHR = ∑ i ∈ I t e I [ Y i = 1 , π ( u i ) = 0 ] ∑ i ∈ I t e I [ Y i = 1 ] .

The false-alert rate is (65) FAR = ∑ i ∈ I t e I [ Y i = 0 , π ( u i ) = 1 ] ∑ i ∈ I t e I [ Y i = 0 ] .

The alert rate is (66) AlertRate = 1 | I t e | ∑ i ∈ I t e I [ π ( u i ) = 1 ] .

When a test fold contains no positive examples, recall and MHR are undefined for that fold and should not be averaged as numerical zeros. Such folds remain informative for false alert analysis. AUROC and AUPRC are computed only for folds where both classes are present.
5.6. Computational Complexity Let F be the number of outer validation folds. For fold f , let n f t r , n f p c , n f c f , and n f t e denote the numbers of model-training, probability-calibration, conformal-calibration, and test units. We define the size of the outer development subset as (67) n f d e v = n f t r + n f p c + n f c f .

The total number of units processed in the fold is (68) n f = n f d e v + n f t e = n f t r + n f p c + n f c f + n f t e .

Let h f denote the number of primitive early-history and auxiliary-record elements processed in the fold. These elements include retained content tokens, engagement records, diffusion nodes, diffusion edges, and admissible context records. Let d f be the retained feature dimension. For each subset a ∈ { t r , p c , c f , t e } , let s f a denote the number of nonzero entries in its design matrix X f a .
Under the feature construction used in the reported experiments, TF–IDF token processing and the temporal and tree-based graph descriptors process each retained token, engagement record, node, and edge a constant number of times. Their combined construction cost is therefore O ( h f ) . Construction of the amplification and joint labels requires O ( n f ) time. A sorting-based computation of the outer-development-fold amplification quantile uses the n f d e v amplification outcomes in the development subset and requires (69) O n f d e v log 1 + n f d e v
time. If a learned text encoder, multimodal encoder, or more expensive graph operator is used, its model-specific training and inference costs must be added to these terms.
For the primary sparse additive linear estimator, assume an iterative solver whose iteration cost is linear in the number of nonzero training entries. Let I f denote the number of optimization iterations. Model fitting then requires (70) O I f s f t r
time. Excluding solver-specific history and raw-input storage, its working memory is O ( s f t r + d f ) .
Let I f c a l denote the number of iterations used to fit the scalar Platt calibration model. Probability calibration requires (71) O I f c a l n f p c
time. Producing the uncalibrated scores for the probability-calibration, conformal-calibration, and test subsets requires (72) O s f p c + s f c f + s f t e
time. Applying the fitted calibration map is linear in the number of scores.
Let n f + be the number of positive units in the conformal-calibration subset. A sorting-based positive conformal threshold requires (73) O n f + log 1 + n f +
time and O ( n f + ) memory. Ranking the test scores and performing the sorting operations required by AUROC and AUPRC require (74) O n f t e log 1 + n f t e
time. Precision at K , recall at K , MHR, FAR, alert rate, and ECE can then be computed in O ( n f t e ) time.
Under these conventions, an upper bound for the complete primary procedure is (75) O ( ∑ f = 1 F [ h f + n f + n f d e v log 1 + n f d e v + I f s f t r + I f c a l n f p c + s f p c + s f c f + s f t e + n f + log 1 + n f + + n f t e log 1 + n f t e ] ) .

When folds are processed sequentially, and excluding the raw input records and model-specific encoder parameters, the peak working memory is (76) O max 1 ≤ f ≤ F s f t r + s f p c + s f c f + s f t e + d f + n f .

The factorized DDVRI trains a factual model and a conditional amplification model. Let s D , f t r and s A , f t r denote their respective numbers of nonzero training entries. Let I D , f and I A , f denote their optimization iteration counts. The model-fitting term becomes (77) O I D , f s D , f t r + I A , f s A , f t r .

The factorized estimator therefore retains linear dependence on the sparse training-input size, with the additional cost of fitting and calibrating a second model.
For the linear additive estimator, all block contributions for the test set can be obtained in O ( s f t e ) time without additional model fitting. Let B = | B | be the number of evidence blocks. A block-isolation profile requires B additional model fits. If I b , f and s b , f t r denote the iteration count and nonzero training size for block b , its additional fitting cost is (78) O ∑ b = 1 B I b , f s b , f t r .

For a nonadditive estimator, exact block-level Shapley explanation requires evaluating up to 2 B block coalitions for each explained unit. Its cost is therefore exponential in the number of blocks. Approximate coalition sampling can reduce the number of evaluations but does not change the cost of each model prediction. A complete pairwise tensor-product interaction expansion may increase the feature dimension by as much as (79) ∑ b , b ′ ∈ B b ≺ b ′ d b d b ′ .

Interaction terms should therefore be prespecified and regularized rather than introduced through an unrestricted expansion.
For online deployment, let s i denote the number of nonzero entries in the representation of unit i . Scoring one unit with the primary linear estimator requires O ( s i ) time. Scoring all units in the current queue requires (80) O ∑ i ∈ Q t s i .

The arithmetic required to update the adaptive budget K t is constant once the queue size and reviewer capacity are known. Sorting a queue of size | Q t | requires (81) O | Q t | log 1 + | Q t |
time. Maintaining only the K t highest scores with a heap reduces this ranking step to (82) O | Q t | log 1 + K t .

The dominant offline costs are therefore feature construction and model fitting. During deployment, the dominant costs are early-feature construction, score computation, and queue ranking.
6. Numerical Experiments This section evaluates the empirical behavior of the proposed DDVRI framework. In particular, the evaluation tests whether the joint label in Equation (11) can be instantiated without look-ahead information, whether the direct joint-risk estimator in Equation (33) produces useful fixed-budget rankings, whether the block representation in Equation (27) yields interpretable differences across evidence classes, and whether the same protocol transfers to an external crisis-related misinformation setting. The evaluation further examines how direct and factorized joint-risk estimation compares with factual-risk detection, early cascade scoring, and nonlinear early-fusion models when the target construction and validation folds are held fixed. A separate qualitative analysis presents high-risk units and block-isolated evidence profiles under the same held-out-event protocol.
All experiments were implemented in Python 3.12 using NumPy, pandas, scikit-learn, SciPy, NetworkX, joblib, PyYAML, and XGBoost. The experiments were executed in a conda environment on a laptop equipped with an AMD Ryzen 9 8945HS processor, 32 GB of RAM, and an NVIDIA GeForce RTX 4070 Laptop GPU with 8 GB of dedicated memory. The reported implementation uses CPU-based scikit-learn and XGBoost estimators, and the GPU was not used. Fixed random seeds were used in all scripts. Whenever alternative block configurations were compared, the same model-training, probability-calibration, conformal-calibration, and test partitions and the same target construction were used, so that differences are attributable to the feature blocks rather than to a different data split.
Unless otherwise stated, the experiments use the direct estimator in Equation (33) with an additive logistic model of the form in Equation (34). Text is represented by a TF–IDF encoder fitted only on the model-training subset I t r . Numerical blocks are median-imputed and standardized using statistics estimated only from I t r , and the classifier is a balanced logistic regression fitted on the same subset. Platt scaling is used for probability calibration according to Equation (48). The principal alerting rule is the top- K rule in Equation (50), because crisis monitoring normally has a fixed verification capacity. Cost-sensitive and positive conformal thresholds, defined in Equations (56) and (60), are retained as alternative alerting rules, while the empirical comparisons reported below use the top- K rule.
To compare DDVRI with representative approaches from adjacent tasks, the evaluation includes reference estimators for factual-risk detection, early cascade prediction, direct joint classification, and nonlinear early fusion. The factual-only estimator is a calibrated linear support-vector machine with word-level TF–IDF features and estimates P ★ ( D i = 1 ∣ z i c , O i = 1 ) from the source text. This model represents the content-based classification family widely used in rumor and fake-news detection [16,17,42]. The early-count cascade estimator ranks units using log ( 1 + M i ( t i o ) ) , which isolates the early popularity signal commonly used in cascade prediction [21]. The direct joint text estimator uses the same TF–IDF representation and a calibrated linear support-vector machine trained on Y i . The nonlinear joint estimator combines a 128-dimensional truncated-SVD representation of TF–IDF text with early amplification and elementary content descriptors in an XGBoost classifier [43]. The factorized DDVRI estimator follows Equation (38). Its factual component is the calibrated TF–IDF support-vector machine and its conditional amplification component is a calibrated balanced logistic model-based on log ( 1 + M i ( t i o ) ) and its square. The conditional amplification classifier is fitted only on model-training units satisfying D i = 1 , and its probability-calibration map is fitted only on probability-calibration units satisfying D i = 1 .
Every estimator uses only information available by the decision time. For each held-out-event or snapshot, the amplification threshold is computed from the corresponding outer development subset I d e v before the joint labels are constructed. The same model-fitting, probability-calibration, conformal-calibration, and test partitions are then used for every method. The conformal-calibration subset remains excluded from fitting even when a method does not use a conformal threshold. The comparison therefore isolates the effect of the scoring model while preserving the target definition, temporal information set, validation units, and alert budget.
6.1. Datasets and Target Instantiation The first empirical setting uses PHEME, an event-structured Twitter rumor benchmark containing rumor and non-rumor conversations associated with breaking-news events [33]. Each source thread is treated as one information unit. The source post provides the content block, while replies and the partial conversation tree provide temporal and graph descriptors. The PHEME experiment is used to test event-level generalization, because complete events can be held out from training.
The second empirical setting uses CoAID, a COVID-19 healthcare misinformation dataset containing fake and real news or claims together with associated social engagement files [42]. CoAID is used as an external health-crisis misinformation validation setting. Its role is to test whether the joint target in Equation (11) can be instantiated beyond an event-rumor benchmark. The CoAID experiment uses the same DDVRI construction, but introduces an absolute amplification floor through Equation (9), because a pure quantile threshold can otherwise classify minimally diffused items as high-amplification cases.
Although the same block notation is retained across the two experiments, the empirical meaning and availability of the blocks are not identical. In PHEME, the information unit is a source tweet together with its reply thread. The source block contains account descriptors based on follower, friend, status, listed, and verification fields. The temporal and graph blocks describe early activity within a rooted conversation. In CoAID, the information unit is an adjudicated news or claim item linked to aggregate tweets and replies. Account-level metadata for the originating user are not available, so the corresponding source variables are treated as unavailable, represented by fixed zero vectors, and paired with zero availability indicators. The CoAID temporal block aggregates tweets and replies observed around the item, while its graph descriptors summarize early breadth and reply activity rather than the topology of a rooted conversation tree. The validation regimes also differ. PHEME holds out complete breaking-news events, whereas CoAID holds out temporal snapshots within the common COVID-19 domain. Block-level results must therefore be interpreted relative to differences in unit definition, feature observability, diffusion structure, and validation shift.
Neither PHEME nor CoAID provides a synchronized external context record containing official severity measurements, online stage transitions, geographical exposure, and contemporaneous bulletins for every decision time. The crisis-context block is therefore excluded from the reported experiments rather than reconstructed retrospectively. The construction in Section 3.3 is an implementation specification for future datasets that provide time-aligned context records.
For both datasets, the factual label is encoded as D i = 1 for the risky factual class and D i = 0 for the complementary adjudicated class. The main amplification quantity is final cumulative amplification M i ( t i f ) . The future-increment definition in Equation (7) is evaluated as a stricter secondary setting in PHEME. All amplification thresholds are estimated from the outer development subset of each validation fold. Test-fold outcomes are never used to define q ^ 1 − α t r , γ α t r , model parameters, calibration maps, or alert thresholds.
6.2. Experiment 1: Held-out-Event Validation on PHEME The first experiment evaluates whether DDVRI can rank rare joint high-risk units when complete crisis events are held out. This experiment validates the event-level version of the splitting protocol described in Section 5.2. The observation window is Δ o = 6 hours and the forecast horizon is Δ f = 72 hours. The main target uses cumulative final amplification with α = 0.40 , so that a positive example satisfies Equation (11) with Q i = M i ( t i f ) and an outer-development-fold amplification threshold. The main operating point uses K = 50 alerts per held-out event.
Table 2 reports the main PHEME summaries and compares two final amplification definitions with one future increment definition. The final amplification setting with α = 0.40 gives the strongest AUPRC and the highest precision at the top-50 alert budget, and is therefore retained as the principal PHEME setting. Under this configuration, the average base rate of the joint target is 0.0199, whereas P@50 reaches 0.0600. The top-50 alert set therefore contains approximately three times more positive joint risk cases than expected from the event level prevalence. The stricter future increment target remains useful as a robustness check, but its weaker AUROC and AUPRC indicate that predicting additional post-observation growth is harder than identifying units that reach high cumulative visibility by the forecast horizon. Thus, these results support the use of DDVRI as a fixed capacity prioritization score under event held-out validation.
Table 3 reports the effect of changing the alert budget for the α = 0.30 final amplification setting. As expected from the top K rule in Equation (50), larger budgets recover more positive joint risk cases, increasing recall and lowering the missed high-risk rate. This gain comes with a higher false-alert rate because more non-positive units are included in the alert set. The top-50 configuration provides an intermediate operating point between the more selective top-25 setting and the broader top-100 setting. The same nominal budget is used in the remaining PHEME comparisons, which employ the principal α = 0.40 target defined in Table 2.
The three values in Table 3 are static capacity scenarios and can be interpreted as low, nominal, and surge review budgets. They demonstrate how the ranking behaves when the operating point changes, but they do not validate the adaptive rule in Equation (53). PHEME and CoAID do not contain continuous analyst queue, staffing, or service-time records. A faithful evaluation of K t therefore requires a prospective deployment or a time-stamped stream replay that couples item arrivals with reviewer availability.
6.3. Experiment 2: Block Ablation on PHEME The second experiment evaluates whether the block representation in Equation (27) provides useful evidence about transportability across crisis events. All configurations use the same target, the same held-out events, and the same top-50 alert budget. The experiment therefore isolates the contribution of each feature block to the risk model in Equation (34).
Table 4 reports the block ablation for the main PHEME setting with α = 0.40 and cumulative final amplification. The content–temporal–graph configuration gives the strongest AUROC, AUPRC, P@50, and R@50, together with the lowest MHR among the compared configurations. The no-temporal configuration gives the highest P@25. The source-only model performs poorly, and removing source variables improves the full model. In PHEME, however, the source block is genuinely observed and consists of account descriptors rather than event labels. The held-out-event protocol changes the active account population together with the narratives and diffusion conditions associated with each breaking-news episode. Coefficients learned from follower, friend, status, listed, and verification fields may therefore be unstable across events. This result does not establish that source information is intrinsically uninformative or entirely event-specific. It shows that the available account descriptors do not provide robust standalone evidence for the joint target under the adopted out-of-event validation regime. Early temporal and graph descriptors are more directly related to the observed development of the conversation and provide more transportable ranking evidence in this experiment.
The ablation remains a diagnostic of the fitted prediction model. It identifies which evidence classes improve out-of-event ranking when included in the model, but it does not imply that changing a source attribute, temporal descriptor, or cascade property would causally change the eventual factual or amplification outcome. This interpretation is consistent with the distinction between predictive block contributions in Equations (43)–(45) and causal explanation.
6.4. Experiment 3: External Validation on CoAID The third experiment evaluates whether the DDVRI formulation can be instantiated in a second crisis-related misinformation setting. CoAID differs from PHEME in both topic and structure. It is centered on COVID-19 health misinformation, and the validation units are organized by dataset snapshots rather than by the same breaking-news events used in PHEME. This experiment therefore tests the generality of the target definition in Equation (11) and the fixed-budget ranking rule in Equation (50).
The CoAID target uses final cumulative social engagement as Q i . A pure quantile threshold can be too permissive in this dataset, because the lower tail of amplification contains many items with very small engagement. For this reason, the main CoAID setting uses the mixed threshold in Equation (9) with α = 0.30 and m min = 5 . The resulting high-amplification label is therefore (83) A i = I M i ( t i f ) ≥ max q ^ 0.70 t r , 5 .

This definition prevents minimally diffused items from being treated as high-amplification cases while preserving the training-fold quantile construction.
Table 5 reports the fold-level results for the main CoAID setting and shows substantial heterogeneity across held-out snapshots. Most positive joint-risk cases appear in the first two snapshots, whereas the last two snapshots contain only one positive case each. This distribution makes fold-level recall summaries sensitive to small denominators and supports the use of fixed-budget precision as the main operational summary for this dataset.
6.5. Amplification-Floor Sensitivity Across Datasets Table 6 reports amplification-floor sensitivity using dataset-specific grids selected relative to the outer-development-fold amplification scale. PHEME uses m min ∈ { 0 , 20 , 30 } , while CoAID uses m min ∈ { 0 , 5 , 10 } . The comparison complements the structural differences discussed in Section 6.1. The raw floor values are not intended to represent equivalent amplification levels across datasets. Each grid contains the pure quantile definition and two increasingly restrictive absolute floors for the corresponding engagement scale.
In PHEME, the fold-specific development quantiles range from 16 to 18. The floor is inactive for m min = 0 , while m min = 20 and m min = 30 are active in all eight held-out-event folds. The mean joint-positive count decreases from 18.625 under the pure quantile definition to 14.375 for m min = 20 and 3.250 for m min = 30 . The corresponding mean base rates are 0.0199 , 0.0158 , and 0.0031 . At m min = 20 , AUROC remains close to the reference value and P@50 remains 0.0600 , while AUPRC decreases from 0.1039 to 0.0814 . At m min = 30 , the target becomes extremely rare. AUPRC decreases to 0.0334 and P@50 decreases to 0.0125 , although AUROC remains 0.8040 . The positive conformal calibration subset contains too few examples for a stable threshold in this strict setting. The enrichment ratio increases from 3.01 to 3.79 and 3.97 , but this increase should be interpreted together with the sharp reduction in prevalence and absolute precision.
In CoAID, the fold-specific training quantiles range from 1 to 9. The floor is inactive for m min = 0 , active in two of four folds for m min = 5 , and active in all folds for m min = 10 . The mean joint-positive count decreases from 162.0 under the pure quantile rule to 24.0 for m min = 5 and 19.0 for m min = 10 . The mean base rate decreases from 0.0721 to 0.0126 and 0.0099 . Because the evaluated label changes with the floor, predictive metrics across rows do not correspond to the same classification problem. They must be interpreted jointly with target prevalence and the number of positive cases. The value m min = 5 is retained as the principal CoAID configuration because it removes minimally diffused positives while preserving more positive cases and stronger AUPRC and P@50 than the stricter floor of 10.
6.6. Experiment 4: Block Ablation on CoAID The fourth experiment examines whether the block-level behavior observed in PHEME is also present in the external CoAID setting. The same target definition as in Equation (83) is used. Table 7 reports the corresponding ablation results.
The source-only row in Table 7 requires a different interpretation from the corresponding PHEME row. CoAID does not provide the account-level follower, friend, status, listed, and verification fields used by the source block. These variables are therefore constant placeholders in the CoAID implementation. The resulting AUROC of 0.5000 and AUPRC equal to the base rate are expected from a constant-input model and do not measure the predictive value of genuine source metadata. The source block is unavailable in this dataset rather than empirically refuted. The precision-at- K values in this row depend only on deterministic tie-breaking among equal scores and should not be interpreted as evidence provided by source features.
The remaining block differences follow from the structure of CoAID. Its content units are longer and more curated news or claim records than the short source tweets in PHEME. They also remain within a common public-health domain across snapshots. These properties provide a plausible explanation for the high content-only AUROC in CoAID. The lower content-only AUPRC and P@50 show that broad factual discrimination does not by itself concentrate the rare joint factual-risk and amplification cases at the top of the ranking. CoAID temporal and graph variables are computed from aggregate tweets and replies linked to each item. They do not represent the same rooted reply-tree geometry available in PHEME. Their partial redundancy also helps explain why removing the graph block increases AUPRC and P@10, while the combined content–temporal–graph model retains the strongest P@50.
Taken together, Table 4 and Table 7 should not be read as a direct comparison of identically observed feature blocks. In PHEME, source metadata are available but do not transfer reliably across held-out breaking-news events. In CoAID, equivalent account-level source metadata are absent. Content transfers more effectively across CoAID snapshots than across PHEME events, while temporal and graph evidence describe different diffusion objects in the two datasets. The observed discrepancies therefore reflect dataset construction, feature availability, communication unit, diffusion representation, and validation regime rather than a universal ordering of evidence blocks.
6.7. Comparison with Task-Specific Baselines The comparison uses the principal PHEME setting with Δ o = 6 h, Δ f = 72 h, α = 0.40 , cumulative final amplification, and a top-50 alert budget. The CoAID comparison uses α = 0.30 , m min = 5 , cumulative final amplification, and the same top-50 alert budget. Each method is evaluated on the same held-out events or snapshots and against the same fold-specific realization of the joint target. The factual-only and direct joint text estimators provide content-based comparisons with established rumor and fake-news classification approaches. The early-count estimator represents an early cascade-prediction baseline. SVD–XGBoost provides a nonlinear early-fusion comparator. The factorized DDVRI estimator evaluates the product decomposition in Equation (38).
Table 8 shows that the relative ordering depends on both the dataset and the evaluation metric. On PHEME, SVD–XGBoost obtains the highest AUROC, AUPRC, and R@50. The factorized DDVRI obtains the highest P@50 and improves this metric from 0.0600 for the direct DDVRI to 0.0850 . The direct DDVRI also exceeds the factual-only, early-count, and direct joint text estimators in AUPRC. These results indicate that the multiblock representation contributes information beyond text-only factual-risk estimation and early amplification alone, although nonlinear early fusion remains competitive for event-level ranking.
On CoAID, the factorized DDVRI obtains the strongest value for every reported metric. Its AUPRC reaches 0.7755 and its P@50 reaches 0.2700 , compared with 0.6707 and 0.2000 for SVD–XGBoost. The direct DDVRI also improves on the factual-only, early-count, and direct joint text estimators in AUPRC and P@50. The results support explicit modeling of both factual risk and future amplification. They also show that separate estimation of the two probability factors can be advantageous when the factual and amplification components have different predictive structures. The direct estimator remains useful when an additive block decomposition and its associated explanation interface are required.
The comparison also quantifies the predictive cost that may accompany the additive direct estimator. On PHEME, SVD–XGBoost increases AUPRC from 0.1039 to 0.1427 and P@50 from 0.0600 to 0.0825 relative to the direct DDVRI. On CoAID, the corresponding values increase from 0.3982 to 0.6707 and from 0.1500 to 0.2000 . The nonlinear comparator is a general early-fusion estimator without an explicit content–graph interaction term. The observed gains therefore show that nonlinear early fusion can improve prediction, but they do not test whether a content–graph interaction is responsible. They nevertheless show that the additive form should not be assumed to maximize predictive accuracy. The factorized DDVRI obtains the highest P@50 on PHEME and the strongest results on CoAID, which also shows that the accuracy and interpretability tradeoff is not determined by a single model family. When a prespecified interaction model or a nonlinear estimator provides a material held-out gain, it can be retained and explained through block-pair terms or block-level Shapley values.
6.8. Qualitative Block-Level Explanations The qualitative analysis uses the principal PHEME configuration with α = 0.40 , cumulative final amplification, and a top-50 alert budget. Three true positive alerts were selected from different held-out events to illustrate distinct block-evidence profiles. Every selected unit satisfies D i = A i = Y i = 1 . The examples were not used for model fitting, probability calibration, or amplification-threshold construction. Table 9 reports the corresponding information-unit identifiers, shortened source-post excerpts, early and final amplification values, fold-specific amplification thresholds, and calibrated DDVRI scores.
Figure 1 shows the calibrated score obtained from each block in isolation together with the full multiblock DDVRI score. All scores were produced under the same outer held-out-event fold used for the main evaluation. The block-only models retain the same target definition and internal fitting and calibration partitions as the full model.
The first example has the strongest content-only score among the three cases. Its content score is 0.0754 , while the temporal and graph scores are 0.0548 and 0.0489 . The full DDVRI score is 0.2248 . The isolated profiles show that content provides the strongest standalone signal, while the temporal and graph blocks also produce nonzero risk scores. The source-only score remains 0.0303 .
The second example has a different profile. Its temporal score is 0.0554 and its graph score is 0.0511 , while the content and source scores are 0.0315 and 0.0267 . The final DDVRI score is 0.1436 . This case illustrates an alert for which early diffusion evidence provides stronger standalone support than textual evidence.
The third example has the largest graph-only score among its isolated blocks. The graph and temporal scores are 0.0431 and 0.0421 , while the content and source scores remain below 0.0260 . Its early amplification already reaches 55 observations and the full DDVRI score is 0.0703 . Across the three examples, the source block remains weak, which is consistent with the held-out-event ablation in Table 4. The profiles also show that similarly labelled alerts can be supported by different evidence configurations. These explanations are model-based and predictive. They do not establish causal effects of content or diffusion characteristics on the final outcome.
6.9. Computational Time Performance This section evaluates the computational time required to execute the experimental analyses reported in Table 2, Table 3, Table 4, Table 5, Table 6, Table 7 and Table 8. The objective is to complement the asymptotic analysis in Section 5.6 with empirical wall-clock measurements for the main validation settings, alert-budget sensitivity, block ablations, amplification-floor sensitivity analyses, and task-specific baseline comparisons. The qualitative case-selection and block-evidence generation procedure associated with Table 9 and Figure 1 is also evaluated. Identical configurations appearing in more than one result table were executed once per repetition and share the same runtime measurement. The reported values characterize the cost of complete experimental configurations rather than isolated model-prediction calls.
Each configuration was executed three times in an independent Python process under the same software environment, data partitions, target definitions, and random-seed settings used for the predictive evaluation. The reported values are the arithmetic mean and one sample standard deviation across the three repetitions.
Each measurement covers the complete fresh-process execution of the corresponding experiment. It includes Python process initialization, loading of the processed input file, fold-specific target construction, amplification-threshold estimation, feature transformation, model fitting, probability calibration, score generation, ranking, metric calculation, and output generation. Dataset downloading, conversion of the original raw datasets into the processed input files, software installation, and manuscript compilation are excluded. When several operating points reuse the same fitted model and calibrated scores, the shared computation and the incremental post-processing time are reported separately to avoid counting the same training procedure multiple times.
Table 10 reports the main validation, alert-budget sensitivity, and amplification-floor sensitivity experiments. The complete PHEME budget-sensitivity time combines the shared model fitting, probability calibration, score generation, and joint post-processing required to evaluate the three budgets. The shared and post-processing durations were added within each repetition before computing the mean and sample standard deviation. The incremental values report the additional in-memory calculation required for each value of K after the calibrated scores are available. The snapshot-level CoAID values report internal fold calculation time and exclude common process initialization and shared input loading, whereas the complete held-out-snapshot validation row includes the entire end-to-end execution.
Table 11 reports the block-ablation timings. Configurations containing the TF–IDF content representation require more time than numerical-only temporal, graph, or source configurations because text-vector construction and sparse text-model fitting are included in the complete measurement.
Table 12 reports the task-specific baseline comparison and the additional qualitative processing. The qualitative row reuses the block-specific model fits measured in Table 11. It includes prediction loading, merging, case selection, and block-evidence figure generation.
The main PHEME configurations require between 3.518 and 3.606 s, while the complete CoAID validation requires 4.712 s. The complete PHEME budget-sensitivity analysis requires 5.848 s, but each additional top- K calculation requires less than 0.03 s once calibrated scores are available. The early-count estimator is the fastest task-specific comparator on both datasets. SVD–XGBoost is the most computationally demanding model and requires 11.242 s on PHEME and 11.614 s on CoAID. The direct and factorized DDVRI implementations remain below five seconds in all complete validation runs.
7. Concluding Remarks This study introduced DDVRI as an explainable early-warning framework for high-amplification crisis-related disinformation in social media streams. The central object of the framework is a joint risk score that estimates whether an adjudicated information unit is both factually risky and likely to reach high-amplification within a future horizon. This formulation separates the proposed task from factual classification alone and from virality prediction alone, and makes the operational target explicit through the joint label in Equation (11).
The proposed construction defines early histories, observation windows, forecast horizons, amplification thresholds, block-structured representations, calibrated risk scores, and alerting rules. The amplification definition combines a training-fold reference threshold with an optional absolute floor, which is useful when the lower tail of engagement contains many minimally diffused units. The sensitivity analysis uses dataset-specific floor grids selected relative to the corresponding amplification scales. In PHEME, m min = 20 and m min = 30 exceed the fold-specific training quantiles and progressively reduce the prevalence of the joint target. In CoAID, m min = 5 and m min = 10 define successively stricter within-dataset targets. The absolute floor values are therefore dataset dependent and should not be interpreted as numerically equivalent thresholds across crises or platforms. The block representation supports diagnostic comparison across evidence classes, including content, source, temporal diffusion, graph structure, platform information, and crisis context when such blocks are available before the decision time. The resulting explanations are predictive and model based, not causal.
The numerical experiments provide empirical support for the proposed formulation in two crisis-related settings. In PHEME, held-out event validation shows that DDVRI enriches fixed-budget alert sets for rare joint rumor and amplification cases. The strongest PHEME configuration combines content, temporal diffusion, and graph structure, while source only features are not robust under held-out-event evaluation. In CoAID, the same joint target can be instantiated in an external COVID-19 health misinformation setting once the amplification floor is introduced. The CoAID results show that the top-ranked alert set contains a substantially higher concentration of joint risk cases than the background prevalence.
The task-specific comparison provides a more qualified assessment of predictive utility. SVD–XGBoost gives the highest PHEME AUPRC, while the factorized DDVRI gives the highest PHEME P@50 and the strongest results across all reported CoAID metrics. The factual-only and early-count baselines remain weaker than the joint-risk estimators in the high-priority region of the ranking. These findings support DDVRI as a framework with complementary direct and factorized implementations rather than as a claim that one estimator must dominate every nonlinear alternative. The qualitative PHEME cases further show that high-risk alerts can arise from different evidence profiles. Content provides the strongest isolated signal in one case, while temporal or graph evidence provides the strongest isolated signal in the other cases.
The results also clarify the intended use of the method. DDVRI should be interpreted as a triage and prioritization framework rather than as an automatic binary intervention system. Small alert budgets necessarily limit absolute recall, and recall estimates become unstable when held-out folds contain very few positive joint cases. Cost-sensitive thresholds can become impractical when high missed risk costs produce alert rates incompatible with limited verification capacity. Positive conformal thresholds are also sensitive to the number of positive calibration examples and to distribution shift across events or snapshots. For these reasons, the primary alerting mechanism evaluated in this study is fixed-budget ranking. The fixed values of K used in the experiments are benchmark operating points rather than universal deployment constants. The adaptive rule in Equation (53) can update K t from current queue size, stream velocity, backlog, and reviewer capacity without changing the DDVRI ranking.
Several limitations follow from the design. Factual labels are available only for adjudicated units, so the supervised target is conditional on the monitored population. Dataset-specific annotation protocols determine what counts as factual risk. The available feature blocks differ across datasets, and optional platform or crisis context variables could not be evaluated when absent. The concrete crisis-context encodings in Section 3.3 are implementation templates rather than empirically validated predictors in the present study. Their value must be evaluated on datasets that link each information unit to the official measurements and documents that were actually available at its decision time. In particular, account-level source metadata are observed in PHEME but unavailable in CoAID, and nominally similar temporal and graph blocks describe different diffusion objects. Cross-dataset ablation values should therefore not be interpreted as direct measurements of the same feature space. The floor-sensitivity study covers event-driven breaking-news conversations and a prolonged public-health setting, but it does not establish a transferable absolute floor for natural disasters or platforms with different exposure and engagement units. Such deployments require a platform-specific operational floor or the training-only scale-normalized construction in Equation (13). The direct additive estimator omits cross-block interaction terms and may be misspecified when such interactions dominate. Interaction-augmented or nonlinear estimators should therefore be retained when their held-out gain is operationally material, with block-pair or Shapley explanations used in place of a purely additive decomposition. The adaptive budget mechanism is not empirically validated in the present study because the datasets do not provide continuous arrival, staffing, queue, or service-time records.
Future work should evaluate the framework on additional crisis datasets with richer temporal metadata, multilingual content, media evidence, and platform-level context. This evaluation should include time-aligned official severity records, stage-transition inputs, and contemporaneous bulletins so that the crisis-context construction can be tested without retrospective information. Further research should also study calibration under temporal dependence and distribution shift, where fold-wise probability calibration and positive conformal thresholds may become fragile. Another relevant direction is the development of block stability diagnostics that distinguish transportable evidence from event-specific proxies. Prospective or replay studies should estimate the adaptive-budget policy parameters and compare fixed and dynamic budgets under observed arrival and analyst service processes. Finally, DDVRI should be evaluated in human verification workflows, because the framework is intended to prioritize limited attention rather than to replace expert assessment.
