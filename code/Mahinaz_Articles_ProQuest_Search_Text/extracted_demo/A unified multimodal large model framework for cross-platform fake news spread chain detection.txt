Introduction Social media has fundamentally reshaped how news is produced, consumed, and redistributed, but it has also amplified the reach and speed of deceptive and fabricated information. Fake news is particularly harmful when it spreads rapidly through reposting, cross-posting, and content re-authoring, creating cascades that can outpace verification and moderation. Prior research has shown that fake news detection on social media is challenging because deceptive content is intentionally crafted to appear credible, and because useful signals may come from multiple sources, the news content itself, user interactions, and the temporal and structural patterns of propagation (Shu et al. 2017a; Vavekanand and Das 2025).
A major practical complication is that misinformation rarely remains confined to a single platform. The same story may originate on one platform, gain traction elsewhere, and then re-enter the original platform in modified form, creating cross-platform spread chains. This cross-platform behaviour weakens single-platform detectors trained on platform-specific cues and incomplete propagation context. While widely used resources such as FakeNewsNet (Shu et al. 2020) and Fakeddit (Nakamura and Levy 2020) have enabled substantial progress in content- and context-based detection, they primarily reflect platform-bounded views of misinformation (e.g., news and social context tied to one ecosystem). Recent efforts toward multi-platform propagation datasets highlight that propagation characteristics differ across platforms and that exploiting these differences can improve detection. However, unified modelling of cross-platform spread chains remains underexplored (Zhao et al. 2025; Abu-Zaid 2026).
In parallel, misinformation has become increasingly multimodal; posts often combine text with images, screenshots, memes, or repurposed visuals. Multimodal fake news detection methods (e.g., event-invariant multimodal representations) demonstrate that jointly modelling textual and visual cues can improve robustness, especially under shifting topics and events (Wang et al. 2018). Meanwhile, propagation-based models capture diffusion structures that are difficult to fake consistently at scale; graph-based rumour/fake-news methods have shown that leveraging top-down and bottom-up diffusion patterns can be highly effective (Ma and Gao 2016). Nevertheless, most existing approaches treat content modelling and propagation modelling as partially separable, and they often struggle when content is paraphrased, visually altered, or recontextualised across platforms.
Recent advances in multimodal large models (MLMs), including strong vision-language pretraining and instruction-tuned multimodal assistants, offer an opportunity to unify these signals within a single framework (Vavekanand 2026). Vision-language pretraining (e.g., contrastive image-text alignment) offers transferable representations for heterogeneous visual and textual content (Radford et al. 2021). More recent multimodal large-model designs can combine powerful frozen vision encoders with large language models, improving zero and few-shot generalisation and instruction following (Alayrac et al. 2022; Li et al. 2023; Liu et al. 2023). At the same time, large language models have demonstrated the capacity to generate fluent, news-like text, raising the stakes for detection systems that rely on shallow lexical cues (Brown et al. 2020). These developments motivate detection pipelines that can (i) represent multimodal posts semantically, (ii) align semantically related items across platforms despite paraphrasing and visual edits, and (iii) detect and attribute cross-platform spread chains using propagation evidence.
This paper proposes a unified multimodal large-model framework for cross-platform fake-news spread-chain detection. We define a spread chain as a temporally ordered sequence (or tree-structured cascade) of cross-platform posts that are semantically and evidentially linked through reuse, transformation, and redistribution of the same underlying claim. Our framework integrates multimodal semantic encoding (text, image, and optional metadata) with cross-platform propagation graph construction to identify candidate chains and determine whether the chain corresponds to misinformation. The goal is not only to classify isolated posts, but also to detect how misinformation traverses platforms and to surface auditable chain-level evidence that supports downstream moderation, fact-checking, and forensic analysis.
The main contributions are summarised as follows:
- We formulate cross-platform spread-chain detection as a unified task that jointly models multimodal content similarity and multi-platform propagation structure.

- We present a multimodal, large-model-based representation and alignment strategy to connect semantically equivalent posts across platforms under paraphrase, cropping, screenshotting, and meme-style transformations.

- We introduce a chain-aware detection module that leverages propagation-graph signals to improve robustness under domain and platform shift.

- We outline an evaluation protocol spanning post-level and chain-level metrics (e.g., chain purity, chain completeness, and early detection) to reflect real-world cross-platform deployment needs.

Related work Fake news (and broader misinformation) detection has evolved from content-based classification toward multi-source, multimodal, and propagation aware modelling. In this work, we focus on a more challenging setting: cross-platform spread chain detection, where semantically linked posts and their diffusion traces must be aligned across heterogeneous platforms and modalities (text, image, and video), forming an end-to-end chain rather than isolated predictions.
Fake news detection and benchmark resources Early research emphasised linguistic cues, user engagement signals, and credibility-related features. The core challenges of fake news detection on social media, including the scarcity of reliable ground truth, rapid topic drift, and the need to model social context (Zhen 2026; Ma and Qu 2026; Suong and Nghiem 2025). To support reproducible evaluation, benchmark datasets and repositories were introduced. FakeNewsNet, a data repository combining news content and social context for studying fake news detection on social platforms (Shu et al. 2020). For fine-grained multimodal fake news detection at scale (Nakamura and Levy 2020), Fakeddit was introduced, which pairs textual post content with images and metadata, enabling multi-class and multi-granularity labelling. In addition, claim verification benchmarks such as LIAR (Wang 2017) have been used as complementary resources for text-centric factuality modelling.
Multimodal fake news detection Multimodal fake news detection aims to exploit complementary evidence from text and visual signals. A representative line of work designs joint encoders and fusion modules to detect incongruence between modalities or capture cross-modal correlations (Lv et al. 2025; Sallami 2025). For example, Wang et al. (2018) proposed an event-adversarial framework to encourage event-invariant representations while integrating multimodal features, addressing the challenge that fake news often emerges in rapidly evolving events. Beyond detection accuracy, explainability has been explored to reveal why content is flagged. Shu et al. (2019) modelled interactions between news sentences and user comments to extract check-worthy evidence. While helpful, most multimodal detectors still operate at the single-post level and do not explicitly reconstruct cross-platform diffusion chains that connect semantically related items over time and across platforms.
Propagation and graph-based modelling of rumours and fake news A major advance in misinformation research is the incorporation of propagation structure. Rather than treating each post independently, propagation-aware methods model how information diffuses through user interactions and repost graphs (Micallef et al. 2022; Vavekanand and Ruzimbaevich 2025). Combined textual signals with temporal user response patterns and source-user characteristics, illustrating that diffusion dynamics provide discriminative cues (Ruchansky and Seo 2017; Brinda and Rajan 2025; Samriya et al. 2025).
Graph neural networks (GNNs) further improved the ability to represent diffusion structure. Bi-directional GCNs over rumour propagation graphs, capturing both top-down and bottom-up diffusion patterns for rumour detection (Bian et al. 2020). For multimodal explainable fake news detection (Lu 2020), graph-aware co-attention mechanisms were introduced that link graph context with content features. Despite their effectiveness, these methods typically assume a single-platform propagation graph. In real misinformation campaigns, however, narratives frequently jump between platforms, requiring alignment of posts, users/accounts, and temporal diffusion traces across different ecosystems.
Cross-platform misinformation and multimodal linkage Cross-platform settings introduce additional challenges: (i) heterogeneous interaction mechanisms, (ii) missing or partial observability of diffusion, (iii) identity fragmentation, and (iv) content remixes (e.g., cropped images, re-uploads, short clips, screenshots of text) (Li and Dai 2025; Samriya et al. 2025; Roumeliotis and Tselikas 2025). Empirical work has shown that misinformation can exhibit distinct diffusion patterns relative to truthful content, with potential acceleration and broader reach (Vosoughi and Roy 2018; Vavekanand and Ali Laghari 2026).
For explicitly cross-platform multimodal misinformation (Micallef et al. 2022), studied textual posts linking to videos (e.g., YouTube), proposed a taxonomy describing post–video relations, and evaluated cross-platform detection using paired representations. These studies motivate a chain-oriented formulation: misinformation should be modelled as a connected sequence of semantically related artefacts and diffusion evidence that spans platforms and modalities, rather than a set of isolated posts (Roumeliotis and Tselikas 2025; Thota et al. 2025; Vavekanand et al. 2026).
In parallel, cross-platform research on identity and linkage emphasises that user/account matching and entity alignment are key enablers for constructing unified diffusion graphs (Shu et al. 2017b). Such linkage components become especially important for spread-chain detection, where chain nodes may include reposts, quoted reposts, cross-posted media, or external URL shares that form multi-hop traces. Positioning and gap addressed by this work Recent work shows a shift from single-source misinformation to mixed-source forgery, where misleading narratives combine multiple content channels. Benchmarks reveal patterns such as paraphrased or edited text, recontextualized images or screenshots, and cross-modal inconsistencies where text and visuals seem plausible alone but contradict each other. These trends indicate that effective detection requires models that jointly analyze multimodal semantics and propagation context across platforms. Our approach addresses this by combining multimodal representation learning with cross-platform spread-chain modeling to detect more complex misinformation patterns. Overall, prior work provides strong foundations in (i) multimodal fake news detection (Thakur and Kavindu Jayasinghe 2026; Ai et al. 2026; Liu and Wang 2025), (ii) explainable modelling (Meng and Wang 2026; Wang et al. 2026), and (iii) propagation-aware rumour detection (Ruchansky and Seo 2017; Bian et al. 2020). However, three limitations remain central for real-world deployments:
- Platform isolation: most propagation methods construct graphs within a single platform, ignoring cross-platform narrative transfer.

- Post-level decisions: many multimodal detectors predict labels per post, without reconstructing spread chains that aggregate evidence across linked artefacts.

- Weak multimodal alignment under remixing: cross-platform settings often involve transformed media (cropping, re-encoding, short clips), making naive similarity brittle.


Motivated by these gaps, our work addresses cross-platform fake news spread-chain detection and develops a unified, multimodal, large-model framework that jointly represents multimodal content and diffusion context, enabling chain-level inference and evaluation. Methodology In this section, we present our unified multimodal large-model framework for cross-platform fake-news spread-chain detection. The framework comprises four major components: (1) Unified Multimodal Large-Model Encoding, (2) Cross-Platform Alignment and Typed Graph Construction, (3) Spread-Chain Extraction, and (4) Chain-Level Classification and Evidence Extraction. Fig. 1 [Images not available. See PDF.]
Overall pipeline of the proposed unified framework. Multimodal inputs from multiple platforms are encoded into unified representations, aligned across platforms, integrated into a typed propagation graph, and used for spread-chain extraction and fake-news classification

Figure  1 illustrates the overall pipeline of the proposed framework. The process begins with multimodal inputs collected from multiple platforms, including textual content, visual media, and metadata. These inputs are first processed by the unified multimodal encoder, which generates semantically rich representations for each post. Next, a cross-platform alignment module retrieves semantically similar posts across platforms and assigns confidence scores to candidate links. The aligned posts are then integrated into a typed propagation graph that captures both in-platform interactions and cross-platform relationships. From this graph, spread chains are extracted using temporal pruning and maximum-weight arborescence. Finally, a chain-level classifier analyses the extracted structures to predict whether a chain corresponds to fake or real news, while also providing interpretable evidence through influential nodes and edges.
Task definition and notation Let P = { p 1 , p 2 , ⋯ , p M } denote a set of M social platforms. Each platform p ∈ P contains a set of posts V ( p ) = { v 1 ( p ) , ⋯ , v N p ( p ) } . The global set of posts is defined as V = ⋃ p ∈ P V ( p ) with N = | V | total posts.
Each post v i ∈ V is associated with:
- Text content x i ,

- Media content m i (image or video frames),

- Timestamp t i ,

- Metadata r i (e.g., URL, hashtags, author ID, engagement metrics).


A cross-platform spread chain C k = ( V k , E k ) is defined as a temporally ordered, connected, directed subgraph where:
- V k ⊆ V consists of posts expressing the same underlying claim (possibly paraphrased or visually remixed),

- E k encodes either (a) in-platform diffusion relations (e.g., reply, reshare), or (b) cross-platform alignment relations inferred via semantic or media similarity.


Semantic Drift Across Platforms: Social media platforms differ in language style, interaction patterns, and content formats, which can cause semantic drift in multimodal representations. For instance, the same claim might appear as a long text post on one platform but as a short caption with an image on another. These differences create distribution shifts that reduce the effectiveness of platform-specific models.
To handle this, the framework uses platform adapters that learn lightweight, platform-conditioned transformations of multimodal embeddings. These adapters align representations across platforms by adjusting for stylistic and contextual differences, improving cross-platform retrieval and the stability of propagation graph construction. Our goal is twofold: Extract candidate chains { C k } k = 1 K from the global post set, and predict a binary chain label y ^ k ∈ { 0 , 1 } where 1 denotes fake news and 0 denotes real news.
Unified multimodal large-model encoding We compute a unified embedding h i ∈ R d for each post v i by jointly encoding its textual and visual content. Figure 2 presents the architecture of the unified multimodal encoder. The model combines textual and visual inputs by first converting them into token-level embeddings using modality-specific encoders. A learnable [CLS] token is prepended to aggregate global information. Modality-specific embeddings are added to distinguish between text and visual features before concatenation. The combined token sequence is then processed through a multimodal transformer, where self-attention enables interaction between modalities. The final representation is obtained from the [CLS] token and serves as a unified embedding of the post. This design allows the model to remain robust under content transformations such as paraphrasing or image modifications, which are common in cross-platform misinformation scenarios. If text or media is missing, we replace the absent token set with a learned null token embedding and apply an attention mask to ignore missing tokens, ensuring consistent inference under partial modalities. Another method for handling missing multimodal inputs is modality dropout, where some modalities are randomly removed during training. In this work, a learned null-token with attention masking is used instead to keep a consistent token structure, which simplifies cross-platform alignment and graph construction. While modality dropout may introduce instability in similarity-based link scoring, combining it with null-token masking could be explored in future work to improve robustness.
Cross-platform alignment and typed graph construction The cross-platform alignment process retrieves and scores candidate links between semantically related posts across different platforms. Algorithm 1 describes the cross-platform alignment process used to identify semantically related posts across different platforms. First, an approximate nearest neighbor (ANN) index is constructed over the post embeddings to enable efficient retrieval. For each post, candidate matches are selected from other platforms within a predefined temporal window. These candidates are ranked based on cosine similarity, and the top-K most similar posts are retained. For each candidate pair, a feature vector is computed that includes semantic similarity, temporal proximity, metadata overlap, and media similarity. A learned scoring function then assigns a confidence score to each pair. Finally, directed alignment edges are added to the graph if the score exceeds a threshold and temporal consistency is satisfied. This process ensures efficient and robust cross-platform linkage even under content transformations.

Spread-chain extraction After constructing the typed graph, we extract spread chains via claim grouping, temporal pruning, and maximum-weight skeleton extraction. Algorithm 2 outlines the procedure for extracting spread chains from the typed propagation graph. The process begins with claim-centric grouping, where posts are clustered in the embedding space to form candidate claim groups. For each group, temporal pruning is applied to remove edges that violate chronological order or fall below a confidence threshold. The earliest post in each group is selected as the root node. A maximum-weight arborescence algorithm is then applied to construct a directed tree structure that maximises the overall edge confidence while ensuring that each node has at most one parent. The resulting structure represents the most likely propagation chain for a given claim. This approach enables efficient extraction of coherent and interpretable spread chains from noisy cross-platform data.

Chain-level classification and evidence extraction This stage focuses on reasoning over the full propagation structure rather than classifying posts individually. In misinformation campaigns, single posts may be ambiguous, but propagation patterns and cross-platform reinforcement provide stronger signals. To capture this, a relation-aware graph neural network models heterogeneous interactions such as replies, reshares, and cross-platform links. It aggregates contextual information across posts and highlights influential nodes in the diffusion process. An attention-based pooling step then produces a compact chain representation while preserving interpretability through node importance scores. Training objectives The loss function jointly optimizes content understanding, cross-platform alignment, and chain-level prediction. Relying only on classification could produce weak multimodal representations and poor cross-platform links. To address this, auxiliary alignment losses bring semantically related posts from different platforms closer in the embedding space. A contrastive objective also improves robustness to content transformations by encouraging modality-invariant semantics. Together, these objectives produce representations that are both effective for misinformation detection and stable for cross-platform retrieval and graph construction. The total loss is a weighted combination of chain classification loss and optional alignment losses: 12
L = L cls + λ 1 L link + λ 2 L con .

Experiments In this section, we present the experimental setup designed to evaluate the effectiveness of the proposed unified framework for cross-platform fake-news spread-chain detection. We describe the datasets, baseline methods, implementation details, and evaluation metrics. All experiments are conducted under a leave-one-platform-out protocol to rigorously assess cross-platform generalisation.
Datasets To evaluate cross-platform spread-chain detection under multimodal evidence, we use six widely adopted benchmarks covering (i) news + social context, (ii) multimodal posts, and (iii) propagation/conversation structures. When a dataset requires “hydration” (e.g., platform IDs), we follow the official scripts and comply with platform policies.

Implementation and training setup We instantiate our multimodal encoder by combining a frozen vision backbone with a configurable language model and a lightweight fusion module. Specifically, the vision encoder is CLIP-ViT-B/16 (Radford et al. 2021), which remains frozen throughout training. For the language encoder, we use either RoBERTa-base (Liu et al. 2019) or LLaMA-2-7B (Touvron et al. 2023), depending on the scale of the experiment. To integrate visual and textual representations, we employ a transformer-based multimodal adapter with four layers, a hidden dimension of d = 768 , and 12 attention heads. To reduce computational cost, we employ Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA) (Zhang and Zhao 2021) on the language backbone and cross-modal projection layers. The vision encoder remains frozen.
Optimization We use AdamW (Loshchilov 2019) with, Learning rate: 1 × 10 - 5 for backbone, 5 × 10 - 5 for adapter, weight decay 0.01. Batch size 32 (per GPU). Warmup steps 10% of total steps. Cosine decay schedule. Early stopping based on validation Macro-F1. All experiments are repeated with 3 random seeds; we report mean ± standard deviation.
The end-to-end training procedure integrates encoding, alignment, chain extraction, and classification. Algorithm 3 outlines the training loop.

Statistical evaluation protocol All quantitative results are reported as the mean and standard deviation over three independent training runs with different random seeds. For datasets supporting cross-platform evaluation, experiments follow a leave-one-platform-out protocol, resulting in multiple platform-specific test splits. Each run therefore produces paired performance measurements across identical data splits for the proposed method and baseline models. These paired observations are used for statistical significance testing.

Ablation study results We conduct an ablation study on the cross-platform subset of FakeNewsNet to quantify the contribution of each component.The ablation results Table 9, show the importance of each component in the framework. Removing cross-modal alignment reduces performance, highlighting the need for semantic consistency across modalities. Eliminating platform-specific adapters also lowers accuracy, confirming the challenge of distribution differences between platforms.
The largest drop occurs when chain-level decoding is replaced with post-level classification, indicating that misinformation should be modeled as a propagation process rather than isolated posts. Removing evidence signals like URL-sharing edges further reduces accuracy, showing that metadata provides useful relational information. Overall, the improvements come from the combined effect of multimodal learning, cross-platform alignment, and graph-based reasoning.

- Platform Adapters improve cross-platform generalisation by ∼ 2.2%.

- Chain Decoder is essential for chain-level reasoning, bringing ∼ 3.5% gain over post-level classification.

- Evidence Signals (metadata, URL-share) improve edge detection by ∼ 2.3%.

Evidence signals, though smaller in isolation, contribute synergistically to both metrics.

Error analysis We analyse common failure modes in our framework and find that 23% of false positives arise from satire or parody content that closely mimics patterns of fake news. Late-arriving evidence, where critical debunking information appears later in the propagation timeline, can also lead to misclassification. In cross-language settings, translation errors weaken semantic alignment across multilingual platforms, while private or deleted posts introduce missing nodes in the propagation graph, reducing chain completeness and overall accuracy. Generative models are increasingly used to create persuasive misinformation, especially through jailbreak prompting that makes large language models produce realistic news-like narratives. The proposed framework offers some robustness because it relies on propagation patterns and multimodal alignment rather than only textual cues. Inconsistencies between generated text, visuals, or diffusion behavior can still reveal deception. However, coordinated campaigns using synthetic text, manipulated images, and cross-platform amplification remain challenging, highlighting the need for future research on adversarially robust training and evaluation.

Discussion Our experimental results demonstrate that the proposed unified multimodal framework significantly outperforms existing methods in cross-platform fake news spread-chain detection. The improvements stem from three key design choices: (1) the integration of multimodal large models for robust semantic representation under content transformations, (2) explicit cross-platform alignment that links semantically equivalent posts despite paraphrasing and visual remixing, and (3) chain-level reasoning that aggregates evidence across platforms and time. The framework’s strong cross-platform generalisation is particularly noteworthy. Traditional single-platform detectors suffer severe performance degradation when deployed across heterogeneous ecosystems due to platform-specific interaction patterns and content norms. Our platform adapters and cross-modal contrastive learning effectively bridge these gaps, maintaining robust performance even in leave-one-platform-out evaluations. This capability is crucial for real-world deployment, where misinformation campaigns deliberately exploit multiple platforms to evade detection.
However, several limitations warrant discussion. First, the framework relies on observable propagation data; private messages, deleted content, and ephemeral platforms create "missing links" that degrade chain completeness. Second, while multimodal alignment handles common transformations (cropping, screenshotting), sophisticated deepfakes and adversarial perturbations remain challenging. Third, although scalable, computational complexity requires careful engineering for real-time monitoring of large-scale platforms. Ethical considerations are paramount. While designed for misinformation mitigation, such detection systems could potentially be misused for censorship or surveillance. We advocate for transparent deployment, human-in-the-loop verification, and adherence to platform policies and privacy regulations. The evidence extraction module provides explainable decisions, allowing moderators to audit chain-level reasoning rather than relying on black-box predictions.
Conclusion This paper presents a unified multimodal large-model framework for detecting cross-platform fake news spread chains. By jointly modelling multimodal content similarity and multi-platform propagation structure, our approach addresses the critical limitation of existing methods that operate in platform isolation or at the post level. The framework integrates multimodal encoding, cross-platform alignment, typed graph construction, chain extraction, and chain-level classification into a cohesive pipeline.
Key contributions include (1) formulating cross-platform spread-chain detection as a unified task requiring both content and propagation reasoning, (2) developing a multimodal large-model-based alignment strategy resilient to content transformations, (3) introducing a chain-aware detection module that leverages propagation signals for improved robustness, and (4) establishing comprehensive evaluation metrics for chain-level detection. Experimental results across six benchmarks show consistent improvements over state-of-the-art baselines in classification accuracy, chain detection quality, cross-platform generalisation, and early detection capability. Ablation studies confirm the importance of each component, particularly cross-modal alignment and chain-level reasoning.
Future work will focus on (1) extending to video and audio modalities, (2) incorporating temporal dynamics modelling for early warning systems, (3) developing federated learning approaches for privacy-preserving cross-platform detection, and (4) creating more comprehensive benchmarks with annotated cross-platform chains. As misinformation continues to evolve in complexity and scale, integrated, explainable, and platform-agnostic detection frameworks will remain essential for maintaining information integrity in the digital ecosystem.
