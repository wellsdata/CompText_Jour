Headnote Abstract
In an era of rapid information diffusion, fake news poses a growing threat to corporate credibility and stakeholder trust. Although firms frequently issue clarification announcements to refute misinformation, such verbal denials are often viewed as cheap talk and may fail to restore confidence. This study examines whether firms use real investment decisions as credible signals to counteract the adverse effects of fake news. Using a sample of Chinese A-share listed firms from 2012 to 2024, we identify exposure to fake news through firm-issued clarification announcements and analyse subsequent investment behaviour. We find that firms significantly increased both R&D and CAPEX following clarifications. The response was systematically differentiated by rumour valence: negative fake news triggered higher R&D spending, signalling long-term technological resilience; positive fake news led to greater CAPEX, demonstrating operational capacity to meet inflated expectations. These effects were stronger among firms with weaker governance or poorer disclosure quality, indicating that costly investment commitments substitute for low institutional credibility when verbal assurances are insufficient. The findings contribute to the literature by showing that real investment decisions serve as strategic tools for managing external belief uncertainty, extending signalling theory beyond traditional financial contexts.
Keywords: fake news, investment signalling, research and development, capital expenditure, external belief uncertainty, China.

Introduction
Fake news has increasingly become a critical external shock disrupting corporate operations. Against the backdrop of social media and algorithmic recommendations accelerating information diffusion, listed companies frequently encounter unverified market rumours. These range from allegations of financial fraud and product defects to rumours of major technological breakthroughs and substantial orders. While the veracity of such content is difficult to discern, its rapid propagation creates a distinct form of high-attention information noise. Although firms can respond by issuing clarification announcements, such disclosures often fail to fully eliminate market skepticism (Shi et al., 2023). This phenomenon raises a core question: how do firms adjust their internal resource allocation to cope with the resulting external belief uncertainty when the information environment is polluted by noise?
This paper aims to reconceptualise corporate responses to fake news not as passive adaptation to distorted market signals, but as proactive strategic crisis management. We argue that firms deploy observable, costly, and irreversible investments-specifically research and development (R&D) and capital expenditures (CAPEX)-as credible signals to restore trust with external stakeholders. Unlike prior literature that interprets investment shifts under information shocks through the lens of either catering to market sentiment (Baker & Wurgler, 2004; Dessaint et al., 2019; Polk & Sapienza, 2008) or defensive retrenchment (Barker & Mone, 1994; Czarnitzki & Giebel, 2025; Grieser et al., 2026; Wang & Zhu, 2025), we propose that these decisions serve a distinct strategic function: they constitute multidimensional crisis responses, calibrated to the specific cognitive dimension disturbed by the fake news.
The significance of this study lies in its theoretical reframing of investment behaviour under misinformation. Existing frameworks struggle to explain counter-cyclical expansion observed in practice. For instance, after clarifying data fraud allegations in 2022, GoodWe (688390) significantly increased R&D expenditure, hired more technical staff, and expanded R&D infrastructure. Similarly, after denying rumours of operational suspension in 2023, RichInfo (300634) substantially raised its R&D investment. These firms did not retrench in the face of negative shocks; instead, they intensified long-term commitments - a pattern inconsistent with both defensive retrenchment and catering logic. We resolve this puzzle by introducing external belief uncertainty as a core construct and showing that when verbal clarifications lack credibility, firms resort to tangible actions to repair the broken trust contract.
Using data from Chinese A-share listed companies between 2012 and 2024, we employ corporate clarification announcements as an identification strategy for fake news shocks. Our empirical results show that firms issuing clarification announcements significantly increase both R&D investment and capital expenditure overall. Upon further distinguishing the direction of rumours, we find that firms clarifying negative news primarily exhibit a significant rise in R&D, while those clarifying positive news are dominated by a significant increase in capital expenditure. This strategic response is more pronounced in firms with lower governance quality and poorer information disclosure, which suggests that investment functions as a credible signalling tool when verbal clarifications alone are insufficient to restore market trust.
This study makes three contributions. First, it moves beyond the traditional dichotomy of defensive versus catering investment by reframing corporate behaviour under fake news as strategic belief management, emphasising the signalling function of real investment. Second, it introduces and operationalises the concept of external belief uncertainty, offering a novel lens to assess how information noise translates into real economic consequences. Third, it demonstrates that a firm's pre-existing credibility conditions its reliance on costly signals, providing actionable insights for regulators aiming to mitigate market distortions during public opinion crises.
The remainder of the paper is structured as follows. Section 1 reviews the literature and develops the hypotheses. Section 2 describes the research methodology. Section 3 reports the empirical results, along with some discussion. Section 4 offers a broader discussion of the findings. We then conclude.
1. Literature review and hypothesis development
1.1. Corporate responses to information shocks: from passive adaptation to strategic crisis management
This study centres on the concept of external belief uncertainty - a state where stakeholders' collective perceptions about a firm's fundamentals become volatile and unreliable due to exogenous information shocks. Strategic crisis management posits that crises are not merely operational disruptions but profound threats to an organisation's strategic legitimacy and stakeholder trust (Bundy et al., 2017). Within this framework, information shocks - particularly fake news - act as catalysts of strategic crises by generating high-attention noise that undermines the implicit trust contract between firms and stakeholders. Unlike traditional market frictions, fake news creates a cognitive distortion that requires active strategic intervention rather than passive financial adaptation. Signalling theory complements this by explaining how firms can resolve information asymmetry under such uncertainty: because verbal denials are often deemed "cheap talk", observable, costly, and irreversible actions serve as credible signals to restore strategic legitimacy (Connelly et al., 2011; Spence, 1973). Thus, the core challenge for firms is to actively manage this external belief uncertainty to prevent transient noise from crystallising into lasting operational damage.
The literature on corporate responses to external information shocks has largely converged on two canonical frameworks, which traditionally treat investment behaviour as a passive adaptation to distorted market beliefs (Baker et al., 2003). Under the defensive retrenchment hypothesis, negative shocks trigger reductions in long-term investment due to weakened customer demand or tightened credit conditions (e.g., Coviello et al., 2022; Danso et al., 2019; Stein, 1989; Zabavnik & Verbič, 2024). Conversely, the catering expansion hypothesis posits that positive shocks generate excessive optimism, prompting managers to undertake inefficient investments to sustain high valuations (Baker et al., 2003; Du & Hu, 2020; Mrad et al., 2024; Polk & Sapienza, 2008).
While these frameworks successfully explain investment distortions when market beliefs deviate from equilibrium, they share a critical limitation: they portray firms as constrained actors unable to effectively correct market misjudgements. In our view, this premise fails to capture the essence of fake news. When the shock source is falsifiable misinformation, firms are not entirely helpless; they possess immediate channels to clarify the situation. Therefore, we argue that corporate responses to fake news should be conceptualised through the lens of strategic crisis management rather than mere market adaptation. Under this paradigm, the objective of corporate behaviour shifts from adapting to a distorted equilibrium toward actively managing external belief uncertainty. Investment decisions thus transcend their traditional economic functions and become deliberate instruments of strategic communication aimed at repairing damaged stakeholder trust.
1.2. Strategic signalling under fake news shocks: a multidimensional crisis response
Building upon the strategic crisis management perspective, the core challenge during a fake news shock is restoring deep trust. Although firms can issue clarification announcements, verbal statements are frequently perceived as self-serving defences and fail to restore strategic legitimacy (Nazari & Poursoleyman, 2025). In this context, observable, costly, and irreversible long-term investment behaviours serve as highly effective strategic signals (Bafera et al., 2026). Firms do not view R&D and capital expenditure as mutually exclusive alternatives; rather, they deploy them as complementary components of a signalling portfolio. Depending on whether the market's perception of long-term value or short-term operational capability is threatened, firms dynamically allocate resources to the relevant signal.
This strategic division of labour implies that, in a pooled sample encompassing both positive and negative fake news events, the aggregate response manifests as a concurrent rise in both investment types. We therefore hypothesise:
H1: Firms that issue clarifications in response to fake news exhibit significant increases in both R&D investment and capital expenditure on average across the full sample.
In the face of false news propagation, the specific allocation of a firm's R&D investment and capital expenditure largely depends on the cognitive dimensions disrupted by the false news.
the face of negative fake news (e.g., allegations of financial fraud or product defects), the strategic crisis primarily targets a firm's long-term value, technical reliability, and moral reputation. Under these circumstances, CAPEX, being strongly associated with short-term operations, might be interpreted as a distraction or misappropriation of funds, thereby possessing lower signalling credibility for trust restoration (Stein, 1989). Conversely, R&D investment is highly aligned with narratives of technological innovation and long-termism. It effectively signals a steadfast focus on core competencies rather than short-term crisis management, thus repairing damaged long-term trust (Nelson & Brown, 2024; Piñeiro- Chousa et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2025). Therefore, negative fake news is more likely to drive a significant increase in R&D.
H2: Firms issuing clarification announcements due to negative fake news exhibit a significant increase in R&D investment.
In contrast, positive fake news (e.g., rumours of major contracts or technological breakthroughs) generates market expectations for the firm to validate favourable prospects and demonstrate fulfilment capabilities. Merely denying such rumours may be perceived as forfeiting a strategic opportunity, while silence risks a credibility deficit. In this context, capital expenditure serves as the most tangible demonstration of strategic readiness. It signals to customers, suppliers, and investors that the firm is operationally prepared for potential growth. R&D investment, despite its long-term value, lacks signalling efficiency in scenarios demanding immediate fulfilment validation (Lev & Sougiannis, 1996). Consequently, positive fake news is more likely to drive a significant increase in capital expenditure.
H3: Firms issuing clarification announcements due to positive fake news exhibit a significant increase in capital expenditure.
Prior to a crisis, corporate governance quality and disclosure transparency shape stakeholders' baseline trust in a firm's public statements, thereby influencing how effectively a clarifying announcement can restore credibility. Firms with weak governance or opaque information environments lack an established reputation for reliability, making their verbal denials less persuasive. For such firms, the marginal benefit of costly investment signals is higher, because they need to work harder to rebuild trust. In contrast, firms with strong governance and high transparency enjoy greater baseline trust and may rely less on high-cost signalling mechanisms.
H4: The positive association between clarification announcements and the combined increase in R&D and capital expenditures is stronger for firms with lower corporate governance quality and poorer information disclosure transparency.
2. Research methodology
2.1. Data
This study uses A-share listed firms in China from 2012 to 2024 as the baseline sample. Clarification announcements are sourced from the Chinese Research Data Services (CNRDS) database, while all other firm-level financial and governance data are obtained from the China Stock Market & Accounting Research Database (CSMAR). The sample is constructed following standard empirical practices in corporate finance research (Song et al., 2024). We first exclude financial firms due to their distinct regulatory and operational characteristics. Second, we remove observations for firms designated as ·ST or ST, as these firms face heightened distress and delisting risk that may confound our identification. Third, to ensure clean identification of rumour sentiment, we exclude all firm-year observations for companies that issue both positive and negative clarifications during the sample period. Fourth, we drop observations with missing values for key variables. Finally, we winsorise all continuous variables at the 1% and 99% percentiles to mitigate the influence of outliers. After these procedures, the final sample consists of 25,749 firm-year observations from 3,878 unique firms.

Clarification announcements are typically issued in direct response to high-attention but unverified market rumours, allowing us to isolate corporate responses to information noise rather than fundamental shocks.

In column (1), with R&D investment as the dependent variable, the coefficient on the clarification dummy (PUB) is positive and significant at the 1% level. This aggregate pattern differs from what prior work has documented under conventional negative shocks. During credit crunches or demand downturns, firms typically cut back on both R&D and physical investment (Campello et al., 2010; Stein, 1989). Here, despite the disruptive nature of fake news, firms do not retrench on average. Nor do they simply cater to short-run sentiment as suggested by the catering hypothesis (B aker et al., 2003). 3.3. Strategic investment adjustments by fake news valence
To examine whether investment responses differ by fake news valence, we estimate the model (1) separately for negative and positive events. The results, reported in table no. 4, reveal a sophisticated, state-contingent investment strategy.
For negative fake news, column (1) shows that the coefficient on PUB for R&D investment is positive and statistically significant at the 5% level. In contrast, the coefficient on PUB for CAPEX in column (2) is not significant. This suggests that firms facing negative fake news do not significantly increase their capital expenditures but instead significantly boost their R&D investments. This selective increase in R&D differs from the broad-based cuts documented in conventional negative shocks (e.g., Campello et al., 2010; Stein, 1989). Here, firms do not retrench; they use R&D to signal continued technological capability, thereby repairing long-term credibility. This finding supports Hypothesis 2.
In the case of positive fake news, column (3) table no. 4 shows that the coefficient on PUB for R&D investment is not significant, while column (4) reveals that the coefficient for CAPEX is positive and statistically significant at the 5% level. Firms thus increase capital expenditure on average after clarifying a positive rumour, with no corresponding rise in R&D. Catering theories (Baker et al., 2003) predict general overinvestment following positive sentiment, yet we observe a targeted CAPEX response rather than a broad increase across both investment types. This selective tangible investment helps substantiate the optimistic narrative (e.g., major orders, policy benefits) through visible, near-term actions, consistent with Hypothesis 3.
Together, these results challenge a monolithic view of managerial reactions to information noise. Firms do not adopt a uniform "catering" posture; instead, they deploy contextcontingent signalling strategies: leveraging long-term, hard-to-reverse R&D investments to rebuild credibility after negative shocks, and employing short-term, observable CAPEX to substantiate - and capitalise on - positive misperceptions. By calibrating their investment to the specific nature of the fake news event, firms actively manage external belief uncertainty in a way that preserves both strategic flexibility and market trust.
3.4. The role of pre-existing credibility in strategic signalling
We examine whether firms' investment responses to fake news vary with their pre-existing credibility, measured by corporate governance quality and information disclosure transparency. These dimensions reflect the extent to which stakeholders trust a firm's public statements prior to a crisis, thereby shaping the effectiveness of verbal clarifications.
We first examine whether governance structures shape firms' reliance on investment-based signalling. Following Ammann et al. (2011), we construct a composite governance index via principal component analysis using eight variables: CEO-chair duality, proportion of independent directors, board and executive ownership, top-1 shareholder concentration, board size, supervisory board size, and total compensation of the top three executives. A higher index indicates stronger governance and greater institutional credibility.
As shown in table no. 5, among firms with low governance quality, clarification of negative fake news is associated with a significant increase in R&D investment. Similarly, after positive fake news, these firms significantly raise capital expenditure. In contrast, highgovernance firms show no statistically significant investment changes in either case.
We next assess how information transparency moderates signalling behaviour. We measure disclosure quality using the KV index from Kim and Verrecchia (2001), where a higher value indicates lower transparency and greater information asymmetry. Firms are split at the industry-year median of this index.
Table no. 6 reveals a parallel moderation effect: only firms with poor disclosure quality exhibit significant investment responses. Specifically, they increase R&D following negative fake news and raise CAPEX after positive fake news. In contrast, firms with high transparency show no statistically meaningful adjustments in either investment type.
Together, these results indicate that strategic signalling is contingent on pre-existing credibility. Firms with weaker institutional trust are more likely to use investment as a tool to manage external belief uncertainty, while those with stronger credibility can rely more on verbal communication. These heterogeneous effects align with the costly signalling principle (Connelly et al., 2011): when external monitoring is weak, firms must invest more heavily to make their clarifications credible.
3.5. Robustness checks
Corporate investment involves implementation lags, particularly for physical assets which require planning and construction cycles. We re-estimate the baseline model using investment outcomes in period t+1. Due to space constraints, table no. 7 reports only the t+1 results. Additional tests using t+2 and t+3 outcomes yield qualitatively similar but weaker results, suggesting that the primary strategic response occurs contemporaneously and gradually dissipates over time.
As shown in table no. 7, the positive effect of fake news on R&D remains significant in the aggregate sample (Column (1)) and is driven primarily by negative rumours (Column (3)). This persistence confirms that firms' R&D increases are substantive strategic signals rather than transient reactions. Conversely, the coefficient for CAPEX becomes insignificant in the t+1 specification (Columns (2), (4), and (6)). Given that our CAPEX measure captures actual cash outflows for long-term assets, this result likely reflects the extended execution cycle of physical investments; while the strategic decision to expand is made immediately upon positive news (as evidenced by the significant t period results), the corresponding cash disbursements are often smoothed over subsequent periods or delayed by project implementation phases.
To ensure the reliability of our main findings, we conduct a series of robustness checks addressing potential estimation biases and measurement issues. The results are summarised in table no. 8, which is structured into three distinct panels corresponding to different identification strategies.
Columns (1) and (2) address potential sample selection bias using Propensity Score Matching (PSM). Since firms issuing clarifications may differ systematically from those that do not, we estimate propensity scores based on all baseline controls and perform 1:1 nearestneighbour matching. Re-estimating our regressions on this matched sample, we find that the coefficient on PUB remains positive and significant for both R&D and CAPEX. This suggests that our baseline results are not driven by non-random treatment assignment or observable firm characteristics.
Columns (3) and (4) tackle concerns regarding reverse causality and cumulative signalling effects. We restrict the treatment group to observations corresponding to a firm's first clarification announcement during the sample period, assigning all subsequent announcements to the control group. This design isolates the initial strategic response to an information shock. As shown in these columns, the PUB coefficient remains significantly positive, supporting the interpretation that clarification announcements trigger investment adjustments rather than merely reflecting pre-existing innovation capabilities.
Finally, Columns (5) and (6) assess sensitivity to the choice of dependent variables. We replace absolute investment values with scaled measures: R&D expenditure-to-total assets and cash paid for long-term assets-to-total assets. The coefficients on PUB remain positive and statistically significant across both specifications, confirming that our findings are robust to alternative measurement choices.
Collectively, these tests reinforce the validity of our core finding: firms strategically adjust their investment behaviour in response to fake news to manage external belief uncertainty, regardless of sample composition or variable definition.
4. Discussion
Our results show that, on average, firms respond to fake news clarification announcements by increasing both R&D and capital expenditure. But this aggregate pattern masks an interesting asymmetry. When we separate negative versus positive misinformation, a clearer picture emerges: firms facing negative rumours raise R&D, while those responding to positive fake news tend to boost CAPEX. These effects are strongest in firms with weaker governance and lower transparency.
This pattern is hard to reconcile with the idea that investment simply tracks market valuation or passively absorbs noise. Instead, it aligns with strategic crisis management. When verbal denial alone is unlikely to be trusted, especially under high information asymmetry, firms put money into tangible, hard-to-fake actions. R&D signals a commitment to long-term legitimacy under attack; CAPEX signals growth credibility when the hype is overly positive.
The existing literature has largely converged on two frameworks. One is the defensive retrenchment hypothesis, which predicts investment cuts in response to tightened credit or demand constraints (Danso et al., 2019; Stein, 1989). The other is the catering expansion hypothesis, where managers over-invest to cater to inflated sentiment (Baker et al. , 2003; Polk & Sapienza, 2008). Both explain behaviour during genuine economic fluctuations reasonably well, but they fall short when it comes to fake news. Unlike standard market frictions, fake news generates a distinct form of external belief uncertainty that demands active correction. Our results show that rather than retrenching following negative rumours or simply catering to positive ones, firms use investment, R&D to rebuild legitimacy and capital expenditure to signal growth, as a strategic tool to back up their clarifications. This extends the work of Baker et al. (2003) by showing that when the distortion is identifiable and falsifiable, firms do not merely adapt to a distorted equilibrium; they try to repair it through visible, irreversible actions.
Our analysis also speaks to signalling theory in a crisis context. Consistent with Spence (1973) and Connelly et al. (2011), cheap talk - here, verbal clarifications - is often met with skepticism. The fact that firms with weaker governance and lower transparency exhibit stronger investment responses provides direct evidence of costly signalling. When trust is already fragile, the signal needs to be more expensive to be credible. Hence the observed increases in R&D and CAPEX are not just operational adjustments; they represent a "credibility premium" that firms pay to counter the cognitive distortion caused by fake news. This insight distinguishes our study from earlier work that treats investment distortions primarily as agency problems or market inefficiencies, highlighting instead their deliberate use as a tool for restoring strategic legitimacy.
Conclusions
This study contributes to the literature by establishing that corporate investment, in the face of fake news, functions as a strategic instrument of belief management rather than a mere economic response. Our analysis of Chinese A-share firms reveals a novel mechanism: clarifications issued in response to fake news are systematically followed by targeted increases in R&D (for negative rumours) and capital expenditure (for positive rumours). This finding introduces external belief uncertainty - the credibility gap between a firm's private truth and public perception - as a critical driver of real decisions, thereby reframing investment under information shocks through the lens of strategic crisis management.
The core novelty of our work lies in demonstrating this active, signalling-based response. It departs from conventional frameworks that view firms as passive adapters to market noise (e.g., defensive retrenchment or catering hypotheses). Instead, we show that managers proactively deploy costly, observable actions to validate their narratives when verbal denials are insufficient.
From a managerial standpoint, our findings underscore that in environments where public information is prone to distortion, credibility must be substantiated through action, not just words. When firms issue clarifications - especially in response to damaging rumours - their subsequent investment choices send powerful signals about their underlying health and strategic intent. Managers should recognise that R&D spending can serve as a credible commitment to long-term technological capability in the face of reputational threats, while capital expenditure can demonstrate operational capacity to meet market expectations inflated by positive misinformation.
Importantly, this signalling imperative is not uniform across firms. Our evidence shows that organisations with weaker governance structures or less transparent disclosure practices face greater skepticism from stakeholders. For these firms, verbal rebuttals alone are insufficient; they must incur higher "credibility costs" through tangible resource commitments to restore trust. Conversely, firms with strong institutional credibility may rely more on communication alone, reserving costly investments for genuine strategic opportunities rather than defensive signalling. This insight offers practical guidance for corporate communication and capital budgeting strategies under conditions of high information uncertainty.
Our study has several limitations that point to fruitful avenues for future research. First, while our use of official clarification announcements provides a clean identification strategy, it may overlook unaddressed rumours. Future work could leverage advanced textual analysis to directly measure rumour characteristics from news or social media. Second, our context is an emerging market with high information asymmetry; it remains an open question whether these signalling dynamics persist in more institutionally robust settings. Third, we document the immediate investment response but not its long-term payoff. Future studies should examine whether such strategic investments successfully restore market valuation, customer relationships, or innovation pipelines over time.
