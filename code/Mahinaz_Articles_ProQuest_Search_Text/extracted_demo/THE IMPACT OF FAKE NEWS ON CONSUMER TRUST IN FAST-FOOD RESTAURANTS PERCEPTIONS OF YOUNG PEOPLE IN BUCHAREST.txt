Abstract
The proliferation of disinformation and fake news, mostly through social platforms, and their impact on the food industry, in general, and the fast-food restaurants, in particular, has been analysed in the scientific literature in the past decades and represented a major concern for their management. The importance of studying this phenomenon becomes critical among young people, a demographic segment recognised for both its high consumption of fast-food products and its intense use of social platforms. There are relatively few studies regarding this topic, both internationally and in Romania, and those that do exist have focused primarily on the consumption of fast-food products, without emphasising the impact of misinformation and fake news on young people's trust in fast-food restaurants. That is why this study aims to investigate the perceptions of young people from Bucharest aged 20-34 about how disinformation and fake news, disseminated mainly through the online environment, influence their trust in the fast-food restaurants. The study contributes to the enrichment of the scientific literature, having both theoretical (e.g., the moderating effect of age) and practical (e.g., the involvement of authorities) contributions. In this regard, the authors used a quantitative research method based on an online survey. A total number of 404 questionnaires were validated from young people who live in Bucharest. The data were processed using the statistical software R, version 2025.05.1+513, and processed using the Confirmatory Factor Analysis and the multi-group analysis. The study results showed that age is a key indicator of how young consumers perceive and manage the interdependence between the physical (fast-food restaurants) and the online (especially social platforms) environments. Those who spend more time on social platforms and frequently consume fastfood products are more aware of the negative impact of fake news on fast-food restaurants.
Keywords: fake news, fast food restaurants, consumers, social platforms, trust, young people, digital self-efficacy.
JEL Classification: D12, L66, L83.
Introduction
Since the beginning of the 1990s, the rapid expansion of globalisation has enabled the global diffusion of communication. Sending and receiving data and information all over the world has become part of our life. Therefore, various means of communication, such as massmedia, Internet, and social media platforms (SP), has increasingly gained in importance in the last decades. This is why the information and communication technologies (ICT) have been ever more embedded in today's global society (Wang et al., 2020) whether it is about politics, economics, business, or sports.
The global spread of the ICT has brought not only benefits (e.g., facile communication), but also disadvantages (e.g., false information). In this respect, the exponential growth of the circulation of massive flows of data and information has raised the problem of trustworthiness among their users. The reliance of a relatively high number of people in social media, especially youngsters, has led to an increasing risk of being misinformed (Valencia- Arias et al., 2023).
As the probability of encountering false information has significantly increased, several concepts, such as fake news (FN), disinformation, misinformation, information pollution, or information disorder, have begun to be conceptualised and analysed in the literature. Although the term FN has been used since the 1920s (McKernon, 1925), it has become highly popular with the emergence of the Internet age. More and more, a plethora of SP (e.g., Facebook, Twitter, YouTube, Instagram, TikTok, WhatsApp) allows people and organisations around the world to exchange information and communicate very rapidly. Thus, the risks associated with generating, spreading, and receiving disinformation and FN, mostly through SP (Olan et al., 2024), have become higher than ever (Lazer et al., 2018).
During that time, the phenomenon of disinformation and FN has penetrated all sectors and industries, from politics to business. This is why addressing FN requires organisations, irrespective of their size and domain of activity, significant directed efforts towards their identification and diminishing their potentially harmful consequences (Thaler, 2024).
In this respect, the proliferation of disinformation and FN, mostly through SP, and their impact on the food industry, in general, and the fast-food restaurants (FFR), in particular, has been analysed in the scientific literature in the past decades and represented a major concern for their management. Some studies explore the exposure to food-related disinformation (Melios et al., 2025), others focus on psychological factors and social influence that confer credibility to food-related FN (Castellini et al., 2021), the relationships between food literacy and food-related disinformation (Gabellone et al., 2025) or the role of critical thinking and ethics in relation with food-related FN (Diekman et al., 2023).
In the case of the FFR, their impact is not strictly linear but strongly modulated by the characteristics of the content, the propagation environment, and the consumer profile (Visentin et al., 2019). The importance of studying this phenomenon becomes critical among young people, a demographic segment recognised for both its high consumption of fast-food products and its intense use of SP, where susceptibility to misinformation is influenced by psychological factors (Sultan et al., 2024) and by the possibility of cognitive "immunization" through exposure (van der Linden, 2022). There are relatively few studies regarding this topic in Romania (Fârte et al., 2021) and they emphasise especially the fast-food product consumption (Florean et al., 2024) without illustrating the impact of disinformation and FN on youngsters trust in FFR.
In order to fill this gap, this study aims to investigate the perceptions of young people from Bucharest aged 20-34 about how disinformation and FN, disseminated mainly through the online environment, influence their trust in the FFR. The study contributes to the enrichment of the scientific literature, having both theoretical (e.g., the moderating effect of age) and practical (e.g., the involvement of authorities) contributions. Based on a quantitative research, the paper intends to answer the following research questions: To what extent is the trust of young people from Bucharest in the FFR determined by their digital self-efficacy (DSE)? Which actors are attributed responsibility for combating disinformation and FN by young people from Bucharest? To what extent does trust in the SP influence the perception of young people from Bucharest regarding the effects of disinformation and FN on the reputation of the FFR? How does the level of trust in the SP mediate trust in the FFR? The paper is structured into five sections as follows: the introduction is followed by a review of the scientific literature. The research methodology is presented in the third section, followed by the results and discussion. The paper ends with the research findings.
1. Review of scientific literature
Given that FN propagated in the online environment, especially on SP, are currently expanding in the field of public catering - which particularly affects FFR - researchers, practitioners, and authorities draw attention to the need to combat the phenomenon of disinformation through the digital literacy of Internet and SP users.
FN comprises deliberately fabricated information intended to mislead the public, whereas disinformation refers, in a broader sense, to false or inaccurate information, regardless of intent (Domenico et al., 2021). In the digital age, unverified news and news propagated in the online environment have become ubiquitous, raising fundamental questions about the credibility of information and about consumers' decision-making processes (Duong et al., 2025).
Consequently, digital literacy emerges as an imperative necessity in combating the phenomenon of disinformation and FN propagated in the online environment among consumers. According to Hopp (2022), digital efficacy in the context of FN is closely linked to an individual's media competences, that is, to their ability to navigate the online environment and, implicitly, to access, analyse, evaluate, and create media content. DSE refers to an individual's self-assessment of their own media competences and, implicitly, to the cognitive appraisal of how they use, in the online environment, these competences acquired over time (Ferrucci & Hopp, 2023). Because the phenomenon of FN is currently expanding in the field of public catering, which particularly affects FFR, Melios et al. (2025) draw attention to the existing relationship between DSE and trust in FFR.
Current research demonstrates that individuals with a higher level of education may, paradoxically, be more vulnerable to food disinformation, especially when it aligns with their existing beliefs or originates from platforms they perceive as trustworthy (e.g., YouTube or blogs) (Majumder, 2025). The extent to which an individual trusts the information circulating in the public space conditions their vulnerability to misleading media content (Poulis et al., 2024). In the context of SP, where information spreads rapidly and traditional control mechanisms are lacking, consumers face significant difficulties in distinguishing true information from false information (Chen et al., 2023). According to Majumder (2025), approximately 25% of consumers share viral posts on social networks without verifying the source or the facts. In the food industry, this type of online behaviour can have significant negative effects on companies in the industry, since FN concerning consumer health and food safety trigger strong emotional reactions and, implicitly, the rapid dissemination of such information. Therefore, consumers' attitudes and behaviour toward these companies are influenced by the trust they place in SP and by the extent to which they perceive a piece of news to be credible or not (Borges-Tiago et al., 2020).
Disinformation and FN are frequently associated with reputational risk. With regard to the food industry, the reputation of companies plays an essential role in the survival and development of their businesses. Consequently, FN concerning the food products marketed by FFR can have a negative effect on their reputation and can influence customers' purchasing decisions (Khalaf et al., 2019; Ahmad et al., 2021).
In the fast-food industry, the behaviour of young consumers is particularly influenced by the presence of FFR on SP, by consumers' trust in FFR and SP, and by the reputation of the restaurants (Sadom et al., 2023; Kumsawat et al., 2025). Starting from the recommendation algorithms on SP, individuals' trust in SP is determined by the following factors (Flew, 2021): the Internet user's attitude toward SP, their receptivity to the content and information transmitted through SP, and their preference for using a particular social network. For this reason, FN concerning consumer health and food safety are frequently shared on SP, thereby leading some consumers to be skeptical about the quality of fast-food products.
It should not be overlooked that online behaviour and the way in which individuals relate to SP and FN differ according to the particularities of the generation to which they belong.
Generation Y/Millennials (1981-1996) comprises those individuals who grew up during the transition to the Internet era and, implicitly, to SP. On the other hand, Generation Z (1997- 2012) has never known a world without smart devices and SP, which has led to more deeply rooted digital behaviours (Choi et al., 2024). While Millennials use all available SP to search for information about FFR (Li et al., 2024), Generation Z mainly limits itself to using Instagram for this purpose (Choi et al., 2024).
With regard to young Internet users, their trust in SP significantly influences the propagation of FN in the online environment, with DSE representing, in this case, a determinant of trust in SP (Gupta et al., 2024). Bilişli (2026) argues that young people with a high level of DSE can identify content that is not real, thereby contributing only to a small extent to the dissemination of FN on SP. Having the competences needed to navigate online on SP, the user with high DSE does not immediately accept media content but analyses the information. Consequently, this user is less prone to being misled, unlike a user with (a) lower DSE.
Gupta et al. (2024) highlight that, although both Generation Y and Generation Z have a high level of DSE, young people in Generation Z are more vulnerable to FN than Millennials. Unlike Generation Z, young people in Generation Y tend to verify information from several sources before sharing media content on SP. Therefore, the fact that young people in Generation Z are digital natives does not necessarily mean that they fully understand how SP work or that they possess the skills needed to decode disinformation and identify FN (Li et al., 2024). Although they are more vigilant about the information propagated on SP, young people in Generation Y also face difficulties in detecting misleading media content.
With regard to the trust that young users in Generation Y and Z place in FFR present on SP, research is limited. Unlike young people in Generation Y, those in Generation Z tend no longer to critically evaluate digital content and no longer to verify the facts when their experience with SP is generally positive (Bilişli, 2026). Because they are active participants in digital ecosystems, SP becomes a viable option through which FFR can be evaluated.
Likewise, for young people in Generation Z, SP represents a space in which their opinions and beliefs can be validated. For this reason, if an Internet user has high trust in SP, this extends to the economic organisations present on SP. Consequently, trust in SP is transformed into trust in FFR only to the extent that the intermediary platform is perceived as a trustworthy environment. Therefore, especially among young people in Generation Z, a favourable perception of SP facilitates the acceptance of messages and of online interactions with FFR. In the case of young people in Generation Y, trust in FFR depends more on the experience they have with the companies themselves and less on whether or not FFR have an identity formed on SP (Choi et al., 2024).
Maione et al. (2025) highlight that both young people in Generation Y and those in Generation Z are aware of the negative impact that the dissemination of disinformation and FN in the online environment has on the reputation of FFR. Consequently, FFR are perceived by them as entities directly harmed, which at the same time bear the strategic responsibility of activating mechanisms to restore their reputation and factual truth. Young people in Generations Y and Z consider that constant and transparent communication in the online environment, and especially on SP, on the part of FFR can restore trust in FFR when the latter face problems deriving from the propagation of misleading content in the online environment and, implicitly, on SP (Maione et al., 2025).
In turn, Poulis et al. (2024) argue that FN whose content targets the sustainability and quality of ingredients spread rapidly in the online environment and on SP. Given that SP represent one of the main sources of information for young people in Generations Y and Z, FN shared in the online environment have a negative effect on the reputation of FFR among young people (Ahmad et al., 2021; Duong et al., 2025). Therefore, trust in SP as a source of information moderates the impact of FN on young consumers. At the same time, trust in the online environment, which comprises trust in SP and in virtual membership groups, mediates the relationship between exposure to information and the consumption behaviour of young people in Generations Y and Z (Kumsawat et al., 2025; Latip et al., 2025).
The long-term consequences of exposure to disinformation and FN extend beyond immediate behavioural responses by shaping consumption habits. Repeated exposure to disinformation and FN about FFR can create generalised skepticism, including among young people, making it more difficult to build consumer trust in FFR.
Based on the considerations expressed above, the following research hypotheses can be formulated:
H1 - The DSE of young Bucharest residents determines their trust in FFR.
H2 - A higher level of trust in SP is associated with the attribution of responsibility for combating disinformation to both the authorities and FFR, as well as to SP, an association that varies according to age.
H3 - A higher level of trust in SP does not preclude the perception that disinformation and FN produce significant negative effects on the reputation of FFR.
H4 - Trust in SP mediates the relationship between trust in the online environment and trust in FFR, but the strength of this mediation effect differs according to the age group.
H5 - The level of trust of young Bucharest residents in SP positively influences the level of trust in FFR.
2. Research methodology
To achieve the purpose of the paper, the authors used a quantitative questionnaire-based research. They developed a structured questionnaire, starting from the review of the specialised literature, which included closed questions with single and multiple answers. The attitudinal questions sought to identify the perceptions of young people from Bucharest aged 20-34 on how disinformation and FN, disseminated mainly through the online environment, influence their trust in the FFR. The questionnaire structure included 29 questions, grouped as follows:
* 17 questions regarding the perceptions of young people from Bucharest about the researched topic;
* 12 questions regarding their socio-demographic data (e.g., gender, age).
The questionnaire was distributed through an online survey using the Google Forms during the period 02-27.02.2026. The measurements were taken using a five-point Likert scale (where 1 = total disagreement, 5 = total agreement). The target population consisted solely of young people from Bucharest aged 20-34, and the authors used exploratory and descriptive research methods taking into account the size of this population (304,035 young people). Among the main reasons that underpinned the authors' choice of the target population were the following:
* Young people spend the most time accessing the online environment.
* Young people are the most ardent users of ICT.
* Young people frequently consume FFR products.
* Young people from Bucharest have the largest number of FFRs in the country. The sampling method used was stratified sampling. To this end, the target population was divided into three age groups based on the distribution of age and gender in Bucharest compiled by the National Institute of Statistics (NIS) (Table no. 1). Taking into account the size of the target population, the calculation of the sample size (for a finite population) led to a minimum number of 385 responses required to obtain reliable statistical results.
The representativeness of the sample was ensured both by its size (404 respondents) and by the age and gender structure close to that of the target population (Table no. 2). The selected sample reflected the characteristics of the target population.
The authors validated 404 questionnaires out of the 487 received from respondents (83 of them were not in the 20-34 age group or sent incomplete responses), which led to a response rate of 82.95%, confirming the quality of the survey (Hox & De Leeuw, 1994).
The relatively small sample size allowed the authors to obtain a higher response rate and greater reliability of the data (Wu et al., 2022). The authors centralised and systematised all the information in a database that was processed using the statistical software R, version 2025.05.1+513.
The testing of the 5 research hypotheses was carried out through structural equation modelling (SEM). In a first stage, the measurement model - Confirmatory Factor Analysis (CFA) - was built to determine whether the items adequately measured the latent constructs. In the second stage, the structural equation model was actually built to verify the validity of the hypotheses. In order to analyse the differences between age groups in terms of the impact of disinformation and FN, a multi-group analysis (MGA) was used. This method allows for the comparison of path coefficients between two or more independent groups and the testing of the statistical significance of the observed differences (Henseler et al., 2009; Rosseel, 2012).
The latent variables used in testing hypotheses H1-H5 are self-efficacy, to which items Q8_1 - Q8_4 correspond; trust in SP, to which items Q9_1 - Q9_4 correspond; trust in online posts, to which items Q10_1 - Q10_4 correspond; and trust in FFRs, to which items Q11_2 - Q11_4 correspond. The observed variables used in the analysis are: attribution of responsibility to authorities, constructed as the arithmetic mean of two items (Q13_1 and Q13_7) measuring the perception that FFRs and the government are responsible for combating disinformation; attribution of responsibility to SP, measured by item Q13_8; and perception of negative impact, measured by a single item, Q7.
The sample consisted of 404 respondents distributed according to gender: 51.24% female and 48.76% male. Convenience sampling was used, intentionally focused on young people aged 20 to 34, as they are the largest consumers of fast-food products and users of SP.
Regarding the average time allocated daily for accessing the Internet, 40.60% said they spend between 121 and 180 minutes, followed by those who said they spend more than 240 minutes, 24% of the total, then by those who said they spend between 181 and 240 minutes, 20.30%, and only 15.10% said they spend between 61 and 120 minutes on the Internet.
Respondents consider the following about FN: 73% strongly agree that FN is complete or partially false information, which appears (often) as news and is usually expressed in the form of textual, visual, or graphic content, with the intention of misleading or confusing users, 46% strongly agree and 44% agree that FN propagates false messages containing wrong or false information, but does not report the inaccuracy of the information and 56% strongly agree that FN are false information that is knowingly circulated with specific intentions.
When asked about the sources of information that spread disinformation and FN about the FFR products, respondents almost overwhelmingly agreed that they are television, WhatsApp, Telegram, Messenger, newspapers, and magazines in electronic format, and faceto-face or online discussions with other people. Generally speaking, they do not agree that websites, discussions with family or friends are sources that spread FN.
3. Results
3.1. The measurement model for hypotheses H1-H5
Before actually testing the hypotheses, it was necessary to assess the validity and reliability of the constructs. For this purpose, a Confirmatory Factor Analysis (CFA) was carried out. Because the data were measured on a five-point Likert scale, the WLSMV (Weighted Least Squares Mean and Variance adjusted) technique was used to estimate the model (Li, 2016).
From the results obtained following the CFA, the measurement model indicated a good fit with the empirical data. The scalar adequacy indices have values higher than the minimum values recommended by Hair et al. (2018), with CFI = 0.931 and TLI = 0.912. The value SRMR = 0.076 is lower than 0.08, as recommended by Hu and Bentler (1999).
In order to establish the reliability and convergent validity of the scales, we calculated in Table no. 3 the values of the Ordinal Alpha, Composite Reliability (CR), and Average Variance Extracted (AVE) indicators. Furthermore, the standardised factor loadings (Table no. 3) are statistically significant (p < 0.001), and the Ordinal Alpha values are between 0.761 and 0.843, which denotes very good reliability of the scales. In the case of composite reliability (CR), the values also indicate good reliability of the scales. The AVE values should be higher than 0.5 (Fornell, 1981). In the case of the constructs "Trust in platforms" and "Trust in online posts," AVE values of 0.49 and 0.40, respectively, were obtained. The criterion of Fornell and Larcker (1981) was applied, according to which an AVE level ≥ 0.4 is acceptable if the ratio between CR and Ordinal Alpha is greater than 0.60.
Next, discriminant validity - the extent to which the constructs are truly distinct from one another - was analysed. For this purpose, the HTMT (Heterotrait-Monotrait Ratio of Correlations) was applied. According to Henseler (2015), the resulting values should not exceed the critical threshold of 0.85. The values in the analysis carried out range between 0.522 and 0.725 (Table no. 4), respecting the imposed condition and confirming the discriminant validity of the entire instrument.
3.2. Evaluation of the structural model and testing of hypotheses H1-H5
To test the hypotheses concerning moderation by age, Multi-Group Analysis (MGA) was used, implemented through the Lavaan package in RStudio (Rosseel, 2012). The three age groups (20-24 years, 25-29 years, and 30-34 years) were compared. For each hypothesis, a constrained model was estimated in which the regression coefficients (Table no. 6) are equal across the groups, and this was then compared with an unconstrained model in which the coefficients were freely estimated for each group. The significant chi-square difference tests indicated the presence of moderation by age (Table no. 5). All the chi-square difference tests Δχ2 are statistically significant at p < 0.001 (99.9% confidence level), indicating that age significantly moderates all the relationships in the hypotheses.
4. Discussion
The results obtained following the testing of the first hypothesis of the study highlight that age statistically significantly moderates the relationship between DSE and trust in FFR (Δχ2(2) = 24.77, p < 0.001). The relationship is statistically significant only for young people in the 20-24 age group (β = 0.656, p < 0.001), while the relationship is not significant for those in the 25-29 (β = -0.005, p = 0.970) and 30-34 (β = 0.155, p = 0.403) age groups. Thus, the results obtained for the first hypothesis suggest that it is partially supported, with DSE determining trust in FFR only in the case of young people aged between 20 and 24.
Previous studies demonstrate that an individual's digital efficacy is a determinant of their trust in FFR (Castellini et al., 2021), which supports the validation of the hypothesis. For young people in the 20-24 age group, the better their perception of their own competences, the more trust they will have in FFR present and active on SP. The reason lies in the fact that they are digital natives and, implicitly, are familiar with the online environment and its components. Since they have been very active users of the Internet and SP from childhood, they consider that, by virtue of their long experience with these and the time spent online, they have acquired the capacities needed to navigate online and access information. This explains their high DSE. Regarding young people's trust in FFR, if the latter have a strong online presence, then young people will perceive FFR as economic organisations integrated into their digital ecosystem. There are also studies that refute these results, arguing that trust in FFR depends to a large extent on the consumer's experience with the respective economic organisation and to a lesser extent on DSE (Bezbaruah et al., 2022).
With regard to the results obtained following the testing of the second hypothesis, it was found that the moderation effect is statistically significant (Δχ2(4) = 38.09, p 0.05), and in the case of the 30-34 age group, the relationship is statistically significant for the attribution of responsibility to SP (β = 0.486, p = 0.002) but not for the attribution of responsibility to the authorities and FFR (β = -0.013, p = 0.833). Therefore, the second hypothesis was partially validated.
The results of the present research are partially confirmed by previous studies, which argue that the authorities bear the responsibility for combating disinformation and FN, especially through the imposition of sanctions and the implementation of information, education, and communication campaigns (Zou & Hsiao, 2025). In addition, these studies consider that SP are merely vectors for transmitting information in the online environment, while FFR, as directly harmed organisations, bear the responsibility for combating these negative phenomena (Zou & Hsiao, 2025). Along the same lines, there is research arguing that FFR bear the primary responsibility for combating disinformation owing to the resources at their disposal (Pundir et al., 2021). These discrepancies may result from the fact that the sample consists only of young Romanians from urban areas, as well as from the online content to which they are exposed.
The results obtained following the testing of the third hypothesis support the existence of a significant moderation effect (Δχ2(2) = 71.59, p < 0.001) between trust in SP and the perception of the negative impact on the reputation of FFR among young people. It can be observed from the results in Table no. 4 that the direction of the relationship differed between the age groups. In the case of young people in the 20-24 age group, a higher level of trust in SP was positively associated with the perception of a negative impact of disinformation on the reputation of FFR (β = 0.973, p < 0.001). The relationship is not statistically significant for the 25-29 age group (β = 0.054, p = 0.657). For young people in the 30-34 age group, the relationship is again statistically significant, but negative (β = -0.384, p = 0.023), indicating that older respondents with a high level of trust in SP tend to perceive a lower reputational impact.
Unlike young people in the 30-34 age group, those in the 20-24 age group are active users on SP (they spend a long time on them) and frequently consume fast-food products. For this reason, they may be more aware of the negative impact of disinformation and FN on the reputation of FFR.
With regard to young people in the 25-29 age group, the result can be explained as follows: they are at a stage in which they take on more responsibilities and, implicitly, the time spent on SP decreases. Being at the point where they need to consolidate their financial and professional independence, these young people are required to establish a structured routine. Although they continue to use SP for entertainment and also for information, young people also access other sources of information. Furthermore, in their case, fast-food consumption becomes relatively occasional, and the online presence of FFR is perceived transactionally rather than in terms of identity (Kaur et al., 2025).
The results obtained following the testing of the fourth hypothesis reveal that the indirect effects differed significantly between the age groups (Δχ2(6) = 97.50, p < 0.001). The indirect effect is statistically significant for the 20-24 age group (indirect effect = 0.335, 95% CI [0.167, 0.502], p < 0.001), indicating full mediation in this case. The indirect effect is not statistically significant for the 25-29 (indirect effect = -0.002, 95% CI [-0.019, 0.014], p = 0.778) and 30-34 (indirect effect = 0.058, 95% CI [-0.012, 0.127], p = 0.106) age groups. Therefore, the hypothesis that a higher level of trust in SP does not preclude the perception that disinformation and FN produce significant negative effects on the reputation of FFR is supported only in the case of those belonging to the 20-24 age group, suggesting that the mediating role of trust in SP for the relationship between trust in the online environment and trust in FFR is age-dependent. Consequently, only in the case of the 20-24 age group was it validated that young people's level of trust in SP projects both onto the online environment as a whole and onto the FFR present online. Thus, a high level of trust in SP is associated with increased trust in the online environment and in the FFR present online.
Previous research confirms these results, arguing that young people in Generation Z tend no longer to critically evaluate digital content and no longer to verify the facts when their experience with SP is generally positive (Borges-Tiago et al., 2020). Thus, because they are active participants in digital ecosystems, SP become a viable option through which the online environment can be evaluated, and, in turn, the FFR present online benefit from the trust associated with the SP on which they are located (Borges-Tiago et al., 2020).
As young people grow older, the time they spend on SP decreases. While young people in the 25-29 age group orient themselves toward building a professional career, young people in the 30-34 age group - being relatively stable from a financial and professional point of view - attach greater importance to the family, implicitly having more numerous and more diverse responsibilities. Therefore, in their case, although they continue to be users of SP, these no longer represent a significant part of their social reality.
In the case of young people in the 25-29 age group, trust in FFR is closely related to the quality of the dishes and the nutritional values of the food products promoted in the online environment (Kaur et al., 2025). In turn, Millennials in the 30-34 age group prove to be the most concerned about the food safety of the family and about the impact of fast-food consumption on their own health, being less exposed to the online environment and, implicitly, to SP. Thus, for them, it matters less whether FFR are present in the online environment.
The results obtained following the testing of the fifth hypothesis indicate that age statistically significantly moderates the relationship between trust in SP and trust in FFR (Δχ2(2) = 30.33, p < 0.001). The relationship is statistically significant for the 20-24 (β = 0.644, p < 0.001) and 30-34 (β = 0.426, p < 0.001) age groups, but it is not statistically significant for the 25- 29 age group (β = 0.041, p = 0.660). Consequently, the hypothesis that age moderates the relationship between young people's trust in SP and trust in FFR is supported only in the case of the 20-24 and 30-34 age groups.
There is previous research arguing that young people in the 20-24 age group, in their capacity as digital natives, consider the online environment and, implicitly, SP to be a central component of their everyday life, positively influencing their level of trust in the FFR present online (Chen et al., 2020). Other studies argue that young people in Generation Y (the 30-34 age group), with a high level of trust in SP, are not as vulnerable when exposed to misleading media content, having higher media competences than young people in the 20-24 age group and thus being able to better evaluate the credibility of SP (Pérez-Escoda et al., 2021). Thus, even though the time spent on SP is shorter compared with that of young people in the 20-24 age group, they may have high trust in SP because, through the capacities they have acquired, they can distinguish false information from true information, and may therefore display an increased level of trust in FFR (Pérez-Escoda et al., 2021).
Like young people in the 20-24 age group, young people in the 30-34 age group also transfer credibility in SP to trust in FFR in the online environment. With regard to young people in the 30-34 age group, the credibility of SP is evaluated on the basis of rational criteria, whereas in the case of young people in the 20-24 age group, this evaluation is based on a predominantly emotional component (Park et al., 2020).
Unlike these age groups, for the 25-29 age group, the relationship between trust in SP and trust in FFR did not prove to be statistically significant. Consequently, it is found that, for these young people, trust in FFR is built independently of the degree of credibility granted to the SP on which FFR are present. This result can be explained by the way in which they use SP and the reasons for which they are present in the online environment, a fact validated by already published research demonstrating that these young people tend to use SP as a space in which they express their own identity (Doster, 2013).
The analysis of the results as a whole confirms that age is a fundamental indicator of the way in which young consumers perceive and manage the interdependence between the physical environment and the online environment, especially SP. Compared with the 25-29 and 30-34 age groups, the results highlight, in the case of the 20-24 age group, a greater vulnerability and a more direct transfer of trust from SP to the FFR present in the online environment. For these digital natives, the ease with which they grant credibility to online sources of information, together with a high DSE, acts as an immediate predictor of trust in FFR. In the absence of a consolidated critical filter regarding the analysis of media content, they are the most exposed to the phenomenon of disinformation and FN in the online environment.
As young people grow older, their media competences improve, and the time spent on SP decreases as a result of the growth of their personal and professional responsibilities. Consequently, young people in the 25-29 and 30-34 age groups are not as vulnerable when exposed to misleading media content and, at the same time, become more aware of the presence of the phenomenon of disinformation and FN in the online environment, especially on SP. These empirical results are of particular relevance for young people in the 25-29 age group because, in their case, trust in FFR is determined by the consumption experience and not by the extent to which FFR are present in the online environment and, implicitly, on SP.
At the same time, the results obtained provide explicit answers to the research questions of the present study, while highlighting that age determines the way in which young consumers perceive and manage the interdependence between FFR and SP, as well as the phenomenon of disinformation and FN in the online environment. Conclusions
The past decades have witnessed the proliferation of disinformation and FN in all fields of activity, especially through ICT. In this respect, the online environment, in general, and SP in particular, constitute important vectors for the propagation of FN nowadays. The present study analyses the perceptions of young Bucharest residents (20-34 years) regarding the way in which disinformation and FN influence their trust in FFR. On the one hand, its novelty lies precisely in the fact that no such research has been carried out in Romania, the emphasis having been placed on the consumption of fast-food products. On the other hand, the originality of the present study consists in the identification and emphasis of the relationships existing between constructs (e.g., trust in SP, trust in FFR) that have not previously been analysed in the domestic specialised literature.
From a theoretical point of view, the paper contributes to the development of the scientific literature concerning the effects of disinformation and FN on companies, focusing on their impact on generations of young consumers. The analysis carried out offers new perspectives on the effects of FN on FFR, taking into account the online behaviour of young people. The study demonstrates that age statistically significantly moderates the relationship between DSE and trust in FFR, with significant intergenerational differences. It also shows that young people in the 20-24 age group, who spend more time on SP and frequently consume fast-food products, are more aware of the negative impact of FN on the reputation of FFR. They tend to transfer credibility in SP to trust in the FFR present in the online environment. Consequently, the study emphasises that age is a key indicator of the way in which young consumers perceive and manage the interdependence between the physical environment (FFR) and the online environment (especially SP). Moreover, among young people in Generation Z, a higher level of trust in SP is observed, which is associated with the attribution of responsibility for combating disinformation and FN to both the authorities and FFR.
From a practical point of view, the research carried out suggests that the management of FFR should pay increased attention to combating the phenomenon of disinformation and FN, propagated especially in the online environment, and to reducing its effects. In this respect, the presence of FFR in the online environment, especially on SP, should constitute a top priority for their management. On the other hand, regulatory authorities and consumerprotection associations should play a more active role in combating the phenomenon of disinformation and FN that affects consumers' trust in FFR. In turn, the mass media should debate this phenomenon and signal it in a timely manner to the interested parties (e.g., society, consumers, FFR). Consequently, all interested parties should collaborate and analyse the results of this research in order to be able to effectively combat the phenomenon of disinformation and FN.
The results of the study must be analysed taking into account some of its limitations. The first limitation is given by the relatively small number of constructs considered (DSE, trust in SP, trust in online posts, trust in FFR). Therefore, future research could also integrate other relevant constructs that influence the impact of FN on young consumers' trust in FFR. A second limitation of the research carried out is given by the size and structure of the sample. Future research may use a larger sample, also including the 16-19 age group and rural areas, thereby increasing representativeness. A third limitation is given by the lack of qualitative research. Therefore, future research could involve representatives of all interested parties.
