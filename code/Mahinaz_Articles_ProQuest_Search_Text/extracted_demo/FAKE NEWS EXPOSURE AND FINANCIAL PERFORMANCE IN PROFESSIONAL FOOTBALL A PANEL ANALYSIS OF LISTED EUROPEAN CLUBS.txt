Abstract
The study examined whether the information environment influences the financial performance of publicly listed European football clubs by incorporating variables associated with disinformation into the analysis of football business. The research contributes to the existing literature by linking two areas that are rarely studied together: fake news as an economic and reputational risk, and the financial valuation of professional football organisations. A weekly panel dataset was constructed covering the period 2016- 2024 for publicly listed football clubs and football-related holding companies from the United Kingdom, Germany, Italy, Portugal, the Netherlands, and Denmark, comprising more than 7,500 club-week observations. The empirical analysis employs fixed-effects panel regressions with robust standard errors, complemented by robustness tests and multicollinearity diagnostics. The results indicate that the relationship between information environment indicators and club financial performance is less robust than suggested by part of the existing literature. After controlling for organisation-specific characteristics and broader economic conditions, exposure to disinformation does not exhibit statistically robust effects on stock returns or the economic valuation of clubs in the main model specifications. Similarly, social media usage and media literacy do not retain statistical significance in all estimated models. In contrast, the robustness analyses highlight a more consistent role for digital connectivity, suggesting that access to information and digital infrastructure may influence market reactions more directly than general perceptions of disinformation. The originality of the study lies in its empirical assessment of the relationship between the information environment and the financial performance of publicly listed European football clubs, using a multi-country comparative approach and an econometric framework that explicitly controls for organisational heterogeneity and macroeconomic conditions. The findings suggest that the economic impact of disinformation on football organisations is difficult to identify using aggregated nationallevel indicators and underscore the need for more granular measures of information exposure in future research.
Keywords: football clubs, financial performance, disinformation, information environment, stock returns, digital connectivity.

Introduction
The expansion of digital communication and social media platforms has significantly transformed the information environment in which economic organisations operate, increasing both the speed of information dissemination and vulnerability to disinformation. In recent years, fake news has become a relevant economic issue, as false or insufficiently verified information can influence trust, investor expectations, and organisational reputation, particularly in sectors characterised by high media exposure. Professional football represents a particularly sensitive environment because publicly listed clubs operate simultaneously as economic organisations, financial assets, and highly visible public brands whose economic performance depends not only on revenues and sporting results, but also on credibility and public perception.
In this context, fake news refers to false or misleading information disseminated through digital channels that influences interpretation and decision-making processes, while the information environment reflects the broader ecosystem in which investors, supporters, and other relevant stakeholders assess credibility, media reliability, and public signals. Football clubs are especially exposed to transfer speculation, managerial rumours, ownership-related debates, and match-related narratives that circulate continuously in sports media and digital platforms, often before institutional confirmation. Compared with many conventional business organisations, football clubs face greater reputational volatility because symbolic value and emotional reactions are directly connected to market expectations.
The importance of this topic stems from a growing body of literature showing that disinformation affects organisational resilience, financial behaviour, and corporate vulnerability, while research in football finance increasingly demonstrates that club valuation depends on traditional financial indicators. However, these two research streams have rarely been integrated. This article addresses this gap by examining the relationship between information environment characteristics and the financial performance of publicly listed football clubs, with a particular focus on concerns about disinformation, social media news consumption, media literacy, and supporter attention.
The study contributes theoretically by linking research on disinformation with football business analysis and empirically using a multi-country panel dataset covering the period 2016-2024. The analysis combines weekly financial indicators with information environment measures available at the country-year level, allowing the investigation of associations between information environment characteristics and the financial performance of listed football clubs. At the same time, the study highlights the methodological limitations associated with the use of aggregated information indicators and contributes to clarifying the conditions under which such relationships can be identified empirically. The analysis includes stock returns, valuation indicators, and measures of public attention estimated through fixed-effects panel regressions and robustness tests. From a practical perspective, the study emphasises the need to strengthen communication strategies, implement faster information verification mechanisms, and improve crisis management practices in football organisations exposed to the dynamics of digital rumours.
Because the main information-related variables employed in the analysis are available only at the national and annual levels, the results should be interpreted as conditional associations between information environment characteristics and club financial performance rather than as estimates of direct causal effects generated by individual disinformation episodes. This approach seeks to capture the broader information context in which investors and football organisations operate rather than identify the impact of specific disinformation events. Consequently, the findings should be interpreted as conditional relationships estimated within an observational framework and not as evidence of direct causal effects.
The article is structured into four main sections: a review of the relevant literature and the development of research hypotheses; the methodological framework and dataset construction; the presentation and interpretation of the empirical results; and a discussion of the implications, limitations, and directions for future research.
1. Review of the scientific literature
The growing economic relevance of the fake news phenomenon has generated an increasingly extensive interdisciplinary body of literature examining how disinformation affects organisations, financial markets, and stakeholder behaviour across various sectors. In the context of professional football, this debate is particularly important because publicly listed football clubs operate simultaneously as business organisations, media brands, and financial assets, making them highly sensitive to changes in the information environment.
Fake News as an Organisational Risk for Economic Performance and Stakeholder Trust.. Recent literature increasingly treats fake news as a structural organisational risk rather than merely a communication disturbance, particularly in sectors where performance depends on the rapid processing of information and stakeholder trust. Khan et al. (2023) show that fake news did not directly reduce firm performance during the COVID-19 period; however, it significantly amplified supply chain disruptions, subsequently affecting organisational resilience and learning processes before influencing economic outcomes. In contrast to this mediated organisational logic, the present study tests a more direct market channel: instead of focusing on internal operational disruptions, disinformation is associated with the weekly stock market performance and valuation of publicly listed football clubs through the information environment, as captured by societal concern about fake news. This perspective shifts the discussion from operational disruptions toward market sensitivity to informational uncertainty.
A similar indirect mechanism is identified by Chatterjee, Chaudhuri, and Vrontis (2023), who demonstrate that fake news reduces operational performance by increasing uncertainty and weakening organisational resilience, with this effect moderated by firms' technological capabilities. While the organisations examined in their study respond through internal capacities, the football clubs analysed in our research are exposed through external informational sensitivity, where investor perceptions may react before managerial adjustments become visible. The literature further develops this argument by emphasising how low-quality information amplifies organisational vulnerability beyond immediate operational effects.
This mechanism is conceptually extended by Petratos and Faccia (2023), who distinguish between misinformation, disinformation, and fake news according to intentionality and argue that low-quality information increases risk within organisational systems. Their blockchain-based solution assumes that stronger verification infrastructures can mitigate informational damage; by contrast, the present study examines a context in which verification is structurally delayed, as football-related rumours circulate through fragmented sports media environments and digital social platforms. In a similar vein, Petratos (2021) defines disinformation as a cyber risk affecting managerial decisionmaking, organisational reputation, and business continuity, particularly in trust-based sectors. This perspective closely reflects the situation of publicly listed football clubs; however, our contribution shifts the focus from conceptual business vulnerability to measurable financial outcomes.
Beyond reputational exposure, recent research has also highlighted the governance implications of disinformation. The governance dimension is reinforced by Davidantha (2025), whose systematic review demonstrates that disinformation weakens leadership credibility and distorts performance indicators in digital environments characterised by weak verification routines. This perspective aligns closely with our approach, as football club executives frequently operate under intense media pressure, while our study extends the debate by testing whether such informational fragility is reflected in market valuation. More broadly, the literature suggests that disinformation also influences how organisations are socially evaluated by stakeholders. Gardberg et al. (2026) argue that fake news alters organisational evaluations by weakening the credibility of intermediaries and amplifying emotionally driven judgments. Their distinction between rational reputation and stigmatisation effects is particularly relevant for football clubs, whose symbolic significance often extends beyond financial fundamentals. Unlike their conceptual synthesis, our study quantifies this informational pressure through a multi-country panel dataset.
This reputational logic is further supported by consumerand brand-oriented studies. Research on consumers and brands provides additional insight into the importance of reputational capital under conditions of disinformation exposure. Adriani (2022) shows that reputational damage often occurs before organisations can verify the accuracy of information, supporting our hypothesis that football club valuations may react even when false information does not directly target the club itself. Sharif, Awan, and Paracha (2022) find that fake news primarily affects consumer behaviour through the erosion of trust, while Hossain et al. (2023) demonstrate that brand reputation and signal credibility moderate this relationship. This comparison is particularly relevant because large football clubs may benefit from stronger reputational buffers, whereas smaller publicly listed clubs remain more exposed (Gomboș et al., 2025). Taken together, these contributions suggest that disinformation operates through both direct and indirect channels of organisational influence. Di Domenico and Ding (2024) distinguish between direct and indirect forms of disinformation, and the present study clearly falls into the latter category: rather than examining isolated information attacks targeting individual clubs, it investigates whether a broader social environment characterised by fake news undermines investor confidence in football-related financial markets.
Fake News, Investor Attention, and Financial Market Adjustment. The strongest empirical evidence linking fake news to measurable economic outcomes comes from studies of financial markets, which demonstrate that disinformation alters investor attention and temporarily distorts asset prices. Clarke et al. (2020) show that false financial news attracts greater investor attention than legitimate information and generates abnormal trading volumes, followed by subsequent price corrections. Their evidence, based on events identified through SEC (U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission) enforcement actions, captures direct instances of fabricated financial news, whereas our study differs methodologically by employing a macro-level information environment indicator rather than isolated disinformation events. However, both approaches converge in suggesting that information unreliability can affect asset valuation before market corrections take place.
A complementary perspective is provided by the behavioural finance literature, which explains how investors react under conditions of informational uncertainty. Fong (2021) offers a behavioural explanation through a representative-agent model, demonstrating that fake news initially generates underreaction because investors face uncertainty regarding the credibility of information. This delayed adjustment mechanism is particularly compatible with our weekly panel design, as football investors may gradually revise valuations when operating in environments more exposed to disinformation rather than reacting instantaneously to individual information events. A similar conclusion is reached by Arcuri, Gandolfi, and Russo (2023), who identify abnormal returns in both U.S. and EU markets following verified fake news events. Their international comparative design is particularly relevant to our study, as our sample also covers multiple European institutional contexts; however, instead of examining specific events, we test whether persistent societal concern about fake news produces comparable effects on market valuation.
The experimental literature further complements these findings by suggesting that the effects of disinformation persist even after official corrections are issued. Experimental evidence confirms that the correction process remains incomplete. Du, Wang, and Lin (2025) show that investors rarely fully revise their judgments after exposure to false financial news, even when corrections are provided, and that delayed corrections prove more effective than immediate ones. This finding helps explain why our valuation models reveal persistent negative associations with concern about disinformation: even when false football-related information is subsequently corrected, investor expectations may remain partially affected. In this respect, our study complements experimental evidence by observing actual market behaviour within a longer-term panel framework.
Another important dimension of analysis concerns the mechanisms through which social media facilitates the transmission and amplification of disinformation. At the level of transmission mechanisms, Obadă and Dabija (2022) demonstrate that social media immersion significantly increases the sharing of fake news through behavioural mediation processes. Their evidence from Romania is directly relevant because our model also includes the variable SMNEWS (social media news use), suggesting that reliance on social media may amplify informational distortions before market reactions occur. In this sense, their micro-level behavioural mechanism supports our macro-level econometric findings. Hossain et al. (2023) reach a similar conclusion, showing that fake news reduces trust more strongly when credible signals are weak; likewise, the positive role identified in our study for media literacy suggests that stronger informational filters reduce vulnerability to disinformation. These contributions also support the distinction between direct and indirect effects of disinformation on financial markets.
These studies further reinforce the distinction proposed by Di Domenico and Ding (2024) between direct and indirect forms of disinformation. Unlike event-based studies centred on fabricated news articles, our research captures indirect informational distrust, meaning that football club valuations may deteriorate not because investors observe a single false story, but because they operate within media systems perceived as less reliable overall. This aspect may be particularly relevant in football, where market narratives frequently overlap with the emotional reactions of supporters.
Most existing studies examine the effects of digital communication at the event level, such as transfers, sporting results, or reputational crises, while relatively few investigate the structural characteristics of the information environment in which clubs operate. From this perspective, indicators such as concern about disinformation or media literacy capture the broader informational context of a country rather than reactions to individual events. This approach makes it possible to investigate how systemic characteristics of the information ecosystem may influence investor perceptions and the market valuation of publicly listed football clubs.
Football Business, Valuation Logic, and Disinformation in Sports Media. The football finance literature helps explain why disinformation may generate stronger effects in this sector than in conventional industries. Unlike ordinary firms, publicly listed football clubs represent hybrid assets whose valuation depends simultaneously on financial indicators, sporting narratives, and emotional attachment. Prigge (2019) shows that football club stocks are frequently overvalued relative to traditional benchmarks because supporterinvestors derive symbolic utility from ownership. This finding directly supports our hypothesis that football club shares may react more strongly to the information climate than conventional stocks, as symbolic value amplifies sensitivity to narratives. This distinctive valuation logic is further reinforced by studies showing that financial and sporting indicators do not fully explain the stock market behaviour of football clubs.
A similar conclusion is reached by Abbas (2022), who finds that financial variables explain the stock prices of publicly listed football clubs more strongly than sporting results, although sporting success becomes relevant when it influences expected revenues. Our findings are consistent with this perspective, as disinformation appears to be more strongly associated with valuation than with short-term returns, suggesting that information conditions affect broader expectations rather than reactions to individual matches. Putra and Wasistha (2017) likewise demonstrate that neither financial nor sporting performance fully explains football club stock price movements, reinforcing our argument that the information environment should be considered an additional explanatory dimension.
The multidimensional nature of football performance also explains why information quality should be treated as a contextual determinant of valuation. Glariosis, Germain, and Zopounidis (2018) demonstrate that economic, sporting, and financial performance do not evolve uniformly. Their framework suggests that sustainability in football depends on balancing dimensions that are often contradictory; our study extends this perspective by introducing information quality as an additional contextual determinant. Similarly, Dapena, Esteban, and Fernández-Gámez (2020) identify social media popularity as one of the strongest predictors of football clubs' financial performance. This finding is comparable to the inclusion of the FAN_INT (Fan Interest Index) variable in our model, although our results additionally show that fan attention does not eliminate the negative role of disinformation.
The importance of this informational dimension becomes even more evident within the media ecosystem dominated by sports-related rumours. Studies focusing specifically on football-related disinformation reinforce this interpretation. Rojas Torrijos and Mello (2021) show that football transfer journalism systematically generates rumour-based misinformation, with between 17% and 25% of transfer-related content containing inaccuracies or insufficient verification. Their media analysis provides a clear explanation of why football clubs constitute an ideal setting for our broader information-based approach: unlike general corporations, football clubs operate continuously within a rumourdriven ecosystem. However, while their analysis focuses on journalistic content, our study investigates whether such information environments generate measurable financial effects. Recent predictive approaches further suggest that football-related disinformation is not random but is shaped by identifiable market and media factors.
A predictive extension is offered by Runsewe, Ahsan, and Haider (2024), who employ machine learning techniques to classify football transfer rumours and demonstrate that market value, source identity, and transfer timing significantly predict rumour credibility. Their study identifies which types of rumours are likely to be perceived as credible, whereas our article examines whether generalised exposure to disinformation influences the valuation of publicly listed football clubs regardless of the specific content of individual rumours. Taken together, these two approaches suggest that disinformation in football is not merely media noise but an economically structured phenomenon. For this reason, football clubs provide an especially suitable setting for linking disinformation research with the analysis of financial valuation.
Football clubs constitute a particularly appropriate context for studying disinformation because they combine market valuation, symbolic visibility, and constant media exposure. While previous research has generally examined organisational disinformation and football club valuation separately, our study connects these domains by demonstrating that the information environment itself may influence how publicly listed football organisations are financially valued.
Research hypotheses
The reviewed literature suggests that disinformation functions not only as a communication disturbance, but also as an economic mechanism capable of influencing investor perceptions, organisational legitimacy, and valuation processes in sectors characterised by high media exposure and strong symbolic value (Gardberg et al., 2026). In professional football, where publicly listed clubs operate simultaneously as financial assets, media brands, and emotionally charged organisations, these effects may manifest through multiple interconnected channels involving market reactions, informational trust, and public attention (Prigge, 2019; Prodea et al., 2025). The conceptual framework guiding this study is presented in Figure 1, which summarises the behavioural links between the information environment and the financial outcomes of football clubs. The model assumes that societal concern about fake news directly affects investor confidence, while exposure to social media amplifies informational uncertainty and media literacy functions as a resilience mechanism (Obadă & Dabija, 2022; Hossain et al., 2023).
Based on the existing literature, we expect higher levels of concern about disinformation to be associated with weaker financial performance among football clubs. However, the literature provides less consistent evidence regarding the mechanisms through which these effects are transmitted to short-term stock market indicators.
H1: Higher levels of concern about disinformation are associated with weaker financial performance among publicly listed football clubs. The theoretical rationale for this relationship derives from the fact that financial markets react to changes in informational credibility, particularly when assets are highly exposed to narrative volatility (Clarke et al., 2020). Financial fake news generates abnormal investor attention and temporary price distortions, suggesting that unreliable information environments may affect returns even when disinformation is not directly related to a specific firm (Clarke et al., 2020). Under conditions of uncertainty, investors initially underreact because they face difficulties in assessing the credibility of available information, a mechanism that is consistent with the football market environment, which is characterised by the circulation of rumours and emotionally driven interpretations (Fong, 2021). In football, this relationship may be more pronounced because publicly listed clubs are hybrid assets whose valuations depend not only on financial fundamentals but also on symbolic narratives and supporter expectations (Prigge, 2019; Abbas, 2022).
H2: Higher exposure to disinformation is associated with the market valuation of publicly listed football clubs. Unlike short-term stock returns, market valuation reflects broader expectations regarding organisational credibility, future revenues, and strategic stability, suggesting a potential association with the characteristics of the information environment (Gardberg et al., 2026). Fake news alters the way organisations are socially evaluated by weakening the credibility of intermediaries and amplifying emotionally driven judgments in sectors characterised by high symbolic exposure (Gardberg et al., 2026). Reputational damage often occurs before information can be verified, indicating that organisations may incur valuation penalties even when disinformation remains indirect or unverified (Adriani, 2022). In this context, the literature suggests that changes in the information environment may influence perceptions of organisational credibility and stability even in the absence of specific communication-related events (Petratos & Faccia, 2023).
H3: Information environment characteristics, such as media literacy and fan interest, are associated with the market performance of football clubs. The information environment operates through a set of mechanisms that may influence investors and other stakeholders interpret available information and evaluate organisations (Hossain et al., 2023). The presence of credible informational signals can contribute to reducing informational uncertainty and improving investors' valuation processes (Hossain et al., 2023). Societies with higher levels of information and media literacy tend to exhibit greater resistance to the distortions generated by disinformation in organisational evaluations (Davidantha, 2025). Media literacy is also frequently correlated with other structural characteristics of the information ecosystem, including levels of digitalisation, internet usage, and trust in media sources. Consequently, the literature suggests that the individual effects of these indicators may be difficult to disentangle empirically. At the same time, football clubs remain highly dependent on public visibility, and existing research suggests a relationship between the level of attention devoted to clubs and their performance in financial markets (Dapena, Esteban, & Fernández-Gámez, 2020).
2. Research methodology
2.1. Data and sample
The empirical analysis is based on a panel dataset comprising publicly listed European football clubs and football-related holding companies operating in developed football markets. The sample includes organisations listed on major European stock exchanges in countries characterised by both developed financial markets and highly professionalised football industries. Representative examples include Manchester United F.C. and Celtic F.C. (United Kingdom), Borussia Dortmund (Germany), Juventus FC, AS Roma, and S.S. Lazio (Italy), AFC Ajax (Netherlands), SL Benfica, FC Porto, and Sporting CP (Portugal), as well as Brøndby IF and PARKEN Sport & Entertainment (Denmark).
These clubs operate as business organisations, generating revenues from broadcasting rights, sponsorship agreements, merchandising activities, and matchday operations. Consequently, their financial performance and market valuation may be influenced not only by sporting outcomes but also by the broader information environment in which investors and consumers operate. The dataset covers the period 2016-2024 and is structured as a weekly panel, allowing the analysis of short-term market reactions and fluctuations in public attention. The final dataset contains more than 7,000 club-week observations.
All variables are obtained from publicly available sources. Indicators related to disinformation exposure and news consumption are derived from the Reuters Institute for the Study of Journalism through the Digital News Report. Macroeconomic variables are obtained from the World Bank database, financial variables are drawn from historical stock market series, and measures of public attention are proxied using Google Trends search intensity.
The variables MISINFO, SMNEWS, and MEDLIT are available exclusively at the annual and national levels. Consequently, their values remain constant across all weekly observations belonging to the same country-year combination and capture variation across countries and over time, rather than genuine week-to-week fluctuations. These indicators should therefore be interpreted as measures of the structural information environment within which football clubs and investors operate, rather than as measures of weekly exposure to specific disinformation episodes.
The weekly structure of the panel is used to integrate these structural characteristics of the information environment with high-frequency financial indicators, such as stock returns and fan attention. Accordingly, the analysis seeks to identify conditional associations between information environment characteristics and club financial performance rather than estimate causal effects generated by individual fake news events.
Because the primary source of variation in MISINFO, SMNEWS, and MEDLIT stems from country-year combinations, statistical inference in the econometric models was further validated using country-year clustered standard errors. This approach reduces the risk of overstating statistical precision arising from the repetition of identical information-related values across weekly observations.
In addition, the high degree of correlation among the structural indicators of the information environment required the use of a more restrictive baseline specification, in which MISINFO is analysed together with the essential economic and financial control variables, while the remaining information-related indicators are evaluated within robustness analyses. This approach aims to reduce the risk of multicollinearity and improve the interpretability of the estimated coefficients.
2.2. Variables
The dependent variable reflects the financial performance of publicly listed football organisations. The baseline specification uses weekly stock returns (STK_RET) as the primary measure of market performance. Additional models employ proxies for club valuation (VALUE) and revenue generation (REV) to capture broader organisational outcomes.
Because weekly accounting information is not consistently available for all listed clubs included in the sample, the variables REV and VALUE were constructed as annual clublevel proxy indicators and subsequently aligned with the corresponding weekly observations for each financial year.
REV was constructed using annual revenues reported in audited club financial statements and the Deloitte Football Money League reports, expressed in millions of euros. 3. Results and discussion
3.1. Regression results
Table 2 presents the results of the fixed-effects panel regressions used to examine the association between information environment characteristics and the financial performance of publicly listed European football organisations. The baseline specification includes the indicator of concern about disinformation (MISINFO), together with the essential economic and financial controls GDP_PC and MKT_RET, a choice intended to mitigate the effects of multicollinearity identified among the structural indicators of the information environment. All models include club fixed effects and year fixed effects, while statistical inference is based on country-year clustered standard errors.
In Model (1), where STK_RET is the dependent variable, the coefficient on MISINFO is negative (β = -0,0872) but not statistically significant (SE = 0,0655), indicating a lack of statistical evidence for a systematic relationship between concern about disinformation and the weekly stock returns of football clubs. By contrast, MKT_RET is positive and highly statistically significant (β = 0,6843; p < 0,01), confirming that football club stock returns closely follow broader stock market movements. The coefficient on GDP_PC is negative (β = -0,000039) but statistically insignificant. The model explains approximately 12.6% of the variation in stock returns (R2 = 0.126).
In Model (2), where LN_VALUE_PROXY is the dependent variable, the coefficient on MISINFO is positive but small in magnitude and statistically insignificant (β = 0,0055; SE = 0,0062), indicating an absence of robust evidence for an association between concern about disinformation and the economic valuation of football clubs after controlling for fixed effects and relevant economic factors. GDP_PC exhibits a negative and statistically significant coefficient (β = -0,000073; p < 0,01), while MKT_RET has a small negative effect (β = -0,0010), significant only at more permissive significance thresholds. The coefficient of determination is very high (R2 = 0,977), suggesting that variation in the valuation indicator is explained primarily by persistent club-specific characteristics captured by the fixed effects.
To clarify the interpretation of this result, the within and between components of the coefficient of determination were also examined. The results indicate a within R2 of approximately 0,10 and a between R2 of approximately 0,37, while the overall coefficient of determination remains close to 0.98. These values suggest that the model's high explanatory power is driven primarily by persistent structural differences across clubs captured by the fixed effects, and only to a much lesser extent by temporal variation in the explanatory variables included in the model. Consequently, the high R2 should not be interpreted as evidence of strong predictive power for information-related variables but rather because of the structural heterogeneity among the organisations analysed and the high degree of persistence in the VALUE indicator over time.
In addition, the VALUE indicator is constructed using market capitalisation data and external valuation estimates provided by KPMG Football Benchmark and Forbes, sources that themselves rely on financial and market information related to football clubs. As a result, a degree of overlap between the information embedded in the dependent variable and the persistent economic characteristics of the clubs cannot be ruled out. Accordingly, part of the high coefficient of determination may reflect both the construction of the VALUE measure and the underlying structural differences among clubs. For this reason, the results of the valuation model should be interpreted with caution.
In Model (3), which extends the baseline specification by including FAN_INT, the coefficient on fan interest is negative but statistically insignificant (β = -0.0036; SE = 0.0075), suggesting that public attention measured through Google Trends does not independently explain weekly stock returns once the relevant controls are introduced. The coefficient on MISINFO remains virtually unchanged (β = -0.0875; SE = 0.0654) and continues to be statistically insignificant, confirming the stability of the findings reported in Model (1). At the same time, MKT_RET retains its positive sign and high level of statistical significance (β = 0.6845; p < 0.01).
Overall, the baseline models do not provide sufficient statistical evidence to support a robust relationship between concern about disinformation and the financial performance of publicly listed football clubs, regardless of whether performance is measured through stock returns or valuation indicators. Instead, short-term stock market performance appears to be driven primarily by general financial market conditions. These findings suggest that the effects of the information environment are more complex than the direct relationships initially hypothesised and therefore justify the exploration of alternative mechanisms through robustness analyses.
3.2. Robustness and identification strategy
Given the high correlations among the structural indicators of the information environment and the fact that the main explanatory variables are available at the national and annual levels, several robustness tests were conducted to assess the sensitivity of the results to the choice of econometric specification and statistical inference procedures. These analyses examine whether the main findings are affected by multicollinearity, the country-year structure of the data, or the inclusion of alternative indicators of the information environment.
3.2.1. Multicollinearity and the baseline specification
To assess the stability of the estimates and the interpretability of the coefficients, the degree of association among the explanatory variables used in the study was examined (Table 3). Since the indicators that describe the information and digital environment are constructed at the national level and capture interconnected dimensions of the same institutional context, there is a possibility of relatively high levels of collinearity.
Table 3 highlights strong correlations among the structural indicators of the information and digital environment, with most coefficients exceeding the 0.90 threshold. This finding suggests that the variables capture closely related dimensions of economic development, digitalisation, and information consumption, which may lead to coefficient instability and difficulties in identifying individual effects. To formally assess this issue, Variance Inflation Factor (VIF) statistics were calculated for the variables included in the baseline specification (Table 4).
The results indicate that MISINFO and GDP_PC exhibit moderate levels of collinearity, reflecting the association between characteristics of the information environment and the level of economic development in the countries analysed. In contrast, MKT_RET has a VIF value close to one, suggesting the absence of collinearity issues. Based on these findings, the main analysis employs a parsimonious specification that includes MISINFO together with the essential economic and financial controls (GDP_PC and MKT_RET), thereby reducing the risk of coefficient instability and improving the interpretability of the results.
Table 2 shows that, under this specification, the coefficient of MISINFO remains negative both in the stock-return model (β = -0.0872) and in the model including fan attention (β = -0.0875); however, in both cases it is statistically insignificant. In the valuation model, the coefficient of MISINFO is positive, but small in magnitude (β = 0.0055) and statistically insignificant. Therefore, the relationship between concerns about misinformation and the financial performance of football clubs cannot be robustly identified within the baseline specification.
By contrast, MKT_RET remains positive and highly statistically significant in the stockreturn models (β = 0.6843 and β = 0.6845; p < 0.01), confirming that overall financial market performance is the primary determinant of short-term variations in football clubs' stock prices. Consequently, the main conclusions of the study are based on this specification, while additional indicators of the information environment are assessed separately through the robustness tests presented below.
3.2.2. Country-year data structure and statistical inference
An important feature of the dataset is that the main information-environment variables (MISINFO, SMNEWS, and MEDLIT) are available only at the national and annual levels. Consequently, their values remain constant across all weekly observations belonging to the same country-year combination. Thus, although the panel contains more than 7,500 club- week observations, the effective variation in the main explanatory variables is largely driven by differences across countries and years. This structure may lead to an overestimation of statistical precision when standard errors are calculated solely at the individual observation level. To address this issue, all models reported in Table 2 employ country-year clustered standard errors, a procedure that accounts for dependence among observations sharing the same information-environment indicators and yields more conservative statistical inference.
The results obtained after clustering indicate that the effects associated with MISINFO do not reach conventional levels of statistical significance. In Model (1), the estimated coefficient is -0.0872 (SE = 0.0655), while in Model (3) it is -0.0875 (SE = 0.0654); both estimates are statistically insignificant. Similarly, in Model (2), the coefficient of MISINFO is 0.0055 (SE = 0.0062) and is likewise not statistically significant. These findings suggest that the relationship between concerns about misinformation and the financial performance of football clubs is weaker than might be implied by an interpretation based solely on the total number of observations. Moreover, the use of clustered standard errors reduces the risk of spuriously identifying statistical effects generated by the repetition of identical information-environment values within the panel.
Therefore, the study's conclusions are based on a conservative approach to statistical inference that is appropriate for the structure of the data and the level of aggregation of the main explanatory variables. The results do not provide sufficient statistical evidence to support the existence of a direct and robust effect of concerns about misinformation on stock returns or on the valuation indicators used in the main analysis.
3.2.3. Alternative specifications of the information environment
To assess the sensitivity of the results to the choice of indicators used to characterise the information environment, alternative specifications were estimated in which SMNEWS, MEDLIT, and INTERNET were introduced individually into the model, alongside MISINFO and the essential economic and financial controls (Table 5). This approach allows the contribution of each indicator to be examined separately without simultaneously including highly correlated variables, thereby reducing the risk of multicollinearity.
The results reported in Table 5 indicate that MISINFO and SMNEWS remain statistically insignificant in all specifications considered, suggesting that concerns about misinformation and the use of social media as a source of information do not independently explain the financial performance of the clubs in the sample. MEDLIT exhibits a positive relationship with both stock returns and the valuation indicator; however, the coefficients do not reach conventional levels of statistical significance. This finding suggests a potential association between information resilience and financial performance, although the evidence is not sufficiently strong to support a definitive conclusion.
The only variable that consistently exhibits statistically significant effects is INTERNET. In the stock-return model, the coefficient is positive and statistically significant (β = 0.4285; p < 0.01), indicating that higher levels of digital connectivity are associated with superior stock market performance. In the valuation model, the coefficient of INTERNET is negative and significant at the 10% level (β = -0.0470; p < 0.10), although the magnitude of the effect is relatively small.
Alternative specifications suggest that digital development and connectivity may be more relevant to the financial performance of football clubs than aggregate measures of concern about misinformation. At the same time, the absence of robust effects for MISINFO, SMNEWS, and MEDLIT points to a more indirect and context-dependent relationship between the information environment and financial performance than is implied by simple theoretical models.
3.2.4. Interpretation of the results and implications for the identification strategy
The results presented in Tables 2-5 suggest that the relationship between the information environment and the financial performance of football clubs is more complex than initially anticipated by the study's hypotheses. Although the coefficients associated with MISINFO generally retain the theoretically expected sign across most specifications, the estimated effects do not reach conventional levels of statistical significance after controlling for multicollinearity and employing country-year clustered standard errors. One possible explanation lies in the aggregate nature of the MISINFO indicator, which captures broad characteristics of the national information environment, whereas weekly stock returns reflect reactions to market-specific information, sporting outcomes, and economic developments. Under these conditions, the effects associated with concerns about misinformation may be too diffuse to generate observable changes in short-term stock market performance.
The findings indicate that stock market performance is explained primarily by general financial market conditions, with MKT_RET remaining positive and highly statistically significant across all specifications that use STK_RET as the dependent variable. At the same time, FAN_INT does not exhibit statistically significant independent effects, while among the alternative information-environment indicators, only INTERNET shows significant effects on stock returns, suggesting that digital connectivity may be more relevant for investor reactions.
The results do not provide sufficient statistical evidence to support the existence of a direct and robust effect of concerns about misinformation on the financial performance of football clubs. Instead, they highlight the importance of rigorously addressing multicollinearity and the aggregation structure of the data when analysing the relationship between the information environment and organisational performance.
3.3. Discussions
The empirical results suggest that the relationship between the information environment and the financial performance of football organisations is more complex than initially anticipated by the study's hypotheses. After controlling for multicollinearity among the structural indicators of the information environment and employing country-year clustered standard errors, the effects associated with the MISINFO variable no longer reach conventional levels of statistical significance in any of the main estimated specifications.
Regarding H1, the results do not provide sufficient statistical evidence to support a robust relationship between societal concern about misinformation and the weekly stock returns of football clubs. Although the coefficient of MISINFO retains the theoretically expected negative sign in the models based on STK_RET, the estimated effects remain statistically insignificant. This finding suggests that short-term variations in stock prices are driven primarily by market factors and organisation-specific information rather than by the general characteristics of the national information environment. This conclusion is consistent with the arguments advanced by Clarke et al. (2020), according to which fake news may generate informational distortions and behavioural responses without necessarily producing persistent adjustments in market value.
The results obtained for the valuation indicator likewise fail to support the existence of a robust relationship between MISINFO and the economic value of football clubs. After controlling for organisation-specific fixed characteristics and general economic conditions, the estimated coefficient for MISINFO becomes statistically insignificant. This finding suggests that the informational effects described in the literature on organisational reputation and credibility are difficult to identify using indicators aggregated at the national level. In this respect, the conclusions of Gardberg et al. (2026) and Adriani (2022) remain theoretically relevant but are not empirically confirmed in the main estimated specifications.
Similarly, the results do not provide convincing evidence regarding the independent role of social media use or media literacy. Both SMNEWS and MEDLIT lose statistical significance in the alternative specifications, suggesting that they largely capture the common structural characteristics of the information and digital environment. This finding is consistent with the arguments put forward by Obadă and Dabija (2022).
By contrast, the robustness analysis reveals a more consistent result for INTERNET. The positive and statistically significant effect identified in the stock-return models suggests that higher levels of digital connectivity may facilitate the circulation of information and investor reactions. This finding indicates that digital infrastructure and access to information may represent mechanisms more closely linked to financial performance than general perceptions of misinformation.
Regarding H3, the results do not support the existence of a robust effect of fan interest on stock returns. The coefficient of FAN_INT remains statistically insignificant in the main model, suggesting that public attention measured through Google Trends reflects reactions to sporting or media events rather than factors that directly influence investors' decisions.
The robustness tests also highlight the importance of the identification strategy and the level of data aggregation. The fact that the main information-related variables are measured at the country-year level limits the variation available for identifying effects and necessitates the use of more conservative inference procedures. In this context, the absence of robust effects for MISINFO should be interpreted with caution and not as evidence that misinformation has no influence whatsoever on sports organisations.
Overall, the results suggest that the effects of the information environment on the financial performance of football clubs are indirect and context-dependent. Rather than directly influencing stock returns or economic valuation, the characteristics of the information ecosystem appear to operate through mechanisms associated with digital connectivity, access to information, and processes of organisational reputation formation. This conclusion is consistent with the observations advanced by Du, Wang, and Lin (2025), Petratos and Faccia (2023), Prigge (2019), and Rojas Torrijos and Mello (2021).
Conclusions
This study examined how the information environment is associated with the financial performance of publicly listed European football clubs, focusing on concerns about misinformation, social media news consumption, media literacy, digital connectivity, and public attention. The results do not provide sufficient statistical evidence to support the existence of a robust and statistically significant relationship between societal concern about misinformation (MISINFO) and the financial performance of listed football clubs, as measured by weekly stock returns and valuation indicators. However, this conclusion should be interpreted with caution, as the lack of statistical significance may reflect not only the absence of a systematic relationship but also limitations in statistical power arising from the structure of the data, particularly the relatively limited variation in informationenvironment indicators available at the country-year level. By contrast, short-term stock market performance is explained primarily by general financial market conditions, while the robustness tests suggest that the level of digital connectivity may be more relevant for investor reactions than aggregate indicators of concern about misinformation. These findings highlight the complexity of the relationship between the information environment and financial performance and suggest the existence of indirect and context-dependent mechanisms.
The study contributes to the literature by linking the economics of misinformation with research on the financial performance of sports organisations. Unlike studies that examine fake news or sports media content in isolation, this research investigates the association between characteristics of the information environment and the financial performance of publicly listed football clubs. The originality of the study lies in the use of a multinational panel of weekly observations that combines financial indicators, public attention data, and societal measures of the information environment. The findings suggest that the influence of aggregated information-environment indicators is more difficult to identify empirically than the conceptual literature often assumes and underscore the importance of controlling for multicollinearity and the level of data aggregation.
From a managerial perspective, the results suggest that football organisations should treat the information environment as a strategically relevant factor, even though direct effects on financial performance are not robustly confirmed. Clubs operating in highly digitalised media ecosystems require effective information-monitoring mechanisms, rapid verification procedures, and communication strategies capable of reducing uncertainty and reputational risks. The results associated with the INTERNET variable suggest that digital infrastructure and access to information may influence investor behaviour to a greater extent than general perceptions of misinformation. At the same time, the effects associated with fan attention should be interpreted cautiously, as FAN_INT does not exhibit an independent and robust impact on stock market performance.
The study also presents several limitations. Variables related to misinformation, news consumption, and media literacy are available exclusively at the national and annual levels, which limits the ability to capture club-specific information shocks. Moreover, although fixed effects and clustered standard errors reduce the risk of erroneous inference, certain mechanisms associated with sporting performance, reputation, and investor sentiment remain difficult to observe within an aggregated panel framework. An additional limitation stems from the construction of the VALUE variable using sources that rely on financial and market information similar to those employed in club valuation, a feature that may contribute to the relatively high levels of explanatory power observed in the fixed-effects models.
Future research could integrate real-time media content analysis, sentiment indicators, measures of actual exposure to misinformation, and the direct identification of fake news episodes associated with player transfers, ownership changes, or sporting crises. Comparative analyses involving other industries characterised by high visibility and strong dependence on reputation could further clarify the mechanisms through which the information environment influences organisational performance in the contemporary digital economy.
