Headnote Abstract
This paper investigates how research on misinformation, green markets, and rational or sustainable consumption has evolved over the past decade and a half. Using a Scopus-based corpus of 451 documents published between 2010 and 2025, it applies a quantitative bibliometric approach to map publication dynamics, main thematic clusters, and international collaboration patterns. The analysis combines descriptive indicators with performance analysis and science-mapping techniques, including keyword co-occurrence, bibliographic coupling, and country-level co-authorship networks implemented in Bibliometrix and VOSviewer. The results reveal a marked acceleration of scientific production after 2018 and a set of interconnected clusters focused on greenwashing and sustainability communication, consumer trust and behavioural responses, fake news, and product-harm misinformation, and ESG disclosure and green finance. Together, these strands highlight the role of information asymmetries, signalling, and perceived authenticity in shaping how consumers and investors respond to environmental claims. The paper synthesises key cross-domain contributions into six thematic strands, providing an interpretive bridge between fragmented literatures on fake news, business sustainability, and rational consumption. It concludes by outlining implications for firms, regulators, and civil-society actors, and by proposing directions for future research on how misinformation and greenwashing affect the transition towards genuinely sustainable markets.
Keywords: fake news, green markets, business sustainability, ESG disclosure, rational consumption, bibliometric analysis, consumer trust.
JEL Classification: D83, M31, M14, Q01, Q56.
Introduction
Over the past decade, the rapid expansion of social media and digital platforms has created a fertile environment for the production and circulation of fake news, disinformation, and misinformation. Research has shown that false information spreads rapidly online, undermining trust in institutions and distorting individual decision-making (Allcott & Gentzkow, 2017; Pennycook & Rand, 2021; Vosoughi et al., 2018). Environmental and climate-related misinformation represents a particularly important dimension of this phenomenon because it shapes public perceptions of sustainability, climate risks, and corporate environmental performance (Treen et al., 2020; van der Linden et al., 2017).
At the organisational level, one of the most visible manifestations of misinformation in green markets is greenwashing. Greenwashing refers to selective, exaggerated, or misleading environmental communication that creates an impression of sustainability without corresponding substantive performance (Delmas & Burbano, 2011; Lyon & Montgomery, 2015). Recent studies show that inconsistencies in ESG disclosure and sustainability communication can erode stakeholder trust and create uncertainty for consumers, investors, and regulators (de Freitas Netto et al., 2020; Szabo & Webster, 2021; Yu et al., 2020).
From the consumer perspective, misleading sustainability claims interact with broader tensions between environmental attitudes and actual market behaviour. Previous research documents a persistent attitude-behaviour gap in sustainable consumption, where consumers often express pro-environmental values but fail to translate them into consistent purchasing decisions (Vermeir & Verbeke, 2006). At the same time, social media communication, green advertising, and online misinformation increasingly shape perceptions of sustainable products and influence trust, scepticism, and purchasing intentions (Bezbaruah et al., 2022; do Paço et al., 2019; White et al., 2019).
These developments can be interpreted through the lens of information asymmetry and signalling theory. In markets characterised by uncertainty, sustainability claims, eco-labels, and ESG disclosures function as signals of otherwise difficult-to-observe quality (Akerlof, 1970; Spence, 1973). However, misinformation and greenwashing may distort these signals, creating reputational risks, inefficient consumer choices, and potential market failures.
Despite growing research on fake news, business sustainability, ESG disclosure, and sustainable consumption, the literature remains fragmented across disciplines. Research on misinformation primarily focuses on political communication and digital media, while studies on greenwashing and ESG are often situated within sustainability and finance, and research on sustainable consumption is mainly rooted in consumer behaviour and marketing. Although bibliometric methods are increasingly used to map complex interdisciplinary domains (Aria & Cuccurullo, 2017; Donthu et al., 2021; Zupic & Čater, 2015), there is still no integrative bibliometric overview that systematically connects misinformation, green markets, and rational or sustainable consumption.
This article addresses this gap by conducting a cross-domain bibliometric analysis of global scientific production at the intersection of misinformation, business sustainability, and rational consumption during the period 2010-2025. Drawing on the Scopus database, the study analyses journal articles and conference papers in business, management, sustainability, economics, marketing, and communication.
The following research questions guide the study:
* RQ1: How has the literature connecting fake news, business organisations, and rational or sustainable consumption evolved quantitatively and thematically over the last 10-15 years?
* RQ2: What are the dominant thematic clusters at the intersection of misinformation, business sustainability, and consumer behaviour?
* RQ3: Which theories and concepts are most frequently employed, and how are they distributed across thematic clusters?
* RQ4: How are existing studies distributed across societal/regulatory, organisational, and consumer perspectives?
The article makes three contributions. First, it provides one of the first integrated bibliometric overviews connecting research on fake news, business sustainability, ESG disclosure, and sustainable consumption. Second, it develops a conceptual link between misinformation, sustainability communication, and consumer decision-making through the perspective of information asymmetries and signalling. Third, it identifies emerging research directions related to digital misinformation, ESG communication, and sustainable market transitions.
The remainder of the paper is structured as follows. The next section presents the theoretical background and literature streams relevant to the study. The methodology section explains the bibliometric design and analytical procedures. The results section reports the main descriptive and science-mapping findings, while the concluding sections discuss implications, limitations, and future research directions.
1. Background
The literature relevant for this study can be grouped into three partly overlapping streams: research on fake news and misinformation in digital environments, work on greenwashing and ESG-related corporate communication, and studies on sustainable or rational consumption.
A first stream investigates the dynamics of fake news and misinformation on social media and their consequences for political, economic, and everyday decision-making. Early empirical studies of the 2016 US election found that false news stories reached wider audiences and circulated more rapidly than accurate information, raising concerns about manipulation and erosion of trust in democratic systems (Allcott & Gentzkow, 2017; Vosoughi et al., 2018). Subsequent work broadened the focus from single events to the structural properties of online misinformation ecosystems, emphasising the role of algorithms, polarisation, and cognitive heuristics (Aïmeur et al., 2023; Pennycook & Rand, 2021). In addition, some studies have moved from politics to the commercial and environmental domain. They show that fake or misleading content about brands and products can shape attitudes and behavioural intentions, especially when users rely on peripheral cues such as source credibility or social endorsement (Borges-Tiago et al., 2020; Visentin et al., 2019). Research on fake news sharing identifies psychological and social motives, including entertainment, status seeking and "dark side" uses of social media that help explain why users disseminate inaccurate information about consumption and health, even when they do not fully believe it (Bezbaruah et al., 2022; Talwar et al., 2019). In environmental communication, specific attention has been paid to climate-related misinformation and deepfakes, which can sow doubt about scientific consensus and generate uncertainty about the credibility of audio-visual evidence (Treen et al., 2020; Vaccari & Chadwick, 2020; van der Linden et al., 2017).
A second stream deals with greenwashing and ESG-related disclosure as forms of corporate misinformation or, at a minimum, highly selective signalling. Foundational contributions conceptualise greenwashing as the discrepancy between symbolic communication and substantive environmental performance, driven by reputational incentives and competitive pressures (Delmas & Burbano, 2011; Lyon & Montgomery, 2015). Systematic reviews and concept analyses map a wide range of practices, from vague, unverifiable claims to legal compliance "green talk" that overstates minor improvements (de Freitas Netto et al., 2020; Spaniol et al., 2024). Beyond communication tactics, firm-level structural characteristics, such as energy consumption intensity, have also been shown to shape incentives for greenwashing, with more energy-intensive firms exhibiting a stronger propensity to overstate their environmental performance (Li et al., 2025). Empirical studies demonstrate that perceived greenwashing can reduce customer satisfaction, increase scepticism, and trigger brand avoidance, especially when consumers feel deceived about a company's environmental performance (Ioannou et al., 2023; Sajid et al., 2024; Szabo & Webster, 2021; Tu, 2024; Xiao et al., 2022). At the same time, work in finance and corporate governance shows that misleading or opaque ESG disclosure affects investor decisions, firm valuation, and market stability by introducing noise into sustainability-related signals (Hu et al., 2023; Kathan et al., 2025; Xu et al., 2025; Yu et al., 2020; Zhang et al., 2024). Studies of high-profile scandals such as Dieselgate illustrate how environmental fraud can spill over into entire industries, reinforcing perceptions of systemic unreliability (Ater & Yoseph, 2022; Siano et al., 2017). More recent contributions adopt a stakeholder and business-model perspective, examining how firms integrate greenwashing into broader communication strategies and how sustainability ratings or social-media content can both deter and amplify deceptive practices (Dutta-Powell et al., 2023; Oppong-Tawiah & Webster, 2023; Parguel et al., 2011; Pedersen & Andersen, 2023; Santos et al., 2024).
A third stream focuses on sustainable and rational consumption, particularly the attitude- behaviour gap between pro-environmental values and actual purchasing decisions (Vermeir & Verbeke, 2006). Recent studies show that social media, influencers, and digital communication increasingly shape sustainable consumption patterns by influencing consumer trust, scepticism, and perceptions of authenticity (Buvár et al., 2023; do Paço et al., 2019; Ng et al., 2025; White et al., 2019).
Across these three streams, a common theoretical thread is the role of information asymmetries and signalling in green markets. Building on the economics of information, current research implicitly or explicitly interprets sustainability claims, eco-labels, and ESG scores as signals of otherwise unobservable quality, whose credibility can be weakened by fake news, strategic communication, and rating disagreements (Akerlof, 1970; Bo et al., 2024; Hu et al., 2024; Kapil & Rawal, 2023; Lublóy et al., 2025; Sneiderienė & Legenzova, 2025; Spence, 1973). However, these insights remain fragmented across disciplines. Existing reviews typically focus either on greenwashing, on fake news in marketing, or on sustainable consumption in isolation.
There is a smaller but growing body of work that uses bibliometric methods to systematise these fields. Studies in management and organisation research demonstrate how performance analysis and science mapping can reveal the intellectual structure of complex domains and inform future research agendas (Aria & Cuccurullo, 2017; Zupic & Čater, 2015). Recent bibliometric reviews in sustainability and finance apply similar tools to map the evolution of ESG investing, greenwashing, and related stakeholder debates (Donthu et al., 2021; Kapil & Rawal, 2023; Lublóy et al., 2025; Sneiderienė & Legenzova, 2025). However, these studies remain confined to a single domain. What is still missing, and what the present article seeks to provide, is a cross-domain bibliometric overview that systematically connects research on misinformation, business sustainability, and rational consumption, and thereby clarifies how concepts, theories, and empirical findings travel across regulatory, organisational, and consumer perspectives.
2. Research methodology
This study adopts a quantitative bibliometric approach to analyse scientific production at the intersection of misinformation, business sustainability, and rational or sustainable consumption. Bibliometric methods are widely used to map the conceptual and intellectual structure of interdisciplinary research fields (Aria & Cuccurullo, 2017; Donthu et al., 2021; Zupic & Čater, 2015).
Scopus was selected as the data source because of its broad coverage of journals in business, management, marketing, economics, sustainability, and communication, as well as its compatibility with bibliometric software tools. The search strategy focused on three domains: (1) fake news and misinformation; (2) greenwashing and ESG-related communication; and (3) consumer behaviour and sustainable consumption.
The bibliometric search was conducted in February 2026 using title, abstract, and keyword fields in Scopus. Three groups of keywords were combined with the Boolean operator AND. The first group captured fake news and information disorders ("fake news" OR misinformation OR disinformation OR "information disorder"). The second group focused on greenwashing and environmental communication (greenwash· OR "misleading environmental claims" OR "false environmental claims" OR "deceptive environmental"). The third group targeted consumer behaviour and sustainable consumption (consumer· OR "purchase intention" OR "buying intention" OR "brand trust" OR "consumer trust" OR "consumer behaviour" OR "sustainab· consumption" OR "responsible consumption").
The exact Scopus query was:
TITLE-ABS-KEY (("fake news" OR misinformation OR disinformation OR "information disorder") AND (greenwash· OR "misleading environmental claims" OR "false environmental claims" OR "deceptive environmental") AND (consumer· OR "purchase intention" OR "buying intention" OR "brand trust" OR "consumer trust" OR "consumer behaviour" OR "sustainab· consumption" OR "responsible consumption")).
The search was limited to English-language publications from 2010-2025 in the subject areas Business, Management and Accounting; Social Sciences; Environmental Science; Energy; Economics, Econometrics and Finance; and Decision Sciences. Only journal articles and conference papers were included. After filtering and screening procedures, the final corpus comprised 451 documents. Although Scopus provides broad international coverage, the focus on English-language publications may underrepresent regional and practitioner-oriented research.
The bibliographic records were exported in CSV format and analysed in the R environment. Standard data-cleaning procedures were applied to harmonise author names, affiliations, journal titles, and keywords to ensure consistency in the bibliometric networks (Zupic & Čater, 2015).
The empirical analysis combined descriptive indicators, performance analysis, and sciencemapping techniques. Descriptive analysis examined publication trends, citations, and geographical distribution of research output. Performance analysis identified influential authors, articles, and sources within the corpus. Science-mapping techniques, particularly keyword co-occurrence and bibliographic coupling, were used to uncover the conceptual structure of the field and detect major thematic clusters.
The analysis was conducted using the Bibliometrix package and Biblioshiny interface in R (Aria & Cuccurullo, 2017), while VOSviewer was used to generate keyword co-occurrence and bibliographic coupling maps (van Eck & Waltman, 2010).
The overall research design consisted of four stages: conceptual delimitation and formulation of research questions; data collection and screening; bibliometric analysis; and interpretation of thematic clusters and economic implications. These stages are summarised in figure no. 1.
3. Findings
The bibliometric analysis is organised into three main parts: a descriptive overview of the publication corpus, a performance analysis of the most influential sources and contributions, and a science-mapping section that examines the field's conceptual structure.
3.1. Descriptive analysis
The final Scopus corpus comprises 451 documents, all dealing with the intersection of greenwashing, environmental communication, and (un)sustainable consumption. The vast majority are journal articles (426; 94.46%), while a smaller share consists of conference papers (25; 5.54%), confirming that the topic is already well established in peer-reviewed journals but still generates contributions in conference proceedings.
Regarding temporal distribution, publications are strongly concentrated in the last few years of the 2010-2025 period (Table no. 1).
Until 2019, the annual output remained below 20 papers, indicating a relatively slow and fragmented development of the field during its early phase. A more consistent upward trajectory begins after 2017, despite some fluctuations, with the number of publications increasing from 14 in 2017 to 17 in 2019 and then crossing the threshold of 20 in 2020.
From that point onward, growth accelerates steadily, reaching 30 publications in 2021, 38 in 2022, and 47 in 2023. The most pronounced expansion occurs in the final two years of the period, with 86 papers in 2024 and 158 in 2025. These two years alone account for just over half of the total sample (approximately 54%), suggesting that the intersection of misinformation, sustainability, and consumption has recently transitioned from an emerging topic to a rapidly expanding and highly visible research area.
In terms of geographical distribution, the corpus is dominated by contributions from a relatively small group of countries. The United States (48 papers), China (47), and India (42) are the three most productive countries, followed by the United Kingdom (23) and Germany (21). European countries such as Italy, Portugal, and Spain, as well as Brazil and Malaysia, also appear among the top contributors. Together, the ten most prolific countries account for more than half of all documents in the sample, indicating that research on greenwashing and sustainable consumption is concentrated in a limited set of national contexts, with strong representation from both advanced and emerging economies.
The geographical profile of the corpus thus reveals a concentration of research activity in a limited set of countries that combine strong academic infrastructures with significant exposure to sustainability and ESG debates. Anglo-Saxon economies and large emerging markets occupy the leading positions, while several European Union member states appear among the main contributors, reflecting both the regulatory salience of green claims in the EU and the growing visibility of greenwashing cases in global media. Importantly, many regions remain only marginally represented, suggesting that current knowledge about misinformation, green markets, and sustainable consumption is still shaped by a relatively narrow set of national contexts. These descriptive patterns provide the backdrop for the performance analysis in the next subsection, which examines the most influential journals, authors, and articles that structure this interdisciplinary field.
3.2. Performance analysis
Based on a sample of 451 Scopus-indexed documents, citation analysis was used to examine the evolution and scientific impact of research on misinformation, greenwashing, and sustainable consumption between 2010 and 2025. During the initial phase (2010-2014), publication output remained limited, with only one to seven documents annually, although several early contributions accumulated a relatively high number of citations, indicating the presence of a small group of highly influential studies. From 2015 onwards, publication activity gradually increased, accompanied by sustained citation growth, suggesting growing visibility of research on greenwashing, sustainability communication, and consumer responses. The most pronounced expansion occurred after 2020, with publication output rising from 21 papers in 2020 to 158 in 2025. Citation activity followed a similar trajectory, with the 2018-2021 cohorts receiving the highest accumulated impact. Lower citation counts for 2024-2025 primarily reflect the shorter time available for recently published studies to accumulate citations.
3.3. Science Mapping: Conceptual structure of research on fake news, greenwashing, and sustainable consumption
This section presents the conceptual and geographical structure of research at the intersection of misinformation, business sustainability, and (un)sustainable consumption, based on science-mapping techniques in VOSviewer. We focus on a keyword co-occurrence map and a country-level bibliographic coupling map to capture both the main thematic axes and the spatial distribution of scientific production.
Conceptual structure - keyword co-occurrence.
The main conceptual map (Figure no. 2) is based on a co-occurrence analysis of all keywords. A minimum threshold of four occurrences per keyword was applied, resulting in a network of 154 harmonised terms. At the centre of the network, greenwashing and sustainability clearly dominate, forming the core around which other topics are organised. Closely connected to this core is a dense group of terms related to consumers and their behaviour (consumer, consumption behaviour, public attitude, green advertising, brand trust), which together form the main "consumer-marketing" cluster.
A second visible cluster aggregates terms linked to supply chains and circular economy (supply chain management, circular economy, environmental impact, transparency), highlighting the structural effects of green communication along the value chain. A third cluster revolves around green marketing and green scepticism, including expressions such as perceived greenwashing, green brand image, and green trust, which indicate how misleading environmental claims influence consumer trust and loyalty. The map also includes nodes such as climate change, environmental communication, and social media, connecting the ecological dimension to digital communication channels. Terms explicitly referring to fake news and related notions appear, but mostly at the margins of the network, suggesting that existing work has primarily focused on greenwashing and sustainable consumption, while the explicit dimension of misinformation is only beginning to emerge.
Geographical structure - bibliographic coupling by countries
The second map (Figure no. 3) depicts the bibliographic coupling of countries based on shared cited references. The analysis uses a threshold of at least one document and one cited reference per country, resulting in a network of 69 countries contributing to research on greenwashing, sustainability, and consumer behaviour.
The map reveals several densely connected clusters. One of the largest nodes is the United States, which occupies a central position and links to many other countries, including the United Kingdom and several Commonwealth and European partners, thus forming a strong Anglo-American research block.
A second large cluster groups major European countries such as Germany, France, Spain, Italy, and the Netherlands, which share similar reference bases and frequently co-appear in the same publications.
A third cluster comprises Asian and Pacific countries, with China, India, Malaysia, and Vietnam among the most prominent. These countries are increasingly involved in international collaborations and combine regulatory, market, and consumer perspectives on green markets. Smaller countries, including those from Central and Eastern Europe, appear as peripheral but clearly connected nodes, indicating the gradual inclusion of a wider range of national research communities in this field.
3.4. Discussion and synthesis of thematic clusters
The science-mapping results reveal several coherent thematic clusters that connect misinformation, green markets, and (un)sustainable consumption. To deepen the interpretation of these clusters and to prepare the ground for the concluding discussion, this subsection briefly synthesises the main insights from a set of influential cross-domain studies. Rather than providing an exhaustive review, the focus is on papers that explicitly link at least two of the three focal domains and that are frequently referenced in the literature. The selected contributions are grouped into six thematic strands that correspond closely to the clusters identified in the co-word and co-authorship maps (Table no. 2).
The first strand comprises conceptual and review work that clarifies what greenwashing is and how it operates. Delmas and Burbano (2011) develop a widely used framework that distinguishes between firm-level, institutional, and communication drivers of greenwashing, showing how misleading environmental claims emerge from the interaction of regulatory pressure, market incentives, and organisational characteristics. Building on this, de Freitas Netto et al. (2020) offer a systematic review that maps the proliferation of greenwashing concepts and proposes a typology of practices, from vague claims and selective disclosure to outright falsification. Together, these contributions provide the conceptual backbone for the "greenwashing" and "sustainability communication" clusters visible in the keyword network.
A second strand investigates how consumers react when they perceive environmental communication as deceptive or exaggerated. De Jong et al. (2020) differentiates between environmental "lies", "half-truths", and legally compliant but exaggerated claims, demonstrating that all three forms can erode corporate credibility, albeit to different degrees. Schmuck et al. (2018) proposes an affective-cognitive model of green advertising, showing that greenwashing triggers negative emotions and scepticism that depress purchase intentions, even among environmentally concerned consumers. More recently, Delgado Martínez et al. (2025) compared consumer responses to genuinely sustainable versus greenwashed service offerings, reinforcing the conclusion that perceived authenticity is a central driver of trust. These studies collectively underpin the consumer-centric part of the "greenwashing-trust-behaviour" cluster.
A third group of contributions links greenwashing directly to firm-level and financial outcomes. Testa et al. (2018) examine whether firms benefit economically by presenting themselves as greener than they are, or whether "brownwashing" (openly disclosing poor performance) is more advantageous in the long run. Their panel-data results suggest that the relationship between environmental disclosure, reputation, and financial performance is complex and contingent. Li et al. (2025) extends this line of work by analysing how energy consumption intensity and other structural characteristics of firms shape the propensity to engage in greenwashing, thereby connecting environmental performance, disclosure strategy, and market incentives. These papers support the interpretation of greenwashing not just as a communication problem, but as part of broader business-sustainability dynamics.
The fourth strand integrates sustainability communication with the literature on fake news and social media. Di Domenico et al. (2021) provide a systematic review of fake news, social media, and marketing, highlighting how misinformation can distort consumers' perceptions of both environmental problems and corporate responsibility. Borges-Tiago et al. (2020) empirically analyse online users' attitudes toward fake news and draw implications for brand management, while Buvár et al. (2023) show that even non-green influencers can promote sustainable consumption when they communicate dynamic social norms. This strand explains why the bibliometric clusters around "social media", "communication" and "green advertising" are tightly connected to both greenwashing and behavioural outcomes.
A fifth thematic strand focuses more narrowly on fake news and product-harm misinformation at the brand level. Chen and Cheng (2020) investigate how exposure to fake news about brands on social media influences brand trust and purchase intentions, and how self-efficacy, media trust, and persuasion knowledge may buffer these effects. In a related study, Chen and Cheng (2023) examine the diffusion of product-harm misinformation, documenting how different actors contribute to its spread and correction. These contributions illuminate the mechanism through which misinformation interacts with brand-related sustainability claims and feeds into the "fake news-trust-consumer response" cluster.
Finally, a sixth strand links misinformation and greenwashing debates to questions of governance, ESG disclosure, and financial communication. Petratos (2021) conceptualises misinformation, disinformation, and fake news as specific forms of cyber risk for business, arguing that firms need to integrate information disorders into their risk-management and governance frameworks. Bo et al. (2024) extends this discussion to the domain of green finance, exploring how ESG disclosure can simultaneously constrain and enable greenwashing and proposing a more sustainable path for ESG communication. These studies connect the bibliometric clusters centred on "ESG", "green finance", and "corporate governance" with the broader literature on information quality and organisational resilience.
The six strands summarised in table no. 3 show that the intersection of misinformation, green markets, and consumption is already supported by a substantial but fragmented body of research. Conceptual frameworks clarify the phenomenon of greenwashing, consumer-level studies document its attitudinal and behavioural effects, firm-level analyses reveal economic incentives and risks, while work on fake news and ESG communication extends the discussion into digital platforms and financial markets. This synthesis provides an interpretive bridge between the bibliometric clusters identified in Section 3.3 and the broader implications discussed in the concluding section.
Conclusions
This paper has provided a cross-domain bibliometric mapping of research at the intersection of misinformation, green markets, and rational or sustainable consumption. Using a Scopusbased corpus of 451 documents published between 2010 and 2025, the study combined descriptive indicators, performance analysis, and science-mapping techniques to trace the evolution of this field in terms of volume, impact, and thematic structure. The results show a marked acceleration in both publications and citations after 2018, indicating that the interplay among fake news, greenwashing, and sustainable consumption has become a visible, rapidly expanding research area rather than a marginal topic within environmental communication or marketing.
The science-mapping results reveal a set of interconnected clusters that structure this field. One cluster centres on greenwashing, corporate social responsibility, and environmental communication; a second focuses on consumer trust, attitudes, and behavioural responses to perceived deception; a third links fake news, social media, and product-harm misinformation; and a fourth brings in ESG disclosure, green finance and questions of governance. By synthesising the key contributions in each of these clusters, the paper shows that the literature is already rich in conceptual models and case-based evidence but remains fragmented across disciplines and problem framings.
Methodologically, the study demonstrates the value of combining co-word analysis, bibliographic coupling, and co-authorship networks to uncover not only what is being studied, but also who collaborates with whom and how research fronts emerge across countries. Substantively, the findings highlight three overarching insights. First, greenwashing and misinformation are not peripheral issues but central factors that shape the credibility of sustainability communication and the effectiveness of pro-environmental policies. Second, consumer-level reactions, trust, scepticism, perceived authenticity, and social norms, constitute a critical transmission mechanism between corporate or institutional communication and market or societal outcomes. Third, governance and ESG disclosure frameworks can either mitigate or amplify the risks posed by misleading environmental information, depending on their design and implementation.
In this sense, misinformation and greenwashing distort sustainability signals. These mechanisms can misdirect both consumer choices and capital allocation, slowing down the transition toward genuinely sustainable markets. At the same time, they increase the risk that markets favour symbolic sustainability efforts over actual environmental impact. Importantly, the relatively peripheral position of fake news in the conceptual maps suggests that this dimension remains underdeveloped compared to greenwashing and consumerfocused research. The findings also suggest that AI-generated misinformation and synthetic environmental content remain underexplored within the current literature, indicating an important avenue for future interdisciplinary research. While public debate increasingly highlights the role of deepfakes, generative AI, and synthetic media in shaping sustainability narratives, these themes have not yet emerged as a central research cluster in the bibliometric corpus analysed in this study.
These insights have several practical implications. For firms, the results underscore that short-term gains from opportunistic greenwashing are likely to be outweighed by longer-term losses in trust, brand equity, and investor confidence. For policymakers and regulators, the mapping of research clusters suggests that measures to improve the transparency and comparability of environmental claims should be complemented by interventions to address digital misinformation and the architecture of social media platforms. For civil-society organisations and consumer advocates, the identified strands underscore the importance of strengthening media literacy and critical capacities in relation not only to political fake news but also to commercial and sustainability-related claims.
At the same time, the study has limitations that open avenues for future work. The analysis is based on a single database, includes only English-language publications, and relies on specific threshold choices in constructing bibliometric networks. Future research could extend the corpus to other languages and databases, perform longitudinal comparisons of preand postpandemic dynamics, and combine bibliometric mapping with in-depth qualitative synthesis of the most influential articles. Another promising direction is to link bibliometric indicators with real-world policy or market events, thereby assessing how scholarly debates on misinformation and green markets respond to major regulatory changes, scandals, or crises.
The paper offers an integrated cartography of a fragmented but increasingly consequential body of research. By situating individual studies within broader thematic clusters and collaboration networks, it provides a foundation for more coherent theoretical development and empirically informed work on how misinformation and greenwashing shape the transition towards genuinely sustainable consumption and business practice.
