1 Introduction
Vaccine hesitancy has been recognised by the World Health Organization as one of the ten leading threats to global health, and the COVID-19 pandemic brought this challenge into sharp focus [1] . Among the factors associated with refusal, conspiracy theories, that is, claims that vaccines contain microchips, alter human DNA, or serve as instruments of population surveillance, achieved unprecedented global reach during 2020 and 2021, complicating immunisation efforts across diverse cultural settings [2,3] . Systematic analyses identified recurring conspiracy narratives that transcended national boundaries, suggesting both coordinated disinformation campaigns and organic rumour amplification through social networks [4,5] .
The microchip conspiracy theory, which posits that COVID-19 vaccines contain electronic surveillance technology, emerged in early 2020 and has been documented in North America, Europe, and parts of Asia [6,7] . Research on such narratives in African contexts remains comparatively sparse, despite the continent's vulnerability to delayed vaccination campaigns [8] . Nigeria offers a cautionary historical parallel: the boycott of polio vaccination campaigns in several northern states during 2003 and 2004, fuelled by conspiracy theories concerning vaccine safety and Western motives, led to polio resurgence and international spread to previously polio-free countries [9] .
Sub-Saharan Africa presents distinctive characteristics for studying misinformation and health decision-making. Rapid mobile technology adoption alongside persistent gaps in digital literacy has created information environments in which traditional media, social networks, and interpersonal communication intersect in ways that may differ from high-income settings [10] . Trust networks, including family members, religious leaders, and community authorities, play a central role in shaping health decisions [11,12] .
With over 218 million inhabitants and marked cultural, linguistic, and religious diversity, Nigeria provides a critical setting for examining how global misinformation narratives interact with local information ecosystems [13,14] . This study investigates the prevalence, correlates, and association with vaccine refusal of specific COVID-19 conspiracy theories among diverse Nigerian populations at a time of low vaccine uptake. Our objectives were to quantify the prevalence of specific conspiracy beliefs among vaccine-hesitant individuals, to identify demographic and behavioural factors associated with endorsement, to examine the relationship between information sources and conspiracy adoption, and to assess the independent association between conspiracy beliefs and vaccination intentions after adjustment for sociodemographic confounders.
2 Methods
2.1 Study design and setting
We conducted a cross-sectional survey between 2 October 2023 and 30 April 2024 to investigate conspiracy theory beliefs and their relationship with vaccination intentions. Although COVID-19 vaccines had been available in Nigeria since March 2021, national coverage remained low when these data were collected, with only a minority of adults fully vaccinated [15] . The survey therefore captured stated intentions and conspiracy beliefs in a setting of persistently low uptake, rather than before any vaccine was available. We framed the primary outcome question hypothetically, as set out below, so that willingness could be gauged independently of local supply or access on the day of interview.
Convenience sampling was employed across Nigeria's six geopolitical regions (Northwest, Northeast, North Central, Southeast, South-South, Southwest). Within each region, we selected urban centres, rural communities, and informal settlements where safe data collection could be conducted. Local research coordinators identified sites on the basis of accessibility, security, and community willingness to participate. At least one urban centre, one rural community, and one informal settlement were sampled in every region. Within each site, research assistants approached prospective participants in public and community settings, including markets, motor parks, places of worship, primary healthcare centres, and residential streets, on weekdays and weekends and during daytime and early-evening hours. We applied no fixed demographic quotas. To limit selection bias, recruitment locations and times were varied within each site, and assistants approached successive eligible adults as footfall allowed rather than selecting people by appearance or apparent attitude.
2.2 Participants
Eligible participants were adults aged 18–70 years who had resided in the study area for at least six months and could provide informed consent in English or a local language. We excluded individuals with severe mental illness precluding consent, those with acute illness, and temporary visitors. Our target of approximately 145 participants per region (total 870) was determined by practical constraints rather than by formal power calculations. Of approximately 1134 eligible adults approached, 870 completed the interview, giving an overall response rate of 76.7%. The remaining 264 individuals (23.3%) declined or did not complete the interview. We calculated the response rate as the number of completed interviews divided by the number of eligible adults approached. Response rates by participant subgroup are reported in Table 1 .
2.3 Data collection
Trained research assistants conducted face-to-face structured interviews lasting 35 to 40 min. All interviewers completed 20 h of training covering interview techniques, ethical considerations, and neutral ways of discussing conspiracy beliefs. Quality assurance comprised daily data review, supervisor observation of 15% of interviews, and re-interviews of 3% of participants. Interviews were conducted in English or, where the participant preferred, in Hausa, Yoruba, Igbo, or Nigerian Pidgin. The English instrument was translated into each local language by bilingual research assistants and then independently back translated into English; the project team reconciled any discrepancies by consensus before fieldwork began.
2.4 Measurements
We assessed specific COVID-19 vaccine conspiracy theories using items adapted from published conspiracy belief measures [6,16] and from a content analysis of misinformation narratives circulating during 2020 and 2021 [17] . Categories assessed included beliefs that vaccines contain electronic surveillance technology, theories about fertility reduction, genetic modification claims, religious or eschatological theories framing vaccines as the “mark of the beast”, and economic exploitation theories. For each category, participants indicated their level of agreement on a five-point scale. We created binary variables comparing believers (the two agreement categories) with non-believers (the two disagreement categories), and analysed uncertain responses separately. Three subject experts reviewed the draft instrument for content validity. We then pilot tested it with 30 adults across two regions who were not included in the final sample, and made minor wording changes in response. The six-item conspiracy belief scale showed acceptable internal consistency (Cronbach's α = 0.79).
The primary outcome was COVID-19 vaccine acceptance: “If a COVID-19 vaccine were available to you today that had been approved by Nigerian health authorities, would you take it?” (Yes, No, or Unsure). We also recorded each participant's primary source of COVID-19 information and the source that the participant felt had most influenced their health decisions.
2.5 Statistical analysis
Prevalence estimates with 95% confidence intervals were calculated for each conspiracy theory. Associations between conspiracy beliefs and demographic characteristics were examined using chi-square tests and logistic regression [18] .
We conducted two multivariable logistic regression analyses. Model A examined predictors of conspiracy theory endorsement. Model B examined predictors of vaccine acceptance, entering the same demographic and contextual covariates together with conspiracy endorsement (as both a binary variable and an ordinal count, in separate specifications). To assess whether conspiracy endorsement might account for part of the observed associations between sociodemographic factors and vaccine refusal, we compared the coefficients for education, region, and information source between models with and without conspiracy endorsement, using a change-in-estimate approach. Interaction terms between conspiracy endorsement and region, and between conspiracy endorsement and education, were tested in Model B. Item-level missing data were uncommon, affecting less than 3% of responses for every variable. We analysed complete cases in the regression models and did not impute missing values.
Population-weighted estimates were derived using post-stratification weights based on 2018 Nigeria Demographic and Health Survey data for educational attainment and urban and rural distribution [13] . All analyses were conducted in Stata 17.0, with statistical significance set at α = 0.05.
2.6 Ethical considerations
The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the University of Freiburg (Protocol No. 140/19) and by the Nasarawa State University, Keffi Ethics Committee. All participants provided written informed consent.
3 Results
3.1 Participant characteristics
We completed interviews with 870 participants across all six regions ( Table 1 ). Participants were predominantly female (71.1%), highly educated (60.4% tertiary), and employed in the civil service (92.5%), with a mean age of 32.4 years (SD 9.7). Television was the dominant information source (82.0%), followed by social networks (17.9%).
3.2 Vaccine acceptance and conspiracy theory prevalence
Overall vaccine acceptance was 14.7% (128/870). Among the 742 vaccine-hesitant participants, 89.4% (95% CI 87.1–91.4%) endorsed at least one conspiracy theory [16] . Microchip theories were the most frequently cited belief (32.1%; Table 2 ). Population-weighted estimates were consistently higher than the crude figs. [19] .
3.3 Predictors of conspiracy theory endorsement (Model A)
Women endorsed microchip theories at higher rates than men (30.0% vs 20.7%; p  = 0.005). Lower education and rural residence were associated with greater endorsement (Supplementary Table S1; Fig. 1 ). Social network use as a primary information source showed the strongest association (aOR 3.12; 95% CI 1.98–4.91). The seven predictors collectively accounted for an estimated 40.2% of the population attributable risk [20,21] .
3.4 Graded association between conspiracy endorsement and vaccine refusal
Vaccine acceptance declined stepwise as the number of endorsed theories rose, from 24.1% among those endorsing none to 0% among those endorsing four or more (p for trend <0.001; Table 3 ; Fig. 2 ). In the ordinal logistic model, each additional theory was associated with an average 69% lower odds of acceptance (aOR 0.31; 95% CI 0.25–0.39). The decline was steepest between zero and one theory and flattened at higher counts ( Fig. 2 ), so this per-theory figure should be read as an average across the range rather than as a constant step. Acceptance reached only 24.1% even among participants who endorsed no conspiracy theory, which indicates that much of the refusal in this sample was associated with factors other than the conspiracy beliefs we measured. Among microchip theory endorsers, acceptance was 2.9%, compared with 24.1% among non-endorsers (OR 0.10; 95% CI 0.05–0.22) [22] .
3.5 Predictors of vaccine acceptance before and after adjustment for conspiracy endorsement (Model B)
Before adding conspiracy endorsement to the model, we examined sociodemographic and contextual predictors of vaccine acceptance on their own. In this demographics-only model, lower education, rural or informal residence, residence in the northern regions, lower government trust, and reliance on non-healthcare information sources were each associated with lower acceptance. When conspiracy endorsement was then entered, several of these associations were attenuated, which suggests that conspiracy beliefs accounted for part, though not all, of the sociodemographic pattern in refusal.
Conspiracy endorsement was the factor most strongly associated with vaccine refusal among the variables we modelled: participants endorsing any conspiracy theory had 89% lower odds of acceptance (aOR 0.11; 95% CI 0.06–0.21; p  < 0.001; Table 4 ). When entered as an ordinal variable, each additional theory was associated with 69% lower odds (aOR 0.31; 95% CI 0.25–0.39).
Including conspiracy endorsement in Model B produced notable changes in the other coefficients. The association between education and vaccine acceptance, significant in unadjusted analysis, was attenuated (aOR 1.78; 95% CI 1.08–2.93; p  = 0.024). The regional effect became non-significant (aOR 0.68; 95% CI 0.40–1.15; p  = 0.152). This pattern of attenuation suggests that the associations between these sociodemographic factors and vaccine refusal may be partly accounted for by differential conspiracy endorsement, although the cross-sectional design precludes establishing the temporal sequence required for formal causal inference. Government trust retained an independent association (aOR 1.89; 95% CI 1.18–3.03; p  = 0.008), which suggests that institutional trust relates to vaccine acceptance through pathways not fully captured by conspiracy beliefs [5,23] .
3.6 Interaction analyses
The conspiracy endorsement by region interaction was not significant ( p  = 0.34), which indicates that the association between conspiracy beliefs and refusal was broadly consistent across regions. The conspiracy by education interaction also did not reach significance ( p  = 0.19). These null findings should be interpreted cautiously given possible underpowering.
3.7 Information sources and decision-making influence
Television dominated reported information consumption, yet the sources that acceptors said had shaped their decision were different ( Table 5 ; Fig. 3 ). When asked what most influenced their choice, vaccine acceptors most often cited their own judgement (54.7%), whereas government sources were named by a single respondent (0.8%) [24,25] . Because both information exposure and decision influence were self-reported and recorded at a single time point, the contrast between the two panels of Fig. 3 should be read as a descriptive association rather than as evidence that any source caused, or failed to cause, a given decision. A respondent could, for example, rely on television for information and still describe the final choice as a matter of personal judgement.
3.8 Regional variations
A consistent gradient from north to south was seen in both conspiracy endorsement and vaccine acceptance ( Table 6 ; Fig. 4 ). The Northwest had the highest endorsement (78.6%) and the lowest acceptance (7.6%), and the Southwest had the lowest endorsement (45.9%) and the highest acceptance (24.7%; p for trend <0.001) [26] . This gradient was consistent across all specific conspiracy types ( Fig. 4 ).
4 Discussion
This study documents widespread endorsement of COVID-19 vaccine conspiracy theories among Nigerian communities at a time of low vaccine uptake. Nearly one third of vaccine-hesitant participants cited microchip or tracking device theories as their main concern, and 89.4% endorsed at least one conspiracy theory, so conspiracy belief was common among those who declined the vaccine. We did not measure conventional concerns, such as doubts about safety, fear of side effects, or problems of access, and we therefore cannot say whether misinformation outweighed these more familiar barriers. The high refusal seen even among participants who endorsed no conspiracy theory, where acceptance reached only 24.1%, suggests that such concerns were also at work [27] .
4.1 Comparison with existing evidence
These findings are consistent with evidence from across sub-Saharan Africa. Multi-country studies have identified trust in government and society as key correlates of vaccine hesitancy, with conspiracy theories serving as consistent associated factors [28–30] . In Ghana, an audio drama intervention showed promise in countering specific misinformation narratives [31] . South African research has documented similar patterns of microchip conspiracy endorsement [32] . Qualitative work from Nigeria by Wonodi and colleagues identified overlapping conspiracy themes but could not quantify their prevalence or assess their independent association with vaccine refusal [33] . Our study extends this evidence in two respects: it quantifies the graded association between the number of conspiracy theories endorsed and vaccine refusal, and it shows, through a change-in-estimate approach, that the associations between sociodemographic factors and refusal are substantially attenuated after accounting for conspiracy endorsement [34] . Our crude acceptance figure of 14.7% sits below the pooled estimates reported for many low- and middle-income settings, where stated willingness was often higher even where hesitancy was substantial [4,23] . Surveys from West Africa have likewise reported high hesitancy alongside low institutional trust [35] , and Nigerian studies of healthcare workers and the general population have described comparable unease [36,37] .
4.2 Information ecosystems and trust networks
Government sources were almost entirely absent from the decision influences that acceptors reported (0.8%; Fig. 3 ). The gap between what people consumed, largely television, and what they said had shaped their decision, largely personal judgement and religious leaders, points to a possible weakness in formal health communication, although it rests on self-report [23] . Social network use was strongly associated with conspiracy endorsement (aOR 3.12), and the association between social network use and vaccine refusal was attenuated after adjustment for conspiracy beliefs. One reading is that misinformation may reach decision-making mainly through interpersonal networks rather than through direct digital exposure [38] , but the cross-sectional data cannot establish that sequence.
Religious leaders were named by a measurable share of acceptors (18.0%), yet religious attendance was simultaneously associated with higher conspiracy endorsement [36,37] . This dual role complicates straightforward prescriptions about engaging faith-based networks.
4.3 Regional patterns
The gradient from north to south ( Fig. 4 ) probably reflects historical tensions between northern communities and federal health initiatives, including the 2003 and 2004 polio boycott, alongside differences in educational attainment [9] . That the gradient held across all five conspiracy types suggests generalised susceptibility rather than selective adoption of particular narratives. The apparent association between Muslim identity and higher conspiracy endorsement (41.2% vs 28.7%) was substantially attenuated after adjustment for region and education, which suggests demographic confounding.
4.4 Implications
The specificity of conspiracy beliefs offers practical entry points for intervention. Rather than generic vaccine education, programmes could address particular false claims with targeted counter-narratives delivered through trusted interpersonal channels [35,39,40] . The comparatively low conspiracy endorsement among participants who received information from healthcare providers (16.7% vs 67.3% for social network users), together with the independent protective association in Model B, offers tentative support for healthcare engagement as a protective factor, although the numbers were small.
Several directions for further research follow from these findings. The graded association we describe should be tested with longitudinal designs that record conspiracy beliefs before a vaccination decision is made, which would help to separate belief from rationalisation after the fact. A validated, locally developed conspiracy belief scale would allow comparison across studies and over time. Trials that pair specific counter-narratives with trusted messengers, rather than generic education, would show whether addressing named claims changes uptake, and the audio drama work from Ghana offers one tested format [31,34] .
For prevention, the pattern of results points away from broad government messaging and towards prebunking, that is, inoculating audiences against specific false claims before they spread, delivered through the channels people actually trust [14,17] . Because endorsement clustered by region, education, and information source, campaigns could be targeted rather than uniform, with the heaviest investment in the northern regions, where endorsement was highest and acceptance lowest.
For policy and pandemic preparedness, the near-absence of government sources among the reported decision influences is a warning. Health authorities may need to work through religious and community leaders, and through primary care, rather than relying on official channels alone, while recognising that faith networks can carry both helpful and harmful messages. Misinformation surveillance, built into routine immunisation monitoring, would let programmes detect and answer emerging claims before the next campaign.
4.5 Limitations
The most consequential limitation is convenience sampling, which yielded a sample skewed towards educated, urban, female civil servants. This likely underestimates conspiracy prevalence, since the over-represented characteristics were associated with lower endorsement. Post-stratification weighting partially addresses, but cannot fully compensate for, this bias. Although the conspiracy assessment instrument was pilot tested and showed acceptable internal consistency, it has not undergone full psychometric validation. The cross-sectional design precludes causal inference: the change-in-estimate findings are consistent with conspiracy beliefs lying on a pathway between sociodemographic factors and vaccine refusal, but alternative explanations, including reverse causation and unmeasured confounding of the conspiracy and refusal relationship, cannot be excluded. Interaction analyses may have been underpowered. Data were collected several years into the pandemic, when vaccines were available but uptake was low, so the attitudes recorded reflect that particular period and may since have shifted. We did not measure conventional barriers to vaccination, such as concerns about safety, side effects, cost, or access, so we cannot weigh misinformation against these factors; the high refusal among participants who endorsed no conspiracy theory shows that such barriers were present. Finally, information sources and decision influences were self-reported, and self-report cannot establish that any source did, or did not, drive a decision.
5 Conclusions
COVID-19 vaccine conspiracy theories were widespread in Nigerian communities at a time of low vaccine uptake, with microchip theories the most frequently cited concern. The graded inverse association between the number of theories endorsed and vaccine acceptance, together with the attenuation of sociodemographic predictors after adjustment for conspiracy beliefs, identifies conspiracy endorsement as both a cumulative correlate of refusal and a potential point of intervention.
The gap between formal health communication and the influences people reported points to structural weaknesses that are likely to recur with future pandemic vaccines. Whilst convenience sampling limits generalisability, the consistency of findings across regions, and the convergence with evidence from other African countries, suggests that conspiracy-associated vaccine refusal is not uniquely Nigerian. Future preparedness must address health misinformation with the same seriousness as the pathogens themselves.
